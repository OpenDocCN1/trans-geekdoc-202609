
# AI and Robotics

AI and robotics are closely linked both in research and in our imagination, notably because of the literary production of science fiction which, since the beginning of the 20th Century, has never ceased to offer us universes populated by robots, endowed with an intelligence equal, if not superior, to that of humans, and always presenting a challenge for the latter.

From the few definitions retained (Table 2.3), we will say the robot:

*   is mainly a machine;
*   is programmable;
*   has a specific function;
*   has a degree of freedom that is controlled and decided by the humans (who program it);
*   has a resemblance to humans which is related to its functionality (seeing, moving, grasping objects, etc.), more than its appearance.

Several ideas are thus associated with the "robot":

*   It is designed to help, to assist humans: the automatons of the 17th and 19th Centuries are machines similar to humans and have an entertainment function, on the one hand (by playing an instrument, or by the quasi-magical nature of their existence and the technical prowess of their creators), and, on the other hand, allow people to imagine a future in which the machine will carry out tasks for people.
*   It is the symbol of a future world: the robot is present in futuristic stories where terrestrial and extraterrestrial universes are mixed. It is omnipresent in spatial conquest.
*   It is an existential threat: Karel Capek's robot (1920) is the archetype of the robot that constitutes a threat to humanity because it will enslave it. The robot first frees humans from their tasks, then the roles are reversed; the humans' creation is turned against them. The robot becomes monstrous.
*   It is an actor of conflict, of violence: the science-fiction news robot is often a fighter who confronts humans or other robots. It confronts other heroes: in 1963, Russ Manning created an anti-robot hero, Magnus, Robot Fighter; also read *The Human Bat V the Robot Gangster* (Edward R. Hime-Gall).
*   It is a pretext for reflections on the human condition, on the relationship between human and machine: if people have their own laws, rules, principles, morals and ethics, can't robots have them too?

## 2.2. Types of AI

Typologies are used to classify, order and create categories, and provide reading grids that allow a concept to be grasped in its multiple facets. The exercise is necessarily simplifying and can be marked by subjectivity.

One of the most common typologies, devised in the 1960s, is based on the distinction between:

*   **Weak or narrow AIs**, which are AIs dedicated to a specific task or function. These machines do not learn by themselves, all rules must be given to them beforehand (example: all the rules of a game). The weak AI is the one that can be produced in the current state of knowledge and can therefore be the subject of State AI strategies: "The Federal Government has oriented its strategy to the use of AI to solve specific problems, i.e. to the 'weak' approach"¹⁵.
*   **Strong or broad/general AI**, capable of self-learning, then deciding and acting like an individual. Such AIs do not yet exist; they are capable of thinking in a general way and making decisions based on learning rather than training. Multiple tests define general AI: the Turing Test; the Coffee Test proposed by Steve Wozniak in 2007; the Robot College Student Test; and the Employment Test proposed by Nils John Nilsson in 2005.

Some authors add a third tier, proposing: Weak AI, General AI, and Super AI or Superior AI:

> "Super AI is an AI far more intelligent than humans. Super AI does not currently exist, but it has been proposed that it could be built in the future, bringing massive and potentially catastrophic consequences." [BAU 18]

This tier of AI is higher than General AI: General AI's intelligence level equals that of humans; Super AI surpasses this. Super AI leads to essentially catastrophic issues, revolving around existential threats, expressing humanity's fear of an intelligent behavior that humans cannot predict or control, behavior that could lead to humans losing control.

**Table 2.5.** `AI Typology`

| | High AI (Strong, General) or AGI (Artificial General Intelligence) | Low AI (Weak, Narrow) or ANI (Artificial Narrow Intelligence) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Refers to any autonomous AI system whose intellectual capabilities equal or exceed those of humans. | This AI system focuses on solving specific problems. |
| | These machines operate strictly within the framework of the scenarios for which they were programmed. | |
| Resulting Intelligent Machines | None. | AlphaGo (Google), Watson (IBM), Siri (Apple), etc. Most current AIs belong to the Low AI category. |

Another two-category distinction of AI types distinguishes AI Type 1 and Type 2:

*   **Type 1** includes the two types of Low AI and High AI mentioned above.
*   **Type 2** differs:
    *   **Reactive machines**, which do not retain past memory to alter decisions or future choices.
    *   **Limited memory systems**, which use past information to guide future decisions (e.g., autonomous driving systems, where environmental observations are not permanently stored).
    *   **AI capable of "understanding" individuals' emotions, behaviors, and thoughts, and capable of social interaction**.
    *   Finally, **self-aware AI**, similar to humans.

Thus, a distinction is made between AI achievable in the current state of knowledge and AI not achievable in the short and medium term.

These types may be subdivided based on AI application fields or main research and development directions: "Artificial Intelligence contains the following main sub-fields: expert or knowledge-based systems, natural language processing, artificial or computer vision, sound perception/understanding, and robotics." [ALB 88]

### 2.3.2. The AAAI Magazine

`AAAI` (Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence), founded in 1979, later renamed the Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence. Since 1980, the association publishes `AI Magazine`, all issues available on its website¹⁶. We searched this corpus (1980–2019) for several terms related to the AI field to observe the evolution of their use by the research community. Our observation is limited to article titles, not including abstracts or full article content. Perhaps analyzing full content would yield significantly different results. But titles alone reflect the core research themes presented; we consider this sufficiently relevant at this stage¹⁷.

We note the "expert systems" concept appeared frequently between 1980–2000, then no longer appears in article titles, seemingly losing attention gradually.
