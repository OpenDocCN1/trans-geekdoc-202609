
# Problem 1 (Faulty Data Detection in Direct Sensory Matrix (FDSM))

Given `S` and `E`, the FDSM problem is to find `D`, the expectation of `D` is as close to `F` as possible. That is, Objective: `min ||D − F||_F` Subject to: `S`, `E`

$$\text{Objective : }\min||D-F||_F\\\text{Subject to : }S,E\tag{5}$$

where `||·||_F` is the Frobenius norm used to measure the difference between `D` and `F`. That is, `||D−F||_F = √∑_{i,j}(D(i,j)−F(i,j))^2`.

The second problem is to reconstruct the real traffic condition (TCM) based on the sensory data (DSM).

# Problem 2 (Traffic Condition Reconstruction in Road Networks (TCRN))

Given `S` and `B`, the TCRN problem is to find the optimal `\hat{X}` that approximates `X` as closely as possible, that is, Objective: `min ||X − \hat{X}||_F` Subject to: `S`, `B`

$$\text{Objective : }\min||X-\hat{X}||_F\\\text{Subject to : }S,B\tag{6}$$

## 3 Traffic Condition Data Mining

We gather the real-time traffic condition data from AMAP [2], which is available on the Internet. The dataset collects the mean velocity of roads in the Chengdu downtown region, covering a period of 24 h on November 25, 2017. It contains 1487 roads and 288 time slots, with slot intervals of 5 min. However, we cannot utilize the raw data from AMAP because of the existence of missing and faulty data, which will lead to the lack of ground truth. To generate TCM, we perform preprocessing and select complete subsets from raw data. The selected subset contains 617 roads × 288 slots.

### 3.1 Discovery Over Low Rank Structure

The mean velocity of different roads over different times are not independent; there are structures. We reveal the inherent structure with the singular value decomposition (SVD), which is a kind of factorization of a matrix. SVD is usually used for creating a low rank matrix approximation. The matrix `(x(i,j))_{n×t}` can be decomposed as:

$$X=U\Sigma V^T=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(n,t)}\sigma_i u_i v_i^T\tag{7}$$

where `U` is an `n × n` unitary matrix (i.e., `UU^T = U^T U = I`), `V^T` is the transpose of an `t × t` unitary matrix, `Σ` is an `n × t` diagonal matrix with the singular value `σ_i` of `X` on the main diagonal, where `σ_i ≥ σ_{i+1}`, `i = 1,2,…, min(n,t)`. The matrix is low rank if `r≪min(n,t)`, `r` is equal to nonzero singular values. `σ_i` represents the `i`-th largest singular value of `X`, which means `X` can be approximately denoted by top `r` singular values.

### 3.2 Temporal Stability Feature

Generally, when the time interval between adjacent time slots is not too large, the mean velocity of roads remains stable. Based on this observation, we analyze the traffic condition data in the temporal dimension to reveal temporal features. We measure the temporal stability of road `i` at time slot `j` by calculating the difference between adjacent time slots:

$$\Delta x(i,j) = |x(i,j) - x(i,j-1)| \tag{9}$$

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of `\Delta x(i,j)` is plotted in Figure 3B. The X-axis represents the normalized difference of adjacent time slots, and the Y-axis shows the cumulative probability of `\Delta x(i,j)`. We can observe that more than 40% of `\Delta x(i,j)` in TCM are 0, and more than 95% of `\Delta x(i,j)` are less than 0.2. This indicates that TCM exhibits temporal stability. Based on this observation, we can improve compressive sensing by adding a temporal feature dimension.

### 3.3 Spatial Correlation Feature

From the road network in Figure 1, it can be seen that there are many adjacent roads, and the mean velocities of adjacent roads are usually similar. Therefore, we can consider the difference from the spatial dimension.

In the traffic condition dataset from AMAP, roads are represented by multi-segment polylines containing several latitude and longitude coordinates. Therefore, we can use these latitude and longitude coordinates to determine whether two roads are adjacent.

$$H(a,b) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } a \text{ and } b \text{ are adjacent} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{10}$$

where `a = 1, 2, ..., n` and `b = 1, 2, ..., n`. ARM is an `n × n` matrix, denoted as `H`, representing the spatial correlation between any two roads.

We measure the spatial correlation of road `i` at time slot `j` by calculating the difference between the mean velocity of road `i` and the average mean velocity of all its adjacent roads. `\theta x(i,j)` is calculated as follows:

$$\theta x(i,j) = \left| x(i,j) - \frac{H(i,:) X(:,j)}{\sum H(i,:)} \right| \tag{11}$$

where `H(i,:)` is the `i`-th row of `H`, `X(:,j)` is the `j`-th column of `X`. `H(i,:)X(:,j)` represents the sum of mean velocities of all adjacent roads of road `i` at time slot `j`. `\sum H(i,:)` represents the number of adjacent roads of road `i`.

# 4 ODC Method Based on Compressive Sensing

Based on the previous observations, we propose and elaborate the ODC method in this section.

The ODC method is used to identify and correct faulty data in the traffic condition dataset; therefore, the ODC method contains two main processes: identification and correction.

The ODC method is based on compressive sensing technology, which performs significantly in reconstructing missing data in structured or redundant datasets.

However, when there is a large amount of faulty data in the dataset, the reconstruction result is not very effective; that is, faulty data leads to large deviations during reconstruction.

Therefore, we need to discover faulty data before correction to mitigate reconstruction deviation.

Figure 4 shows the procedure flowchart of the ODC method. We take Direct Sensory Matrix `S`, Existence Matrix `E`, ARM `H`, `\lambda_1`, `\lambda_2`, `\lambda_3`, rank bound `r`, and iteration count `maxIter` as inputs, and output Reconstruct Matrix `\hat{X}` and Faulty Data Detection Matrix `D`.

`D` is initialized to all 1 at the beginning of the process because the ODC method aims to discover more faulty data.

The ODC method contains three main functions: `FaultyDataDetection()`, `ASD()`, and `updateFDM()`. The task of `FaultyDataDetection()` is to distinguish faulty data using their spatiotemporal correlations and mark faulty data points in `D`.

Subsequently, ODC merges `E` with `D` and marks trusted data points in `B`.

`B` serves as the input for `ASD()` to reconstruct missing data in the dataset (both faulty data and missing data are treated as missing data in `ASD()`).

The output of `ASD()` is Reconstruct Matrix `\hat{X}`, and `updateFDM()` compares `\hat{X}` with `S` to update the Faulty Data Detection Matrix `D`.

The ODC method stops when `D` remains unchanged after an update or when the iteration count reaches `maxIter`.

## 4.2 Optimization of Compressive Sensing

Compressive sensing is an effective technique for reconstructing missing data of sparse matrices. The core idea of compressive sensing is that real-world datasets usually contain structures or redundancies, which we demonstrated for the traffic condition dataset in Section 3.1. Therefore, we utilize compressive sensing to compute the estimation of traffic condition matrix `\hat{X}` to approximate the real traffic condition matrix `X`.

Due to the low-rank property of `X`, we can approximate `X` by its top `r` largest singular values. According to Eqs. (7), (8), we can represent `X` as

$$\hat{X} = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T \tag{12}$$

In fact, `\hat{X}` is the best rank-`r` approximation that minimizes the Frobenius norm between `X` and `\hat{X}`.

However, since we do not know `X` and the correct rank, directly solving for `\hat{X}` is impossible. Therefore, we can solve the following rank minimization problem:

Objective: `min(rank(\hat{X}))`

Subject to: `\hat{X} \circ B = S` \tag{13}

where `\circ` denotes the Hadamard product (`\hat{X} \circ B = S` means `\hat{X}(i,j)B(i,j)=S(i,j)`).

However, since this minimization problem is non-convex and difficult to solve, we adopt the SVD-like decomposition of `\hat{X}`:

$$\hat{X} = U \Sigma V = L R^T \tag{14}$$

where `L = U \Sigma^{1/2}`, `R = V \Sigma^{1/2}`. According to compressive sensing theory, if the restricted isometry property [6] is satisfied, minimizing the nuclear norm [7–9] can exactly achieve rank minimization of low-rank matrices; that is, we only need to find matrices `L` and `R` that minimize the sum of their Frobenius norms:

Objective: `min(||L||_F^2 + ||R||_F^2)`

Subject to: `(L R^T) \circ B = S` \tag{15}

In practice, `L` and `R` that strictly satisfy the constraints often fail for two reasons. First, noise in the sensory data may lead to overfitting problems. Second, TCM may be a low-rank matrix but not necessarily an exact low-rank one. Therefore, we relax the constraints using Lagrange multipliers:

$$\min\left(\|(L R^T)\circ B - S\|_F^2 + \lambda_1\left(\|L\|_F^2 + \|R\|_F^2\right)\right) \tag{16}$$

The spatial constraint matrix is denoted by `\mathbb{H}`, which describes the difference between adjacent roads in the same time slot; its definition is similar to `H` in [10].

`\mathbb{H}` is indeed a transformation of ARM `H`: (1) The main diagonal of `\mathbb{H}` is given by `\mathbb{H}(i,i)=(-\sum H(i,:))`, where `H(i,:)` denotes the `i`-th row of `H`. The other elements of `\mathbb{H}` are the same as `H`. (2) Normalize the rows of `\mathbb{H}` to obtain the final `\mathbb{H}`.

For example, if there is an ARM `H`:

$$\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & 1 \\
1 & 0 & 1 \\
1 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}\tag{18}$$

After the aforementioned transformation steps, the spatial constraint matrix is:

$$\mathbb{H} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & -1 \\
\frac{1}{2} & 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\
-1 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}\tag{19}$$

Obviously, `\mathbb{H} L R^T` represents the difference between the `i`-th road and the average of its adjacent roads.

Similar to `L R^T \mathbb{T}`, `\mathbb{H} L R^T` captures the spatial correlation of the traffic condition dataset, which was mined in Section 3.3.

We can also introduce the spatial constraint `\|\mathbb{H} L R^T\|_F^2` into Eq. (16) to help estimate `L R^T` more accurately.

Therefore, after utilizing the spatiotemporal stability features of the traffic condition dataset, we optimize the compressive sensing method by constructing Eq. (16):

$$\min\left(\|\left(LR^T\right)\circ B - S\|_F^2 + \lambda_1\left(\|L\|_F^2 + \|R\|_F^2\right) + \lambda_2\|\mathbb{H}LR^T\|_F^2 + \lambda_3\|LR^T\mathbb{T}\|_F^2\right)\tag{20}$$

where `\lambda_2` and `\lambda_3` serve as scaling coefficients for `\|\mathbb{H} L R^T\|_F^2` and `\|L R^T \mathbb{T}\|_F^2`, respectively.

### 4.2.3 Optimized Compressive Sensing Algorithm

The goal of the optimized compressive sensing algorithm is to find `L` and `R` that minimize Eq. (20).

We define the objective function as

$$f(L,R)=\|(LR^T)\circ B - S\|_F^2 + \lambda_1\left(\|L\|_F^2 + \|R\|_F^2\right) + \lambda_2\|\mathbb{H}LR^T\|_F^2 + \lambda_3\|LR^T\mathbb{T}\|_F^2\tag{21}$$

Obviously `f(L,R)` is non-convex. However, if we fix `L` or `R`, the function with respect to the other variable is convex.

Therefore, we use the Alternating Steepest Descent (ASD) algorithm [11,12] for minimization, which is commonly used for low-rank matrix completion. The pseudocode of ASD is shown in Algorithm 1, where `\Sigma_r` denotes the first `r` columns of `\Sigma`.

The core idea of ASD is to perform steepest descent gradient descent on `f(L,R)` with respect to `L` and `R` alternately. First, `L` and `R` are randomly initialized. Subsequently, we fix `L` and update `R` by a single step line search along the gradient descent direction. Then we fix `R` and update `L` in a similar manner. This process repeats until convergence (we consider convergence when the change in function value is less than a threshold).

To elaborate on the line search along the gradient descent direction, we denote

$$f(L,R)=f_1(L,R)+f_2(L,R)+f_3(L,R)+f_4(L,R)\tag{22}$$

where

$$f_1(L,R)=\|(LR^T)\circ B - S\|_F^2$$

$$f_2(L,R)=\lambda_1\left(\|L\|_F^2 + \|R\|_F^2\right)$$

$$f_3(L,R)=\lambda_2\|\mathbb{H}LR^T\|_F^2$$

$$f_4(L,R)=\lambda_3\|LR^T\mathbb{T}\|_F^2$$

The gradient descent directions are

$$\nabla_L = \nabla_L^1 + \nabla_L^2 + \nabla_L^3 + \nabla_L^4$$

$$\nabla_R = \nabla_R^1 + \nabla_R^2 + \nabla_R^3 + \nabla_R^4\tag{23}$$

$$\nabla_L^1 = 2((LR^T)\circ B - S)R$$

$$\nabla_L^2 = 2\lambda_1 L$$

$$\nabla_L^3 = 2\lambda_2 \mathbb{H}^T \mathbb{H} L R^T R$$

$$\nabla_L^4 = 2\lambda_3 L R^T \mathbb{T} \mathbb{T}^T R$$

$$\nabla_R^1 = 2((RL^T)\circ B^T - S^T)L$$

$$\nabla_R^2 = 2\lambda_1 R$$

$$\nabla_R^3 = 2\lambda_2 R L^T \mathbb{H}^T \mathbb{H} L$$

$$\nabla_R^4 = 2\lambda_3 \mathbb{T} \mathbb{T}^T R L^T L$$
