
这将进一步扰乱商业模式，尤其是当协作工具使星级表现者能够在没有大量支持人员的情况下高效工作时。大律师事务所、咨询公司以及投资银行可能会陷入困境：他们的最高计费合伙人，配备人工智能助手来完成琐碎工作，可能会离开并创办工作室。

星级价值的提升只是由人工智能带来的多种趋势之一，这些趋势预计会给许多公司带来剧烈波动。在许多情况下，协作工具将使竞争环境更加平等，使小企业能够更有效地与大企业竞争。以 João Ferrão dos Santos 为例，他是一家位于葡萄牙的在线 T‑恤公司的首席执行官。Dos Santos 于 2023 年 3 月在 LinkedIn 上宣布，他将把 ChatGPT 任命为自己初创公司的“首席执行官”。他说他将直接执行 ChatGPT 推荐的任务。该公司使用文本到图像生成软件 Midjourney 设计 T‑恤。Dos Santos 命名公司为 “AIsthetic Apparel”。公司最初投资 1,000 美元，并从外部投资者那里获得 2,500 美元，第一周即销售了 10,000 美元价值的 T‑恤。

除了微型企业外，还有其他公司试图从人工智能中获取战略方向：拥有约 10 亿美元年销售额的中国视频游戏公司 NetDragon Websoft 也声称任命了名为 Tang Yu 的 AI 机器人为首席执行官。

这些尝试彻底颠覆了人们与人工智能协作伙伴的传统关系。在此，人们自愿将决策权让给人工智能，转而成为其智能助手。但无需走到如此极端，人们已经可以看到协作伙伴的出现将促使更多专业人士创办小企业或独立执业，从而挤占大型公司的市场份额。

这一趋势将与另一种倾向——即在许多领域中更大的集中度——并存。数据在人工智能时代是权力的象征，行业中规模最大的玩家通常拥有最多数据——无论是客户数据、供应商数据、合同条款数据，还是其他形式的知识产权。通过人工智能对这些信息进行分析，他们能够比数据较少的竞争者做出更精准的预测。对大型建筑公司而言，这可能意味着更好的蓝图；对大型电影工作室而言，更好的剧本；对大型律师事务所而言，更好的合同。总体而言，数据飞轮效应将导致许多行业权力高度集中。中型企业可能会受苦最深：它们既不足以利用人工智能赋能的工作室的低成本优势，也缺乏最大玩家的数据优势，因而将被逐渐挤出市场。

## THE NEW AGE OF IP

在新时代，最关键的数据将是知识产权（IP）。所有企业都需要制定明确的策略，以最佳方式利用自身的 IP。拥有用于微调人工智能算法的正确数据，可能成为竞争优势的重要因素，决定哪些公司主导领域、哪些公司 struggle。

但拥有一本可以参考的过去 IP 库仅是一项资产。同样，也存在相互制约的力量：生成式人工智能依赖过去的 IP 进行训练，但它大幅降低了新内容和新 IP 的生产成本与时间。这将迫使传统依赖 IP 盈利的公司——如好莱坞工作室和出版房——与新一代创作者建立合作关系。

2023 年 5 月，导演 Paul Trillo 使用 Runway 的 Gen 2 生成式人工智能软件——该软件可从文本提示生成短视频片段——制作了一部名为 *Thank You for Not Answering* 的两分二十五秒短片。Trillo 将多个由 Gen 2 生成的短片剪辑组合，形成影片的视觉部分，并使用 ElevenLabs 的 AI 配音软件为剧本配音。他还通过其他后期制作软件添加了音效和音乐。一个月后，总部位于底特律的视频制作与技术公司 Waymark 制作了十二分钟的短片 *The Frost*，采用了不同的 AI 技术。公司使用 OpenAI 的文本到图像生成器 DALL‑E 2 根据执行制作人撰写的脚本生成一系列静态图像来插图故事，随后利用另一款 AI 软件 D‑ID 为这些静态图像添加部分动画（如嘴唇移动、眼神眨动、镜头拉近等）。Waymark 同样加入了音乐和音效，最终呈现出一部令人信服的心理惊悚片。2024 年 2 月，OpenAI 预览了其文本到视频生成系统 Sora，仅凭单一文本提示即可生成一分钟的视频——远超此前的时长——并因其超写实和对“电影语法”（即镜头顺序、角度、缩放和平移等）的把握而震撼了众多人。

在未来几年，通过这种方式创建长篇电影已成为可能，并且只需花费是独立电影通常所需预算的几分之一，就能完成。这种电影和电视创作的根本性民主化将是好莱坞历史上最大的变革——比从无声电影向有声电影的转变，甚至比 VHS 和流媒体的出现更大。这些变革虽大，但并未根本改变对大量人才和劳动力的需求。现在，曾经需要数十人甚至数百人、大量专用设备才能完成的工作，现在可以由一名持有笔记本电脑的人完成。我们将在 8 章中探讨这对个人电影制作者意味着什么，但此处我将重点放在对娱乐和媒体公司的影响。

尽管担心工作室会消失，但在生成式 AI 导致电影大量涌现的世界里，大型工作室的分发和营销实力比以往任何时候都更为关键。人们可以在社交媒体上传内容，但社交媒体不适合长篇电影。要获得广泛观众，AI 电影的导演将需要大型公司的支持。工作室同样拥有大量知识产权，可以用于开发自己的 AI 电影，甚至自研视频生成软件。

然而，随着工作室利用其档案和旧库开展此类工作，劳动力紧张将会加剧。2023 年好莱坞编剧和演员工会的罢工——AI 是主要议题——预示着这些未来的冲突。工作室同意演员的形象在未经同意和公平补偿的情况下不得用于创建数字化角色，这点值得欢迎。但许多年轻且不够知名的演员可能会感到压力，在有利于工作室的条款下出售这些权利。作家的妥协可能会在其他行业得到效仿：AI 可以辅助人类作家，但不能降低他们的署名和报酬。

Beyond entertainment, one of the more profound shifts will be in publishing, where AI might not just help authors write, but also change the way books are read, changing the industry’s economics. It’s easy to imagine readers will wish to have an AI “reading companion” that can answer questions, look up unfamiliar references, or provide literary analysis. It might even function a bit like a virtual book club, without having to wait for monthly gatherings. Publishers will compete to build such AI reading aides. Prolific and popular authors, especially genre writers, could create their own AI “co-readers” well-versed in their own oeuvre. One can imagine AI reading companions geared to Harry Potter or Sherlock Holmes fans. Desire to have ready access to AI reading aides could, pardon the pun, rekindle demand for ebooks, which has plateaued since 2012. The tech company YouAI created a book-reading companion chatbot called Book AI and has talked to publishers about offering similar tools. People have also created book-reading “GPTs” (ChatGPT’s equivalent of apps) and offered them in OpenAI’s GPT store for the same purpose. News publishers such as Semafor, *Fortune* (where I work), as well as tech-oriented publications such as *MacWorld* and *PCWorld* have trained chatbots on their own archives to answer reader questions and suggest additional articles.

Publishers are unlikely to want to have AI write books since AI-produced content cannot be copyrighted. But they might expect that writers will work with an AI assistant to produce manuscripts faster. As we will explore in chapter 8, which addresses AI’s impact on the creative arts, a tussle is brewing between authors and publishers over who has the right to use an author’s work to train an AI system. But in the meantime, publishers are deploying AI to help optimize the metadata used to list individual titles so that they are easier to find on Amazon and other websites. They are also experimenting with AI-generated voices to create audiobooks—particularly for lower-selling titles. Some publishers are also exploring AI-generated art for cover designs or to illustrate children’s books. As elsewhere, this ability to deliver these services with fewer people may mean smaller, independent publishing houses will be able to compete with large publishers.

For the past twenty years, a handful of companies have dominated the technology sector: Apple, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Meta. In China, two companies, Baidu and Tencent, have had more market power than the rest. AI is likely to further cement these leaders’ positions. But AI might just pry open a window that will allow a new player or two to sneak into tech’s star chamber. The most potent general-purpose AI models, known as *foundation models* because many applications can be built on top of them, will remain the exclusive domain of the existing tech giants and a handful of well-funded start-ups closely aligned with them. Only these firms have the resources needed to create and maintain data centers large enough—tens or hundreds of thousands of GPUs—to train and run the most capable AI models. Microsoft spent hundreds of millions of dollars to build the supercomputing cluster used to train OpenAI’s GPT models, according to Scott Guthrie, Microsoft’s executive vice president in charge of cloud and AI. On top of this, OpenAI itself spent more than $100 million training its trillion-parameter GPT-4 model. Google’s most powerful Gemini model, Ultra, is said to be five times more powerful and significantly larger than GPT-4.

While OpenAI’s Altman has questioned whether the advantage from developing larger and larger AI models has plateaued, it’s unlikely that bleeding-edge capabilities will be available to firms without direct access to supercomputing clusters anytime soon. It’s true that many top machine learning researchers have left Big Tech companies in the past few years to work for start-ups. But most of these AI start-ups have formed close alliances with the technology giants in order to access the computing power they need. The large technology companies exercise considerable control over the start-ups, even amid regulatory scrutiny. Microsoft, under the unusual terms of its investment in OpenAI, essentially owns the rights to 75 percent of the AI company’s profits for years to come. Amazon has invested heavily in Anthropic, and Nvidia has invested in Cohere.

Big Tech companies are also unlikely to lose ground to start-ups when it comes to building the most general-purpose AI technology. Microsoft is rapidly infusing generative AI into its products, like the Microsoft 365 suite that includes Microsoft Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Teams. Google is doing likewise with its Workspace apps. But start-ups will play an important role in building a new generation of more narrowly tailored, industry-specific tools. These systems are designed not just for general-purpose tasks—such as creating spreadsheets, presentations, or invoices—but for more targeted functions: human resources, corporate finance, portfolio management, law, and architecture, to name a few. While some of this software will run on top of foundation models created by the Big Tech companies, they’ll be fine-tuned for narrower functions and will feature a dedicated user interface geared toward people who work in those areas.
