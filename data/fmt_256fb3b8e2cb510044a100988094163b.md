
# The classification of autonomous vehicles will be based on the demand characteristics of actual scenarios, enabling differentiated design and operational management for autonomous vehicles. From different perspectives, autonomous vehicles can be categorized as follows:

## (1) From the perspective of driving range
They can be divided into short-distance vehicles operating at low speeds within cities and long-distance vehicles operating at high speeds between cities.

## (2) From the perspective of the transport object
They can be divided into passenger cars serving people and trucks serving freight.

## (3) From the perspective of passenger capacity
They can be divided into single-occupant, dual-occupant, and multi-occupant vehicles.

## (4) From the perspective of user type
They can be divided into police cars, commercial vehicles, business vehicles, and private/family cars.

## (5) From the perspective of special scenarios
They can be divided into emergency vehicles, fire trucks, energy replenishment vehicles, and engineering rescue vehicles.

Applying classification by vehicle type holds significant value in reducing resource waste, controlling costs, and improving service quality. For example, short-distance vehicles operating at low speeds within cities only need to update local maps in real-time to retain more detailed local traffic information, reduce total data volume, and improve vehicle system speed; indoor short-distance vehicles and inter-city vehicles can adopt differentiated design solutions for tires, lighting, body materials, sensors, etc., to reduce resource waste and improve resource utilization.

After delineating vehicle operational areas, the vehicle fleet size within the area will be effectively controlled, thereby improving traffic operational efficiency and quality, and reducing congestion and accidents. With the popularization of mobile payment and toll policy reforms, toll stations will cease to exist in the future, replaced by transfer stations providing passenger and cargo transfer services for users.

In the future, passengers or luggage can complete transfers without getting off the vehicle. For example, when a high-speed train arrives at a station, passengers can board and alight without the train stopping; when autonomous vehicle passengers encounter impassable roads ahead due to accidents or congestion, passengers in oncoming vehicles do not need to return the same way but can continue to reach their destinations via transfer.

## 12.4.3.2 Lane-Separated Driving

Lane-separated driving will designate dedicated lanes for different vehicles such as pedestrians, bicycles, electric vehicles, private cars, and buses, with each traveling in its own lane and controlling its driving route. Currently, expressways and rail transit have basically achieved lane-separated driving. In the early application stage of autonomous vehicles, dedicated lanes need to be set up to improve safety and reduce the negative impact of accidents.

When traffic lights at intersections are eliminated in the future, the separation of humans and vehicles into different lanes will become an inevitable choice: ordinary people find it difficult to judge the path of autonomous vehicles, and vehicle systems also find it difficult to find fixed patterns in large amounts of disordered flow. Although pedestrians can interact with autonomous vehicles after the popularization of IoT technology, passengers in the vehicle may be unwilling to compromise, triggering conflicts; lane separation can effectively solve this problem.

In addition, when autonomous vehicles and human-driven vehicles share the road, the unified scheduling intelligent traffic center system lacks binding force over human-driven vehicles, making instructions difficult to execute effectively and leading to frequent conflicts, which is by no means the desired outcome.

### 12.5 Key Factors for Autonomous Driving from Concept to Practice

#### 12.5.1 Safety: The Inevitable Destination of Autonomous Driving Development

The pursuit of safety has driven the rapid growth in demand for autonomous vehicles. According to WHO data, more than 1.2 million people die globally each year due to road traffic accidents. Human error accounts for 93% of accidents in China, with an average of 500 deaths per day. With the advent of autonomous vehicles, the traditional "human-vehicle-road" ternary control will shift to "vehicle-road" binary control, the out-of-control driver is eliminated, achieving "zero violations," and the efficiency and safety of the traffic system are greatly improved, with accident rates significantly reduced. Research shows that rear-end collisions decrease by 40% after equipping automatic braking systems; if all motor vehicles are equipped, 700,000 accidents can be reduced annually.

Safety is the prerequisite for autonomous driving development. Because the technology is still in the trial-and-error improvement stage, safety and reliability have not yet gained widespread public recognition. For example, the UK requires on-road autonomous vehicles to have a human monitor who can switch to manual driving mode at any time; Germany plans to issue automated driving regulations requiring steering wheels and the installation of aviation-style black box devices for accident liability determination.

A Volvo survey shows that over half of consumers support retaining steering wheels in autonomous vehicles, and 80% believe accident liability should be borne by the manufacturer (due to autonomous driving system failures). In reality, when users distrust the autonomous driving system in emergencies and wrestle for control, it easily leads to severe consequences: during autonomous driving, the driver relaxes and does not pay attention to road conditions, and misjudgment in sudden situations easily leads to danger.

##### Safety and Development of Autonomous Vehicles

When the driver and the vehicle vie for control (traffic accidents), traffic authorities struggle to determine liability. If drivers are required to stay alert and be ready to switch to manual mode while the vehicle is in motion, one might as well adopt manual driving directly. This "human or vehicle" design renders autonomous driving meaningless, increases the driver's psychological burden, and dashes the desire for free and comfortable driving.

Essentially, the "human or vehicle" design is an irresponsible manifestation regarding autonomous driving safety. If there is a "fallback" of manual driving, relevant personnel will not focus on autonomous driving safety issues; unified standards for the design and manufacture of autonomous vehicles cannot be established in the short term, and user safety cannot be effectively guaranteed.

On May 7, 2016, a Tesla with the `Autopilot` driver-assistance system engaged collided with a white tractor-trailer; the `Autopilot` roof was completely destroyed, and the driver was killed instantly. Tesla explained: the white truck was not recognized against the bright sky background, and from the `Autopilot`'s perspective, the side of the trailer appeared suspended in mid-air, causing the system to misjudge and fail to activate automatic emergency braking, resulting in the collision. Furthermore, because the impact occurred at the `Autopilot`'s windshield area, the crash safety system failed, leading to the driver's instant death.

The accident caused a sensation, as Tesla had claimed a fatal accident occurs every 94 million miles in the US, while the `Autopilot` system had logged 130 million miles. Tesla emphasized that autonomous driving technology is continuously evolving and that `Autopilot` is not perfect. Who exactly should be held responsible? Some blame Tesla, some blame the negligent driver, and others blame the sensor supplier `Mobileye`. This perception of autonomous vehicle accidents recalls the "War of Currents" between Tesla and Edison.
