# ### 3.4.3 The univariate Gaussian model

In this section, we derive the posterior \(p(\mu, \sigma^2 | \mathcal{D})\) for a univariate Gaussian. For simplicity, we consider this in three steps: inferring just \(\mu\), inferring just \(\sigma^2\), and then inferring both. See Section 3.4.4 for the multivariate case.

# #### 3.4.3.1 Posterior of \(\mu\) given \(\sigma^2\)

If \(\sigma^2\) is a known constant, the likelihood for \(\mu\) has the form

```
\( p(\mathcal{D}|\mu) \propto \exp \left( - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - \mu)^2 \right) \) (3.97)
```

One can show that the conjugate prior is another Gaussian, \(\mathcal{N}(\mu | \tilde{m}, \tilde{\tau}^2)\). Applying Bayes' rule for Gaussians (Equation (2.121)), we find that the corresponding posterior is given by

```
\( p(\mu | \mathcal{D}, \sigma^2) = \mathcal{N}(\mu | \hat{m}, \hat{\tau}^2) \) (3.98)
\( \hat{\tau}^2 = \frac{1}{\frac{N}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tilde{\tau}^2}} = \frac{\sigma^2 \tilde{\tau}^2}{N \tilde{\tau}^2 + \sigma^2} \) (3.99)
\( \hat{m} = \hat{\tau}^2 \left( \frac{\tilde{m}}{\tilde{\tau}^2} + \frac{N \bar{y}}{\sigma^2} \right) = \frac{ \frac{\sigma^2}{N \tilde{\tau}^2 + \sigma^2} \tilde{m} + \frac{N \tilde{\tau}^2}{N \tilde{\tau}^2 + \sigma^2} \bar{y} }{1} \) (3.100)
```

where \(\bar{y} \triangleq \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N y_n\) is the empirical mean.

This result is easier to understand if we work in terms of the precision parameters, which are just inverse variances. Specifically, let \(\lambda = 1/\sigma^2\) be the observation precision, and \(\tilde{\lambda} = 1 / \tilde{\tau}^2\) be the precision of the prior. We can then rewrite the posterior as follows:

```
\( p(\mu | \mathcal{D}, \lambda) = \mathcal{N}(\mu | \hat{m}, \hat{\lambda}^{-1}) \) (3.101)
\( \hat{\lambda} = \tilde{\lambda} + N \lambda \) (3.102)
\( \hat{m} = \frac{N \lambda \bar{y} + \tilde{\lambda} \tilde{m}}{\hat{\lambda}} = \frac{N \lambda}{N \lambda + \tilde{\lambda}} \bar{y} + \frac{\tilde{\lambda}}{N \lambda + \tilde{\lambda}} \tilde{m} \) (3.103)
```

These equations are quite intuitive: the posterior precision \(\hat{\lambda}\) is the prior precision \(\tilde{\lambda}\) plus \(N\) units of measurement precision \(\lambda\). Also, the posterior mean \(\hat{m}\) is a convex combination of the empirical mean \(\bar{y}\) and the prior mean \(\tilde{m}\). This makes it clear that the posterior mean is a compromise between the empirical mean and the prior. If the prior is weak relative to the signal strength (\(\tilde{\lambda}\) is small relative to \(\lambda\)), we put more weight on the empirical mean. If the prior is strong relative to the signal strength (\(\tilde{\lambda}\) is large relative to \(\lambda\)), we put more weight on the prior. This is illustrated in Figure 3.5. Note also that the posterior mean is written in terms of \(N \lambda \bar{x}\), so having \(N\) measurements each of precision \(\lambda\) is like having one measurement with value \(\bar{x}\) and precision \(N \lambda\).
