
### 2.3.3.1 Moment and canonical parameterization

We can represent a Gaussian distribution in moment form or in canonical (information) form. In moment form we have

$$\phi(\boldsymbol{x}; p, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = p \times \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\mathsf{T}} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right)$$

where \(p = (2\pi)^{-n/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\) is the normalizing constant that ensures \(\int_{\boldsymbol{x}} \phi(\boldsymbol{x}; p, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = 1\). (\(n\) is the dimensionality of \(\boldsymbol{x}\).) Expanding out the quadratic form and collecting terms we get the canonical form:

$$\phi(\boldsymbol{x}; g, \boldsymbol{h}, \boldsymbol{K}) = \exp \left( g + \boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{h} - \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x} \right) = \exp \left( g + \sum_i h_i x_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_i \sum_k K_{ij} x_i x_j \right)$$

where

$$\boldsymbol{K} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$$

$$\boldsymbol{h} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}$$

$$g = \log p - \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathsf{T}} \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\mu}$$

\(\boldsymbol{K}\) is often called the precision matrix. Note that potentials need not be probability distributions, and need not be normalizable (integrate to 1). We keep track of the constant terms (\(p\) or \(g\)) so we can compute the likelihood of the evidence.

### 2.3.3.2 Multiplication and division

We can define multiplication and division in the Gaussian case by using canonical forms, as follows. To multiply \(\phi_1(x_1, \dots, x_k; g_1, \boldsymbol{h}_1, \boldsymbol{K}_1)\) by \(\phi_2(x_{k+1}, \dots, x_n; g_2, \boldsymbol{h}_2, \boldsymbol{K}_2)\), we extend them both to the same domain \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) by adding zeros to the appropriate dimensions, and then computing

$$(g_1, \boldsymbol{h}_1, \boldsymbol{K}_1) * (g_2, \boldsymbol{h}_2, \boldsymbol{K}_2) = (g_1 + g_2, \boldsymbol{h}_1 + \boldsymbol{h}_2, \boldsymbol{K}_1 + \boldsymbol{K}_2)$$

Division is defined as follows:

$$(g_1, \boldsymbol{h}_1, \boldsymbol{K}_1) / (g_2, \boldsymbol{h}_2, \boldsymbol{K}_2) = (g_1 - g_2, \boldsymbol{h}_1 - \boldsymbol{h}_2, \boldsymbol{K}_1 - \boldsymbol{K}_2)$$

### 2.3.3.3 Marginalization

Let \(\phi_W\) be a potential over a set \(W\) of variables. We can compute the potential over a subset \(V \subset W\) of variables by marginalizing, denoted \(\phi_V = \sum_{W \setminus V} \phi_W\). Let

$$\boldsymbol{x} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{x}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{x}_2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{h} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{h}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{h}_2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{K} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{K}_{11} & \boldsymbol{K}_{12} \\ \boldsymbol{K}_{21} & \boldsymbol{K}_{22} \end{pmatrix},$$

with \(\boldsymbol{x}_1\) having dimension \(n_1\) and \(\boldsymbol{x}_2\) having dimension \(n_2\). It can be shown that

$$\int_{\boldsymbol{x}_1} \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_1, \boldsymbol{x}_2; g, \boldsymbol{h}, \boldsymbol{K}) = \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_2; \hat{g}, \hat{\boldsymbol{h}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{K}})$$

where

\(\hat{g} = g + \frac{1}{2} (n_1 \log(2\pi) - \log |\mathbf{K}_{11}| + \mathbf{h}_1^T \mathbf{K}_{11}^{-1} \mathbf{h}_1)\) (2.140)
\(\hat{\mathbf{h}} = \mathbf{h}_2 - \mathbf{K}_{21} \mathbf{K}_{11}^{-1} \mathbf{h}_1\) (2.141)
\(\hat{\mathbf{K}} = \mathbf{K}_{22} - \mathbf{K}_{21} \mathbf{K}_{11}^{-1} \mathbf{K}_{12}\) (2.142)

### 2.3.3.4 Conditioning on evidence

Consider a potential defined on \((\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y})\). Suppose we observe the value \(\boldsymbol{y}\). The new potential is given by the following reduced dimensionality object:

\(\phi^*(\boldsymbol{x}) = \exp \left[ g + (\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{y}^T) \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{h}_X \\ \boldsymbol{h}_Y \end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{2} (\boldsymbol{x}^T \boldsymbol{y}^T) \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{K}_{XX} & \mathbf{K}_{XY} \\ \mathbf{K}_{YX} & \mathbf{K}_{YY} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{x} \\ \boldsymbol{y} \end{pmatrix} \right]\) (2.143)
\(= \exp \left[ \left( g + \boldsymbol{h}_Y^T \boldsymbol{y} - \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{y}^T \mathbf{K}_{YY} \boldsymbol{y} \right) + \boldsymbol{x}^T (\boldsymbol{h}_X - \mathbf{K}_{XY} \boldsymbol{y}) - \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{x}^T \mathbf{K}_{XX} \boldsymbol{x} \right]\) (2.144)

This generalizes the corresponding equation in [Lau92] to the vector-valued case.

### 2.3.3.5 Converting a linear-Gaussian CPD to a canonical potential

Finally we discuss how to create the initial potentials, assuming we start with a directed Gaussian graphical model. In particular, consider a node with a linear-Gaussian conditional probability distribution (CPD):

\(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{u}) = c \exp \left[ -\frac{1}{2} ((\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu} - \mathbf{B}^T \boldsymbol{u})^T \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu} - \mathbf{B}^T \boldsymbol{u})) \right]\) (2.145)
\(= \exp \left[ -\frac{1}{2} (\boldsymbol{x} \ \boldsymbol{u}) \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} & -\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{B}^T \\ -\mathbf{B} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} & \mathbf{B} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{B}^T \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{x} \\ \boldsymbol{u} \end{pmatrix} \right.\) (2.146)
\(\left. + (\boldsymbol{x} \ \boldsymbol{u}) \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} \\ -\mathbf{B} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} \end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\mu}^T \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} + \log c \right]\) (2.147)

where \(c = (2\pi)^{-n/2} |\mathbf{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\). Hence we set the canonical parameters to

\(g = -\frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\mu}^T \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{n}{2} \log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2} \log |\mathbf{\Sigma}|\) (2.148)
\(\boldsymbol{h} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} \\ -\mathbf{B} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} \end{pmatrix}\) (2.149)
\(\mathbf{K} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} & -\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{B}^T \\ -\mathbf{B} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} & \mathbf{B} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{B}^T \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{I} \\ -\mathbf{B} \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{I} & -\mathbf{B} \end{pmatrix}\) (2.150)

In the special case that \(x\) is a scalar, the corresponding result can be found in [Lau92]. In particular we have \(\Sigma^{-1} = 1/\sigma^2\), \(B = b\) and \(n = 1\), so the above becomes

$$g = \frac{-\mu^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{1}{2} \log(2\pi\sigma^2)$$

$$\boldsymbol{h} = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -b \end{pmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{K} = \frac{1}{\sigma} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -b^T \\ -b & bb^T \end{pmatrix}$$

### 2.3.3.6 Example: Product of Gaussians

As an application of the above results, we can derive the (unnormalized) product of two Gaussians, as follows (see also [Kaa12, Sec 8.18]):

$$\mathcal{N}(x|\mu_1, \Sigma_1) \times \mathcal{N}(x|\mu_2, \Sigma_2) \propto \mathcal{N}(x|\mu_3, \Sigma_3)$$

where

$$\Sigma_3 = (\Sigma_1^{-1} + \Sigma_2^{-1})^{-1}$$
$$\mu_3 = \Sigma_3(\Sigma_1^{-1} \mu_1 + \Sigma_2^{-1} \mu_2)$$

We see that the posterior precision is a sum of the individual precisions, and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted combination of the individual means. We can also rewrite the result in the following way, which only requires one matrix inversion:

$$\Sigma_3 = \Sigma_1(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2)^{-1}\Sigma_2$$
$$\mu_3 = \Sigma_2(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2)^{-1}\mu_1 + \Sigma_1(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2)^{-1}\mu_2$$

In the scalar case, this becomes

$$\mathcal{N}(x|\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\mathcal{N}(x|\mu_2, \sigma_2^2) \propto \mathcal{N}\left(x|\frac{\mu_1\sigma_2^2 + \mu_2\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}, \frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}\right)$$

## 2.4 The exponential family

In this section, we define the **exponential family**, which includes many common probability distributions. The exponential family plays a crucial role in statistics and machine learning, for various reasons, including the following:

- The exponential family is the unique family of distributions that has maximum entropy (and hence makes the least set of assumptions) subject to some user-chosen constraints, as discussed in Section 2.4.7.
- The exponential family is at the core of GLMs, as discussed in Section 15.1.
- The exponential family is at the core of variational inference, as discussed in Chapter 10.
