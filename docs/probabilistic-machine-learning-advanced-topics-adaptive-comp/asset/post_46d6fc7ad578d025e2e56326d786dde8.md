
number of datapoints \( N \). For \( \sigma^2 \), we work instead with the sum of squares: the posterior sum of squares, \( \tilde{\nu}\tilde{\tau}^2 \), is the prior sum of squares \( \tilde{\nu}\tilde{\tau}^2 \) plus the data sum of squares, \( \sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - \overline{y})^2 \), plus a term due to the discrepancy between the prior mean \( \tilde{m} \) and the MLE \( \overline{y} \). The strength of this posterior, \( \tilde{\nu} \), is the prior strength \( \tilde{\nu} \) plus the number of datapoints \( N \);

The posterior marginal for \( \sigma^2 \) is just

\[
p(\sigma^2|\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\mu, \sigma^2|\mathcal{D}) d\mu = \chi^{-2}(\sigma^2 \mid \bar{\nu}, \tilde{\tau}^2) \tag{3.125}
\]

with the posterior mean given by \( \mathbb{E}[\sigma^2|\mathcal{D}] = \frac{\tilde{\nu}}{\tilde{\nu}-2} \tilde{\tau}^2 \).

The posterior marginal for \( \mu \) has a Student distribution, which follows from the fact that the Student distribution is a (scaled) mixture of Gaussians:

\[
p(\mu|\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\mu, \sigma^2|\mathcal{D}) d\sigma^2 = \mathcal{T}(\mu \mid \widehat{m}, \tilde{\tau}^2 / \hat{\kappa}, \tilde{\nu}) \tag{3.126}
\]

with the posterior mean given by \( \mathbb{E}[\mu|\mathcal{D}] = \widehat{m} \).

#### 3.4.3.4 Posterior of \( \mu \) and \( \sigma^2 \): uninformative prior

If we “know nothing” about the parameters a priori, we can use an uninformative prior. We discuss how to create such priors in Section 3.5. A common approach is to use a Jeffreys prior. In Section 3.5.2.3, we show that the Jeffreys prior for a location and scale parameter has the form

\[
p(\mu, \sigma^2) \propto p(\mu)p(\sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2} \tag{3.127}
\]

We can simulate this with a conjugate prior by using

\[
p(\mu, \sigma^2) = NI\chi^2(\mu, \sigma^2 \mid \widehat{m}=0, \tilde{\kappa}=0, \tilde{\nu}=-1, \tilde{\tau}^2=0) \tag{3.128}
\]

With this prior, the posterior has the form

\[
p(\mu, \sigma^2|\mathcal{D}) = NI\chi^2(\mu, \sigma^2 \mid \widehat{m}=\overline{y}, \widehat{\kappa}=N, \tilde{\nu}=N-1, \tilde{\tau}^2=s^2) \tag{3.129}
\]

where

\[
s^2 \triangleq \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - \overline{y})^2 = \frac{N}{N-1} \hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{mle}} \tag{3.130}
\]

\( s \) is known as the sample standard deviation. Hence the marginal posterior for the mean is given by

\[
p(\mu|\mathcal{D}) = \mathcal{T}(\mu|\overline{y}, \frac{s^2}{N}, N-1) = \mathcal{T}(\mu|\overline{y}, \frac{\sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - \overline{y})^2}{N(N-1)}, N-1) \tag{3.131}
\]

Thus the posterior variance of \( \mu \) is

\[
\mathbb{V}[\mu|\mathcal{D}] = \frac{\tilde{\nu}}{\tilde{\nu}-2} \tilde{\tau}^2 = \frac{N-1}{N-3} \frac{s^2}{N} \rightarrow \frac{s^2}{N} \tag{3.132}
\]

The square root of this is called the standard error of the mean:
se($\mu$) \(\triangleq \sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\mu|\mathcal{D}]} \approx \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}\)

Thus we can approximate the 95% credible interval for $\mu$ using
\(I_{.95}(\mu|\mathcal{D}) = \bar{y} \pm 2 \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}\)

## 3.4.4 The multivariate Gaussian model

In this section, we derive the posterior \(p(\mu, \Sigma|\mathcal{D})\) for a multivariate Gaussian. For simplicity, we consider this in three steps: inferring just $\mu$, inferring just $\Sigma$, and then inferring both.

### 3.4.4.1 Posterior of $\mu$ given $\Sigma$

The likelihood has the form

\[
p(\mathcal{D}|\mu) = \mathcal{N}(\bar{y}|\mu, \frac{1}{N}\Sigma)
\]

For simplicity, we will use a conjugate prior, which in this case is a Gaussian. In particular, if \(p(\mu) = \mathcal{N}(\mu|\tilde{m}, \tilde{V})\) then we can derive a Gaussian posterior for $\mu$ based on the results in Section 2.3.2.2 We get

\[
p(\mu|\mathcal{D}, \Sigma) = \mathcal{N}(\mu|\hat{m}, \hat{V})
\]
\[
\hat{V}^{-1}=\tilde{V}^{-1}+N\Sigma^{-1}
\]
\[
\hat{m}=\hat{V}(\Sigma^{-1}(N\bar{y})+\tilde{V}^{-1}\tilde{m})
\]

Figure 3.8 gives a 2d example of these results.

### 3.4.4.2 Posterior of $\Sigma$ given $\mu$

We now discuss how to compute \(p(\Sigma|\mathcal{D}, \mu)\).

#### Likelihood

We can rewrite the likelihood as follows:

\[
p(\mathcal{D}|\mu, \Sigma) \propto |\Sigma|^{-\frac{N}{2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr}(\mathbf{S}_\mu \Sigma^{-1}) \right)
\]

where

\[
\mathbf{S}_\mu \triangleq \sum_{n=1}^N (\mathbf{y}_n - \mu)(\mathbf{y}_n - \mu)^\top
\]

is the scatter matrix around $\mu$.

![](img/ca72c1626f4850f058a9edc138c1ac73_122_0.png)

#### Prior

The conjugate prior is known as the **inverse Wishart** distribution, which is a distribution over positive definite matrices, as we explained in Section 2.2.5.5. This has the following pdf:

\[
IW(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mid \tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}, \tilde{\nu}) \propto |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-(\tilde{\nu}+D+1)/2} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}) \right)
\]

Here \(\tilde{\nu} > D - 1\) is the degrees of freedom (dof), and \(\tilde{\Psi}\) is a symmetric pd matrix. We see that \(\tilde{\Psi}\) plays the role of the prior scatter matrix, and \(N_0 \triangleq \tilde{\nu} + D + 1\) controls the strength of the prior, and hence plays a role analogous to the sample size N.

#### Posterior

Multiplying the likelihood and prior we find that the posterior is also inverse Wishart:

\[
p(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mid \mathcal{D}, \boldsymbol{\mu}) \propto |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{N}{2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{S}_\mu) \right) |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-(\tilde{\nu}+D+1)/2}
\]
\[
\exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}) \right)
\]
\[
= |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{N+(\tilde{\nu}+D+1)}{2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr} \left[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{S}_\mu + \tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}) \right] \right)
\]
\[
= \text{IW}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mid \tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}, \tilde{\nu})
\]
\[
\tilde{\nu} = \tilde{\nu} + N
\]
\[
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}} = \tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}} + \mathbf{S}_\mu
\]

In words, this says that the posterior strength \(\tilde{\nu}\) is the prior strength \(\tilde{\nu}\) plus the number of observations N, and the posterior scatter matrix \(\tilde{\Psi}\) is the prior scatter matrix \(\tilde{\Psi}\) plus the data scatter matrix \(\mathbf{S}_\mu\).

### 3.4.4.3 Posterior of $\Sigma$ and $\mu$

In this section, we compute \(p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma} | \mathcal{D})\) using a conjugate prior.

#### Likelihood

The likelihood is given by

\[
p(\mathcal{D} | \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) \propto |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{N}{2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=1}^N (\boldsymbol{y}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{y}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right)
\]

One can show that

\[
\sum_{n=1}^N (\boldsymbol{y}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{y}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{S}) + N(\overline{\boldsymbol{y}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\overline{\boldsymbol{y}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})
\]

where

\[
\mathbf{S} \triangleq \mathbf{S}_{\overline{\mathbf{y}}} = \sum_{n=1}^N (\boldsymbol{y}_n - \overline{\boldsymbol{y}})(\boldsymbol{y}_n - \overline{\boldsymbol{y}})^\mathsf{T} = \mathbf{Y}^\mathsf{T} \mathbf{C}_N \mathbf{Y}
\]

is empirical scatter matrix, and \(\mathbf{C}_N\) is the centering matrix

\[
\mathbf{C}_N \triangleq \mathbf{I}_N - \frac{1}{N} \mathbf{1}_N \mathbf{1}_N^\mathsf{T}
\]

Hence we can rewrite the likelihood as follows:

\[
p(\mathcal{D} | \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) \propto |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{N}{2}} \exp \left( -\frac{N}{2} (\boldsymbol{\mu} - \overline{\boldsymbol{y}})^\mathsf{T} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{\mu} - \overline{\boldsymbol{y}}) \right) \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{S}) \right)
\]

We will use this form below.

#### Prior

The obvious prior to use is the following

\[
p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu} \mid \tilde{\mathbf{m}}, \tilde{\mathbf{V}}) \text{IW}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mid \tilde{\mathbf{\Psi}}, \tilde{\nu})
\]

where IW is the inverse Wishart distribution. Unfortunately, \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) appear together in a non-factorized way in the likelihood in Equation (3.151) (see the first exponent term), so the factored prior in Equation (3.152) is not conjugate to the likelihood.

The above prior is sometimes called **conditionally conjugate**, since both conditionals, \(p(\boldsymbol{\mu} | \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) and \(p(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} | \boldsymbol{\mu})\), are individually conjugate. To create a fully conjugate prior, we need to use a prior where \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) are dependent on each other. We will use a joint distribution of the form \(p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = p(\boldsymbol{\mu} | \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) p(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\).
