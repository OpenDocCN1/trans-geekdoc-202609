Despite the impressive performance of these **large language models** or **LLMs** (see Figure 22.5 for an example), there are several open problems with them, such as: they often confidently **hallucinate** incorrect answers to questions (see e.g., [Ji+22]); they can generate biased or toxic output (see e.g., [Lia+]); and they are very resource intensive to train and serve. Indeed, these concerns are why Google has not (at the time of writing) released its version of ChatGPT, known as **LaMDA** [Col21]. The basic ideas behind LLMs are quite simple (maximum likelihood training of an autoregressive transformer), and they can be implemented in about 300 lines of code.1 However, just by scaling up the size of the models and datasets, it seems that qualitatively new capabilities can emerge (see e.g., [Wei+22]). Nevertheless, although this approach is good at learning formal linguistic competence (surface form), it is not clear if it is sufficient to learn functional linguistic competence, which requires a deeper, non-linguistic understanding of the world derived from experience [Mah+23].
