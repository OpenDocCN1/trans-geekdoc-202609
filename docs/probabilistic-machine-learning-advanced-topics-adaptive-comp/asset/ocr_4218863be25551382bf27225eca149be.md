Multiplying the likelihood and the prior, we see that the posterior is also IG:
\(p(\sigma^2|\mu, \mathcal{D}) = \text{IG}(\sigma^2| \tilde{a}, \tilde{b})\) (3.110)
\(\tilde{a} = \tilde{a} + N/2\) (3.111)
\(\tilde{b} = \tilde{b} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - \mu)^2\) (3.112)

One small annoyance with using the IG(\(\tilde{a}, \tilde{b}\)) distribution is that that the strength of the prior is encoded in both \(\tilde{a}\) and \(\tilde{b}\). Therefore, in the Bayesian statistics literature it is common to use an alternative parameterization of the IG distribution, known as the (scaled) **inverse chi-squared distribution**:
\(\chi^{-2}(\sigma^2| \tilde{\nu}, \tilde{\tau}^2) = \text{IG}(\sigma^2| \frac{\tilde{\nu}}{2}, \frac{\tilde{\nu} \tilde{\tau}^2}{2}) \propto (\sigma^2)^{-\tilde{\nu}/2 - 1} \exp(-\frac{\tilde{\nu} \tilde{\tau}^2}{2\sigma^2})\) (3.113)

Here \(\tilde{\nu}\) (called the degrees of freedom or dof parameter) controls the strength of the prior, and \(\tilde{\tau}^2\) encodes the prior mean. With this prior, the posterior becomes
\(p(\sigma^2|\mathcal{D}, \mu) = \chi^{-2}(\sigma^2| \tilde{\nu}, \tilde{\tau}^2)\) (3.114)
\(\tilde{\nu} = \tilde{\nu} + N\) (3.115)
\(\tilde{\tau}^2 = \frac{\tilde{\nu} \tilde{\tau}^2 + \sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - \mu)^2}{\tilde{\nu}}\) (3.116)

We see that the posterior dof \(\tilde{\nu}\) is the prior dof \(\tilde{\nu}\) plus \(N\), and the posterior sum of squares \(\tilde{\nu} \tilde{\tau}^2\) is the prior sum of squares \(\tilde{\nu} \tilde{\tau}^2\) plus the data sum of squares.

# 3.4.3.3 Posterior of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\): conjugate prior

Now suppose we want to infer both the mean and variance. The corresponding conjugate prior is the **normal inverse gamma**:
\(\text{NIG}(\mu, \sigma^2| \tilde{m}, \tilde{\kappa}, \tilde{a}, \tilde{b}) \triangleq \mathcal{N}(\mu| \tilde{m}, \sigma^2 / \tilde{\kappa}) \text{IG}(\sigma^2| \tilde{a}, \tilde{b})\) (3.117)
