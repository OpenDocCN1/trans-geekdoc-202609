## Green synthesized silver nanoparticles in two stages: Box Behnken Design to machine learning

Gulizar Caliskan and Ayca Kumluca Topalli

a Faculty of Engineering, Department of Genetics and Bioengineering, Izmir University of Economics, Izmir, Turkey; b Faculty of Engineering, Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering, Izmir University of Economics, Izmir, Turkey

## ABSTRACT

In order to solve the modeling issues due to data scarcity problems in the disciplines utilizing statistical approximations, a novel two-stage idea is proposed. As a use case, nanoparticle biosynthesis was selected, for which an environmentally friendly process is of vital importance. First, Box Behnken Design was used for experimental setup, quadratic model formulation and data generation. The second stage consists of Machine Learning, in which the data generated in the previous stage were fed into a Neural Network to determine the relationship between the parameters. Obtained results showed that the proposed combined strategy provided better nanoparticle size estimations than the statistical approach alone. In the absence of publicly available databases, data generation using experimental design and machine learning, as proposed here, could be a faster, lower-cost, and greener solution. Our proposed method can be applied to a wide range of biotechnology and bioengineering applications with significant advanced knowledge.

## Introduction

Nanotechnology has become one of the most promising technologies applied in many areas of science, such as medicine, agricultural applications, pharmaceutical industries, energy, electronics and aerospace industries. Recently, there has been growing interest in novel synthesis approaches for nano-scale materials, such as nanofilms, nanoparticles, etc., which are increasingly being used in nanoscience, and are expected to lead to many different innovations in the future. The great advantages of nanotechnology stem from specific properties, such as high surface to volume ratio, quantum size effects, enhanced magnetic properties, biocompatibility, chemical stability, biodegradability, low toxicity, and structural diversity and biomimetic character.

Generally, physical and chemical methods are preferred for the production of nanoparticles due to their low cost at high volumes. However, these methods necessarily require the use of toxic chemicals, such as organic solvents, reducing agents, stabilizers and extreme reaction conditions during nanoparticle synthesis (NPS). As metal nanoparticles are widely used in human contact applications, there is an increasing need to develop environmentally friendly NPS processes. The use of toxic solvents in the chemical and physical methods used to prepare nanoparticles limits their potential applications in bio-based products. Therefore, it is important to develop a safer, nontoxic synthesis method to allow nanoparticles to be used in a wider range of industries.

Recently, there has been a trend in nanotechnology toward green synthesis, which is low-cost, sustainable, environmentally friendly, cost-effective to scale up and relatively easy to synthesize. In addition, the yield is higher in green synthesis, which uses various biological sources such as plant extracts, bacteria, microalgae and fungi as reducing agents. Due to the absence of toxic reducing and stabilizing agents, biosynthesized nanoparticles are considered safer and more biocompatible than chemically synthesized nanoparticles and could be used in biotechnological fields such as tissue engineering, pharmaceutical industry, medicine etc.

The synthesis of nanoparticles is affected by many factors, such as temperature, pH, solution concentration, amount and ratio of the source material. Various statistical programmes can be used to design experiments to understand the effectiveness of each of these factors and their interaction on NPS. Any statistical design should involve the analysis of results to draw objective conclusions, and ensure the sustainability of the synthesis for further studies.

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is one of the experimental design methods used for optimization based on various statistical and mathematical techniques, such as linear, square polynomial functions, and others. These techniques are able to identify conditions that allow high efficiency production using a small number of experiments. Box Behnken Design (BBD) proposes experiments for RSM with a three-level fraction coded as −1 (low), 0 (middle) and +1 (high) to fit the design to a second order polynomial equation suitable for quadratic models.[16,19]

BBD, an economical approach with fewer factor levels, has been widely used in NPS process; however, it does not permit extremely high or low levels.[19,20] BBD has poor prediction quality due to lack of extreme values and regions, restrictions that only allow the model to be fitted with second order polynomials, and its need to use at least three experimental factors.[21] Moreover, increasing the number of variables in the process, and the generation of experimental data are costly and time consuming, especially in biotechnology and materials sciences.[22]

These difficulties can be overcome by incorporating Machine Learning (ML). ML, a branch of artificial intelligence, is a data-driven model that can learn knowledge without being explicitly programmed, using available data with remarkable accuracy.[23–25] It is a powerful tool for supporting decision-making and problem-solving tasks in which the relationship between the inputs and outputs is not precisely known.

ML has been applied to many fields in recent years, including pharmaceuticals and drug delivery systems.[26–30] These are generally used to predict the output of the NPS: size, shape, color, and their interactions, etc.[18,31,32] Previously reported works focused on the prediction of silver NPs,[33,34] droplet size in microfluidic devices,[35] and identification of operating parameters in microfluidic devices.[36–38] Furthermore, in Ocampo et al. compare ANNs and data analysis (DA) methods for predicting liposome size in a periodic disturbance micromixer. According to their results, ANN has a lower MSE than the DA.[39] Another study combines Bayesian optimization with DNN to optimize the synthesis of silver nanoprisms, using data provided by a high-throughput experimental platform.[22] Ban et al. used a random forest algorithm to learn the complex relationships between nanoparticle properties and their corona structure. The functional protein in complex coronas was accurately predicted with R² = 0.80.[32] In addition, Abhishek et al. applied convolutional neural networks (CNN), a branch of machine learning techniques, to reduce the traditional use of very expensive, time-consuming, repetitive and cumbersome scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis for nanoparticle identification. In their work, a CNN model classifies images of colloidal silver and gold nanoparticle solutions according to their colors, which are based on their size and surface plasmon resonance. A suitable SEM image is then generated by augmentation using the previously loaded images, and image processing algorithms are used to determine the approximate size of the nanoparticles in the sample. As the identification of nanoparticles along with their sizes is a common task in optimization studies for nanoparticle formation, their proposed approach improves the delays in the sample preparation and characterization phase.[18]

Compared to traditional RSM, where the function is represented by a polynomial, the ML model provides a more flexible estimation. Due to the nature of ML, the need for experimental re-runs and three-step cycle repetitions is reduced, resulting in process acceleration and a reduction in labor/equipment costs.[40] In this sense, ML provides a time and cost effective alternative to BBD; however, similar to RSM and BBD, ML also performs iterations to achieve successful estimates. This means that the ML also requires a satisfactory amount of pre-acquired data.[40]

This problem of data unavailability has been addressed in the previous works by several workarounds. For example, experimental data for ML-assisted NPS are typically collected from reported results and laboratory notebooks, or generated by new experiments, either manually or by robotic setups.[31] In addition to the difficulties of performing new experiments, data collection from the literature has its own issues. Transforming literature information into training datasets poses challenges, due to variation in the quality and consistency of data from different sources, and data are sourced generally from the scientific literature, rather than central or curated databases.[31]

For instance, in Wang’s work,[41] the aim was to build an ML model to predict lipid nanoparticle formulations for mRNA vaccines against viruses, and data were collected from publications identified by searching several keywords in Web of Science and Scopus; they retrieved 325 samples from 65 studies. Similarly, Ban et al. used ML to find the complex relationships between nanoparticle properties and corona composition, and then to predict the formation of protein coronas and related cell responses with data extracted from 56 papers.[32] As can be seen from these examples, it is challenging to parse the unformatted literature and find useful data for the same setup, and therefore, data sharing tools and data collection initiatives between different research groups would greatly benefit the field of ML-assisted NPS, to overcome the data scarcity problem.[31]

This study brings a new low cost, simple, and environmentally friendly approach to resolve this issue of data unavailability and proposes combining two methods:

- BBD to fit the model statistically and experimentally and then to generate input/output data; and
- ML to learn these input/output relationships for generalization.

Since BBD can fit a polynomial equation for the given input ranges, it is considered as a starting point for the generating of data samples to be used in the ML stage. A neural network (NN) is then formed for training and testing in the next stage. This proposed method is appropriate for any field using RSM approaches. In order to show its applicability, the NPS process was selected, and the performance of the system was validated with real experimental data, leading to results showing great potential.

In the current study, the reduction potential of the plant extract for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles was investigated.
