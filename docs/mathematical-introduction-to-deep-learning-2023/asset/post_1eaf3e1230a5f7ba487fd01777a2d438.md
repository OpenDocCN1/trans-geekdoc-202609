
## 5.7.4 Approximation error with respect to the objective function

**Corollary 5.7.5** (Approximation error with respect to the objective function). Let $\mathfrak{d} \in \mathbb{N}$, $c, L \in (0, \infty)$, $r \in (0, \infty]$, $\vartheta \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$, $\mathbb{B} = \{w \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} : \|w - \vartheta\|_2 \leq r\}$, $\xi \in \mathbb{B}$, $\mathcal{L} \in C^2(\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}, \mathbb{R})$ satisfy for all $\theta \in \mathbb{B}$ that

$$(\theta - \vartheta, (\nabla\mathcal{L})(\theta)) \geq c\|\theta - \vartheta\|_2^2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \|(\nabla\mathcal{L})(\theta)\|_2 \leq L\|\theta - \vartheta\|_2 \tag{5.189}$$

(cf. Definitions 1.4.7 and 3.3.4). Then

- (i) there exists a unique continuous function $\Theta : [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ such that for all $t \in [0, \infty)$ it holds that
  $$\Theta_t = \xi - \int_0^t (\nabla\mathcal{L})(\Theta_s) \, \mathrm{d}s, \tag{5.190}$$
- (ii) it holds that $\{\theta \in \mathbb{B} : \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \inf_{w \in \mathbb{B}} \mathcal{L}(w)\} = \{\vartheta\}$,
- (iii) it holds for all $t \in [0, \infty)$ that $\|\Theta_t - \vartheta\|_2 \leq e^{-ct}\|\xi - \vartheta\|_2$, and
- (iv) it holds for all $t \in [0, \infty)$ that
  $$0 \leq \tfrac{\xi}{2}\|\Theta_t - \vartheta\|_2^2 \leq \mathcal{L}(\Theta_t) - \mathcal{L}(\vartheta) \leq \tfrac{L}{2}\|\Theta_t - \vartheta\|_2^2 \leq \tfrac{L}{2} e^{-2ct}\|\xi - \vartheta\|_2^2. \tag{5.191}$$

*Proof of Corollary 5.7.5.* Theorem 5.7.4 and Lemma 5.6.9 establish items (i), (ii), (iii), and (iv). The proof of Corollary 5.7.5 is thus complete. $\square$

### 6.1 GD optimization

In this section we review and study the classical plain-vanilla **GD** optimization method (cf., for example, [303, Section 1.2.3], [52, Section 9.3], and [57, Chapter 3]). A simple intuition behind the **GD** optimization method is the idea to solve a minimization problem by performing successive steps in direction of the steepest descents of the objective function, that is, by performing successive steps in the opposite direction of the gradients of the objective function.

A slightly different and maybe a bit more accurate perspective for the **GD** optimization method is to view the **GD** optimization method as a plain-vanilla Euler discretization of the associated **GF ODE** (see, for example, Theorem **5.7.4** in Chapter **5** above).

**Definition 6.1.1 (GD optimization method).** Let $\mathfrak{d} \in \mathbb{N}$, $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq [0, \infty)$, $\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ and let $\mathcal{L}: \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ and $\mathcal{S}: \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ satisfy for all $U \in \{V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}: V \text{ is open}\}$, $\theta \in U$ with $\mathcal{L}|_U \in C^1(U, \mathbb{R}^d)$ that

$$\mathcal{S}(\theta) = (\nabla \mathcal{L})(\theta). \tag{6.1}$$

Then we say that $\Theta$ is the **GD** process for the objective function $\mathcal{L}$ with generalized gradient $\mathcal{S}$, learning rates $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, and initial value $\xi$ (we say that $\Theta$ is the **GD** process for the objective function $\mathcal{L}$ with learning rates $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ and initial value $\xi$ if and only if it holds that $\Theta : \mathbb{N}_0 \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ is the function from $\mathbb{N}_0$ to $\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ which satisfies for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ that

$$\Theta_0 = \xi \quad \text{and} \quad \Theta_n = \Theta_{n-1} - \gamma_n \mathcal{S}(\Theta_{n-1}). \tag{6.2}$$

### 6.1.2 Euler discretizations for GF ODEs

**Theorem 6.1.3** (Taylor's formula). *Let* $N \in \mathbb{N}$, $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, $\beta \in (\alpha, \infty)$, $a,b \in [\alpha, \beta]$, $f \in C^N([\alpha, \beta], \mathbb{R})$. *Then*

$$f(b) = \left[\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)(b-a)^n}{n!}\right] + \int_0^1 \frac{f^{(N)}(a + r(b-a))(b-a)^N(1-r)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!} \, \mathrm{d}r.$$ (6.6)

*Proof of Theorem 6.1.3*. Observe that the fundamental theorem of calculus assures that for all $g \in C^1([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ it holds that

$$g(1) = g(0) + \int_0^1 g'(r) \, \mathrm{d}r = g(0) + \int_0^1 \frac{g'(r)(1-r)^0}{0!} \, \mathrm{d}r.$$ (6.7)

Furthermore, note that integration by parts ensures that for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $g \in C^{n+1}([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ it holds that

$$\int_0^1 \frac{g^{(n)}(r)(1-r)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} \, \mathrm{d}r = \left[- \frac{g^{(n)}(r)(1-r)^n}{n!} \right]_{r=0}^{r=1} + \int_0^1 \frac{g^{(n+1)}(r)(1-r)^n}{n!} \, \mathrm{d}r$$
$$= \frac{g^{(n)}(0)}{n!} + \int_0^1 \frac{g^{(n+1)}(r)(1-r)^n}{n!} \, \mathrm{d}r.$$ (6.8)

Combining this with (6.7) and induction shows that for all $g \in C^N([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ it holds that

$$g(1) = \left[\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{g^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\right] + \int_0^1 \frac{g^{(N)}(r)(1-r)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!} \, \mathrm{d}r.$$ (6.9)

This establishes (6.6). The proof of Theorem 6.1.3 is thus complete. $\square$
