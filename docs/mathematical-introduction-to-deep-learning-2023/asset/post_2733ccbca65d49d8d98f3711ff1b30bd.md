
We now sketch this approach for the GD optimization method in (7.4) resulting in the popular SGD optimization method applied to (7.2).

Specifically, let $S = \{1, 2, \ldots, M\}$, $J \in \mathbb{N}$, let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space, for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $j \in \{1, 2, \ldots, J\}$ let $\mathbf{m}_{n,j} : \Omega \rightarrow S$ be a uniformly distributed random variable, let $\ell: \mathbb{R}^{9} \times S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ satisfy for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{9}$, $m \in S$ that

$\ell(\theta, m) = |\left(\mathcal{N}_{\frac{\partial d}{\partial \mathbf{w}_{n,1}}, \mathbf{w}_{n,2}, \ldots, \mathbf{w}_{n,\ell}, \mathrm{id}_{G}}(x_m) - \boldsymbol{y}_m\right)|^{2},$ (7.5)

and let $\Theta = (\Theta_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0} : \mathbb{N}_0 \times \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{9}$ satisfy for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ that

$\Theta_{0} = \xi \quad \text{and} \quad \Theta_{n} = \Theta_{n-1} - \gamma_{n}\left[\frac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\nabla_{\theta} \ell\right)\left(\Theta_{n-1}, \mathbf{m}_{n,j}\right)\right].$ (7.6)

The stochastic process $(\Theta_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}$ is an SGD process for the minimization problem associated to (7.2) with learning rates $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}_0}$, constant number of Monte Carlo samples (batch sizes) $J$, initial value $\xi$, and data $(\mathbf{m}_{n,j})_{(n,j) \in \mathbb{N} \times \{1, \ldots, J\}}$ (see Definition 7.2.1 below for the precise definition). Note that in (7.6) in each step $n \in \mathbb{N}$ we only employ a Monte Carlo approximation

$\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\nabla_{\theta} \ell\right)\left(\Theta_{n-1}, \mathbf{m}_{n,j}\right) \approx \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\nabla_{\theta} \ell\right)\left(\Theta_{n-1}, m\right) = \left(\nabla \mathcal{L}\right)\left(\Theta_{n-1}\right)$ (7.7)

of the exact gradient of the objective function. Nonetheless, in deep learning applications the SGD optimization method (or other SGD-type optimization methods) typically result in good approximate minimizers of the objective function. Note that employing approximate gradients in the SGD optimization method in (7.6) means that performing any step of the SGD process involves the computation of a sum with only $J$ summands, while employing the exact gradient in the GD optimization method in (7.4) means that performing any step of the process involves the computation of a sum with $M$ summands. In deep learning applications when $M$ is very large (for instance, $M \geq 10^{6}$) and $J$ is chosen to be reasonably small (for example, $J = 128$), this means that performing steps of the SGD process is much more computationally affordable than performing steps of the GD process. Combining this with the fact that SGD-type optimization methods do in the training of ANNs often find good approximate minimizers (cf., for instance, Remark 9.14.5 and [100, 391]) is the key reason making the SGD optimization method and other SGD-type optimization methods the optimization methods chosen in almost all deep learning applications. It is the topic of this chapter to introduce and study SGD-type optimization methods such as the plain-vanilla SGD optimization method in (7.6) above.

## 7.2 SGD optimization

In the next notion we present the promised stochastic version of the plain-vanilla GD optimization method from Section 6.1, that is, in the next notion we present the plain-vanilla **SGD** optimization method.

### **Definition 7.2.1** (**SGD** optimization method). 

Let $\mathfrak{d} \in \mathbb{N}$, $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq [0, \infty)$, $(J_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space, let $(S, \mathcal{S})$ be a measurable space, let $\xi: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ be a random variable, for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $j \in \{1, 2, \ldots, J_n\}$ let $X_{n,j}: \Omega \to S$ be a random variable, and let $\ell = (\ell(\theta, x))_{(\theta, x) \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} \times S}: \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} \times S \to \mathbb{R}$ and $\boldsymbol{g}: \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} \times S \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ satisfy for all $U \in \{V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}: V \text{ is open}\}$, $x \in S$, $\theta \in U$ with $(U \ni \vartheta \mapsto \ell(\vartheta, x) \in \mathbb{R}) \in \mathcal{C}^1(U, \mathbb{R})$ that

$\boldsymbol{g}(\theta, x) = (\nabla_\vartheta \ell)(\theta, x).$

Then we say that $\Theta$ is the **SGD** process on $((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P}), (S, \mathcal{S}))$ for the loss function $\ell$ with generalized gradient $\boldsymbol{g}$, learning rates $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, batch sizes $(J_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, initial value $\xi$, and data $(X_{n,j})_{(n,j) \in \{k\}_{k \in \mathbb{N}^2}: 1 \le J_k}$ (we say that $\Theta$ is the **SGD** process for the loss function $\ell$ with learning rates $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, batch sizes $(J_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, initial value $\xi$, and data $(X_{n,j})_{(n,j) \in \{k\}_{k \in \mathbb{N}^2}: 1 \le J_k}$) if and only if it holds that $\Theta: \mathbb{N}_0 \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ is the function from $\mathbb{N}_0 \times \Omega$ to $\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ which satisfies for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ that

$\Theta_0 = \xi \qquad \text{and} \qquad \Theta_n = \Theta_{n-1} - \gamma_n \left[\frac{1}{J_n} \sum_{j=1}^{J_n} \boldsymbol{g}(\Theta_{n-1}, X_{n,j})\right].$

### 7.2.1 **SGD** optimization in the training of **ANNs**

In the next example we apply the **SGD** optimization method in the context of the training of fully-connected feedforward **ANNs** in the vectorized description (see Section 1.1) with the loss function being the mean squared error loss function in Definition 5.4.2 (see Section 5.4.2). Note that this is a very similar framework as the one developed in Section 7.1.

### **Example 7.2.2.** 

Let $d, h, \mathfrak{d} \in \mathbb{N}$, $l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_h \in \mathbb{N}$ satisfy $\mathfrak{d} = l_1(d+1) + [\sum_{k=2}^{h} l_k(l_{k-1}+1)] + l_h+1$, let $a: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable, let $M \in \mathbb{N}$, $\boldsymbol{x}_1, \boldsymbol{x}_2, \ldots, \boldsymbol{x}_M \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $\boldsymbol{y}_1, \boldsymbol{y}_2, \ldots, \boldsymbol{y}_M \in \mathbb{R}$, let $\mathcal{L}: \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} \to [0, \infty)$ satisfy for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ that

$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{M} \left[\sum_{m=1}^{M} \left( \left( \mathcal{N}^{\theta,d}_{\mathfrak{w}_{l_1, l_1}, \mathfrak{w}_{a,l_2}, \ldots, \mathfrak{w}_{a,l_h}, \mathrm{id}_\mathbb{R}} \right)(\boldsymbol{x}_m) - \boldsymbol{y}_m \right)^2 \right] ,

let $S = \{1, 2, \ldots, M\}$, let $\ell: \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}} \times S \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfy for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$, $m \in S$ that

$\ell(\theta, m) = \left| \left( \mathcal{N}^{\theta,d}_{\mathfrak{w}_{l_1, l_1}, \mathfrak{w}_{a,l_2}, \ldots, \mathfrak{w}_{a,l_h}, \mathrm{id}_\mathbb{R}} \right)(\boldsymbol{x}_m) - \boldsymbol{y}_m \right|^2,$

let $\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$, let $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, let $\vartheta: \mathbb{N}_0 \to \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ satisfy for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ that

$\vartheta_0 = \xi \qquad \text{and} \qquad \vartheta_n = \vartheta_{n-1} - \gamma_n \left(\nabla \mathcal{L} \right) \left( \vartheta_{n-1} \right),$

let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space, let $(J_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $j \in \{1, 2, \ldots, J_n\}$ let $\mathbf{m}_{n, j}: \Omega \rightarrow S$ be a uniformly distributed random variable, and let $\Theta: \mathbb{N}_0 \times \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ satisfy for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ that

$\Theta_0 = \xi$ and $\Theta_n = \Theta_{n-1} - \gamma_n \left[ \frac{1}{J_n} \sum_{j=1}^{J_n} \left( \nabla_\theta \ell(\Theta_{n-1}, \mathbf{m}_{n,j}) \right) \right]$ 

(7.13)

(cf. Corollary 5.3.6). Then

- (i) it holds that $\vartheta$ is the GD process for the objective function $\mathcal{L}$ with learning rates $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ and initial value $\xi$

- (ii) it holds that $\Theta$ is the SGD process for the loss function $\ell$ with learning rates $(\gamma_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, batch sizes $(J_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, initial value $\xi$, and data $(\mathbf{m}_{n,j})_{(n,j) \in \{(k,l) \in \mathbb{N}^2 : l \leqslant J_k\}}$, and

- (iii) it holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ that

$\mathbb{E} \left[ \theta - \gamma_n \left[ \frac{1}{J_n} \sum_{j=1}^{J_n} \left( \nabla_\theta \ell \right)(\theta, \mathbf{m}_{n,j}) \right] \right] = \theta - \gamma_n \left( \nabla \mathcal{L} \right)(\theta).$

(7.14)

Proof for Example 7.2.2. Note that (7.12) proves item (i). Observe that (7.13) proves item (ii). Note that (7.11), (7.10), and the assumption that for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $j \in \{1, 2, \ldots, J_n\}$ it holds that $\mathbf{m}_{n,j}$ is uniformly distributed imply that for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $j \in \{1, 2, \ldots, J_n\}$ it holds that

$\mathbb{E}[\ell(\theta, \mathbf{m}_{n,j})] = \frac{1}{M} \left[ \sum_{m=1}^M \ell(\theta, m) \right]$

$= \frac{1}{M} \left[ \sum_{m=1}^M \left( \left\langle \mathcal{N}_{\mathfrak{M}_{n,i_1}}^{\theta, d}, \mathfrak{m}_{n,i_2}, \ldots, \mathfrak{m}_{n,i_d}\mathrm{\boldsymbol{d}\mathrm{g}} \right\rangle \left( \boldsymbol{x}_m \right) - y_m \right]^2 \right] = \mathcal{L}(\theta).$

(7.15)

Therefore, we obtain for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{d}}$ that

$\mathbb{E} \left[ \theta - \gamma_n \left[ \frac{1}{J_n} \sum_{j=1}^{J_n} \left( \nabla_\theta \ell \right)(\theta, \mathbf{m}_{n,j}) \right] \right] = \theta - \gamma_n \left[ \frac{1}{J_n} \sum_{j=1}^{J_n} \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta, \mathbf{m}_{n,j})] \right]$

$= \theta - \gamma_n \left[ \frac{1}{J_n} \sum_{j=1}^{J_n} \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta) \right]$

$= \theta - \gamma_n(\nabla \mathcal{L})(\theta).$

(7.16)
