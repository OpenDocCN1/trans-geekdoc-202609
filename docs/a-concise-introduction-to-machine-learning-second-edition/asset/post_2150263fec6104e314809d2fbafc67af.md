
the noise $\epsilon$, which leads to

```
$$\begin{aligned} E[(t-f(x))^2] &= E[t^2 + f(x)^2 - 2tf(x)] \\ &= E[t^2] + E[f(x)^2] - 2E[tf(x)] \\ &= var[t] + var[f(x)] + E[f(x)]^2 - 2E[tf(x)] \\ &= \sigma^2 + t(x)^2 + var[f(x)] + (E[f(x)] - t(x))^2 \\ &= \sigma^2 + var[f(x)] + (E[f(x)] - t(x))^2 \end{aligned}$$
```

The first term is the irreducible error due to the noise in the data. It is also the minimum achievable error. The second term is the *variance*. It varies around their mean arising from different snapshots of data. In other words, how sensitive the model is to a particular dataset. The last term is the square of the *bias*. The bias represents the extent to which the average prediction over all snapshots differs from the true process. In others, a small bias indicates that the generated models are robust to perturbations in the data. In general, making one of them smaller increases the other. This is called the *bias-variance trade-off*.

Note that the analysis so far was only concerned with one particular unseen data point $x$. To make a general statement about the model, we need to take the expectation with respect to $x$.

```
$E[(t-f(x))^2] p(x)dx = \int \left( \sigma^2 + var[f(x)] + (E[f(x)] - t(x))^2 \right) p(x)dx$.
```

This simplifies greatly, if $p(x)$ is a uniform distribution. Then, measurements $E[f(x)]$ and $var[f(x)]$ are equally likely taken everywhere.

In general, it is not possible to calculate $E[f(x)]$ and $var[f(x)]$. But it can be estimated, if multiple runs of the experiment are possible. For the 100 runs, the approximate squared bias, variance and their sums are in the following table:

### 8.7 Cross-validation

The analysis of cross-validation relied on the fact that experiments are repeatable, and thus data can be easily generated. However, often the acquisition of data is associated with substantial costs, and it is essential to use data well. If data are partitioned into complete subsets, one for training and one for validation, multiple rounds of cross-validation are performed, and the results are averaged over the rounds. This way the goodness of the model can be assessed. There are two types of cross-validation: exhaustive and non-exhaustive. The former employs all possible ways to choose $p$ validation samples from $n$ samples, which means the regression algorithm has to run $n$ times even for moderate $n$ and become computationally prohibitive. In $k$-fold cross-validation, the dataset is randomly subdivided into $k$ sized subsets. One of these is used as the validation set while the other $k-1$ form the training set. The model generation is repeated $k$ times (the folds) with each of the subsets used once. All samples are used for both training and validation. When $k=n$, $k$-fold cross-validation is the same as leave-one out.

### 8.8 Multicollinearity and Principal Component Regression

Multicollinearity is a problem which arises when one or more predictor variables are highly correlated. In this case, one dimension of the model space is redundant. A high degree of correlation increases the variance in the coefficients, since different models model the measurements but differ in the coefficients. As a consequence, small changes in the input data can lead to large changes in the model. Perfect multicollinearity means the regressors are linearly dependent, that is, one can be expressed by the others. In this case $(\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}$ does not exist, the matrix is singular.

In a good regression model, the regressors correlate minimally with each other, but are each highly correlated with the regressor. The aim is to find a linear combination of few regressors which summarize and explain the data without too much loss of information. This section and the following show how to obtain new regressors from the dictionary. Principal regression uses the principal components of $D$ as regressors instead of the columns of $D$. In particular, let $d_1, \dots, d_M$ denote the columns of $D$. In this case $d_i$ is the $i^{th}$ basis function evaluated at $x_1, \dots, x_n$. In the following, we assume the columns are standardized, that is they mean 0 and have length 1. The first assumption is valid since the measures are centred, and the second assumption is valid since regressors are invariant to scaling. Any scaling will be absorbed in the multiplicative coefficient.

The correlation between the $i^{th}$ and $j^{th}$ regressors evaluated at the $N$ measuring points is $d_i^T d_j$.

The correlation matrix of all regressors is $D^T D$.

A set of regressors is generated as linear combinations of basis functions from the dictionary, say $v_1(\mathbf{x}) + \dots + v_M(\mathbf{x})$. Evaluating this at the $N$ sampling points, we arrive at a linear combination of the columns of $D$:

$v_1 d_1 + \dots + v_M d_M = \mathbf{v}$ where $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, \dots, v_M)^T$ is chosen such that $D\mathbf{v} \neq 0$. The zero vector would make a very poor regressor.

The correlation between two linear combinations is

fracfrac{\mathbf{v}_1^T D^T \mathbf{v}_2}{\|\mathbf{v}_1\|\|\mathbf{v}_2\|}$ where $v_1$ and $v_2$ are such that $D\mathbf{v}_1 \neq 0$ and $D\mathbf{v}_2 \neq 0$. Here we have to divide by the lengths since $D\mathbf{v}_1$ and $D\mathbf{v}_2$ no longer have length 1.

Now, the matrix $D^T D$ is symmetric and positive semi-definite. Hence it has $M$ nonnegative eigenvalues and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors. Thus choosing $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ to be eigenvectors of $D^T D$, the new regressors are uncorrelated, since

$\mathbf{v}_1^T D^T \mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_2 \mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{v}_2 = 0$.
