
With this we can simplify the terms in $\mathcal{L}$. First,

$$\log |C| = \log |C_{-j}| + \log (1 + \sigma^{-2}\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j). \quad (7.4)$$

Differentiating this with respect to $\mathbf{w}_j$ using **A2.1** gives

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{w}_j}\log |C| = \frac{1}{1+\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j}\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{w}_j = \frac{2}{\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j}\mathbf{w}_j.$$

On the other hand,

$tr(C^{-1}\Sigma) = tr(C_{-j}\Sigma)\frac{\sigma^{-2}}{\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j}tr(\mathbf{w}_j^T\Sigma) = tr(C_{-j}\Sigma)\frac{\sigma^{-2}}{\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j}\mathbf{w}_j,$

where we used the cyclic permutation property of the trace. Using the product rule, **A.2** and **A2.3**, the derivative with respect to $\mathbf{w}_j$ is

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{w}_j}tr(C^{-1}\Sigma) = \frac{\sigma^{-2}}{\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j}[(\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j)\Sigma\mathbf{w}_j - (\mathbf{w}_j^T\Sigma)2\mathbf{w}_j].$$

Therefore, the derivative of the likelihood with respect to $\mathbf{w}_j$ is

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{w}_j}\mathcal{L} = \frac{\sigma^{-2}}{\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j}[(\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j)\mathbf{w}_j - \Sigma\mathbf{w}_j].$$

This vanishes, if $\mathbf{w}_j$ is an eigenvector of $\Sigma$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_j$ and the length of $\mathbf{w}_j$ is that

$$\frac{\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{w}_j}{\sigma^{-2}+\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j} = \lambda_j,$$

Using $\mathbf{w}_j = \lambda_j\mathbf{w}_j$, we arrive at $\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j = \lambda_j$, or equivalently $\mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{w}_j = \lambda_j$; since $\Sigma$ has a set of mutually orthogonal eigenvectors this agrees with our assumption.

Figure 7.5 shows two-dimensional data and the principal component $\mathbf{w}_1$ is drawn as a red arrow with length $\sqrt{\lambda_1}\sigma^{-2}$ starting at the sample mean, where $\lambda_1$ is the length of the covariance matrix. In the following we will see that choosing the largest eigenvectors is the right choice to maximize the log likelihood.

Letting $\mathbf{w}_k, k=1, \dots, K$, be some eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ with squared length $\mathbf{w}_k^T\mathbf{w}_k = \lambda_k - \sigma^{-2}$ and inserting this into (7.4), we get

$$\|C| = |C_{-j}|(1+\sigma^{-2}(\lambda_j-\sigma^{-2})) = (\sigma^{-2})^{D-K}\prod_{k=1}^K |\lambda_k|^2\prod_{k=1}^K \lambda_k.$$

![](img/c6c76bf52016db7e78a5e30d4766ef81_233_0.png)

Equally using this in (7.3), we arrive at

$$C^{-1} = \sigma^2 \left(I - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\mathbf{w}_k\mathbf{w}_k^T}{\lambda_k}\right),$$

and

$$tr(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}) = \sigma^{-2} \left(tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\mathbf{w}_k^T\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}_k}{\lambda_k}\right) = \sigma^{-2} \left(tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lambda_k\right) + K,$$

where we used the cyclic permutation of the trace and the fact that $\mathbf{w}_k$ is an eigenvector of $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ with squared length $\mathbf{w}_k^T\mathbf{w}_k = \lambda_k - \sigma^2$.

With these results, the expression for the log likelihood is

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{N}{2} \left[ D\log(2\pi) + (D-K)\log\sigma^2 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log\lambda_k + \sigma^{-2} \left( \sum_{k=K+1}^{D} \lambda_k \right) \right]$$

since the trace of a matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues. We differentiate this with respect to $\sigma^2$ in order to optimize this with respect to noise variance.

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma^2}\mathcal{L} = -\frac{N}{2} \left( (D-K)(\sigma^2)^{-1} - \sigma^{-2} \sum_{k=K+1}^{D} \lambda_k \right)$$

This will be zero for

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{D-K} \sum_{k=K+1}^{D} \lambda_k.$$

![](img/c6c76bf52016db7e78a5e30d4766ef81_234_0.png)

(a) Viewing the plane spanned by two principal components.
(b) Viewing the data along one of principal components.

Figure 7.6: Probabilistic Component Analysis for three-dimensional data.

In other words, $\sigma^2$ is the average of all the other eigenvalues apart from $\lambda_1,...,lambda_K$. As mentioned before, $\sigma^2$ explains the data beyond the $K$-dimensional subspace. This needs to be as small as possible. Therefore $\lambda_1,...,lambda_K$ are chosen to be the $K$ largest eigenvalues of $\Sigma$. The likelihood takes value

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{N}{2}[D\log(2\pi) + (D-K)\log(\frac{1}{D-K}\sum_{k=K+1}^D \lambda_k) + \sum_{k=1}^K \log \lambda_k]$$

Figure 7.6 illustrates the concepts. The data is viewed from two angles, first onto the plane spanned by the two principal components and secondly along one of principal components. The latter shows that not all data points lie in the plane of the two principal components. The noise variance $\sigma^2$ explains this deviation and is equal to the smallest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix.

In this section we derived PCA by maximizing the likelihood. More generally, Bishop et al. (203) show that many techniques for dimensionality reduction optimize a lower bound of the likelihood. The method in the next section utilises this lower bound to maximize the likelihood iteratively.

## 7.3 Expectation-Maximization

The previous section was based on the assumption that the data samples $\mathbf{v}_1,...,mathbf{v}_N$ are related to vectors in a lower dimensional space via a linear mapping given by (7.2) with zero mean and covariance matrix $\sigma^2I$. Therefore, each $\mathbf{v}_n$ follows a conditional distribution with mean $\mathbf{Wu}_n + \mu$ and covariance matrix $\sigma^2I$, where $n=1,...,N$, involving hidden latent variables following the standard normal distribution. The solution in the previous section effectively calculated the marginal distribution from which $\mathbf{v}_n$ are drawn by marginalizing the latent variables. The likelihood of the dataset was then maximized with respect to $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$. The likelihood takes a maximum with respect to $\mu$, when $m=\mu$, which is the sample mean and can be computed. The maximization with respect to $\mathbf{W}$ and $\sigma^2$, involves finding the $K$ largest eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix is in itself a computationally expensive problem. Calculating the covariance matrix, the computational complexity of finding the $K$ largest eigenvalues is $\mathcal{O}(ND^2)$ (see for example [16]).

In the following, we will use the result $m=\mu$ and we employ the Expectation-Maximization technique introduced in Section 6.4 to find $\mathbf{W}$ and $\sigma^2$, making use of the joint distribution of $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{a}$ and the distribution of $\mathbf{W}$ conditioned on $\mathbf{v}$. Note that in 6.4 the latent variables are discrete, here they are continuous. The principles remain the same.

By the product rule, the joint probability is

$$p(v, \mathbf{u}) = p(u)p(v|u) \times (2\pi)^{-K/2} \exp(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{u}) \times (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-D/2} \exp(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}(v-\mathbf{W}u-\mu)^T(v-\mathbf{W}u-\mu))$$

Combining the exponential terms gives exponent

$$-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}(v-\mathbf{W}\mu)^T(v-\mathbf{W}\mu)^T\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{W}^T(v-\mu)+\mathbf{u}^T(\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W}+\sigma^2\mathbf{I}) \quad (7.5)$$

This can be written as

$$-\frac{1}{2}(\begin{pmatrix} v \\ \mu \end{pmatrix})^T \sigma^{-2} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{I} & -\mathbf{W} \\ -\mathbf{W}^T & \mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W}+\sigma^2\mathbf{I} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v \\ \mu \end{pmatrix}$$

Hence the mean of the joint distribution is $(\mu, 0)^T$, while the variance with scalar factor $\sigma^{-2}$ is the precision matrix, the inverse of the covariance matrix. Therefore, using the inversion formula for a block matrix in A.1.1, the covariance matrix of the joint distribution is

$$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{W} \\ \mathbf{W}^T & \mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W}+\sigma^2\mathbf{I} \end{pmatrix}$$

Using the formula for the calculation of the determinant of a block matrix in A.1.2, the determinant of the joint covariance matrix is $(\sigma^2)^D$ which agrees with the factor of the joint probability distribution.
