# Preface

Machine Learning is known under many other names such as Artificial Intelligence (AI), Pattern Recognition, Data Mining, Data Assimilation, and Big Data, to list but a few. It developed in many areas of science, for example physics, engineering, computer science, and mathematics in parallel and independently. Optical Character Recognition (OCR), search engines, computer vision, Natural Language Processing (NLP), discovery, fraud detection, robotics, data prediction, and drug discovery, makes it sometimes difficult to find a solution for a particular problem in the future, simply because of the fact that different words and phrases are used for the concept.

This book aims to alleviate this. A common concept, but known in sciences under different names, is described using the common language. Readers will find the index useful to find a particular topic as it is known to them. The index is comprehensive, making it easy to find the required information. Hopefully, this will prove the book useful as a reference and make it essential on the bookshelves of anybody learning Machine Learning techniques.

Often, in teaching Machine Learning, the emphasis is on the questions "What?” and "How?", and the question "Why?” is neglected. In my opinion, this is the most important question. If it is understood why an algorithm is successful it can be properly applied. On the other hand, if it is not understood why it arrived at its results, these results cannot be trusted. A more recent reason, is the necessary processing power, and energy consumption, of very large models such as Large Language Models (LLMs). Here algorithmic improvements can reduce the size and cost. These are only possible, if the inner workings are understood.

Algorithms are often taught by side by side showing the similarities and differences between them. This book addresses this by introducing the commonalities. Most techniques to find an approximate model modeling the data in a finite, low-dimensional space. They differ in how a solution to this approximate model is chosen. This approach helps to keep the book concise while still giving a thorough and in-depth treatment. In some places, where further detail was felt to be out of the scope of this book, the reader is referred to further reading. # Introduction

When thinking about Machine Learning, it seems prudent to start thinking about how we learn. King Frederick II (1194 – 1250) was Holy Roman Emperor and King of Sicily in the 13th century. King Frederick was a passionate patron of the sciences and arts. He spoke six languages which were Latin, Sicilian, German, French, Greek and Arabic. He desired to determine the “God given” language, as translated by Bairde et al. (1986), that Frederick bade:

> ...foster-mothers and nurses to suckle and bathe and wash the children, but in no ways to practice or speak with them; for he would have learned whether they would speak the Hebrew language (which had been first), or Greek, or Latin, or Arabic, or perchance the tongue of their parents whom they had born. But he laboured in vain, for the children could not live without clappings of the hands, and gestures, and gladness of countenance,

In other words the physical needs of the children were satisfied, but they were raised without any human interaction. It is doubtful whether this is a true account, since this is the only account and Salimbene was a political opponent of Frederick II. Nevertheless, nobody doubts that sensory stimulation and experiences are essential for learning in any respect.

Blemore & Cooper (1970) experimented with kittens. The kittens were brought up in a dark room and only brought out at certain times and placed in an environment with either only horizontal or vertical lines. Kittens brought up in the horizontal environment showed no reaction to vertical lines. There was no brain activity. Indeed, when the inclination of horizontal line was changed gradually towards a vertical line the brain activity became less and less.

To answer this question, we need to take a step back and look at how we learn, but how we teach. Traditional teaching is from the front of the classroom. The pupils are given a set of instructions and are expected to reproduce this. This is very similar to procedural programming where the flow of instructions is identified. Object-oriented programming was a further development where instructions depend on the nature of the task.
