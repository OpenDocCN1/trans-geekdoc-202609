$p(X) = \sum p(X|Y)p(Y)$.

We see that this is the sum of the numerator in Bayes' rule over all instances of Y. Therefore it is sometimes referred to as the *normalizing factor*, since it ensures that

$p(Y|X) = 1$.

The sum of probabilities over all instances always adds to one. Lastly, the probability $p(Y|X)$ quantifies our *posterior* belief hence we have observed and taken into account the data. The equivalent is revising our opinion once experiences have been made. While many other factors influence a human revis their opinion or not (e.g. their stubbornness), Bayes' rule is a mathematical process to do. If there are different hypotheses with associated variables Y of explaining the data, $p(Y|X)$ can be used to select a hypothesis. It is then called the *evidence*. Robert (2001) elaborates on the Bayesian methodology, while McGraw (2011) gives many examples how the repeated application of Bayes' rule solved real world problems.
