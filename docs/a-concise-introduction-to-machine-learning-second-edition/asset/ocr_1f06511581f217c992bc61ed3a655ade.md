Applying this to the setting at hand,

```
$$p(\mathbf{c}|\mathbf{D}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{t}|\mathbf{c})p(\mathbf{c})}{p(\mathbf{t})}$$
```

In other words, we answer the question: What is the probability of the coefficients given the mean observed data?

Since the noise is i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$, we can write for the probability of $t$ given the design matrix $\mathbf{D}$, coefficients $\mathbf{c}$ and noise variance $\sigma^2$

```
$$p(\mathbf{c}|\mathbf{D}, \sigma^2) = (2\pi)^{-N/2}\sigma^{-N}\exp\left(-\frac{\{\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{Dc}\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$
```

We define a *prior* distribution for $c$ using all information apart from the data itself, quantifying our belief about the coefficients. For example, a simple assumption is that each coefficient $\alpha$ is *a priori* normally distributed with mean zero and variance $\alpha^{-1}$. The multivariate distribution of $c$ is given by

```
$$p(\alpha) = \frac{\alpha^{M/2}}{(2\pi)^{M/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}c^2\right)$$
```

In the absence of any other knowledge, this is one possible assumption. $\alpha$ is a *hyperparameter* and known as the *precision* of the distribution. If $\alpha$ becomes very large, the distribution becomes peaked at its mean and we have more confidence in the value than $\alpha$ is small and the width of the distribution large.

After observing $N$ samples of the dependent variable $t$, the *posterior distribution* of the coefficients $c$ is given by

```
$$p(\mathbf{c}|\mathbf{D}, \alpha, \sigma^2) \propto p(\mathbf{t}|\mathbf{D}, \sigma^2)p(\mathbf{c})$$
```

An estimate of $c$ could be obtained by choosing the $c$ where the posterior distribution or equivalently its logarithm is maximal,

```
$$log p(\mathbf{c}|\mathbf{D}, \sigma^2) \propto \log p(\mathbf{t}|\mathbf{D}, \sigma^2) + \log p(\mathbf{c})$$$
```

The first term is exactly the log likelihood (8.1), which is maximized when $|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{Dc}\|^2$ is minimal. The second term is

```
$$-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|c\|^2 + \frac{M}{2}(\log \alpha - \log(2\pi))$$$
```

and can be regarded as the negative of a penalty term. In fact, taking the negative logarithm of the posterior distribution and minimizing instead of maximizing, recreate regression.
