The cumulative distribution function is zero for $x < 0$ and $x \geq 0$ given by

$F(x) = p(t \leq x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} Exp(t|\beta)dt = \int_{0}^{x} \exp\left(-\frac{t}{\beta}\right)dt = 1 - \exp\left(-\frac{x}{\beta}\right).$

It gives us that the event has happened in this time frame. On the other hand, $1 - F(x) = \exp\left(-\frac{x}{\beta}\right) = p(t > x)$ is the probability that the event has not occurred yet.

Examples of the probability density function and the cumulative distribution function for various values of $\beta$ are given in Figures 2.15 and 2.16 respectively.

$p(t > x + y|t > x)p(t > x) = p(t > x + y)$ and $p(t > x + y) = p(t > y)$.

Solving for $p(t > x + y|t > x)$, we arrive at

$p(t > x + y|t > x) = \frac{p(t > x + y)}{p(t > x)} = \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{x+y}{\beta}\right)}{\exp\left(-\frac{x}{\beta}\right)} = \exp\left(-\frac{y}{\beta}\right) = p(t > y).$

In practical terms, this means that the time one has to wait for an event, is not influenced by all the time spent waiting so far $x$. This means the exponential distribution is memoryless, a property it shares with the geometric distribution. The exponential distribution is the continuous equivalent of the geometric distribution which describes the number of attempts of a binary experiment to succeed. Instead of thinking about failures and successes, it is more convenient to think about a change of state. $\lambda$ gives the rate of change over a time interval, and the exponential distribution describes the probability of how much time states changes. Constant rate of change is a crucial assumption not satisfied by real-world processes. For example, the rate of emergency calls on Friday and Saturday nights and staffing levels need to be adjusted compared to other times. The exponential distribution is inappropriate to model the overall lifetime of organisms or technical devices, where the event of death or failure depends on the lifetime so far. If the rate is constant, it is used in queuing theory.

Using integration by parts, the expectation is given by

$E[x] = \int_{0}^{\infty} x \exp\left(-\frac{x}{\beta}\right) dx = \left[ -\frac{x}{\beta} \exp\left(-\frac{x}{\beta}\right) \right]_{0}^{\infty} + \int_{0}^{\infty} \exp\left(-\frac{x}{\beta}\right) dx = \left[ -\beta \exp\left(-\frac{x}{\beta}\right) \right]_{0}^{\infty} = \beta.$

The variance can be calculated similarly and $var[x] = \beta^2$.

If the positive part of probability density function is mirrored across the vertical axis at zero shifted and scaled such that it integrates to one, it becomes the Laplace probability density function as seen in Figure 2.17. The probability density function of the Laplace $x \sim Laplace(\mu, \beta)$ is given by

$Laplace(x|\mu, b) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\left(-\frac{|x - \mu|}{b}\right).$

where $\mu$ is known as the location parameter and $b$ is a scale parameter called the diversity. Its expectation is $E[x] = \mu$ and the variance $var[x] = \beta^2$. Examples of the cumulative distribution function are shown in Figure 2.2 and 2.4 illustrating the normal distribution, since the mean is the same, and $b$ chosen such that the variance is the same as well. This distribution is a suitable choice if the data suggest a more peaked density function than the normal distribution.

Next, we consider a probability density known as the beta distribution, $x \sim Beta(\alpha, \beta)$. It is only defined on the interval $(0,1)$ and its probability density function is

$Beta(x|\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1},$

![](img/c6c76bf52016db7e78a5e30d4766ef81_62_0.png)

Figure 2.17: The Laplace probability density function for various values of $\mu$ and $b$, chosen such that the mean and variance are the same as in Figure 2.2.

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are real numbers and positive. The function $\Gamma(z)$ is known as the *gamma function* and defined as

$$\Gamma(z) = \int_{0}^{\infty} x^{z-1} \exp(-x)dx.$$

Using *integration by parts*, we can calculate

$$\Gamma(z+1) = \int_{0}^{\infty} x^z \exp(-x)dx = \left[ -x^z \exp(-x) \right]_0^{\infty} + \int_{0}^{\infty} z x^{z-1} \exp(-x)dx.$$

The first term vanishes since at zero we have a positive power of $z$, while when $x$ tends to infinity, $\exp(-x)$ tends to zero quicker than any power of $x$. Therefore,

$$\Gamma(z+1) = \int_{0}^{\infty} x^{z-1} \exp(-x)dx = z\Gamma(z), \quad (2.14)$$

and it is regarded as extension of the factorial to real, positive numbers. Indeed, if $z=n$ is an integer, then using the above relation again and again leads

$$\Gamma(n+1) = n\Gamma(n) = n(n-1)\dots\Gamma(1) = n!,$$

since $\Gamma(1) = 1$. The fraction

$$B(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}$$

is known as the *beta function*, giving the distribution its name. It is related to the inverse of the binomial coefficient. Indeed, if $\alpha = m$ and $\beta = n$ are integers,

$$\frac{1}{B(m,n)} = \frac{(m+n-1)!}{(m-1)!(n-1)!} = \frac{mn}{m+n} \binom{m+n}{m}$$

With this, we see that the beta density function has a functional form similar to the probability density function of the binomial distribution (2.7). The main difference is that in the binomial distribution the random variable is the number of successes $m$, while in the beta distribution the random variable is $x$. It takes place of the probability of success. Because of this, the beta distribution is often seen as a distribution over proportions. It gives the probability of certain $x$. We will see in Section 2.6 how we can use the functional similarity to our advantage.

Figure 2.19 shows the beta density function for different choices of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ which encompasses many different shapes. For $\alpha = \beta = 1$, the beta distribution becomes the uniform distribution. When $\alpha$ or $\beta$ is less than one, the density function will go to infinity at the corresponding edge of the interval.

The expectation is calculated as

$E[x] = \int_{0}^{1} x \text{Beta}(x|\alpha,\beta)dx = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} \int_{0}^{1} x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)} \int_{0}^{1} \text{Beta}(x|\alpha+1,\beta)dx = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}.$

where we used the property given in 2.14 of the gamma function and the fact that the integral of any probability density function over its range is one. The variance is given

$$var[x] = \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}$$

When $\alpha=\beta$, the expectation is always $1/2$, while the variance is $(4(2\alpha+1))^{-1}$. Therefore, the graphs of the probability density function for these values are symmetric about $1/2$, while the graphs of the cumulative distribution function in Figure 2.20 pass through $(1/2, 1/2)$.

- The Beta distribution can be viewed as the distribution over the probabilities of experiments with binary outcomes, where $x$ is the probability of success and $1-x$ the probability of failure. If $x$ has the distribution $\text{Beta}(x|\alpha,\beta)$, then $1-x$ has the distribution $\text{Beta}(1-x|\beta,\alpha)$.

Just as we moved from the Bernoulli distribution of binary variables to the categorical distribution for experiments with multiple, mutually distinct outcomes, we extend the beta distribution to a distribution of $K$ probabilities $0 \le x_k \le 1, k=1, \dots, K$, which have to sum to one:

$$\sum_{k=1}^K x_k = 1$$

The probabilities are gathered in the random variable vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1,\dots, x_K)^T$. Because the constraints $x$ can only lie in a subset of the space of vectors of length $K$. For $K=3$, this is illustrated in Figure 2.21. The generalization to higher dimensions is known as a *simplex*. For $K=3$ the 2-simplex is the triangle.

The distribution is known as the *Dirichlet distribution* for $\mathbf{x} \sim Dir(\alpha)$ and the probability density function is given by

$$Dir(\alpha) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_1 + \dots + \alpha_K)}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\dots\Gamma(\alpha_K)} \prod_{k=1}^K x_k^{\alpha_k-1}, \quad (2.15)$$

The parameters $\alpha = (\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_K)^T$ are known as *concentration parameters*. They are all positive. When $K=2$, the Dirichlet distribution is the beta distribution.
