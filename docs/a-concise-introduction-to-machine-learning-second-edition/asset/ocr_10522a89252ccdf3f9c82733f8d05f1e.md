We can conclude that there is a need for experiences. However, how does a machine "experience?" We can view our senses as taking measurements, and our brain interprets and draws conclusions. A machine can take various measurements and perform calculations, but can it emulate the power of a human brain?

To answer this question, we need to take a step back and look at how we learn, but how we teach. Traditional teaching is from the front of the classroom. The pupils are given a set of instructions and are expected to reproduce this. This is very similar to procedural programming where the flow of instructions is identified. Object-oriented programming was a further development where instructions depend on the nature of the task.

However, our brain seems to learn much better when it has the positive experience of discovery. This is teacher-led learning. Galileo Galilei (2003) illustrates this in his book Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Relating to New Sciences. One of the sciences is science of motion. The ideas are developed as a dialogue between three characters, Salvati, Sagredo and Simplicio. The latter is portrayed as a simpleton, the pupil to be instructed. The starting point is the observation that even though objects have different masses, they reach the ground at the same time. However, this is very difficult to quantify; it is all too fast.
