
Exponential kernel $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \theta_1^2 \exp \left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}{\theta_2}\right)$.

Multiquadric kernel $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \theta_1^2 \left(1+\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2}{2\theta_2^2}\right)$.

Inverse multiquadric kernel $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \theta_1^2 \left(1+\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2}{\theta_2^2}\right)^{-1/2}$.

Rational quadratic kernel $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \theta_1^2 \exp \left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2}{2\alpha\theta_2^2}\right)^{-\alpha}, \alpha > 0$.

## Thin plate spline kernel

As an example we show the Gaussian is a valid kernel. Firstly,

$$\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2 = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x} - 2\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}$$

and the kernel can be written as

$$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \theta_1 \exp \left(-\frac{1}{2\theta_2^2}(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x})\right) \exp \left(-\frac{1}{\theta_2^2}(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y})\right) \theta_1 \exp \left(-\frac{1}{2\theta_2^2}(\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y})\right)$$
$$= \theta_1 f(\mathbf{x}) \exp \left(-\frac{1}{\theta_2^2}(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y})\right) f(\mathbf{y}).$$

This is a valid kernel, since it is the exponential of the inner product multiplied by a positive constant and because of Equation (5.1).

The Gaussian kernel is also known as *squared exponential kernel* or *exponentiated quadratic kernel*. It is an intuitive similarity measure. If the sample $y$ is the same as sample $x$, the Gaussian kernel evaluates to $\theta_1^2$, with its maximum value. $\theta_1$ is known as the *signal deviation*. With the Euclidean distance increasing, the similarity measured by the Gaussian kernel decreases and the degree of decrease is determined by $\theta_2$, which is the *characteristic length scale*.

Figure 5.4 shows two examples of Gaussian kernels, for different choices of $\theta_2$. The choice of $\theta_2$ is crucial in the performance of the kernel, since it determines the width of the kernel. The larger $\theta_2$, the wider the base of the kernel. When the support vector machine employs kernels, they are centred at the support vectors. If it is chosen too zero, the kernels evaluate nearly zero for all samples except the ones close to support vectors. If it is chosen too wide, the kernels evaluate nearly similar to each other; the slope is not at all. It is then hard to make a distinction between samples.

The exponential kernel is also an intuitive similarity measure. Equality of $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ is, however, more emphasized by the Gaussian kernel with the exponential kernel being sharply peaked at zero. Figure 5.5 gives two examples for different choices of $\theta_2$.
