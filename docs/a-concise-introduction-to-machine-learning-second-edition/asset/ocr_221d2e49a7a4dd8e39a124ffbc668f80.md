The above relies on the fact that classes are balanced. Imbalanced classes have their own challenges. For example, if there are nine times more samples of one class than the other class, a classifier which assigns the first class, will be correct 90% of the time. However, He & Ma (2013) show how to tackle imbalanced classes.

So far we have not considered the cost of acquiring features. The sources of cost can be quite varied. For example, when considering the computational cost, it may be the low cost associated with a simple algorithm. At the other end of the scale are computationally intensive algorithms which require large storage. There is also the difference between cheap and expensive diagnostic tools. It is expensive to check the oil level in one's car, which goes beyond, often a mechanic is required, however hourly rate is a considerable cost. Invasive procedures put much more strain on a patient than a simple medical test.

A cascade is a procedure which takes into account the cost. Each stage of a cascade of classifiers uses features with predictive power and increasing cost. For example, in medical diagnosis a general practitioner does a simple inexpensive test which can exclude a diagnosis. Should the test, however, reveal the possibility of an underlying health problem, the patient may be referred for further tests.

Let $C_0$ be the negatives and class $C_1$ be the positives. The number of samples in $C_0$ is commonly denoted $N$, while the number of samples in $C_1$ is denoted $P$.

A confusion matrix visualizes the goodness of a classifier by listing the number of samples of $C_0$ correctly classified, known as *true negatives*, TN, the number of samples of $C_0$ misclassified, which are the *false positives*, FP, on one hand, and on the other the number of samples of $C_1$ correctly classified, the *true positives*, TP, and the number of samples of $C_1$ misclassified, the *false negatives*, FN:

```
$$\begin{pmatrix} TN & FN \ FP & TP \end{pmatrix}$
```
