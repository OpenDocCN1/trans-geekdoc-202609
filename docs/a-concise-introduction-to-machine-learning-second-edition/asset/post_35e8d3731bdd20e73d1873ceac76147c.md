
Let **V** be the matrix with columns $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_K$. Then $\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{DV}$. The predictions are 

$f = (\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^T t = \mathbf{DV}(\mathbf{V}^T\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{V}^T)\mathbf{D}^T t.$

Now $\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D}$ is the matrix with columns $\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \lambda_K\mathbf{v}_K$, since $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_K$ are eigenvectors of $\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D}$. It follows from the orthogonality of $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_K$ that

$(\mathbf{V}^T\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\lambda_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \dots & & \vdots \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & \dots & 1/\lambda_K \end{pmatrix}$

With this result, we obtain the predicted values $f = (f_1, \dots, f_N)^T:$

$\mathbf{f} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\lambda_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \dots & & \vdots \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & \dots & 1/\lambda_K \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} (\mathbf{Dv}_1)^T \\ \vdots \\ \vdots \\ (\mathbf{Dv}_K)^T \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{t}$

$f = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{(\mathbf{Dv}_k)^T t}{\lambda_k}\mathbf{Dv}_k,$

where $f_i = f(\mathbf x)$ is the constructed model. Geometrically, this is the projection of the regressor $\mathbf{t}$ onto the space spanned by the columns of the new design matrix $\mathbf{D}$. The coefficient for the new regressor $c_i = (\mathbf{Dv}_i)^T t/\lambda_i$ in this model.

To analyse, we decompose the regressor $\mathbf{Dv}_1, \dots, \mathbf{Dv}_K$ into one portion lying in the subspace spanned by the columns $a_k$ and a remainder $\mathbf{a}$,

$\mathbf{t} = \sum_{k=1}^K a_k\mathbf{Dv}_k + \mathbf{a},$

where $\mathbf{a}$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{Dv}_1, \dots, \mathbf{Dv}_K$. We then have

$c_i = \frac{1}{\lambda_i}(\mathbf{Dv}_i)^T (\sum_{k=1}^K a_k\mathbf{Dv}_k + \mathbf{a}) = \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\sum_{k=1}^K a_k\mathbf{v}_i^T\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{Dv}_k = a_i,$

as expected because of the orthogonality of $\mathbf{v}_i, \dots, \mathbf{v}_K$.

We can calculate the distance between **t** and **f**

$$|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{f}\|^2 = \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t} - 2\mathbf{f}^T\mathbf{t} + \mathbf{f}^T\mathbf{f}$$
$$= \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t} - 2\sum_{i=1}^K \frac{(Dv_i)^T\mathbf{t}}{\lambda_i} (Dv_i)$$
$$+\sum_{i,j=1}^K \frac{(Dv_i)^T\mathbf{t}}{\lambda_i}\frac{(Dv_j)^T\mathbf{f}}{\lambda_j}(T^Dv_j)$$
$$= \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t} - \sum_{i=1}^K \frac{((Dv_i)^T\mathbf{t})^2}{\lambda_i}=\|a\|^2$$

where we used the fact that $(Dv_i)^Tv_j = \lambda_i$, if $i=j$ and zero otherwise. Thus the predictions are closest, all eigenvectors with nonzero eigenvalues are used. Using the decomposition of **t**, we have

$$(Dv_i)^T\mathbf{t} = \sum_{k=1}^K a_kv_k^TDv_k + v_k^T D^T a = a_i\lambda_i,$$

where we made again use of orthogonality. This introduces into the distance **t** and **f**, we obtain

$$|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{f}\|^2 = \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t} - \sum_{i=1}^K \alpha_i^2\lambda_i.$$

If sparsity is required, not all eigenvectors can be used, those for which $\alpha_i^2\lambda_i$ is largest chosen. However, this implicitly depends on the data **t** via the coefficient $\alpha_i$, and can cause the model to not generalize well to unseen data. To avoid this, it is customary to choose the eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues. Determining the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of a matrix is no trivial matter in itself, and can be computationally expensive, especially if M is large. Faul (2016) gives some iterative techniques to find the largest eigenvalues. Note that generating regressors is equivalent to generating new features. These features are linear combinations of the original. Principal component regression does not address the correlation with the regression. The method presented in the next section does.

## 8.9 Partial Least Squares

*Partial Least Squares (PLS)* aims to maximize the correlation between regressors and target. A new set of regressors are generated iteratively as linear combinations of the original, in the first iteration

$z_1\mathbf{d}_1 + \dots + z_M = \mathbf{Dz}.$

Without loss of generality, we can ensure $\|\mathbf{Dz}\| = 1$, since regressors are invariant to scaling. Recall that **t** is the target. The matrix $\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{Dt}$ is a symmetric, positive semi-definite $M \times M$ matrix, since

$\|\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{Dt}\| = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{t}\|^2} \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{Dt} \geq 0.$

It has M nonnegative eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_M$, where the eigenvectors are ordered with regards to eigenvalues from largest to smallest. We set $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^M$ and thus can be expressed as a linear combination of eigenvectors: $\mathbf{z} = z_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + z_M\mathbf{v}_M$.

This is for $z_2 = \dots = z_M = 0$, since the length of $\mathbf{z}$ is fixed by $\|\mathbf{Dz}\| = 1$ and $\lambda_1$ is the largest eigenvalue. We choose the normalized mapping of the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue as the new regressor $\mathbf{w}_1 = \mathbf{Dv}/\|\mathbf{Dv}\|$. We know that $\mathbf{Dw}_1 \neq 0$, otherwise $\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{Dt}$ would only have eigenvalues. Having weighted, we calculate

$$\mathbf{D}_1 = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{w}_1\mathbf{w}_1^T)$$

Note

$$\mathbf{D}_1\mathbf{v}_1 = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{w}_1\mathbf{w}_1^T)\mathbf{Dv}_1 = \mathbf{w}_1(1 - \|\mathbf{w}_1\|^2)\mathbf{w}_1 = 0.$$

This implies that the rank of $\mathbf{D}_1$ is less than the rank of $\mathbf{D}$, because a vector $(\mathbf{v}_1)$ which previously was not mapped to zero, is now mapped to zero. Now let $\|\mathbf{v}_2\| = 1$ be the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of $\mathbf{D}_1^T\mathbf{D}_1$. The new regressor is $\mathbf{w}_2 = \mathbf{D}_1\mathbf{v}_2$ normalized such that $\|\mathbf{w}_2\| = 1$. Again, the correlation remains

$$\mathbf{D}_2 = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{w}_2\mathbf{w}_2^T)\mathbf{D}_1.$$

and the process continues until $\mathbf{D}_j$ is a null matrix, i.e. its rank is zero. $\mathbf{D}_j$ maps $\mathbf{v}_j$ to zero, $\mathbf{v}_j$ is the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of $\mathbf{D}_{j-1}^T\mathbf{D}_{j-1}$. Hence $\text{rank}(\mathbf{D}_{j-1}) \geq \text{rank}(\mathbf{D}_j)$.

PLS can be used for multivariate regression, that is the regressand has multiple components and measuring at $\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N$ gives rise to a matrix $\mathbf{t}$ with $N$ rows and each column represents one component. However, this is beyond this text. Haenlein & Kaplan (2004) give more insight.

## 8.10 Regularization

Recall, we are trying to find suitable coefficients $c$ minimizing $\|t - Dc\|^2$ where $t$ is the target and $D$ is the design matrix, while avoiding unnecessary complexity which leads to overfitting. Often, this can be achieved by introducing a penalty term $\Omega(c)$ and minimizing

$$\frac{1}{2}\|t - Dc\|^2 + \lambda\Omega(c),$$

$\Omega(c) \geq 0$ for all $c$ and $\Omega(0) = 0$.
The penalty $\Omega(c)$ is also known as *entropy measure*. The parameter $\lambda$ controls the trade-off between fitting the data and reducing complexity which has been chosen. Often, a model is obtained for different choices of $\lambda$, and the goodness of the model is assessed on a *validation set*. If $\lambda = 0$, we obtain the OLS solution. If the other hand $\lambda = \infty, c = 0$, since the zero vector of coefficients is the least complex. For $\lambda$ in these two extremes, we are balancing between fitting a linear model and keeping model complexity small. Note that we still explain the same model space as before, the inclusion of $\Omega(c)$ gives preference to certain areas of the model space. The parameter controls how strong this preference is.

In the following, norms different from the Euclidean norm ($L_2$) are distinguished by subscripts, which we continue to denote the Euclidean norm by $|\cdot|$. The choices for $\Omega(c)$ are numerous:

| $\Omega(c)$ | Regression method |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\|c\|_0$ | number of nonzero elements in $c$ | $L_0$ regularization |
| $\|c\|_1 = \sum |c_m|$ | $L_1$ regularization | Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) |
| $\frac{1}{2}\|c\|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\sum c_m^2$ | $L_2$ regularization Ridge regression |
| $\lambda\|c\|_1 + (1 - \lambda)\|c\|^2$ | Elastic net regularization |

$L_0$ regularization is an NP-hard problem. This means there is no known algorithm to solve this in polynomial time. The aim of $L_0$ regularization is to arrive at a *sparse* vector of coefficients, i.e. a vector where many entries are zero. That is only a few components of $c$ are nonzero. If it is known that $c$ is 1-sparse, i.e. only one component is nonzero, we have $\binom{n}{1}$ possibilities and each needs to be checked whether it is the minimum.
