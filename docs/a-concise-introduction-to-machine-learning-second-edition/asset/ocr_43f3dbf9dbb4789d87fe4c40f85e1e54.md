Next we tried other distances, compared the time for the whole length with that we had, or for two-thirds, three-fourths, or indeed for any fraction; in each experiments, repeated a full hundred times, we always found that the spheres traversed to each other as the squares of the times, and this was true for all inclinations of the plane, i.e., of the channel, which we rolled the ball. This experiment is repeated by school children over the whole world and again, stacking their books to create an inclined plane. This is supervised learning and a regression problem. In we try to find a relationship between two or more parameters. In this case it is distance, d, and time, t.

Note that the relationship between the two parameters, distance and time, is not linear. In fact,

$$rac{d_1}{d_2} = \frac{t_1^2}{t_2^2}$$

Distance and time are primary parameters and the square of the time is a secondary parameter. Then we have a linear relationship between a primary parameter, the distance, and a secondary parameter, the square of time. We will encounter this again when discussing the kernel touch. Another way of viewing this is an instance of deep learning since another layer of abstraction is added by the square. Deep learning is trying to uncover hidden relationships.

How Galileo arrive at this experiment? Remember that the starting point was the observation that objects of different masses reach the ground at the same time. He realized that air resistance is a factor, becoming dominant for extremely light objects with a lot of air resistance as feathers. Then he needed to make his experiment as independent from air resistance as possible and arrived at a bounce. More importantly, he needed to order to make accurate measurements. Galileo noticed that a ball rolling down a ramp which is shortly connected to an upward ramp will roll exactly as it started from. It will roll backwards and forwards until it comes to a rest of friction. If both ramps have the same inclination, this is not surprising, but the ball will reach the same level even if the second ramp is much steeper. This is illustrated in Figure 1.1. Making this second ramp steeper and steeper, we approach a vertical ramp. This means that the experiment of a falling ball and a rolling ball are equivalent. In the latter, the motion is however slower and thus measurements can be obtained.
