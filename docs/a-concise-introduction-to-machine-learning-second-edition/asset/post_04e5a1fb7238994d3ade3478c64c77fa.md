
Or, by defining $\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{x})^T = (d_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, d_M(\mathbf{x}))$,

$$t = \mathbf{d}(\mathbf{x})^T.$$

In the following we assume that the dictionary of functions and the design matrix are fixed.

### 8.3 Polynomial Regression

Polynomial regression dates back to Lagrange (1805) and Gauss (1809). Fitting a line to the data is a special case of polynomial regression, where the polynomial is a linear one. Here $t$ is modelled as a polynomial of degree $M-1$. The dictionary is $d_1(x) = d_2(x) = \dots, d_{M-1}(x) = x^{M-2}, d_M(x) = x^{M-1}$ forms a basis of polynomials of degree $M-1$. The design matrix is

$$ D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_1 & \dots & x_1^{M-2} & x_1^{M-1} \\ & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_N & \dots & x_N^{M-2} & x_N^{M-1} \end{pmatrix} $$

Assuming noise, the solution is unique if $N=M$. For various degrees of polynomials and numbers of measurements (which are equally spaced in $[0, 2\pi]$), Figure 8.4 shows polynomial regression for $y=\sin(x)$ and Figure 8.4a shows the regression solution is a horizontal line through zero. This is because the locations of measurements are all chosen. It is important to ask where to measure to obtain the most information. For $N=1$ the measurement point is $\pi$ in the middle of the interval where $\sin(x)=0$ while for $N=2$ the measurements are at the ends of the interval and for $N=3$ the points and the middle of the interval are used. With more measurement points and higher degrees of polynomials the reconstructions improve. It seems that a fifth degree polynomial describes the sine adequately, even though this is an infinite sum of powers and lies in an infinite model:

```math
$$ \sin(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)!} x^{2n+1} $$
```

However, this is only the case, because we are considering the interval $[0, 2\pi]$, where the convergence properties of the infinite sum are very good, because the terms in the sum small quickly due to the factor in the denominator. After three terms the approximation to the true value $\sin(x)$ is adequate. If, however, when $x=20$ for example, the powers grow much faster than the first. The $20^{th}$ term in the sum is the first with an absolute value less than one. The solution here is to use the identity $\sin(x\pm2\pi) = \sin(x)$ to shift the argument into the area of good convergence. ### 8.4 Ordinary Least Squares

Recall that we assume the dictionary of basis functions is fixed. How best to solve $t = Dc + \epsilon$? One approach is to consider the probabilistic nature of the noise. Remember, the $\epsilon_i$ are independent and identically distributed random variables with zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$. The likelihood of observing $t$ specified by $D$ and $\sigma^2$ is

$$
\mathcal{L}(t|\sigma^2) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(t-Dc)^T(t-Dc)\right).$$

The likelihood quantifies the difference between the predictions and the targets. It makes sense to consider the logarithm of the likelihood known as log likelihood:

$$
\log L(t|\sigma^2) = -\frac{N}{2}\log 2\pi\sigma^2-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(t-Dc)^T(t-Dc).$$

A suitable choice for $c$ is the one which maximizes the log likelihood. The first term is $\|c\|^2$, which is equivalent to minimizing

$$
(t-Dc)^T(t-Dc) = \|t-Dc\|^2 = \sum_{n=1}^N (t_n-d(x_n)^T c)^2,$$

since this positive is subtracted. Here $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm or $L_2$ norm and $t_n-d(x_n)^T c$ is called the $n^{th}$ residual. It is the difference between the $n^{th}$ target and the prediction. Note that $d(x_n)^T$ is the $n^{th}$ row of $D$. The sum is known as sum of squared residual (SSR), error sum of squares (ESS), or residual sum of squares (RSS). The technique is known as Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), since we use squares. Writing

$$
(t-Dc)^T(t-Dc) = D^TDc - 2c^TD^Tt + t^Tt,
$$

we can differentiate with respect to $c$ using A.2.3 and set to zero to find the extremum,

$$
D^TDc = D^Tt.$$

Since $D$ is an $N \times M$ matrix, $D^TD$ is an $M \times M$ matrix. If the inverse $(D^TD)^{-1}$ exists, then the choice for $c$ is

$$
c = (D^TD)^{-1}D^Tt.
$$

However, even if the inverse exists, the inversion might be ill-conditioned leading to large numerical errors. Numerical analysis provides many methods to Solve Equation (8.2) safely, for example see (206).

We now have a model of the data specified by $D$ and $c$. For $x_1, \dots, x_N$ the predicted values $f = (f_1, \dots, f_N)^T$ are given

$$
\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{Dc} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{t}.
$$

Geometrically, this is the projection of $\mathbf{t}$ onto the space spanned by the columns of $D$. If $\mathbf{t}$ lies in that space, then $\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{t}$. In this case, the regressors are perfectly suited to predict the data. For the minimal value of $c$ the error sum of squares takes value

$$
\sum_{n=1}^N r_n^2 = (\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{t})^T(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{t}) = \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{D}^T\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{t}^T\mathbf{t} - (\mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{c}).
$$

The inner product between $t$ and the error can be viewed as finding an approximate solution in a finite dimensional space spanned by the basis functions, which is in some sense closest to the true solution in a possibly infinite dimensional space. Projection projects the closest solution with the *Euclidean norm* $\|\cdot\|$, also known as the $L_2$ norm.
