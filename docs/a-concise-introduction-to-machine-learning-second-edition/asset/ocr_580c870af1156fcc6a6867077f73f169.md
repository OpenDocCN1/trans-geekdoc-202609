involving hidden latent variables following the standard normal multivariate distribution. The solution in the previous section effectively calculated the marginal distribution from which the $\mathbf{v}_n$ are drawn by marginalizing the latent variables. The likelihood of the dataset was then maximized with respect to $\mathbf{m}$ and $\sigma^2$. The likelihood takes a maximum with respect to $\mathbf{m}$, when $\mathbf{m} = \mu$, which is the sample mean and can be computed. The maximization with respect to $\mathbf{W}$ and $\sigma^2$, involves finding the $K$ largest eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix is in itself computationally expensive problem. Calculating the covariance matrix, the computational complexity of finding the $K$ largest eigenvalues is $\mathcal{O}(ND^2)$ (see for example [2016]).

In the following, we will use the result $m = \mu$ and we employ the Expectation-Maximization technique introduced in Section 6.4 to find $\mathbf{W}$ and $\sigma^2$, making use of the joint distribution of $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{a}$ and the distribution of $\mathbf{W}$ conditioned on $\mathbf{v}$. Note that in 6.4 the latent variables are discrete, here they are continuous. The principles remain the same.

By the product rule, the joint probability is

```
$p(v, \mathbf{u}) = p(u)p(v|u) \times (2\pi)^{-K/2} \exp(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{u}) \times (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-D/2} \exp(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}(v-\mathbf{W}u-\mu)^T(v-\mathbf{W}u-\mu))$
```

Combining the exponential terms gives exponent

```
$-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{-2}(v-\mathbf{W}\mu)^T(v-\mathbf{W}\mu)^T\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{W}^T(v-\mu)+\mathbf{u}^T(\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W\|+\sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ (7.5)
```

This can be written as

```
$-\frac{1}{2}(\begin{pmatrix} v \ \mu \end{pmatrix})^T \sigma^{-2} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{I} & -\mathbf{W} \ -\mathbf{W}^T & \mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W\|+\sigma^2\mathbf{I} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v \ \mu \end{pmatrix}$
```

Hence the mean of the joint distribution is $(\mu, 0)^T$, while the variance with scalar factor $\sigma^{-2}$ is the precision matrix, the inverse of the covariance matrix. Therefore, using the inversion formula for a block matrix in A.1.1, the covariance matrix of the joint distribution is

```
$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{W} \ \mathbf{W}^T & \mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W\|+\sigma^2\mathbf{I} \end{pmatrix}$
```

Using the formula for the calculation of the determinant of a block matrix in A.1.2, the determinant of the joint covariance matrix is $(\sigma^2)^D$ which agrees with the factor of the joint probability distribution.
