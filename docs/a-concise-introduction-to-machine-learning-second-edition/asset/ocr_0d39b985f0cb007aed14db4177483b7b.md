It also gives us flexibility in the choice of noise model and in the method to determine the coefficients.

Linear regression developed in several fields. Therefore the terminology is varied. There first is the scalar function $t$, also known as *dependent, endogenous, response, measured, criterion variable, or regressor or target*. In the previous example this is the colon:

The $d_1(x)$, $d_m(x)$ are known as *independent, exogenous, input, explanatory variables or regressors*. Remember that $d_m$ is not a coordinate of a vector. It is a basic function.

The coefficients $c_1, \dots, c_M$ are also referred to as *parameter vector or vector of weights*, also known as *regression coefficients*. They can also be viewed as *latent variables*. Regression determines these by various techniques.

The relationship to the measurements is

$t_n = f(\mathbf{x}_n) + \epsilon_n,$$

where $\epsilon_n$ is noise intrinsic to the measurement process and assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) following the normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. This is known as *homoscedastic*. Note that the assumption of the constant noise variance for each measurement might be wrong for two reasons. Firstly, there might be different sources of the noise with different effects. In this case the noise is modeled by a mixture of probability distributions. Secondly, restrictions on the data might make different noise distributions necessary. For example if it is that all data samples are always positive, the noise variance for smaller values has to be smaller to ensure positive predicted values.

Now

$t_n = f(\mathbf{x}_n) + \epsilon_n = \sum_{m=1}^M c_m d_m(\mathbf{x}_n) + \epsilon_n,$$

**D** be the matrix with entries

$D_{n,m} = d_m(\mathbf{x}_n)$

and let $\mathbf{t}^T = (t_1, \dots, t_N)$ and $\mathbf{\epsilon}^T = (\epsilon_1, \dots, \epsilon_N)$, then the relationship between measurements and model is

$\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{c} + \mathbf{\epsilon}.$

The regression problem has become a system of linear equations. Solutions of these have been studied extensively, see for example Faul (2016). However, it differs from a standard system of linear equations in that it incorporates a noise model.

The $N \times M$ matrix **D** is called the *design matrix*. The challenge is to find the dictionary of basic functions and the coefficients. Once these are found, predictions for new unseen data $\mathbf{x}$ can be made by

$\mathbf{t} = \sum_{m=1}^M c_m d_m(\mathbf{x}).$
