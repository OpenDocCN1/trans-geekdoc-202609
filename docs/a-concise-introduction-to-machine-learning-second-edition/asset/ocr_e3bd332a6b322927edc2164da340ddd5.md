The probability of the observed data (or even more extreme data) by chance under the null hypothesis is known as the $p$-value. In the case of five sigma significance, the probability of data outside this region is $1-0.9999994 = 6 	imes 10^{-7}$. There is a subtlety however, which is equally likely to be in either the high or low tail of the bell curve. The probability is halved to give a $p$-value of $3 	imes 10^{-7}$, or approximately 1 in 3.5 million. This is not the probability that the Higgs boson does not exist. It is the probability that the data would be observed, if it does not exist. Wolf (2015) describes the scientific story of the discovery of the Higgs boson.

Setting the level of significance in terms of the standard deviation sigma, is one way. Another way to set it is to an arbitrarily pre-defined threshold value $\alpha_1$, for example $\alpha_1 = 0.05, 0.01, 0.005$, or $0.001$. The chosen value depends on the field of research. High-energy physics requires a threshold of $3 	imes 10^{-7}$ to announce there is evidence of a particle, and a threshold of $3 	imes 10^{-7}$ to claim a discovery. This stringent threshold, is due to the "look elsewhere effect."
