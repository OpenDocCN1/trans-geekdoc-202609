If any two of $\alpha, \beta$ and $\gamma$ are both on the diagonal, meaning that it is impossible to leave that state. This state is called *absorbing*. If there is a nonzero probability of every state to reach that state, then the Markov chain is an *absorbing Markov chain*. This is not the case here, since the other two states change between each other but not to the absorbing state. This also means that the chain is not irreducible, since the absorbing state cannot be reached from the other two states. Thus in this case, the distribution of $x_n$ not converge to the invariant distribution, and the Markov chain cannot be used to generate approximate samples.

If, on the other hand, at most one of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ is zero, the Markov chain is irreducible and can be used to generate approximate samples for the invariant distribution.

An example of a Markov chain on the state space of integers is the *dunkard's walk*, where the drunkard starts at zero and when taking a step, steps forward $x_{n+1} = x_n+1$ with probability $1/2$, or steps backwards $x_{n+1} = x_n-1$ also with probability $1/2$, independent of how he got in the previous step. The probabilities of all states greater than one are zero. This is an example of a *random walk*. The transition matrix would be infinite with zero on the diagonal and 1/2 on the sub-diagonal and super-diagonal. The state diagram is

![](img/c6c76bf52016db7e78a5e30d4766ef81_108_0.png)

The expectation $E[x_n]$ is zero, since the expectation of each individual step is that the drunkard stays put. On the other hand, $E[x_n^2] = n$, which implies that the distance traveled from the pub is of order of $\sqrt{n}$. The drunkard's partner will find them the pub. It also shows that this random walk is relatively ineffective in exploring the state space of the integers.

However, if the random walk carries on indefinitely, it will reach each infinite number of times. This property is known as the *level-crossing phenomenon, recurrence*. A gambler with finite money playing a fair game against a bank with an infinite amount of money will eventually lose, because the amount he has follows a random walk, and will be at some point cross the (zero crossing) level and the game is over.

The aim is to construct a Markov chain, for a given distribution with probability mass function or probability density function $f$ is invariant, such that the elements of the Markov chain for $n$ large enough are samples of the given distribution.

# 3.5 Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Let

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{f(\mathbf{x})}{c_f}$$

Duplicating the chain we want to sample from, where value of the normalizing constant $c_f$ is not necessarily known. We construct a Markov chain by using a proposal distribution $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$, which describes the probability of drawing $\mathbf{x}$ when $\mathbf{x}$ is given. That is, the next element in the Markov chain $\mathbf{x}_{n+1}$ is drawn from the distribution $g(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x})$ where $\mathbf{x}_n$ is the current element in the Markov chain, which are only homogeneous if $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ is independent of $\mathbf{x}$.

If, addition, the proposal distribution is symmetric, i.e. $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}) = g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ for all, a candidate $\mathbf{x}^*$ is drawn from the proposal distribution and accepted with probability

$$\min(1, \frac{f(\mathbf{x}^*)}{f(\mathbf{x})}) = \min(1, \frac{f(\mathbf{x})}{f(\mathbf{x}^*)})$$

Note if $f(\mathbf{x}^*) \geq f(\mathbf{x})$, the candidate is certain to be kept. If the candidate sample is accepted, then $\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}^*$, otherwise $\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n$. This is known as the \Metropolis algorithm.

Duplicating the chain is in contrast to rejection sampling. In practical implementations, no extra copies of sample are stored. Instead a counter of how often the sample was is updated. This counter acts as weighting, for example; the expectation is calculated. As long as $f$ is nonzero over the state space, $\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n$ and thus the Markov chain is aperiodic. The elements of the Markov chain for $n$ large enough are samples of the given distribution.

This choice for the proposal distribution is common, since techniques to generate samples from the normal distribution such as the Box-Muller transform are readily available, and these samples only have to be scaled by $\sigma^2$ and translated by $\mathbf{x}$.

    % Evaluate the target distribution for the candidate.
    new = feval(target,candidate);
    % Evaluate acceptance probability.
    acc = min(1,new);

        last = new;

        plot(candidate(1), candidate(2),'*');
