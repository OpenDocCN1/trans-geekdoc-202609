The more samples with large weights the correctly the larger $\alpha_m$ and the confidence in $k_m$. On the other hand, $k_m$ classifying a sample with incorrect weight has little effect, since a small weight indicates that $C_{m-1}$ classifies this sample correctly.

The above relies on the fact that classes are balanced. Imbalanced classes have their own challenges. For example, if there are nine times more samples of one class than the other class, a classifier which assigns the first class, will be correct 90% of the time. So far we have not considered the cost of acquiring features. The sources of cost can be quite varied. For example, when considering the computational cost, it may be the low cost associated with a simple algorithm. At the other end of the scale are computationally intensive algorithms which require large storage. There is also the difference between cheap and expensive diagnostic tools. It is expensive to check the oil level in one's car, which goes beyond, often a mechanic is required, however hourly rate is a considerable cost. Invasive procedures put much more strain on a patient than a simple medical test.

A cascade is a procedure which takes into account the cost. Each stage of a cascade of classifiers uses features with predictive power and increasing cost. For example, in medical diagnosis a general practitioner does a simple inexpensive test which can exclude a diagnosis. Should the test, however, reveal the possibility of an underlying health problem, the patient may be referred for further tests.

Let $C_0$ be the negatives and class $C_1$ be the positives. The number of samples in $C_0$ is commonly denoted $N$, while the number of samples in $C_1$ is denoted $P$.

A confusion matrix visualizes the goodness of a classifier by listing the number of samples of $C_0$ correctly classified, known as *true negatives*, TN, the number of samples of $C_0$ misclassified, which are the *false positives*, FP, on one hand, and on the other the number of samples of $C_1$ correctly classified, the *true positives*, TP, and the number of samples of $C_1$ misclassified, the *false negatives*, FN:

```
$$\begin{pmatrix} TN & FN \\ FP & TP \end{pmatrix}$$
```

This concept can be easily extended to multiple classes. The $(i,j)$ entry in the matrix contains the number of samples in class $C_j$ classified as belonging to class $C_i$. From these numbers we can calculate the *sensitivity*, also known as *recall*, *true positive rate (TPR)* and *probability of detection*, as the fraction of positive samples correctly classified.

```
TPR = \frac{TP}{P}
```

The specificity or true negative rate (TNR) is the fraction of negative samples correctly identified:

```
TNR = \frac{TN}{N}
```

The false negative rate, also known as *miss rate*, is given by:

```
miss = \frac{FN}{P}
```

The false positive rate, also known as *fall-out*, is:

```
FPR = \frac{FP}{N}
```

It is the percentage of time a negative sample is registered as positive. However, $FPR = 1 - specificity$. It is the percentage of time a positive sample is overlooked. However, $miss = 1 - TPR$.
