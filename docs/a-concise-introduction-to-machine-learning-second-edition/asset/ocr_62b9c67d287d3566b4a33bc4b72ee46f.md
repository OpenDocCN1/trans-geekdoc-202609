# 3.5 Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Let

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{f(\mathbf{x})}{c_f}$

Duplicating the chain we want to sample from, where value of the normalizing constant $c_f$ is not necessarily known. We construct a Markov chain by using a proposal distribution $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$, which describes the probability of drawing $\mathbf{x}$ when $\mathbf{x}$ is given. That is, the next element in the Markov chain $\mathbf{x}_{n+1}$ is drawn from the distribution $g(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x})$ where $\mathbf{x}_n$ is the current element in the Markov chain, which are only homogeneous if $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ is independent of $\mathbf{x}$.

If, addition, the proposal distribution is symmetric, i.e. $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}) = g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ for all, a candidate $\mathbf{x}^*$ is drawn from the proposal distribution and accepted with probability

$$\min(1, \frac{f(\mathbf{x}^*)}{f(\mathbf{x})}) = \min(1, \frac{f(\mathbf{x})}{f(\mathbf{x}^*)})$

Note if $f(\mathbf{x}^*) \geq f(\mathbf{x})$, the candidate is certain to be kept. If the candidate sample is accepted, then $\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}^*$, otherwise $\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n$. This is known as the \Metropolis algorithm.

Duplicating the chain is in contrast to rejection sampling. In practical implementations, no extra copies of sample are stored. Instead a counter of how often the sample was is updated. This counter acts as weighting, for example; the expectation is calculated. As long as $f$ is nonzero over the entire state space, there is always a nonzero probability of $\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n$ and thus the Markov chain is aperiodic. If, addition, $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ is nonzero over the entire state space, the Markov chain is obviously irreducible.

The elements of the Markov chain, $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \dots$, are not independent samples in most cases, because successive samples are highly correlated if $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ depends on $\mathbf{x}$. In fact, the chain represents a random walk through the state space. For practical purposes, taking every $m^{th}$ element from the chain approximates for $m$ sufficiently large. This is known as thinning.

Symmetry of $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ can be achieved by letting $g(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ describe the normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance which is $\sigma^2$ times the identity matrix, in this case

$$\g(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})^T(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})) = g(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{x})$

This choice for the proposal distribution is common, since techniques to generate samples from the standard normal distribution such as the Box-Muller transform are readily available, and these samples only have to be scaled by $\sigma^2$ and translated by $\mathbf{x}$.
