## Linear Classification

Distinguishing between objects is called *classification*. Already before we are born to learn to distinguish between different sounds in the womb, and we continue to learn and distinguish easily between different objects, different situations and different concepts. In fact, we differ or are similar to others is fundamental to our sense of self. Humans have an uncanny ability in the area of classification. For example, despite the variety of dog breeds and Domestic cats are visually much similar to each other. We identify a dog as a dog. A child is not what is different between a cat and a dog. It is a cat and that is a dog. At most it is, this dog meows and that goes woof. On these examples it to work by itself how to distinguish between them. What are features and what are dog features?

More formally, there are three categories of features. *Boolean* or *binary* features can only have two values. They relate to questions answered yes or no. Is it red? Is the person tall? Do we have a storm? *Discrete* features took categorical also known as *ordinal values*. What colour is it? Is the person short, medium built or tall? What is the wind force on the Beaufort scale? Lastly there are *continuous*- that is, *real-valued* features. Here the questions are answered for example: What is the wavelength of the colour? What is the person's height? What is the wind speed?

There are many examples of classification in every life. Spam filtering is an application of text categorization. Face detection in images and optical character recognition are examples from the field of machine vision. For businesses, market segmentation is of great importance, while every one of us hopes that the fraud detection algorithms of our credit card company are effective.

## 4.2 Projections onto Subspaces

Each sample has two features and we can write these as vectors,

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} v_{11} \\ v_{12} \end{pmatrix} \quad \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} v_{21} \\ v_{22} \end{pmatrix}.$$

The *length of a vector* is the square root of the sum of the squares of the coordinates.
$$|\mathbf{v}|| = \sqrt{v_{11}^2 + v_{22}^2}.$$

This is also known as Euclidean norm or $L_2$ norm. Between two vectors an *inner product*, also known as *scalar product* or *dot product* is defined:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = (\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = v_{11}v_{21} + v_{12}v_{22}.$$

More generally, this can be viewed as a *matrix product* between the *transpose* of the first vector and the second vector.

$$\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} v_{11} & v_{12} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_{21} \\ v_{22} \end{pmatrix} = v_{11}v_{21} + v_{12}v_{22}.$$

A $1 \times 2$ matrix times a $2 \times 1$ matrix is a $1 \times 1$ matrix. A common technique is to reduce a higher dimension to a lower one where it can be solved easily. More generally, many Machine Learning techniques can be viewed as transforming vector spaces, where the data lies, in spaces where information within the data can be extracted easily. In this case, we reduce the two-dimensional problem to one-dimensional one by projecting onto a line through zero in the direction of a vector $\mathbf{w}$. The formula for the *projection of a vector* $\mathbf{v}$ onto $\mathbf{w}$ is given by

$$\proj_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|^2}.$$

Figure 4.2 illustrates this. We then select a *threshold* $b$, also known as *bias* that for $\mathbf{v}$

- $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{v} < b \implies \mathbf{v} \in C_0$,
- $\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{v} > b \implies \mathbf{v} \in C_1$.

In Figure 4.2 the bias has to be 0 for $\mathbf{v}_1$ being in class $C_0$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ being in class $C_1$. The task is to find the direction $\mathbf{w}$ and separation threshold $b$, is also known as the vector of weights. The naming is because in the inner product each component of $\mathbf{v}$ is multiplied by a component of $\mathbf{w}$. Since each component of $\mathbf{v}$ is a feature, the components in $w$ signify how much each feature contributes to separating the data. One idea could be to use the line through the mean of each class segment and the midpoint between the sample means as the separation threshold. The sample mean of class $C_i$ is

$$\mu_i = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{v \in C_i} v, \quad i = 0, 1. \quad (4.1)$$

The line through the mean is then parametrized by

$l(a) = \mu_0 + a(\mu_1 - \mu_0)$.

For $a \in [0, 1]$, we define the line segment between the sample means and the midpoint is given when $a = 1/2$, hence $b = 1/2(\mu_0 + \mu_1)$.

However, it fails to take into account the *variance* within each class, which is how and in what way the samples differ from the mean. In Figure 4.5, we have also drawn the 95% *confidence ellipse* and its *axes*. This means that 95% of the samples lie within the ellipses. The shorter axis is the direction of most variation among the samples. The long axis of the confidence ellipse of the iris setosa is nearly parallel to the separation line, meaning that the data will be bunched together when projected onto the line through the means. The axes of the confidence ellipse of the iris versicolor are roughly diagonal to the separation line, meaning the projected data is spread out.
