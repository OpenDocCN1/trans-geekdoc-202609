The kernel does not need to be defined via a mapping $\phi$ and an inner product in a higher dimensional space. Section 5.2 gives several examples of kernel functions. To summarise, let $k(x,y)$ be the kernel. The kernel matrix $\mathbf{K}$ has $(i,j)$ entry equal to $k(v_i, v_j)$. Kernel Principal Component Analysis calculates the $K$ largest eigenvalues and eigenvectors $\mathbf{w}_k$ of $(I - \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{E})\mathbf{K}(I - \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{E})$, where $\mathbf{w}_k$ has length $\sqrt{N\lambda_k}$. In the principal subspace the $r$-th coordinate of the transformed sample $\mathbf{v}_j$ is given by

$$\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{w}_l = k(v_N, v_j)$$ 

Figure 7.8 shows the results of kernel Principal Component Analysis applied to the data in Figure 7.7a for various choices of kernels. Visually the results are quite different, but what they have in common is that the two datasets are grouped towards the left or right, respectively. This means they can be separated along one principal component.
