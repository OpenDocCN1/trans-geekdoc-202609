# PREFACE

Evolutionary science advances our thinking about life’s long and astonishing history on Earth. Adding more observations and facts improves our understanding, but doesn’t necessarily reveal the patterns or trends that connect them. For that, we use narrative. Seeing a diving beetle grasping an air bubble under water doesn’t tell us much more, until we add a narrative about how the beetle breathes. Narratives place observations and facts into a broader and more meaningful context. They help us map, very roughly, the ways of the natural world.

Describing a primary narrative and pattern of change for the proliferation of life-forms on Earth is difficult. At 3.8 billion years old, life’s time frame transcends our human experience. It’s hard for us to relate. Just try to imagine life’s 3.8-billion-year time span. Go ahead and see what happens. I suspect you will draw a blank. Humans live a scant 73 years on average, and our impatience is triggered by a few slow minutes in traffic. So, let’s try an analogy. If the 73 years of an average human life were made equal to the 3.8-billion-year estimate for life’s existence, a nine-month pregnancy would last 39 million years. And ten seconds of sexual climax would last for a full 16 years. That’s a long ten seconds.

Evolutionary “events” can also be unexpectedly long. The birth of new species of organisms can require a million years or more, and some mass extinction events span 20 million years. In this light, it is not surprising that the influencing factors for the evolution of organismal species seem inscrutable. Yes, there are many evolutionary events that do operate on human time scales, such as the evolution of antibiotic resistance in bacteria and of viral escape from human immune systems. And those rapid changes do allow us to study and to extrapolate such change over longer time frames. However, we are handicapped in grasping the exotic pace and the near-infinite, unseen influences in the seemingly endless history of life’s change and growth over time. Even though that history and narrative includes our own. Our human evolution never pushed us to conceive time over thousands, much less billions, of years. We are a bit like ants gazing at the full moon. Yet, our wondering about and exploration of evolution’s lengthy narrative is irresistible, and we have more facts and observations to consider than ever before.

Why is this story about evolution’s narrative one that I want to tell? First, as a naturalist and biologist, I find evolution profoundly inspiring and feel compelled to share the experience and ideas. Evolution has brought living order from the primordial elements and chemicals of our planet. It has created and shaped all of life’s diverse forms over time, massively multitasking to create and hone everything from cells and genes to the human capacity for love, treating disease, and coding for artificial intelligence. And this living order persists, in one form or another, despite cataclysmic changes in Earth’s environments. For me, evolution has always been *the* most compelling story. It is our own thriller. We are embedded in it, as it is embedded in us. New knowledge regarding the plot should be brought to light so we may better understand ourselves and our world. Second, I have long been bugged by the disconnect between the existing, common narrative for evolution and more-recent findings regarding horizontal evolution (you can find simple definitions for terms in [table 1.1](#08_ch1.xhtml#tab1-1)). This has been especially acute for someone like myself who studies phylogeny—that is, genealogy—among life-forms. The overall pattern of life’s genealogy is much more complicated, and dazzling, than we let on. The gaping disconnect between the existing narrative and new knowledge needs consideration and repair.

Having life’s story better aligned with the facts of evolution is justification enough, as truth is its own reward. However, there is more than accuracy about life’s history at stake. Narratives and their metaphors provide mental coordinates for mapping observations and ideas. And by reorienting our thinking, new narratives, especially scientific narratives, can open minds, challenge assumptions, and reveal new approaches to problem-solving. In this light, I think evolution’s new narrative has key relevance for our understanding of the broad topics of evolutionary innovation and of health, including both human and environmental health.

There are, of course, many aspects of evolution that impact the overarching narrative in addition to the patterns of relatedness and horizontal evolution that I emphasize, and covering all of them is beyond the scope of this book. For example, our understanding of non-genetic inheritance, involving environmental, behavioral, and cultural influences on evolution, has also grown dramatically in recent years, and is discussed only briefly here. I refer readers to excellent and comprehensive reviews of non-genetic inheritance and related topics by others.^([1](#17_notes.xhtml#fm5-1))
