
There may come a time, futurist and computer scientist Hans Moravec supposes, when individual humans will have immortal avatars.^([16](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-16)) A time when your brain’s 86 billion neurons can be precisely recreated, one by one, as transistors, and appropriately connected to others, in the head of a robot. And as each neuron is recreated, the corresponding neuron of your own is removed. Your brain remains connected to the robot’s, and consciousness is maintained throughout the transfer process. At the end, with your consciousness held by the machine and you feeling everything the robot experiences, you can look back through the robot’s eyes at your former body. The robot, of course, has access to the latest AI in addition to the transistorized brain powering your consciousness.

This begs the question, is the avatar actually you? Keep in mind that the avatar will have your exact memories, habits, and personality. So, answers will vary depending on how you are defined.

## Our Coevolution

Our technology touches every aspect and every moment of our lives. And, yet, it has a kind of invisibility. We grow accustomed to the earthly miracles. A comedian describes people complaining about a delayed airplane drink service while “sitting in a chair—in the sky!” “Flying like a bird!”^([17](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-17)) We see most clearly the newest technologies. We push their limits, and show them to friends. Then we wait impatiently for the next versions, with a growing sense of deprivation. We forget the agricultural revolution behind the little bag of pretzels.

The reality is that our technology has a been primary influence on human cultural evolution for millennia. And the pace is quickening. We shape our technology and our technology shapes our culture and our minds. Technologies can be shared, horizontally, and applied by distant relatives or populations, without the need for physical transfer of any genes. This technology transfer among human populations and individuals, who can then change and adapt the technology, is a form of coevolution and, hence, a form of horizontal evolution.

I discussed coevolution among species earlier, in which different species influence genetic change in each other, as between predators and prey. In the coevolution between technology and humans the mutual influence occurs not between different species but between our species and our technology. Visible or not, technology pervades our culture and is essential to our varied ways of life. Though the mechanisms and time scales differ, both humans and technology evolve over time. We inhabit a rich human cultural environment in addition to the natural environment that envelops all life.

Cultural, technological change can be favored by natural selection if it improves reproductive success of individual humans. It can also be favored when it is either preferred by individuals or amplified through chance events. Our capacity for developing and adapting culture and technology has been a key feature of our evolutionary success as a species. In many ways, culture is our operating system. Evolutionary biologist Kevin Laland notes: “The human cultural capability did not evolve in isolation but in intricate coevolution with central aspects of cognition and behavior, including our language, teaching, intelligence, perspective-taking, powers of computation, cooperative tendencies, tool use, memory and control of the environment.” Biologists have described a positive feedback loop called “cultural drive,” in which greater cognitive ability, especially social learning and transmission of adaptive behaviors, can accelerate biological evolution. In simple form, this feedback loop can be described as: improved cognition → more behavioral innovation → richer culture → more skills to be learned → new cognitive challenges → further improvements in cognition and innovation.^([18](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-18))

Accelerated biological evolution is reflected in the increasing brain volume (both absolute and relative to body weight) in our lineage over the past 3.2 million years. Brain volume expanded approximately threefold from 450 cm³ in Lucy, the fossil *Australopithecus afarensis*, to roughly 1,400 cm³ for the past 15,000 years in humans.^([19](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-19)) A variety of other physical changes in humans have also been linked to cultural change. An evolved tolerance to cow”s milk in adults started about 10,000 years ago on the heels of livestock domestication. Loss of dark skin pigments enhanced production of vitamin D from sunshine, following movements from Africa to darker and colder northern climates where individuals had less sun exposure. More recently, average height across populations has increased by about 10 centimeters and life-spans by 20 years, in association with better nutrition and hygiene. Adaptations have enabled more efficient oxygen metabolism in small human populations settling at high altitudes where oxygen is less abundant, in Tibet, Ethiopia, and the Andes Mountains.

We are poised to give genes a much larger role in our future evolution, depending on our choices and success in pursuing technologies. Thousands of gene therapy trials are already underway. Though most aim to improve health, the methods have potential to enhance other traits, such as size, strength, and intelligence. Our technology for traveling and living beyond Earth also has potential to impact our biological evolution. Extraterrestrial environments with reduced gravity and increased radiation would impose their own selection pressures, and discussion has begun regarding possible human enhancement for future space missions by gene editing and implants.^([20](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-20))

Human reproductive rates and success are relatively less dependent on genes than in the past, and more attuned to local cultural trends and economics. Despite some linkage as noted above, technological change is happening much faster than physical human evolution, which requires many generations. Consider a few technology changes that have developed while humans have aged but have not evolved physically. Karl Benz patented the first car in 1886, humans landed on the Moon in 1969, and space tourism by private companies is well underway in 2023. Global farm productivity for wheat, rice, and maize increased by 214, 150, and 205 percent respectively between 1960 and 2018,^([21](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-21)) and this was at least partly owing to improvements in seed genetics such as greater resistance to insect pests, livestock genetics, equipment, and weather prediction. Smartphones appeared in 1992, and by 2023, 84 percent of the global population are estimated to be users. Facebook launched in 2004, and now about 40 percent of humanity uses social media at least occasionally. Rapid technological changes appear decoupled from physical evolution. At least for now.

Although the long-term biological impact of exponential growth in technology is unknown, our symbiotic relationship with technology is irreversible. Human intellect and subsequent technology, despite their shortcomings, are integral to both our evolutionary past and future. We rightly seek better technologies to minimize their environmental damage and resource use, but few if any prefer a world without medicines or electricity.
