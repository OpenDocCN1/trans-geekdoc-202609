
# Life’s Origins and Early Evolution

Life arose about 3.8 billion years ago following hundreds of millions of years of chemical interactions in which carbon compounds and water were key players. The natural properties of chemicals, together with the constraints of physics, led to self-replicating chemical compounds and the emergence of what would become cell membranes made with lipids, which, importantly, do not dissolve in water. These features enabled the natural evolution of single-celled life ancestral to all species. Although many details and questions remain to be resolved, numerous steps in this process of life’s origins have been observed in both nature and lab experiments.^[13](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-13)

Microbiologist Carl Woese first developed the scenario in which this earliest time in life’s evolution, prior to the divergences of archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes, was dominated by horizontal gene transfer. He suggested that the ancestor for those primary lineages “was communal, a loosely knit, diverse conglomeration of primitive cells that evolved as a unit.… [A]ny lineages evolving barriers to acquisition or expression of foreign genes would be left behind in the evolutionary progression toward modern life. Lateral gene transfer of this kind … would completely dominate the primitive evolutionary dynamic.”^[14](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-14) He coined the term “Darwinian threshold” for the time when life transitioned from primarily horizontal evolution, with widespread sharing of genes, to greater vertical evolution with more or most genes moving between single-celled parents and their offspring as cell design became more complex and less flexible.^[15](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-15) Although this idea is plausible, there is no evidence to support it, and this is as might be expected. No species with enough permanence to leave a genealogical trace could exist on the early side of any such threshold owing to the ephemeral nature of the “conglomeration.” We are left with a reasonable yet untestable supposition, at least for now. The important roles of horizontal evolution and decentralization in life’s origins, along with the involvement of viruses, will be considered in more detail in [chapter 4](#11_ch4.xhtml).

## Bacteria and Archaea

Life’s earliest divergences gave rise to our two largest groups of single-celled organisms, bacteria and archaea, between 3.5 and 4 billion years ago.^[16](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-16) These two groups combined represent an estimated 78 percent of Earth’s species today (excluding viruses; see [table 1.3](#08_ch1.xhtml#tab1-3)). They are ubiquitous across Earth’s land and waters, including some found in extreme environments such as hot springs, hydrothermal vents, the Earth’s crust (more than a mile below the ocean floor), and extremes of salinity, desiccation, cold, and radioactivity. They also compose the microbiome residing within individual humans and all other eukaryotes. There are even some bacteria living it up within other bacteria.^[17](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-17) No doubt about it, they have been wildly successful in diversifying and adapting to Earth’s many opportunities.

Bacteria and archaea are asexual. There are no reproductive cells, like eggs or sperm, and no individuals are deemed to be males or females. But does that mean microbes are barred somehow from the benefits of sex? Must they abstain from the genetic diversity brought when males and females contribute their differences to offspring? Is diversity-limiting clonal reproduction their only option? Of course not. Genetic recombination happens across all life-forms but is particularly frequent in bacteria and archaea (and viruses), and is analogous to sexual reproduction, and potentially hybridization, among eukaryotes. We just don’t call it sex. There are three basic mechanisms for bacterial and archaeal recombination, but their impacts in genetic mixing are similar. They are (1) transformation, importing DNA from the environment; (2) transduction, transferring DNA among bacteria by viruses; and (3) conjugation, transferring DNA by cell-to-cell contact. All forms of recombination increase genetic diversity within those groups.

Recombination by bacteria and archaea is estimated to be nearly as old as the groups themselves, based on estimated ages of relevant genes.^[18](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-18) This places extensive horizontal evolution in the early stages of life’s evolution. The potential for early microbial life-forms to share, recombine, miscopy, and share again their genetic materials, including suites of genes honed to accomplish particular functions, makes for a powerful engine of innovation. This can result in new species with new capabilities. New genetic material and new combinations of genes may serve organisms well when they find themselves in altered environments, or when new competitors and pathogens threaten their populations. Recombination is also commonplace among close relatives within bacteria and archaea.

Like recombination, horizontal gene transfer has occurred within bacteria and within archaea throughout their evolution. The bacterial species *Mesotoga infera* provides a good example of the extensive horizontal gene transfer and decentralization of genetic materials that can be identified in living individuals. Identification of horizontal gene transfer is done by comparing genomes from *M. infera* with representatives of all the major groups of bacteria. Individuals donating gene copies to *M. infera* via horizontal gene transfer, over time, span the full diversity of bacteria, as described by the arrows in [figure 2.2](#09_ch2.xhtml#fig2-2).^[19](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-19) Let that sink in. A rough analogy would be if a human genome were found to have unique genes from diverse eukaryotes, including a selection animals, plants, and fungi.

Those diverse gene origins within *Mesotoga infera* denote decentralization of its genome over time within bacteria. But what is the average extent of genome decentralization across bacterial species? Another recent study estimates that 30 to 50 percent of bacterial genomes on average stem from horizontal gene transfer events.^[20](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-20) The horizontally transferred genes and the potential innovations they represent are functionally important in the daily lives of their bacterial recipients. They encode genes used in metabolizing food; copying and translating DNA into proteins; and the defense, signaling, and movement of bacterial cells. The transferred genes, and the innovations they entail, matter. Estimates of the amounts of horizontal gene transfer represented within genomes will change as tools improve and more genomes are sequenced. But the existence of meaningful decentralization of genetic materials and the resulting integration among diverse groups of bacteria is clear.
