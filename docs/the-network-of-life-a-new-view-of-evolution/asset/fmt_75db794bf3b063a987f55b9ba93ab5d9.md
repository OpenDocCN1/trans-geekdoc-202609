
In the eighteenth century, botanist and physician Carl Linnaeus was also interested in explaining contagious disease. He offered some rudimentary experiments and speculations about this in the course of working with students, in a volume titled *Amoenitates academicae* (Academic pleasures). Perhaps most relevant is his student John Nyander’s dissertation, *Exanthemata viva* (roughly translated as “Living skin eruptions”) explicitly proposing microscopic animals as contagious agents for a variety of diseases including measles, smallpox, plague, and syphilis. Still, direct physical evidence of agents was lacking.

Definitive evidence proving germ theory was finally provided by biologists in the late nineteenth century as a result of refinements in microscopy, including the ability to stain and visualize bacteria, and to isolate and grow bacteria from infected individuals. Robert Koch and colleagues were instrumental, demonstrating anthrax to be caused by *Bacillus* infection, and the linking of six different infectious diseases to six different bacteria. Broad acceptance of germ theory quickly followed its successful application in minimizing the presence of pathogens in people’s daily lives. Within a few decades, rates of infant mortality were down and average life-spans were moving up.

These successes helped inculcate a basic lesson. Microbes are bad. This is continually reinforced by movies, novels, and news stories about deadly viral outbreaks and bacterial superbugs. And of course infectious diseases continue to wreak havoc. But, as we’ll see, this view needs revision, because microbes are not all bad.

We rely on bacteria living inside us for help in food digestion, nutrient uptake, production of vitamins, metabolism of drugs, regulation of the immune system and inflammation, and protection against infection by other microbes. These processes are all complex systems, influenced by coevolution among humans and various microbes. Think of them as ecosystems for bodily function, and our understanding of them is far from complete. Given their importance in the development of antibodies and the long-term evolution of our immune systems, microbes are, as Homer Simpson said of beer, both the cause and cure for many of our problems.

While a classic one-to-one correspondence between microbial species and disease remains true in some instances, in others disease stems from changes in the relative abundance of and interactions among multiple microbe species and their hosts. The concept of a bodily ecosystem pertains here too, as interactions among microbiome constituents, genetics of the host, and the environment are all involved.

### Holobionts and Health

All people, like all animals, are accompanied throughout their lives by a swarm of microbes. Not just now, but throughout their species’ evolutionary history. These are long-term evolutionary relationships. Microbes have colonized and adapted for living in the many spaces of human bodies, as birds might colonize and adapt to different environments on a continent. They are at home in our guts, urinary tracts, lungs, and mouths, and across our skin surfaces from scalp to soles. The collective of all microbes living in and on a particular host is known as the microbiome, and it includes bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses. A human microbiome collective has about 200 times as many different genes as found in the genome of the host, and it weighs roughly five pounds. We have only recently become aware of the great diversity of species within microbiomes, their changing presence and abundance over time, and their impacts on human health. Our knowledge is rudimentary, but clear evidence is building for correlations and some causation between aspects of human health and microbiome activity and change.

As noted in [chapter 4](#11_ch4.xhtml), the assemblage of a host and its microbiome is known as a holobiont. For a human holobiont, this subsumes the human and all its microbes into a single, inclusive entity that can be studied in its own right ([figure 5.1](#13_ch5.xhtml#fig5-1)). Recognition of holobionts is recognition of the integration of hosts and their microbes, including their potential coevolution. The individual organisms are still a key focus for research, but they can be considered as part of a larger whole, the holobiont. In the context of health, recognizing and studying holobionts is an important step in expanding our knowledge of microbiome species’ impacts on the host’s health.

There is a fascinating debate among evolutionary biologists regarding the extent to which holobionts are acted on by natural selection as units, distinct from the individual organisms involved.^([3](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter5-3)) Given growing recognition that natural selection operates at multiple levels of organization (genes, individual organisms, and groups of organisms), I think the holobiont concept does reflect and enhance our understanding of evolution. Identifying units of selection has always been challenging, as the neat definitions of units become fuzzy when closely examined. Genes, often viewed as targets of natural selection, are hard to define and isolate as potential targets. Some encode proteins, some don’t. Some are contiguous strings of DNA, others are discontinuous though working in concert. Relatively few control single traits or functions (once part of the definition of genes) without assistance. Individual organisms are similarly difficult to define, though this may seem counterintuitive at first. We can recognize a lone wolf but have trouble identifying a single aspen tree, which grows clonally and can have hundreds of trunks. Considering holobionts as targets of evolution may be even more difficult, including a changeable collection of individual microbes of many different species and their genes, all of which are potential subjects of natural selection.

Researchers Ford Doolittle and Andrew Inkpen offer an alternative approach. They suggest that the “collective functions of microbial communities,” rather than the microbial species themselves (individually or collectively), may be the units of selection for holobionts, promoting their persistence over time. They call this theory “It’s the song not the singer,” or ITSNTS.^([4](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter5-4)) That is, the metabolic activities of microbes, key to their host’s health and survival, may be the target for natural selection. Regardless of how this plays out, the associations among organisms and species within a holobiont are important to the health of the host, and for our purposes the concept is apt.
