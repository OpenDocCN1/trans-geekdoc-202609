
# Vaccination and Innovation

Vaccination is a changing set of technological innovations that has saved millions of lives over the course of 450 years. A report from China in the 1570s describes people having trace amounts of human smallpox scabs put in their noses as a protection against the disease. Similar methods were applied in Turkey and India in the early and middle eighteenth century, with variation in their methods of inoculate preparation, storage, and delivery. Vaccination against smallpox using cowpox scabs became widespread after Edward Jenner’s experiments and publication of 1798. Indeed, the English word “vaccination” derives from the Latin *vaccinus*, from cows. This crazy-sounding innovation—infect yourself to protect yourself—was certainly discovered and altered intentionally and by accident in multiple locations at different times by different people, before being refined and passed on as folk wisdom, all long before its science was understood.

The most recent vaccine innovations have built on the early use of scabs and pus containing viruses, with thousands of modern innovations allowing the molecular production and delivery of the exact gene snippets from viruses that are key for development of immunity. Relevant innovations include electricity, computer chips and software for data analyses, and all the specialized equipment and ideas applied to making specific genes from chemicals. The set of novel vaccines most widely used against COVID-19 is the result of a torrid lab race among hundreds of groups of scientists from around the world, experimenting with the best ideas, both old and new, that they can find. A widely used single-shot vaccine uses a harmless virus as a vehicle to carry an engineered gene that is copied by the recipient’s cells, stimulating antibody production. Two other vaccines deliver engineered messenger RNAs (chemical instructions) directly to cells without any vehicle, which then make virus proteins that stimulate antibody production. These approaches were preceded in the mid-twentieth century by the weakening of viruses by forcing them to grow without key nutrients, prior to using them in vaccines. In these ways and others, the ancient practice of infection for protection has been improved by serial innovations.

## Smartphones as Combinatorial Innovation

Considering an entirely different form of technology, the ubiquitous smartphone is also not a single invention. It is an innovative and dizzying combination of preexisting technologies, including telephones, cameras, clocks, calculators, maps, GPS service, pedometers, blood-pressure monitors, email, texting, social media, touch screens, apps, videos, face recognition, payment systems, and internet searches.

The innovations that have driven civilization, from vaccines to laws, language, telescopes, money, computers, and DNA sequencing are rarely, if ever, implemented from scratch, fully formed. Rather, they result from the mixing of ideas and from serendipity and chance. Ridley puts it this way: innovation “happens when ideas can meet and mate, when experiment is encouraged, when people and goods can move freely.”^([2](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-2))

As I will argue in this chapter, horizontal evolution is a decentralizing force, and it is adept at experimentation and innovation, where genes and the features they encode move across groups of organisms and meet in new combinations. I begin by considering two nonliving systems where decentralization has been explicitly sought out and implemented as part of the innovation process. This will help us see some of its advantages ([box 2.1](#09_ch2.xhtml#box2-1)). The first system is communications; the second is money. After that, I trace the role of horizontal evolution and decentralization in the evolution of life’s primary groups (Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya). Along the way, I review some major innovations in life’s evolution that have been influenced by decentralization owing to horizontal evolution. That discussion follows an evolutionary timeline, proceeding from life’s origins to the innovations in eukaryotes of harvesting oxygen and sunlight, multicellular living, and sexual reproduction. I clarify that the extent of decentralization and centralization of genes varies broadly over time and across species. They exist along a continuum.

# Communications

In 1959, 30-year-old electrical engineer Paul Baran was asked by the RAND Corporation in California to develop a communication system that could survive a nuclear attack. This was a pressing concern during the Cold War era. Conjuring the possibilities for communication networks, he identified three general forms ([figure 2.1](#09_ch2.xhtml#fig2-1)). A fully centralized version was highly vulnerable to attack, having a single hub for communications. A series of hubs, connected to one another by single lines, was decentralized to a degree, but still vulnerable. For Baran, the least vulnerable structure was essentially a mesh—called a distributed network—with enough alternative paths for communication among nodes should some of the nodes be destroyed. His insights and network protocols for distributed communication networks were essentially ignored by the military funders of RAND at the time. However, they were rediscovered independently by the US Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) in the late 1960s as they sought secure connection for computers being used for research in different locations. ARPA’s computer network (ARPANET) ultimately gave rise to what we now call the internet. Its current structure is far from an optimal distributed design; however, some of the network communication protocols first envisioned by Paul Baran were successfully implemented.

The internet innovation has now remade global communications and transactions. About 95 percent of all information existing on the planet is estimated to be digitized, and most of that is accessible on the internet.^([3](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-3)) Newspaper publishers and radio and television broadcasters had served as gatekeepers for the news, opinions, and related information, and only those with control or access could tell the stories they wanted. However, the internet allowed many to bypass the controlling powers of twentieth century. But with decidedly mixed results, as it has spread misinformation and confusion too. Consumers can choose the news they prefer, regardless of it’s veracity. Inevitably, the internet has also led to recentralization of some services, as Amazon, Facebook, and Google dominate aspects of commerce and information services.

Decentralization may resolve some problems while introducing others. And those new problems may require further decentralization. In 2020, sensitive online data were hacked in a single large-scale event and stolen from thousands of organizations around the world, including the US Treasury and Commerce Departments and the US National Telecommunications and Information Administration.^([4](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-4)) A Department of Homeland Security (DHS) spokesperson had noted earlier that “our adversaries exploit a fundamental asymmetry in our network infrastructure: while nearly all of our systems and networks are globally interconnected, our defensive capabilities are not.”^([5](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-5)) In response, the DHS is developing a network of networks to connect US federal, state, and local agencies; law enforcement; and the private companies that run a lot of the nation’s critical cyber infrastructure. Officials refer to this effort as building a cyber immunity system. Decentralized networks require decentralized defenses.
