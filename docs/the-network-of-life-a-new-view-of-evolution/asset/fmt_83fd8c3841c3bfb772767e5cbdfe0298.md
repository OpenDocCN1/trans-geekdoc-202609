
Earthworms, including *Lumbricus terrestris*, which are well known to many as night crawlers or lob worms, are similarly beneficial for soils and life. They help convert organic matter like dead leaves into rich humus through their feeding habits, make nutrients newly available to plants, and also pump air in and out of soils by their burrowing activities. Earthworms grind their food into a fine paste, which is further digested in the intestine. When this material is excreted it has been found to be five to ten times richer in the nutrients potassium, nitrogen, and phosphate compared with the levels found in surrounding surface soils. Charles Darwin had been fascinated by the “unsung” earthworm since his youth, and he rushed his last book, focused on earthworms, to have it finished “before he joined them.” He estimated there were 53,767 earthworms per acre at work on his estate in England, recycling organic matter. More recent estimates suggest fertile farmland may have up to 1.75 million per acre. Regardless of the estimates, Darwin’s supposition remains true that because of their efforts in transforming portions of Earth’s surface, “worms have played a more important part in the history of the world than most persons would at first suppose.”^([4](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter6-4))

### Archaea

Archaea are best known as single-celled denizens of extreme environments, such as hot springs and salt ponds. However, increasing numbers of nonextremophiles are being found. *Nitrososphaera viennensis* is a recently discovered archaeal species from nonextreme soil environments that is able to oxidize ammonia into nitrite. This focuses new attention on the species and the group archaea because, as noted earlier, oxidation of ammonia to nitrite has a great influence on the availability of nitrogen for plants. It is still unclear whether *N*. *viennensis* and other newly discovered soil-dwelling archaea are significant players in the global nitrogen and carbon cycles, but there is growing evidence that this is the case. We still have much to learn regarding which species are performing which functions in the maintenance of healthy soils.

Earth’s teeming soils are a rich mix of minerals, organisms, and their decomposing bits. They are also a living garden bed, where life recycles, proliferates, and sustains itself. Without organisms like the bacteria, archaea, fungi, protozoans, arthropods, and earthworms above, our vital soils would be no more than mineral dust—and we wouldn’t exist.

## Air

You’ll breathe about 12 times per minute while sitting and reading; and you’ll consume roughly 145 gallons, or the volume of 10 beer kegs, of pure oxygen during the next 24 hours. The oxygen is vital, free, and ubiquitous—as long as you stay below 12,000 feet in altitude. With a very few exceptions, all the other animals breathe it too. This feeds the common perception that oxygen has always been available and never a limiting factor for Earth’s species. But this is not so. In fact, life preceded oxygen, and oxygen is a consequence of life on Earth and not a prerequisite for it.

When life began about 3.8 billion years ago, there was essentially no oxygen in the atmosphere. Photosynthesizing bacteria and archaea arose later, about 2.4 billion years ago, but all the oxygen they produced was initially absorbed by iron and organic matter dissolved in the oceans, and not available to organisms. It was only about 850 million years ago, following saturation of various oxygen sinks, that oxygen began to accumulate in the atmosphere. Oxygen first reached current levels of 20 percent roughly 350 million years ago. By this accounting, oxygen has been present at current levels, as a minimum, for only about 9 percent of the time that life has existed on Earth.

As you would expect, increasing oxygen in Earth’s atmosphere has had major impacts on life’s evolution. It led to the extinction of many anaerobic life-forms that were not able to survive oxygen’s toxic effects. For other life-forms, increasing oxygen provided the impetus to adapt, as seen with the evolution of cellular respiration. This is the ingenious process in which cells use oxygen to make the energy they need to function. This turned out to be more than just a means for survival. Cellular respiration is nearly 20 times more efficient as a means of energy production compared with fermentation as used by anaerobic organisms. This greater efficiency in energy production (as measured in molecules of adenosine-5′\-triphosphate—ATP—output per molecule of glucose) has allowed oxygen-metabolizing organisms to grow larger, develop faster, be more active, and ultimately dominate many of Earth’s habitats.^([5](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter6-5)) A key feature of the evolution of cellular respiration is the symbiotic coevolution of eukaryotic (nucleated) cells having chloroplasts and mitochondria. As noted earlier, these intracellular organelles are descendants of formerly free-living aerobic bacteria and are capable of photosynthesis and oxygen metabolism, respectively. Their presence allows the organisms bearing them to thrive in oxygenated environments. In this way, oxygen stimulated the evolution of multicellular organisms like plants and animals, with their first appearance post-dating the early accumulation of atmospheric oxygen. Finally, oxygen availability is linked to many adaptive innovations in animals requiring its abundance, including warm-bloodedness, permeable eggshells, and live births like your own.

Increasing atmospheric oxygen has significantly altered Earth’s environment as well. It warms Earth’s surface by retaining heat (part of the greenhouse effect) and reducing temperature differences between day and night. Oxygen forms ozone in the presence of ultraviolet (UV) light or lightning, and the ozone layer, 10–50 kilometers above Earth’s surface, filters out more damaging UV sunlight than does oxygen alone. Unabsorbed UV sunlight causes sunburn in humans and can cause DNA damage in living organisms. In these ways, life has facilitated its own evolution.

Every breath you take demonstrates the coevolution of our ancient ancestors with their photosynthesizing bacterial cousins. These bacterial oxygen producers have molded our lifestyle via natural selection and helped to set, and currently maintain, the optimal atmospheric conditions for human health. The descendants of the early oxygen producers, including chloroplasts, are all around us, and yet their diversity and our dependency on them are often overlooked. Some estimate that it would take about 2,000 years to regenerate all the oxygen in the atmosphere at current rates of photosynthesis.

Let’s consider the nature and role of three extant photosynthesizing species that form part of our life-support system: a single-celled marine cyanobacterium, a freshwater alga, and a terrestrial plant. The three oxygen-producing species will be shown to be distinct from one another, yet linked to one another and to their communities through a series of symbiotic relationships.
