The knowledge being gained and applied by our species is unique in the 3.8-billion-year history of life on Earth. Though complex societies and behaviors such as cooperation in raising young have evolved in other species, including various ants and bees, mole rats, meerkats, and a few birds like scrub jays, the second and third stages (cultural and technological) distinguish us from other life-forms. The trajectory we are on is novel and its course is unpredictable.

We’re in a long-term relationship with technology. We can’t break up, even if we wanted to. Sure, we can forgo some personal conveniences. I’d happily frequent local stores more often and even read physical newspapers if online access disappeared. But our relationship with technology is about much more than convenience. Our institutions of governance, defense, commerce, health care, travel, finance, communications, and learning all depend on technology that even luddites are unlikely to quit. Don’t forget our basic reliance on technology services from utilities and governments for water distribution, electricity, home heating, cooling, refrigeration, and light. A 2021 winter storm in Texas knocked out a major power grid and caused at least 700 deaths in just 42 hours.^([4](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-4)) Our lives and even our civilization are dependent on sustained technology.

### Bionic Bodies and Longer Life-Spans

#### Bodies

The integration of humans and technology, once fantasy, is a growing reality. Edgar Allan Poe wrote a piece in 1850 titled “The Man That Was Used Up” about a fictitious general who was so injured in battle that he required assembly from inert parts. Darth Vader, Iron Man, Terminator, and Terminatrix are all fictional cyborgs (cybernetic organisms), combining humans and machines or machinelike properties. In *The Singularity Is Near*, futurist Ray Kurzweil alludes to hypothetical technology that could one day replace body parts, including software that supersedes the brain. He predicted at the turn of the twenty-first century that a “technology singularity” is approaching, a time when machine intelligence becomes more powerful than human intelligence and technology growth becomes uncontrollable. He attributed these developments to exponential increases in technologies of computer science, information processing, genetics, engineering and robotics.^([5](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-5)) Where are we now in the ongoing integration of humans and technology?

Physicians can replace damaged and painful joints. Hips, knees, ankles, shoulders, and fingers can be rebuilt with prosthetics of titanium, cobalt-chromium, zirconium, nickel, or ceramics. If all goes well, hospital time is just one to a few days (see [figure 8.1](#16_ch8.xhtml#fig8-1)). Costs for these procedures are high, and of course no surgery is without some risk. Nonetheless, their frequency is growing. Usually lasting 15 or more years, over one million hip and knee replacements are done annually in the United States, and this is predicted to expand to four million per year by 2030 as the population ages.

Organ transplants are also being performed with increasing success, especially for kidneys, livers, and hearts. Less common transplants include lungs, pancreas, and intestines. Bone marrow can be transplanted as a means to fight cancers. Blood transfusions are critical following injury and blood loss and in fighting anemia. Vision can be restored by replacing damaged corneas. Repair and replacement of teeth is routine, and in some cases requires bone grafts to strengthen the tooth socket using materials from the recipient, from another person, or even from cows.

Researchers are also developing methods for 3D printing of livers, kidneys, and hearts. These synthetic organs will use the recipient’s own cells to minimize immune system resistance following the replacement. There is promise in 3D printed organs for saving lives of those on the waiting list for receipt of the small number of organs available from donors. These methods are still in development; however, pieces of printed organs are currently in use for testing drugs. We are already using 3D printing with human cells for reconstructing ears and noses for burn and accident victims.

Brain-computer interfaces are in development. Brain implants, often attached to the brain surface through a small hole in the skull, can establish a functioning work-around for brain areas damaged after a stroke or head injury. Other implants stimulate brain regions to relieve symptoms of Parkinson’s disease and depression. There is potential to address issues of paralysis, blindness, and deafness, though these remain hypothetical. Gene therapy seeks to treat diseases by repairing or replacing the underlying genes. This is complicated, especially compared with straightforward joint replacement. One of the reasons is the difficulty of gene delivery and uptake. Another reason is that most diseases have multiple underlying genes, while many genes also have multiple effects. Gene therapy efforts began in the 1980s and have been recently enhanced by the introduction of advanced gene editing tools. So far, only a few therapies have received approval by regulatory agencies. One of these takes immune cells from a patient’s body, manipulates them in a lab to target specific cancer cells, and then returns them to the patient. Another treats an inherited form of blindness, using a virus to deliver a gene, which produces a missing enzyme in the eye, thereby improving the retina and vision. Hundreds more gene therapies are in development, targeting diseases such as blood cancers, skin cancers, and cardiovascular diseases. It seems to be just a matter of persistence until the catalog of addressable diseases grows.

These procedures, however, affect only the individual person having them. Replacement body parts, including genes introduced into body cells such as blood cells, are not passed to subsequent generations. The replacements do not directly impact our evolution. They might have an indirect effect on reproductive rates for individuals and possibly the maintenance of genes that might otherwise be selected against. But this is likely a very small effect, particularly given the low incidence of such procedures.

Gene therapy that could become heritable would target sex cells (eggs or sperm) or embryos. Though possible in theory using `CRISPR-Cas9`, and attempted as a means to block HIV infection in one controversial case, this raises questions about the ethics of tinkering directly with human design and its potential unintended consequences. Gene therapy with embryos is currently banned in many but not all countries, while international efforts among scientists and government officials seek consensus on regulations. Although reducing heritable disease is desirable, ethical issues loom regarding what heritable traits might be changed, who has access to such treatments, and whether parents have a right to genetically edit their descendants.
