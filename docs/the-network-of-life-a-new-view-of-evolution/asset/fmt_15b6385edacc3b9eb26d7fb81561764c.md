
# An Overview of Topics and Chapters

An overview of topics and chapters for the remainder of the book is as follows. [Chapters 2 through 4](#09_ch2.xhtml) compose [part 1](#07_pt1.xhtml), and will establish the framework for the new narrative of evolution. In [chapter 2](#09_ch2.xhtml), I place the new narrative about life’s diversification into the context of centralized (treelike) and decentralized (networked) systems in general and the network of life in particular. This entails synthesis of life’s genealogy and the relative roles of centralized and decentralized inheritance within the primary groups (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya). The concepts of centralization and decentralization have been considered to be important in understanding complex systems in governance, economics, financial markets, information technology, and social and cultural evolution. However, they have been little considered by evolutionary biologists, and the potential for new insights regarding their relative strengths and weaknesses in the evolution of biodiversity are considered.

[Chapter 3](#10_ch3.xhtml) explores the early history of discoveries relating to the mechanisms of horizontal evolution and decentralization that support the new evolutionary narrative. This chapter will dive into the processes of material horizontal evolution: hybridization among species, merger or joining, and horizontal gene transfer. I highlight the mismatch between the implications of the discoveries and the conventional narrative for evolution.

[Chapter 4](#11_ch4.xhtml) explores the difficulty of defining, or the fuzzy boundaries of, some entities at the heart of life’s narrative, such as individuals, species, viruses, and life itself. Recent research shows viruses in particular to have a major role in life’s horizontal evolution, and new knowledge of virus natural history is leading biologists to reconsider the integration between living and nonliving systems and the status of viruses as a part of life’s network. Current understanding of all these entities attests further to the theme of life’s deep and recurrent integration.

Having established the framework for this new narrative, [part II](#12_pt2.xhtml) explores why developing a new narrative for evolution matters. [Chapters 5](#13_ch5.xhtml) and [6](#14_ch6.xhtml) focus on the new narrative’s implications for understanding human health and environmental needs, respectively. These chapters extend the concept of species integration from genealogy to natural selection and coevolution, the heritable change that species impose on each other over time. Human and environmental health are deeply influenced by, and have coevolved with, the activities of other life-forms.

[Chapter 7](#15_ch7.xhtml) provides an outlook on the future of biodiversity. I discuss current status for a variety of species, where data are sufficient, as well as how some species are adapting to human-induced environmental change. We are witnessing losses and redistribution of biological diversity, as well as a surprising level of gains via hybridization associated with climate change. I explore the role of horizontal evolution in human hands, as applied to conservation of species, and developments in the relatively new endeavor of synthetic life, including creating new life-forms, mostly microbial, and extensively editing the genomes of others.

In [chapter 8](#16_ch8.xhtml), I explore the future of our own species in the context of technology. I examine how technology augments our bodies, enhances life-spans, and dramatically extends our brainpower. I argue that human technology has strong similarities to living systems, and I make the case that humans are coevolving with technology. This cultural coevolution is in keeping with evolution’s new narrative, as cultural evolution, driven by sharing (teaching and learning) among groups, is also horizontal evolution. Knowledge of the mechanisms and consequences of horizontal evolution will provide practical lessons, as we navigate the health and environmental challenges ahead.

## 2 Decentralization and Innovation

> Decentralization can be seen as a form of anti-fragility: a system that is not only resilient to shocks and disruptions but thrives on them.
>
> —ARVIND NARAYANAN ET AL., *BITCOIN AND CRYPTOCURRENCY TECHNOLOGIES*

As successful systems age, they risk obsolescence. Once new and daring, their ideas and implementation may become rigid and inefficient, falling out of sync with changing conditions. This can happen in systems of government, business, communication, and countless others, where the controlling factions and features fail to innovate and adapt over time. As Matt Ridley, author of *How Innovation Works*, notes, “One of the peculiar features of history is that empires are bad at innovation. Though they have wealthy and educated elites, imperial regimes tend to preside over gradual declines in inventiveness, which contribute to their eventual undoing. The Egyptian, Persian, Roman, Byzantine, Han Aztec, Inca, Hapsburg, Ming, Ottoman, Russian and British empires all bear this out.” In contrast, “Italy’s most fertile inventive period was in the Renaissance, when it was the small city states, run by merchants, that drove innovation: in Genoa, Florence, Venice, Luca, Siena and Milan. Fragmented polities proved better than united ones. Ancient Greece teaches the same lesson.”^([1](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-1))

Failing systems can be rescued, if not buried, by a more decentralized and innovative approach. Decentralization of key controls, often unwelcome by entrenched powers, can bypass the controlling powers, fostering new ideas and vigor. Johannes Gutenburg’s mid-fifteenth-century printing of the Bible in Latin is an example. He removed sole access to holy documents from clergymen and scholars of Hebrew and Greek, and provided it to lay readers. In the same vein, the printing and spread of Martin Luther’s “95-Theses” in the early sixteenth century, led to further decentralizing of the Church’s power, contributing, in turn, to the Protestant Reformation.

Democracy is another innovative and decentralizing force. Having started in ancient Greece by allowing all citizens to speak and vote in legislatures, this began an approach intending to better serve the interests of human populations globally. It can remove or weaken control by authoritarian groups. Programs in support of education, health, and business can thrive with support and funding from the public. However, democracy as a social innovation depends entirely on the rule of law, and is vulnerable to attack by those seeking power by other means.

Perhaps more often, decentralization stems from technological innovation. During the past century, innovations in health care, energy production, agriculture, commerce, and transportation have contributed to dramatic improvements in human living standards. Improvements range from reduced infant mortality, longer lives, and better health to less poverty, and greater access to clean water and comfortable housing. This is not to say the current situation is sufficient, just better than it had been.
