
# Beyond Services: AI and Large-Scale Challenges

Beyond all these services, AI might be directed to large-scale challenges facing humanity. Computer scientist Stuart Russell notes: “Our intelligence is responsible for our civilization. With access to greater intelligence we could have a greater—and perhaps far better—civilization.” He outlines the challenge of raising the living standard of everyone on Earth, by achieving a tenfold increase in the global GDP (gross domestic product), the production of goods and services per capita, with the help of AI. “The tenfold increase in GDP posited … is predicated not on further revolutionary technologies but on the ability of AI systems to employ what we already have more effectively and at greater scale.”^([13](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-13)) A small group of individuals wanting to build a hospital, school, or waste-treatment facility in their community would have access to all the best knowledge and skills of our species, to be completed with the help of AI-driven design, materials choice, budgeting, logistics, physical robots, including industrial-scale 3D printing, and permitting. The abundance of potential projects when so many are empowered by access to the requisite knowledge represents the sought-after net increase in GDP. Wildly ambitious? Yes. And why not? It’s a worthy goal and can, of course, be approached incrementally. The point is that there is a positive vision for what might be accomplished with AI.

The potential for AI to take a wrong turn, however, seems real. After all, if we create entities with intelligence more powerful than our own, how can we be sure they won’t wield that power over us? Could they develop agency somehow? AI machines will be able to read all the books and manuals ever written before lunch, and digest all the global surveillance-camera videos, phone records, social media, and television and radio broadcasts around the world by dinner. Its understanding of our world and attitudes may outstrip our own. Stuart’s solution is to change the incentives for AI systems. Instead of training AI systems to accomplish a fixed goal, they could be trained, with a technique called `inverse reinforcement learning`, to be aligned with human goals, seeking to learn what it is that humans really want and remaining open to being redirected and turned off if need be. This sounds reasonable. Ideally, we can select for this alignment in the adaptive coevolutionary process for humans and technology. But is it foolproof? So much depends on the vagaries of what humans really want. Neither AI nor any other technology is inherently evil. But humans can be, and that seems, as always, to be the greater threat.^([14](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-14))

## Lifelike Technology

Technology has fascinating similarities to living entities. As noted in [chapter 4](#11_ch4.xhtml), for many years life was considered to be characterized by a common set of properties: growth, reproduction, metabolism, heritable features, cellular composition, response to the environment, and homeostasis (the ability to maintain stable internal conditions). Technology has many of those same properties. Machine learning, a branch of AI, can grow and reproduce computer code, responding to changing data about its environment, thereby providing a next-generation algorithm, which is ready for another round of evaluation and change. Metabolism is the way our bodies turn food into energy, and technology can, with human assistance, also produce its own energy by harnessing nuclear reactions, solar and wind power, or fossil-fuel combustion.

Technology is similar, in needing assistance, to life’s many obligate parasites, which are unable to reproduce without resources provided by another species. There are obligate parasites among all the main eukaryote groups, so the phenomenon is not uncommon among life-forms. We can admit that defining life is difficult and arguably subjective because there are ample gray zones, like viruses and plasmids. But we can also admit the circularity of excluding anything from being alive because it’s not biological—in other words, because it’s not already part of life. Revisiting the issue raised in [chapter 4](#11_ch4.xhtml), some philosophers and biologists see life not as discrete physical entities but as processes. These life processes happen at different scales of time and organization, ranging from replication of genes to differentiation of cells, development of organisms, life-cycle changes, speciation, and the mechanisms of horizontal evolution.

For example, rather than seeing the living world as comprising independent species with well delineated boundaries, life would comprise interdependent species having fuzzy boundaries. This view of life as process provides a better fit when considering horizontal evolution, whose processes underlie widespread integration and interaction among species. Dupré and Nicholson note: “The world—at least insofar as living beings are concerned—is made up not of substantial particles or things, as philosophers have overwhelmingly supposed, but of … a hierarchy of processes, stabilized and actively maintained at different timescales.” They point out that the relationships and interdependence among species—think of our relationship with our microbiome—poses a major problem for understanding the natural world as composed of discrete entities rather than processes.^([15](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter8-15))

Technology, of course, is largely process as it is applied to humans. It is inert prior to application, as code not being run and implants not implanted, and the technology comes to life, metaphorically, when activated in our service, offering analyses, predictions, solutions, and more.

Imagine a single person integrating an extreme degree of technology. Suppose this imaginary person were conceived in vitro, born by cesarian section, recipient of six artificial joints, three metal heart stents, two pig heart valves, a silicon brain implant, half a 3D-printed ear, glasses, two hearing aids, and dentures. Plus, they have been saved on numerous occasions by timely antibiotics, vaccines, insulin injections, blood transfusions, antidepressants, and the interventions of a loving spouse they met online at `Match`. They were, at the moment of your imagining, listening to a Bach cello suites podcast while flying a helicopter (despite having lost their license for obvious reasons) on instruments through turbulence to a landing pad on an artificial island in the South China Sea, where the helicopter rental fees were paid in `bitcoin`.

Consider this as the modern extension of human technology going back to Ötzi’s time and earlier, when tool use, language, and agriculture began. It would seem churlish, if not impossible, to deny an obligate dependency on technology, regardless of current lifestyle. Sure, individual humans can forgo much, but not all, technology. In some cases. For a while. But for our species as a whole, our relationship with technology has become both a physical and psychological codependency. Alexa the machine app will never be alive. We reserve that designation, at least for now, for the billions of our cousins, regardless of form, who share evolution and common descent with us. Our technology, however, is increasingly lifelike, and like a true soul mate, it completes us.
