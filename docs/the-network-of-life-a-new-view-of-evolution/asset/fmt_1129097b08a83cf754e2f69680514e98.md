Horizontal evolution accelerates the transmission of DNA and all that it encodes or influences. This includes proteins, immunities, body parts, behaviors, and more. Horizontal evolution events are mutations, and, as is well known, most mutations disrupt the lives of organisms, long honed by natural selection, in ways that are not helpful. But some of these mutations may be advantageous by chance or in changed environments, allowing those mutations to be selected for and to succeed over time. Horizontal evolution also brings potential for genetic variation of greater consequence, compared with vertical, parent-to-progeny change. This includes bringing genes and functions that are more divergent, or simply absent, from those of the recipient species. The horizontal sharing of heritable materials and the traits they influence adds elements of speed and unpredictability to organismal change, beyond the confines of vertical evolution from parents to progeny.

In many ways and for many groups, the living world is more fluid than previously imagined. Ridley notes: “The secret sauce that leads to innovation is freedom. Freedom to exchange, experiment, imagine, invest, and fail.”^([10](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-10)) In the context of heritability, the mechanisms of horizontal evolution can be that freedom and secret sauce for innovation.

#### Mutation and Creativity

There is an interesting paradox here. Horizontal evolution can be a secret sauce for innovation. Yet, events of horizontal evolution begin as mutations, which are also known to be errors in copying genetic materials. It’s inauspicious, but it’s also how evolution works. Life’s nearly infinite innovations all begin as mutations. Wings, brains, immune systems, sex, multicellularity, and eusociality—as in bees with sterile workers—all trace their origins to errors in copying and transferring of DNA sequences as they pass from parents to offspring or donors to recipients. Mutations are often neutral or detrimental in their effects on organisms. There are countless ways to make already-functioning genes and organisms worse, and relatively few to make them better. However, without mutations, change by natural selection is hobbled. Life without mutation would be caught in a 3.8-billion-year loop, a bad version of the movie *Groundhog Day*. So, let’s admit, mutation is also good.

Given its link to error, mutation receives much less attention as a creative force in evolution than natural selection. Darwin’s and Wallace’s seminal works in the nineteenth century were preoccupied with making the case for the powers of natural selection, shaping nature’s glory from an ocean of mostly minor variants. But they were unaware of molecular biology or then-invisible genomes and their potential for quick and consequential change. The study of bacteria was only beginning, and an interoperability of heritable materials across very different species could not be foreseen.

One of the oldest continuing controversies in evolutionary biology is whether evolution is primarily driven by selection or mutation. Personally, I think the controversy is more imagined than real. Nearly everyone agrees that both are important and mutually dependent in the flow of evolution. However, selection has often been seen as primary when the mutations being discussed are small-scale mutations, often changes of single nucleotide positions in a string of DNA. But as we’ve learned more about the full range of mutations that happen, and the range of biases in mutation that organisms show, the idea of mutation as creative force in evolution has been expanding. We now know of myriad kinds of DNA duplications, from single nucleotides to parts of genes to whole genomes. There are DNA insertions and losses, as well as mutations in chromosome structure to contend with, along with all the mutations arising from the material horizontal evolution mechanisms (hybridization, microbial recombination, endosymbioses, and horizontal gene transfer) that are the focus of this book. Mutation as mere error is too narrow. Mutations are also unwitting experiments in which DNA sequence alterations might play out as sustaining innovation and adaptivity.

Masatoshi Nei, a pioneer in evolutionary genetics, argued that most functional genes are constrained—they can’t change or they lose their function—and that rare constraint-breaking mutations are the actual drivers of evolution, more so than selection. Further, he suggests the existence of suitable habitats without species, poorly adapted species, and species extinctions argues against the power of natural selection and small mutations (single nucleotide changes within DNA sequences) to accomplish what evolution has wrought.^([11](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-11))

Horizontal evolution, when it persists, tends to be more disruptive than change at single DNA sequence positions. Processes of horizontal evolution operate on a different and larger scale. Hybridization can mix the full set of genes from different species. Joining can add an entire genome, yielding a composite taxon, and horizontal gene transfer can bring full genes and sets of genes that are new to a species. This results in not only the presence and potential of new genes but new gene combinations as well. Species that are only distantly related can share genetic material, and become integrated in this way.

Sets of genes may be gained by horizontal evolution that had been shaped by natural selection in their previous host to work together. This also means innovation can happen faster. Gaining photosynthesis is a bigger and faster change compared with a single nucleotide substitution. Although these features may reduce functional stability of the organisms receiving genetic materials horizontally, they may also provide, by chance, variation that is useful and adaptive, perhaps when the environment is variable. That variability may include unusual weather or new competitors, predators, parasites, and pathogens. Changing knowledge of the scope and pace of mutation to include horizontal evolution events lends it a greater and more creative role in evolution than once thought. Imagine that some of your day-to-day mistakes yield brilliant, unexpected consequences. You go to the wrong airport terminal, miss your flight, and then meet the love of your life while cursing and killing time.

### Four Billion Years of Horizontal Evolution

To appreciate the scope and significance of horizontal evolution across all life’s history, let’s consider horizontal evolution events, following an evolutionary timeline. This includes overviews of horizontal evolution in life’s origins and across the two primary groups of organisms, bacteria and archaea, and their combined offshoot, eukaryotes. I’ll call attention to horizontal evolution’s influence on several innovations deemed to be major transitions in the history of life.^([12](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-12)) These are the transition to multicellularity, use of oxygen and sunlight, and sexual reproduction. My purpose here is to exemplify the support for evolution’s new narrative.
