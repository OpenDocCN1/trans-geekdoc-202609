
# For a fascinating example, consider one of the best known holobiont systems, the Hawaiian bobtail squid and its microbial partners ([figure 4.3](#11_ch4.xhtml#fig4-3))

This is a nocturnal, walnut-sized squid that preys on shrimp. The Hawaiian bobtail squid themselves are vulnerable to predators such as eels, seals, and fish, and they have an elaborate adaptation known as a light organ, embedded in their ink sac, that helps them avoid detection. The light organ is packed with bacteria belonging to a single species, *Vibrio fischeri*. Collectively, they emit light emulating diffuse star and moonlight when conditions are right. That light helps cancel the squid’s shadow and hide it from predators. But there is even more to this holobiont and integration.

Female Hawaiian bobtail squid have another set of symbioses, with partners of multiple bacterial species housed in a second organ known as the accessory nidamental gland (ANG). The bacteria in the ANG have been found to provide defense against pathogens during squid embryogenesis. The complex interactions among the squid and its bacterial symbionts housed in the light organ and ANG are all necessary parts of the living process for the squid and for at least some of the bacterial species involved.^([15](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter4-15)) Other microbial species living in close association with the host but not housed in those two glands are also part of the holobiont. The Hawaiian bobtail squid holobiont is a more comprehensive and inclusive individual than the squid alone. The holobiont is also a better demonstration of evolution’s new narrative and integration among individuals. I include the discussion of holobionts here as they are also a recently emerging arena for evolution’s new narrative, like viruses and life’s beginnings, including horizontal and vertical evolution.

This discussion of holobionts and individuals as functional units is particularly relevant to the next chapter, focusing on the impact of our symbiotic microbes on our health. The degree to which holobionts matter to health is another consequence of evolution’s new narrative.

# PART II Why It Matters

## 5 Biodiversity and Human Health

> You are not a drop in the ocean. You are the entire ocean in a drop.
>
> —RUMI, *THE ESSENTIAL RUMI*, TRANSLATED BY COLEMAN BARKS

Why should anyone care about the narrative for life’s history on Earth? Why shine a brighter light on horizontal evolution’s role and the deep and ongoing integration among species? For many, having life’s story better aligned with the facts of evolution is enough: truth is its own reward. And I agree. No justification for a new narrative is required. However, there is much more than accuracy about life’s history at stake. Narratives and their metaphors provide mental coordinates for mapping observations and ideas. When the mental coordinates change, the context and alignment among ideas change. By reorienting our thinking, new narratives, especially scientific narratives, can open minds, challenge assumptions, and reveal new approaches to problem-solving. In this light, I think evolution’s new narrative has key relevance for our understanding of the broad topic of health. This includes the overlapping realms of human health and environmental health. The new narrative emphasizing integration among species can already be seen in the work of many researchers, physicians, epidemiologists, and environmental scientists, and that can be expected to increase over time. Further, making the connections between health and other species’ activities better known to the public should help where an informed public is needed for adoption of expert recommendations.

[Chapters 5](#13_ch5.xhtml) and [6](#14_ch6.xhtml) will focus on the intersection of evolution’s narrative with human health and environmental health, respectively. I make the case that human health depends upon our relationships with many other species. We have coevolved with other species, and many of them are part of a vital support system, sustaining our lives and the environmental resources we need. I call this the symbiotic theory of health ([table 5.1](#13_ch5.xhtml#tab5-1)). This is complementary to the well-accepted, well-known germ theory of disease, in which specific microbes are linked to specific diseases. Germ theory focuses on the short-term roles of other organisms and species in infectious disease, whereas the symbiotic theory of health considers both short- and long-term roles of other species in human health and well-being.

This is not to say that all our relationships with other species are beneficial, or that the nature of the relationships and particular species involved are constant. They are neither. I am saying that human health is strongly influenced by our relationships with other species as a consequence of our long horizontal coevolution with them, and by our ability to manage change in relationships, whether by mitigating harm or promoting benefits.

| Table 5.1. Comparison of two theories |
| --- |
| Germ Theory of Disease | Symbiotic Theory of Health |
| Focus on human disease | Focus on human and environmental health |
| Some human diseases stem from infection with certain microorganisms (pathogens or germs) | Human health stems from the capacity to recover from disease and injury, and to adapt to change over time; considers both infectious and human microbiome species |
| No direct focus on environmental health | Environmental health stems from the capacity of the environment to sustain humans and other life-forms over time, and is linked to the ability of humans and other species to adapt to change |
| Focus on short-term relationships between humans and other species in infectious disease; not explicitly evolutionary | Focus on both short-term and longer-term, coevolutionary, relationships of other organisms and species in human and environmental health; species relationships play out globally, from the present time back to life’s origins |
