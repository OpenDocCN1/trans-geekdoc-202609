
# Lots of people love sushi

But some are better equipped than others to digest it because of horizontal evolution. The code for a digestive enzyme has been transferred, via horizontal gene transfer, from marine bacteria to human gut bacteria in Japanese human populations. Sushi’s dark seaweed wrapping, nori, is difficult if not impossible for most people to digest, as its carbohydrates include hardened molecules not found in land plants. This story was uncovered when scientists studying enzymes in marine bacteria that are able to digest nori found one of the same enzymes in gut bacteria of Japanese people but absent from gut bacteria of North Americans.^[11](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-11) The marine bacteria were likely ingested along with the nori, enabling the horizontal gene transfer and the digestive benefits for humans that followed. But the full story of horizontal evolution and human digestion is bigger still. Most of the necessary carbohydrate-digesting enzymes in our guts are provided by bacteria. Relatively few are encoded directly in human genomes, feeding the digestive collaboration.

# Moving on

There’s nothing pure about recent human history, genetically speaking. Modern humans (*Homo sapiens*) had been hybridizing on occasion with some of our closest relatives up to the time of their disappearance from the fossil record ([figure 3.5](#10_ch3.xhtml#fig3-5)). Genetic data acquired from the remains of now-extinct organisms suggests that our lineage was interbreeding as recently as about 60,000 years ago with Neanderthals (*H. neanderthalensis*) and 30,000 years ago with Denisovans (*H. denisova*). Whether the latter two distinctive groups are better designated as subspecies of modern humans or as separate species is debatable, but the point about interbreeding and horizontal evolution among distinctive groups remains. This interbreeding has apparently provided us with some beneficial traits. Mutations providing adaptability to high altitudes have been traced to Siberian Denisovans, and mutations strengthening keratin proteins, improving skin and hair durability, come from Neanderthals. Current genomic studies attest that an earlier, conventional view of modern human races as the result of each group branching off neatly from a single ancestral population following migration out of Africa is simplistic and mistaken. Rather, modern races are an amalgamation of groups whose individuals have a long history of geographic movement and occasional interbreeding.^[12](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-12)

# Pregnancy and live birth

Pregnancy and live birth are essential human experiences. They are also essentially the result of horizontal evolution. Their evolution required some neutralizing of the mother’s immune system, which would naturally attack the embryo as an antigen, a set of foreign cells different from her own. This neutralizing of the immune system has been and is still being wrought by a mind-boggling set of relationships between placental mammals, including humans, and retroviruses. Roughly 46 percent of the three billion bases in the human genome are derived from prior infection with retroviruses. This includes the human endogenous retroviral sequences known as HERVs. We are still learning about their varied functions, but current evidence indicates their vital roles in mediating mothers’ immune system response to the fetus and in development of the placenta. Beyond the workings of internal gestation for mammals, experiments with human cells also show a role for HERVs in the growth of neurons and maintenance of their functions in the brain.^[13](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-13) Our debt to HERVs and their horizontal gene transfer may be deeper still.

# We’re hooked on oxygen

We’re hooked on oxygen. It can be fatal, but we’re able to successfully metabolize it as a result of ancient horizontal evolution. The key event, as noted earlier, was an endosymbiotic relationship, let’s call it a swallowing, of a bacterium, by an archaean about 1.8 billion years ago. The bacterium survived, reproduced, and over time its progeny accommodated to life inside an archaean. Ultimately, the bacterium gave rise to mitochondria. The swallowed bacteria had previously evolved an ability to transform oxygen and sugar into energy, in form of ATP (adenosine triphosphate). Its archaea host, lacking that ability, was a beneficiary. The two lineages, in combination with many others, gave rise to eukaryotes. This early species-joining event is not merely part of our human evolution, it is vital to our existence.

# Infectious disease

We are learning the hard way that infectious disease will always be with us. Population growth, frequent interaction with microbes, and global travel set us up for their rapid spread. Especially so when the pathogens are airborne. Our immune systems, though stunning in complexity and speed of response, can be overwhelmed. Why? Because microbial pathogens can be equally fast in their ability to evolve and elude our immune defense. The viruses causing human influenza routinely swap gene segments among themselves. This is a kind of recombination and horizontal evolution that allows them to elude the immune defense and vaccines developed against prior forms just a few months earlier. This is why influenza is a perennial problem. The SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus causing COVID-19 shows a different, less frequent kind of recombination and horizontal evolution. But these viruses’ recombination, combined with the rapid mutation of genes, means the target for immunity can shift quickly.

# Bird watchers’ connection

Finally, channel your ancient naturalist’s instincts and consider the bird watchers’ connection with horizontal evolution. Birding, done well, forces your focus. Tune out human noise. Search the skies, listen for songs beneath the wind, associate species with terrain, and feel the changing seasons. Hybridization among bird species, typically closest relatives, is hard to detect, but it is much more common than previously thought. Estimates for the percentage of bird species that hybridize have risen, based on improving data and methods, from 9.2 percent in 1992, to 16 percent in 2015.^[14](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-14) The latter is surely an underestimate as molecular analyses of hybridization, with greater discriminating power than even dedicated birders, are only beginning. Hybridizing species are known from all the major bird groups, excluding those with relatively few species members, from albatrosses to owls to hummingbirds. Hybridizing species include various Darwin’s finches from the Galapagos Islands, birds-of-paradise from Papua New Guinea and Australia, and blue-winged and golden-winged warblers in North and Central America, to name just a few.^[15](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-15) This has significance for conservation efforts, as some species may be lost by means of hybridizing. Though our current understanding of horizontal evolution is much improved, there is still more hiding in plain sight that we’ve not yet found.
