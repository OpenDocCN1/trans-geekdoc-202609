
Perhaps the most extreme form of our coevolution with technology would be editing of our sex cells (eggs and sperm), and thereby directing our own future evolution. As noted earlier, we have the capacity with `CRISPR-Cas9`, though the practice is banned in many countries. We do already direct our own evolution in a soft manner, by choosing mates whose appearances, personalities, behaviors, and health appeal to us. We also already screen embryos for genetic diseases. But supposing for the moment that editing of sex cells were pursued unchecked, we could alter the genetic heritage of humans. Many, including political scientist Francis Fukuyama, argue this is among the world’s most dangerous ideas, likely to increase inequality among people and undermine democracy. Coupled with colonizing other planets and sufficient time, it could also lead to effectively new forms or new human species, whose genetics might be tailored to life in different parts of the solar system with different atmospheres, temperatures, and gravity.

This is just science fiction. But imagine that future humans disperse and become interstellar voyagers and denizens. Doing so would be a natural extension of our incessant technological innovation as well as our exploration and dispersal activities across the face of Earth over the past 200,000 years. It then becomes conceivable that intelligent beings, descended from life on Earth (ourselves and perhaps other species), could evolve into a great diversity of forms, carrying life throughout the galaxy. In this fictional though long-term and evolutionary view, we are not now in the last chapter of humans’ or of life’s reign, but on the cusp of new opportunity.

Technology suffuses our lives—and our possible futures. It always has. Humans have never existed without technology. At this point, our lifestyles, population sizes, and distribution are influenced more strongly by technology and politics than by natural selection operating on the genetic variation within our species. Technologies for distribution of clean water and electricity have done more to promote human health and population growth in modern times than any particular genetic innovation.

Of course, our genetics remain fundamental to our existence and basic reproductive success. We embody a marvelous legacy of evolution, stretching back to life’s origin. That legacy enables our athletics, our art, and our intellect. However, the ancient legacy brings restrictions. How could it be otherwise? We have what appear, in hindsight, to be design flaws. Birth canals are narrow relative to the large heads of infants. The tube draining the urinary bladder runs through the prostate gland in males, which swells with age. Our genomes are littered with the detritus of past viral infections and DNA copying mistakes. Mutations themselves, though they empower evolutionary innovation, are undirected and mostly harmful or neutral in their effects. Our emotions, though vital, often cloud our judgement and can lead to debilitating depression.

Our technology, at its best, offers powers once reserved for science fiction. We can refurbish our bodies, extend our brains, extend life-spans, edit our genetic instructions, create novel life-forms, travel and possibly live beyond Earth. It is now possible that future change in the human genome may owe more to genetic engineering than to random mutation and natural selection. The tools are improving, though discussions and consensus on ethics and best practices are lagging. We don’t know how the environmental changes from technology might impact our future physical evolution, but technology’s considerable influence on our cultural and lifestyle evolution is clear.

Technologies driving our cultural evolution are shared and transferred horizontally among humans, explaining their rapid spread. We create, change, and disburse the technologies that in turn color and compose our cultural environment. Even as we struggle to control our technology, it enhances our power and influence among life-forms.

Knowledge and technology as our cultural inheritance provide the final example, focused on humans, of the important role of horizontal evolution in life’s evolution. The mechanisms of hybridization and recombination among species, endosymbioses, horizontal gene transfer, coevolution, and cultural inheritance all support an overarching narrative for evolution with new emphasis on the pattern of mutual influence and integration radiating across life-forms.
