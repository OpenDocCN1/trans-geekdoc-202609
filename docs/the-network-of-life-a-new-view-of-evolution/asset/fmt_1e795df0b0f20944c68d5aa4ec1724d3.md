Similarly, about 1.5 billion years ago, ancient protists feeding on photosynthesizing cyanobacteria led to another joining of lineages. This time the endosymbionts were cyanobacteria, which eventually gave rise to chloroplasts. The resulting protists now had both mitochondria and chloroplasts. In turn, these protists gave rise to three different photosynthesizing groups: (1) all land plants and green algae, (2) red algae, and (3) the glaucophytes, a small but distinctive group of freshwater algae.

In various protists, there were a series of secondary, tertiary, and higher-order endosymbioses, when a protist having a chloroplast was swallowed by another protist, sometimes also having a chloroplast, which was swallowed by yet another, and so on, in Russian doll fashion. These higher-order endosymbioses appear to have happened on two occasions involving green algae endosymbionts, and on two or more occasions involving red algae as endosymbionts. [Figure 2.5](#09_ch2.xhtml#fig2-5) provides a visual take on some of the serial mergers that have happened in eukaryotes on their way from antiquity to the present. This is an active area of research and details for these and similar events are still being evaluated.

Researchers have recently suggested an additional layering of horizontal evolution in the story of the endosymbiotic origin of chloroplasts. The evidence supporting the suggestion is extensive horizontal gene transfer from bacteria in the Chlamydiae group into the nuclear genome of the initial eukaryotic host of the incipient chloroplast. The metabolic functions of these genes may have been vital precursors in the later success of the endosymbiotic cyanobacteria. If this view holds up, one kind of horizontal evolution will have facilitated another kind of horizontal evolution (lineage merger).^([25](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-25)) Bacterial species in the group Chlamydiae have proven quite adaptable and are ubiquitous symbionts of protists. Unfortunately for us, species from this same group are responsible for chlamydia, a sexually transmitted infection.

Species-joining events enabling photosynthesis (chloroplasts) and oxygen metabolism (mitochondria) seem, in hindsight, inevitable for life on Earth. Abundant sunlight and oxygen were shining opportunities for innovation. Mutation, selection, and time naturally found them. The origin of all plants is derived from the lineage-joining events responsible for their signature feature: the chloroplasts enabling photosynthesis. All the innovations in plant form and function, from towering redwood trees to creeping vines and small flowerless mosses, rely and riff on the early innovation of making food from sunlight. Diversity for all eukaryotes is similarly linked to the endosymbiotic origins of mitochondria. The innovation and ability to convert oxygen into energy that mitochondria provide is requisite for their survival. The only known eukaryotes without mitochondria are single-celled flagellates in the genus *Monocercomonoides*, which are descended from groups having mitochondria. The ancient lineage-joining events yielding chloroplasts and mitochondria alone indicate the catalytic powers of horizontal evolution reverberating across all eukaryotes.

### MULTICELLULARITY

The evolution of multicellular organisms from single-celled organisms is one of life’s major transitions. It allowed for specialization of different functions in different cells, as well as for increases in organism size and longevity. And it happened on multiple occasions. Estimates for the number of times depends on how multicellularity is defined. If we restrict multicellularity to mean organisms with cell-to-cell communication and cooperation, then we can count 11 separate origins. Once each in slime molds, which are protists, and in animals; three times in fungi; and twice in each of the major photosynthetic groups: plants, red algae, and the Stramenopiles, the latter also being protists, including flagellates and diatoms.^([26](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-26))

The origins of multicellularity involve the evolution of many complex features, and it appears different groups of organisms traveled a variety of different paths in getting there. There is no broad agreement about which mechanisms have been most important across the multiple origins of multicellularity, and relatively little can be said about the potential role of horizontal evolution. Among many possible influencing factors, some researchers think it is likely that the same predatory behavior in protists discussed above for endosymbioses and leading to chloroplasts was an influence on the multiple origins of multicellularity. Recent lab experiments have shown that predation on groups of single-celled algae by the fearsome single-celled protist *Paramecium tetraurelia* results, repeatedly, in the coevolution of multicellular algae, with multicellular prey being larger and more difficult to swallow.^([27](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-27)) Other researchers point to horizontal coevolution between unicellular hosts and pathogens as a potential key influencing factor, as computer-based simulations of competitive host-pathogen “arms races” indicate benefits of being multicellular over unicellular.^([28](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-28))
