
# Sex is decentralizing too

Whether plant, animal, fungal, or protist, both males and females contribute half of their genes to the embryo. Those genes are then shuffled in the embryo and that creates new gene combinations for selection to act upon. Given the biological imperative for individuals to pass on their genes, and the alternative of donating all their genes by being asexual, rather than half by being sexual, it’s a wonder that sex is so popular. And it is popular, especially among vertebrate species. There are relatively few asexual species of vertebrates, and those that do appear are more extinction prone compared with sexual species. Experts explain the relative abundance of sexual vertebrate species as a result of the benefits of the greater genetic diversity they create. That genetic diversity provides more potential for adaptive change, especially in variable environments. Sexual reproduction also provides an opportunity to select against and remove harmful mutations present in the individual parental contributions. No such opportunity exists for asexuals.

Sexual recombination is not horizontal evolution in the same sense or on the same scale as hybridization, lineage joining, and horizontal gene transfer. However, it facilitates novel gene combinations among populations, and it differs strongly from the centralized asexual reproductive process in which progeny receive all their genes from a single parent. Evolution tends to favor asexual reproduction in groups of organisms having high rates of reproduction, like bacteria, where less is invested in individual progeny.

## Horizontal Gene Transfer

Horizontal gene transfer both into and among eukaryotes was thought at first to be absent, then rare, and now, as databases grow, merely uncommon. Horizontal gene transfer for eukaryotes tends to occur in its microscopic groups, where it is roughly 80 percent less common than in bacteria and archaea. Current estimates indicate that microbial eukaryotes have genomes comprising 0.04 to 6.49 percent genes that have experienced horizontal gene transfer, though these numbers are likely to change as more surveys are done.^[29] Researchers Julia Van Etten and Debashish Bhattacharya write that “horizontal gene transfer is a small but significant player in the evolution of microbial eukaryotes,” and they provide examples where horizontal gene transfer has facilitated gain of adaptive functions and, in some cases, underpinned major lifestyle transitions. This includes transitioning to life in extreme environments of hot springs and of glaciers, and in organisms moving from salt water to freshwater.^[30]

But there has also been considerable horizontal gene transfer from bacteria into nonmicrobial eukaryotes. Various insects, mollusks, sea anemones, and fungi are able to protect themselves from bacterial invaders using adopted bacterial defense genes that destroy cell walls. Some insects, slime molds, and diatoms have picked up bacterial genes allowing them to synthesize vitamins and sequester iron. Select fungi, land plants, diatoms, and algae have received and incorporated bacterial genes helping them cope with extreme environmental toxicity and stress.^[31] Horizontal gene transfer among plants has been strongly implicated in the evolution of some parasitic plants, with parasitic species gaining genes from their hosts.^[32]

Keep in mind that horizontal gene transfer is not easy to identify. It requires exacting care in both lab and computer analyses. There have been more than a few reports of horizontal gene transfer that have turned out to be false and due to contamination of samples in the lab or problems in analysis and the assembly of genomes. However, given time, science is self-correcting, errors are identified, and the extent of known horizontal gene transfer continues to grow.^[33]

The events of horizontal evolution result in multiple lines connecting single species—or genes—in phylogenetic networks. What do we know about the shape and complexity of these networks? The power law distribution for network connections describes the situation in which a few nodes (individual species or genes) serve as hubs having most of the network connections, while most nodes have relatively few. Examples of power law distributions include much of the internet, many social networks, and spreaders of infectious disease.^[34] All of these show the presence of relatively few hubs serving more traffic than most other network participants. Power law distributions show a tendency for the richly connected to become even more so.

Much remains to be learned about the direction and connectedness of phylogenetic networks for different organisms. However, as databases for whole genomes from organisms grow, experts have identified a few broad trends across life’s diversity. Phylogenetic horizontal gene transfer networks across bacterial genomes appear to be hub dominated, with power law distributions. For example, analyses of horizontal gene transfer among the genomes of bacteria living in human digestive tracts all showed a power law distribution, often called scale free, with some bacterial genes being hubs in the horizontal gene transfer network relative to other genes that transferred less often, if at all. Similarly, a study of horizontal gene transfer among a large set of virus genomes showed relatively few genes as hubs and moving most often within the horizontal gene transfer network.^[35]

## Lichens as Innovation

Lichens deserve special mention. They are an emergent innovation in and of themselves, based on the activities of multiple species. You may know them as the flat, crinkly plantlike organisms hugging rocks and fallen trees, or the low and small, coral-like figures scattered across the tundra. But “plantlike” doesn’t do them justice.

Lichens are composites. Or, if you prefer, call them meta-organisms or even an ecosystem of species partners. A single lichen houses two or more different species living symbiotically and jointly creating the body of the lichen, known as the thallus. Usually, one species is fungal (the mycobiont) the other is either algal or bacterial (the photobiont). In some lichens, there are more than one contributing photobiont and/or mycobiont species. In addition to the primary symbionts, there are other species of fungi, algae, and nonphotosynthetic bacteria, all found within lichen thalli. These latter additional species are part of the lichen microbiome, the full set of its inhabiting microbes.^[36]

The functions for the species composing this complex meta-organism, often called a holobiont, and the extent of their genetic integration are not precisely known. Many bacterial species residing within lichens are known to contribute to the nutrient supply for mycobionts, the production of hormones, and resistance to pathogens.^[37] However, many lichen symbionts retain the ability for independent living.
