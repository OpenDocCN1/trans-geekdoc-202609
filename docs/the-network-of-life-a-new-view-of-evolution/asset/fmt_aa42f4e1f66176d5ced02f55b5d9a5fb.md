
Archaea show similar trends to bacteria in the frequency of horizontal evolution. Comparisons of archaeal genes and genomes across diverse groups show that an average of 12 to 22 percent of genes are the result of past horizontal gene transfer events ([figure 2.3](#09_ch2.xhtml#fig2-3)). As with bacteria, these estimates for archaea span important categories of gene function and innovation, indicating their integral roles in the structure and maintenance of living archaeal individuals.^([21](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-21))

### Eukaryotes

Next within the sequence of divergence among life’s primary groups is the divergence within archaea that gave rise to eukaryotes about 1.5 billion years ago. Both archaea and eukaryotes have continued a degree of genetic sharing with bacteria from their first divergence up to recent times. Eukaryotes is our group, including an estimated 22 percent of Earth’s species. These are the animals, plants, fungi, protists, and their immediate ancestors.

Diversification within eukaryotes began with small organisms, now long gone, that we would consider to be protists, the name for eukaryotes that are not animals, plants, or fungi. Though still controversial, some experts posit the early diverging protists were “almost certainly” single-celled predators able to swallow individual bacteria or archaea.^([22](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-22)) This ability to ingest organic material as food is a hallmark of eukaryotes, distinguishing them from bacteria and archaea. Three different eukaryotic protists caught in the act of predation are shown in [figure 2.4](#09_ch2.xhtml#fig2-4). Microscopic observations of protist predation are relatively recent, and as the behavior of more protists is scrutinized, predation is turning out to be more common than imagined.

#### MAKING USE OF OXYGEN AND SUNLIGHT

One of the most consequential lineage joining events for eukaryotes was the endosymbiosis (one organism living inside another) giving rise to mitochondria, about 1.8 billion years ago. The lineages that joined were an archaea species ancestral to eukaryotes and a bacteria species from a species-rich group called the Alphaproteobacteria.^([23](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-23)) To set the stage, the amount of oxygen in Earth’s air had been increasing owing to the activities of photosynthesizing bacteria. Most life-forms at the time were anaerobic, and unable to cope with the increasing oxygen levels. Some ancient Alphaproteobacteria, however, evolved the ability to metabolize oxygen, breaking it down and creating a useful energy source (adenosine triphosphate, or ATP). Over time, anaerobic archaea that engulfed and joined with an incipient oxygen metabolizer would be an evolutionary winner, giving rise to eukaryotic cells containing and powered by mitochondria.^([24](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-24)) This primary endosymbiosis, yielding mitochondria, has been of fundamental importance to the evolution of nearly all eukaryotic species, helping them diversify and colonize a richly oxygenated Earth.
