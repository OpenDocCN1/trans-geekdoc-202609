There are three key points to be drawn from this information. First, the relationship between core genome and pan-genome is influenced by MGE activities, especially replicators with reduced autonomy, such as transposons and viruses ([figure 4.2](#11_ch4.xhtml#fig4-2)). These MGE activities complement evidence presented in [chapter 2](#09_ch2.xhtml) on the frequency and functional significance of horizontal gene transfer, contributing to the genetic integration among life-forms, as emphasized in evolution’s new narrative.

Second, there is a continuum for replicators in their degree of autonomy for those two measures (the ability to produce resources for replication, and their association with vehicles for movement). The continuum ranges from full autonomy for what we recognize as living organisms to no autonomy for plasmids and retroelements. Between those replicator extremes are different kinds of viruses. This continuum is consistent with the idea of life having fuzzy, inexact boundaries. This, in turn, is consistent with life as a dynamic, evolutionary process. We expect to see entities having variable degrees of autonomy as part of the processes of the origins of life from nonlife. The unlikely alternative is life’s complex processes being fully formed, or nearly so, from the beginning. There is also a network of evolutionary relationships between replicators without vehicles, such as plasmids, and viruses, based on their genes. This indicates that transitions from one type of replicator to another could have and likely did occur on multiple occasions over the long course of evolution.^([4](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter4-4)) I’ll address this further below.

Third, the existence of such replicators as nonliving genetic parasites able to move within and among individuals appears to be inevitable in evolving systems. Parasitic replicators are found in all known life-forms, and they have been found to arise repeatedly and to persist in computer simulations of life’s evolution.^([5](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter4-5)) This suggests that parasitic replicators and their capacity to move genetic materials horizontally have been an integral part of life’s evolution from the beginning, or close to it, over 3.8 billion years ago.

### What Are Viruses?

Viruses are also difficult to define, though we all experience them as infectious agents that are too small to see.^([6](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter4-6)) When you conjure in your mind an image of a spiky blob encasing some genes, what you have come up with is a virion. That is just one stage in the larger life cycle of a virus. Collectively, virus species present an enormous diversity of systems and lifestyles. A general virus life cycle includes stages for attachment to cells, entry to cells, removal of the virion’s protein coat, potential insertion into the host’s genome, replication and expression of genes, assembly of progeny, and release and dispersal of progeny by air or water or by lying dormant on surfaces to be taken up inadvertently by new hosts. Within hosts, viruses may seek particular tissue types whose cells they are uniquely adapted to infect.

There are many steps within each of these stages, of course, and their timing varies across viruses and hosts. SARS-CoV-2 may complete most of these steps within just one or two days, before onset of COVID-19 symptoms. HIV, causing AIDS, can remain integrated into the DNA of host cells for 15 years or longer before reactivation and production of offspring. Observing the integrated HIV genes during their dormancy alone misses their full life cycle process, just as watching an adult frog misses theirs. During their intracellular stage, viruses cleverly co-opt the host’s cellular machinery and genes for combined use together with the virus’s genes to produce thousands upon thousands of virus progeny.
