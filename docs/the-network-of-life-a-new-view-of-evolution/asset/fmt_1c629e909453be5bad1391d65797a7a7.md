
# Extending the decentralized approach of the internet to international business and politics

Anne-Marie Slaughter makes the case for the high value of innovative network approaches to problem-solving.^[6](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-6) She argues that individual countries should not be the sole focus of policy concerns, as if individual countries or institutions are engaged in a one-on-one chess match. Rather, it is the web of connections among countries, their links to one another, that demand our focus in understanding the consequences of policy changes. More links among institutions can provide more resilience with more alternative paths for shared success in case of local problems. In this view, governments, economies, and societies adopting openness, with relationship and policy networks rather than strict hierarchies, will compete and succeed most effectively.

## Money

In 2008, a white paper titled “Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System” was privately emailed to a small group of cryptographers—people interested in securing communication networks—under the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto.^[7](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-7) This paper resolved a long-standing problem in protecting computer networks from attack, known as the Byzantine generals’ problem. Essentially, the problem is that all computer nodes in a network, like generals in an army, must agree on a concerted strategy, despite the possibility that some of the generals or their messengers might be compromised. The resolution in Nakamoto’s paper involved several innovations in securing of communications including unforgeable digital signatures, large-scale redundancy, and an economic disincentive to attacks called proof of work.

Spurred by the global economic decline of 2007–9 known as the Great Recession, and its root causes in excessive high-risk and undisclosed centralized banking practices in the United States and elsewhere, Bitcoin emerged as a nascent decentralized system of money. In the United States, the Great Recession caused losses in housing prices and stock markets of about $11.5 trillion, and about 8.6 million people lost their jobs, sending the unemployment rate to 10 percent in October 2009.^[8](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-8) Arguably, the legacy money and economic system in the United States and the world—because the dollar functions as a world reserve currency—had been corrupted.

Bitcoin’s appeal stems from the fact that it is money without a government, a CEO, or a central bank. No group owns or controls it, and, crucially, no group or person can print more to pay for budget deficits, tax cuts, mismanagement, or wars. The total supply is capped at 21 million Bitcoin. It runs on a decentralized network of computers (the nodes), which validate and record all transactions on an open digital ledger called a blockchain. Think of Bitcoin’s network and blockchain as maintaining a huge public spreadsheet that can be added to only if all nodes (the Byzantine generals), and there are currently about 12,000 of them, agree on every transaction.

Initially appealing to a small set of cryptographers and computer scientists driven by problem-solving, Bitcoin soon attracted an array of technologists, libertarians, entrepreneurs, investors, and gawkers recognizing an innovative alternative to a deeply flawed economic system. The first known Bitcoin transaction was purchase of two pizzas for 10,000 Bitcoin in 2010. That cost would be US$230 million at the recent price of $23,000 per Bitcoin. The current market value for all Bitcoin is about $432 billion. This is roughly one-tenth the current value of the world’s gold supply. However, Bitcoin’s value has been growing faster than gold’s by an order of magnitude over the past decade, as it becomes increasingly mainstream.

Bitcoin is extremely volatile, with price reductions of 80 percent or more on multiple occasions, and it remains a high-risk bet. Many critics view it as a threat to governments, whose power is linked to their control of money supplies. Proponents, however, believe this decentralized monetary innovation has impressive advantages.^[9](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter2-9) It can’t be diluted by printing more, unlike all national (fiat) currencies, and it can’t be forged. Bitcoin itself, unlike some centralized exchanges and custodians, has never been hacked. Fees charged by banks or other financial institutions for accessing and sending money (friction) are eliminated or reduced, and the speed of transfer across national and international jurisdictions is measured in seconds or minutes rather than days or weeks. It’s value is increasing over time, unlike fiat currencies, because, as advocates say, it is based on scarcity, which will only increase with increasing demand. People living in countries with local currency crises (e.g., El Salvador, Venezuela, Turkey, Argentina, Brazil) are said to have an option for maintaining their savings, as long as there are no political or social barriers to their participation in the Bitcoin network.

## Life

The terms “centralization” and “decentralization” are most often applied to systems of governance, economics, or technology to describe forms of control.

Their application to life’s evolution is unusual; however, it brings a new conceptual framework and the potential for insight and comparison with other systems where the terms are more common. *Centralization* denotes control restricted to one or a few authorities. In *decentralized* systems, control is shared among many entities. A government where one person makes all important decisions is centralized. A government where local officials make their own decisions is decentralized. Here, I use the concept of decentralization for life’s phylogeny—its evolutionary relationships—to augment the term “network.” In saying life’s phylogeny is networked, and therefore decentralized to some degree, I am emphasizing the sharing of genetic materials outside the confines of parents and their progeny. For example, decentralization for plant evolution, by horizontal gene transfer into plants from nonplants, is shown in [figure 1.3](#08_ch1.xhtml#fig1-3).

To the extent that creatures experience any of the mechanisms of horizontal evolution, whether recently or in the distant past, they embody at least some decentralization of life’s genetic material. To the extent they have not experienced horizontal evolution, their genealogy is more centralized and more hierarchical. These are not binary, all-or-none, conditions for organisms and their genomes. Groups of organisms and species fall along a continuum of more or less centralized. And these may vary among individuals and over time.

Decentralizing horizontal evolution brings a similar potential for innovation among life-forms, as noted above for nonbiological entities, like communications and money (see [box 2.1](#09_ch2.xhtml#box2-1)). Without horizontal evolution, genetic variation within species is limited to that which already exists plus the incremental mutations associated with reproduction and DNA replication. Asexual reproduction is clonal and, in the absence of horizontal evolution, even more restrictive of variation.
