
We now have the ability to evaluate the frequency of past hybridization events by comparing entire genomes across many species. Researchers are finding hybridization among species to be much more common than previously thought, in both plants and animals. Recent estimates are that roughly 25 percent of the world’s flowering plant species and 10 percent of animals have arisen through hybridization.^([4](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-4)) Though hybrid origins are less common in animals, recent discoveries include examples in insects, cichlid fishes, and birds, one being a species of Darwin’s finch from the Galapagos Islands.

Furthering the narrative of widespread integration among species, hybridization has knock-on effects, known as introgression, that continue the genetic mixing among species even if a new species does not arise. This is seen when hybrid individuals keep their ability to reproduce with one or both parent species, or with closely related species. Hybrid introgression has been found in all sexually reproducing species radiations (see [table 1.1](#08_ch1.xhtml#tab1-1)) evaluated to date with genome-scale data, including studies of stickleback fishes, `Heliconius` butterflies, and `Anolis` lizards. Many other examples of introgression are known, such as between coyotes and gray wolves and between polar and brown bears. Genetic mixing for the two bears dates back to the last glacial maximum about 20,000 years ago, when polar and brown bears overlapped in range more broadly. Estimates for the frequency of introgression are certain to increase as methods for revealing it improve.^([5](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-5))

All hybridization phenomena, including introgression, qualify as horizontal evolution, because genetic material is exchanged between different species, rather than between parents and offspring, the path of vertical evolution. They denote networking rather than branching.

## Recombination and Horizontal Gene Transfer

The hybridization discussion above applies to sexually reproducing plants, fungi, and animals. However, as noted in [chapter 2](#09_ch2.xhtml), recombination among bacterial lineages accomplishes genetic mixing analogous to hybridization. The three primary mechanisms (transformation, transduction, conjugation) are also forms of horizontal gene transfer, and for this reason the early history for recombination and horizontal gene transfer is shared.

The beginning of their shared history is tightly linked with one of the most consequential biological discoveries of any era, the discovery that DNA is the material of inheritance. We can begin in 1928 when British microbiologist Frederick Griffith ([figure 3.1](#10_ch3.xhtml#fig3-1)) published results of an exciting set of experiments. Using mice and `Streptococcus pneumoniae`, the bacterium causing pneumonia, he demonstrated the ability of a nonvirulent bacterial strain to be “transformed” into a virulent one. He did this by mixing the two strains together in mice. Prior to mixing, the virulent strain was killed by boiling, yet it still managed to transform the living benign strain into a living virulent one. Griffith died in his laboratory during a German air attack on London in 1941. That same year, Oswald Avery, a researcher at the Rockefeller Institute in New York City, picked up on Griffith’s efforts to identify the transforming substance in the killed virulent strain of `Streptococcus`.

Avery and two colleagues, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty, went on to demonstrate and publish in 1944 that DNA was the mysterious transforming factor, and more broadly, DNA was the material of inheritance. Avery wrote to his brother, hinting at the horizontal nature of the transformation, that “nucleic acids [DNA] … induce predictable and hereditary changes in cells. Sounds like a virus—may be a gene.”^([6](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter3-6)) But bacterial transformation, with direct uptake of genetic material by one bacterium from another, would not be appreciated as a form of horizontal gene transfer until decades later. Perhaps the role of DNA in vertical, parent-to-progeny inheritance had to be understood first. Nonetheless, the earliest evidence of horizontal gene transfer in bacteria was published and considered well before its evolutionary consequences for other microbes and other life-forms were understood.

Another mechanism for horizontal gene transfer was discovered in 1951 when microbiologist Victor Freeman at the University of Washington in Seattle found that he could use a virus to infect and carry genes that changed a nonvirulent strain of bacteria to virulent one. The bacterium involved, `Corynebacterium diphtheriae`, was the cause of diphtheria, and the intention of the study was to learn about possible ways to manage contagious disease. Similarly, in 1952, Zinder and Joshua Lederberg working at the University of Wisconsin found evidence for genetic exchange among `Salmonella` bacteria, which can cause stomach flu, mediated by viruses, and coined the term “transduction” for it. These authors realized they were looking at a mechanism of inheritance that differed from the vertical, parent-to-progeny, mode. The frequency and speed of lateral gene transfer for bacterial evolution was not recognized until the 1950s, with the rapid and global spread of bacterial resistance to antibiotics. Still, the role of horizontal gene transfer in the evolution of life would remain poorly known until many more species and their genomes could be explored.

## Joining of Lineages

Simon Schwendener was on to something ([figure 3.2](#10_ch3.xhtml#fig3-2)). And he had no way of knowing how big. He knew simply that what he saw when looking at lichens through his microscope in the 1860s defied the experts. As far as they were concerned lichens were plants, and one lichen was one plant. Instead, the 28-year-old Swiss botanist saw algae and confusing masses of threadlike filaments from a microscopic fungus. This made no sense if lichens were plants. Over months and years of painstaking observation with equipment we’d now deem primitive, he watched as threadlike fungal filaments called hyphae developed independently from the algae that were clustered along the lichen surfaces. He watched as hyphae first surrounded the algae and then stimulated them to reproduce. He worked tirelessly to enhance the power of his light microscope and repeat his experiments in order to dispel his own initial doubts about his observations. Eventually, he became certain his lichens resulted from the interactions between two different life-forms, algae and fungi, and that no plants were involved. Not only was he defying the plant experts, he contradicted the conventional view of species origins, as explained by Darwin, in which new species evolved by splitting from others, not by joining them.
