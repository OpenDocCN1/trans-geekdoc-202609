
# Quaking Aspens and the Role of Water in Life

Quaking aspens are unusual in reproducing both sexually and clonally through root sprouts. Their seeds lack a protective coating and stored nutrients, so they are viable for a relatively short period of time, and their role in reproduction is limited, especially in arid western North America. There is a huge quaking aspen individual, a colony of clones, in Utah with genetically identical trees (or stems) connected by a single root system, which covers 105 acres, weighs 6.6 tons, and is estimated to be at least 80,000 years old. This individual must rank high in terms of the greatest oxygen contribution by a plant.

It is the ability of land plants like the quaking aspen to adapt and colonize Earth’s diverse habitats, from deserts to tropics and tundra, that provided the secondary push in adding oxygen to the atmosphere, following the evolution of photosynthesizing bacteria. The colonization of land began about 475 million years ago, well before oxygen first reached current levels of 20 percent, and has resulted in a proliferation of forms, including an estimated 435,000 extant species of land plants.

## Water

All known life-forms require water, just as we do. The need for water is about more than satisfying thirst or even growing food. Water provides the requisite solvent that allows carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen to react and form structures like `DNA` and proteins. This, in turn, allows individual organisms to exist and metabolize, as necessary for reproduction and survival. Without water as a chemical solvent, our cells could not produce energy. Life’s need for water is fundamental. No substitute is known.

How did Earth’s water arise? Unlike atmospheric oxygen or soil, Earth’s water is not a by-product of life. Rather, evidence suggests that water, now observed to be prevalent in the universe in gaseous clouds, is a by-product of star formation and was most likely brought to Earth by asteroids. Earthbound processes such as leakage from water-containing minerals in rocks may have contributed as well. The important roles that species play in maintenance of water have to do with purifying water by removing sediments and pollutants, and by helping regulate the flow of water around the world, including mitigating floods. We’ll briefly consider caddisflies and mangrove plants as examples.

### Aquatic Insect Larvae

The grey flag caddisfly (*Hydropsyche pellucidula*) is one of 15,000 caddisfly species in the exclusively aquatic insect order `Trichoptera` ([figure 6.8](#14_ch6.xhtml#fig6-8)). The adults look like small, narrow-winged moths with unusually long antennae, and they ingest only liquids during their few weeks or months on land. The aquatic larvae, however, feed on detritus, which they collect by seining freshwater streams with elaborate nets of their own design. They spin the nets from silk produced in their specialized salivary glands, adding sand, pebbles, and twigs as needed. The collected and consumed detritus includes small algae, plant, and animal materials, as well as their many fungal and bacterial decomposers. The relative abundance of *H*. *pellucidula* and other caddisflies is a positive indication of water quality, as the larvae quickly accumulate water contaminants and suffer the effects of them, and of and low oxygen levels. Although the impact of individual caddisflies on water purity is negligible, the cumulative effect of large populations is significant. Caddisfly experts estimate there may be as many as 60,000 species globally, though surveys for them have been limited.

### Mangroves

The red mangrove (*Rhizophora mangle*) is found in tropical estuaries around the world ([figure 6.9](#14_ch6.xhtml#fig6-9)). Like all 110 known species of mangrove trees, the red mangrove is adapted for growth in waterlogged soils having little available oxygen, high salinity, and frequent tides. Few other plants are able to survive in such environments. Its adaptations include a rubberlike coating on its roots to keep salt out, and stilt roots to prop the mangrove trees up above high tide and to help in aeration through specialized pores in the roots and trunk.

Like many other plants, red mangroves remove some water contaminants at leaf surfaces when returning water vapor to the atmosphere. Red mangrove root systems help purify water by slowing freshwater runoff through estuaries, trapping organic debris, and promoting deposition of fine-grained sediment layers. Decomposing organic materials and fine-grained sediments both have a high capacity for binding heavy metals and other contaminants, making mangrove forests effective in protecting nearby water from such toxins. Removal of mangroves, however, can lead to release of those same contaminants.

Red mangroves and their associated soil bacteria can further improve estuary water quality by consuming excess nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus in runoff from farm fields. Unchecked, excess nutrients can cause rapid growth of marine algae, depleting oxygen supplies in the water and creating coastal dead zones where few organisms can survive.

Mangroves provide vital habitats for many other species, such as sponges, barnacles, and oysters, but especially so for fish. Recent estimates show that mangrove forests make up only 0.12 percent of the world’s total land area, but support 80 percent of the global fish catch during some part of their life cycle, providing food, habitat, or both.
