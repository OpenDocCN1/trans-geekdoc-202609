
Our growing knowledge of beneficial relationships between a few soil bacteria and plants and, subsequently, humans, belies our general ignorance about soil bacterial diversity and the unknown functions that the unknown diversity may serve. Bearing this out, a recent survey of soil microorganisms from farm fields in the Netherlands identified over 33,000 different bacterial and archaeal taxa based on variation in a commonly used genetic marker, the 16S ribosomal RNA gene,^([3](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter6-3)) and about 20 percent of the taxa are previously unknown to science. Although we are far from a comprehensive understanding of bacterial diversity, we do know that human populations are dependent on many of them.

#### Protozoa

*`Colpoda steinii*` is a representative protozoan species. *`C. steinii`* has hairlike cilia used in moving, feeding, and sensing its environment, which is often moist soil. *`C. steinii`* is a voracious predator of bacteria, ingesting five million or more of them on a good day. Many thousands of individual protozoans similar to *`C. steinii`* can be found in a single teaspoon of fertile soil. This translates into a pivotal role for *`C. steinii`*, and its fellow protozoans, in regulating, and often increasing, bacterial population sizes, as many bacteria respond to population thinning with increased population growth. In turn, this can boost soil nutrients by increasing net activity of nutrient-fixing bacteria and by increasing the activity of more protozoans in excreting and making available the considerable amount of nutrients that are tied up in the bacterial cells themselves. All of this makes protozoans vital for soil productivity. In some locations, protozoans are estimated to provide 40 percent of the mineralization for soil nitrogen. Protozoans are also important food sources for other soil-dwelling species, and, like some bacteria, are known to suppress plant diseases by competing with or feeding on pathogenic nematodes. Thus, *`C. steinii`*, like many other protozoans, contributes significantly to soil productivity and helps sustain humans through its impacts on bacterial populations, on mixing and mineralization of soils, and in support for plant growth.

#### Fungi

*`Rhizophagus intraradices`* is a widespread species of soil-dwelling fungus that has evolved to live in close association with plant roots ([Figure 6.4](#14_ch6.xhtml#fig6-4)). *`R. intraradices`* reproductive cells grow as tiny threads in the soil until they contact plant roots, at which point they penetrate the root to take up residence. Once inside the root, *`R. intraradices`* forms branched structures called hyphae that serve as sites of nutrient exchange with the host plant. *`R. intraradices`* captures phosphorus, sulfur, nitrogen, and water from soil and brings them into the root cells, receiving food in return, often in the form of sugar. Many different species of soil fungi live in similar obligate symbioses with plants, and those fungi are called mycorrhizae (see [Figure 1.5](#08_ch1.xhtml#fig1-5)). Mycorrhizal networks probe up to 20 percent more soil volume than typical plant roots and root hairs, owing to their much smaller size. Like bacteria, fungi are valuable decomposers of organic materials, able to convert and store nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus in useable form. More than 90 percent of plant species absolutely depend on these mycorrhizal relationships for their survival—and don’t forget that we depend on plants for food and air. Soils must support a variety of fungal species if they are to support plants and other life. Though some estimate that over 165 million species of fungi exist (see [Table 1.2](#08_ch1.xhtml#tab1-2)), only about 150,000 have been described.

#### Nematodes

*`Caenorhabditis elegans`* is a nematode, or roundworm, averaging one millimeter in length, that frequents soils and compost piles. Although *`C. elegans`* is a star and leading model organism for research on genetics and development, its habits in the wild are less well known. Roughly 20,000 nematode species have been described, yet this is thought to represent just 1 percent of the total in existence. Nematodes like *`C. elegans`* are valuable in soils for making nutrients available to plants by converting organic materials from their prey, which are bacteria, fungi, and other nematodes. Similar to ciliate protozoans, nematodes can stimulate bacterial population growth by feeding and further increase soil nutrients available for other organisms. They also provide food for soil-dwelling insects, spiders, and centipedes. Nematodes help distribute bacteria and fungi through soils by transporting live or dormant bacterial forms in their digestive tracts or on their surface.

#### Arthropods

*`Atta sexdens`* is a species of leafcutter ant, ranging from the southern United States to northern Argentina ([Figure 6.5](#14_ch6.xhtml#fig6-5)). Individuals cut and carry fresh leaves, grasses, and flowers into their large multichambered subterranean colonies, where members chew the vegetation into a pulp, fertilize it with their waste, and carefully tend it as a garden where they transplant and grow selected fungi—their primary food source. Some fungus-growing ants even protect their crops from microbial infection with the help of their own symbiotic bacteria, which produce antibiotics. *`A. sexdens`* plays important roles in soil productivity by recycling and increasing essential soil nutrients in the course of digesting vegetation, and enhancing soil populations of fungi and bacteria; by moving and aerating soil and improving its drainage and cohesion while excavating their colonies; and by dispersing plant seeds to newly productive soils. *`A. sexdens`* is just one of about 12,000 described ant species, many with similar benefits for soils and ultimately people.
