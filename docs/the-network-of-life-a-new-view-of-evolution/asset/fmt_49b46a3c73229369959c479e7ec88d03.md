
*Prochlorococcus marinus* is a tiny photosynthesizing marine bacterium, with a prodigious collective output of oxygen ([figure 6.6](#14_ch6.xhtml#fig6-6)). These cyanobacteria average 0.7 micrometers in width, or one-hundredth the diameter of a human hair, and are smaller than some viruses. Individuals have only 1,800 or so genes, compared with 20,000 for humans. But their broad oceanic distribution and their unique ability to survive in nutrient-poor areas of the ocean render them superabundant. They are estimated to provide more than 50 percent of all photosynthesis in the open ocean and, roughly, they provide the oxygen in one of every five breaths that you take. Their abundance and use of carbon dioxide during photosynthesis make them high-impact players in the global cycling of carbon as well. Nutrient-poor areas of the ocean are currently increasing, sadly, and *Prochlorococcus*’s relative contribution to global oxygen supplies and carbon cycling is very likely to grow over time.

Identifying and naming discrete species of bacteria, which reproduce clonally, is more subjective than doing so in sexually reproducing groups like birds or mammals, where reproductive isolation helps in designating species. Currently, a single species of *Prochlorococcus* is identified (*P*. *marinus*); however, there are a growing number of ecotypes (populations uniquely adapted to local environments) being recognized.^([6](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter6-6)) *Prochlorococcus* ecotypes include populations inhabiting ocean layers with various combinations of light (depth) levels and nutrient levels. One of the keys to *Prochlorococcus*’s success is its ability to inhabit places inhospitable to competitors, and mounting evidence suggests that small cell and genome sizes are adaptive advantages for *Prochlorococcus* in nutrient-poor waters. Small genomes can be replicated faster and with less metabolic cost. They can also be packaged in smaller cells, having more surface area per unit volume, which can, in turn, optimize light absorption.

Given our goal of examining a few species that we rely on for oxygen, let’s go one step further and consider how *Prochlorococcus*’s evolution has been helped by some viruses. The genome size of *Prochlorococcus* averages about two million bases (2 Mb) for the 1,800 genes mentioned. This is among the smallest of genome sizes found in cyanobacteria, which range in size up to 9.1 Mb. *Prochlorococcus* and its relatives are considered to approximate the minimum possible size for photosynthetic microbes. One of the perils of evolving small genome size is loss of genes and genetic variation, which are important for survival in different and changing environments. The compact *Prochlorococcus* genome is, however, known to benefit from the horizontal transfer of genes by viruses that infect cyanobacteria, known as cyanophages. This includes horizontal transfer of genes involved in photosynthesis both among *Prochlorococcus* populations and between *Prochlorococcus* populations and some cyanophages, which maintain photosynthesis genes of their own. By providing a mechanism for adding novel genes and gene variants, horizontal gene transfer mediated by cyanophages helps enable adaptive evolution of *Prochlorococcus* forms specializing in survival under changing light and nutrient regimes in the ocean. This horizontal gene transfer via marine viruses adds yet another group of organisms, cyanophages, to the network supporting global oxygen supplies. It’s also another example of the importance and speed of horizontal evolution. Evolution’s new narrative incorporates and highlights these patterns of horizontal connection.

### Green Algae

*Volvox carteri* is a single-celled, chloroplast-bearing freshwater alga and another impressive oxygen producer ([figure 6.7](#14_ch6.xhtml#fig6-7)). Well known among biologists for its ability to span single and multicellular lifestyles, thousands of *Volvox* individuals can join in forming a translucent colonial sphere, which rolls and twirls to the coordinated beating of each individual’s tail-like flagella. Colonies swim in search of light for photosynthesis, and, occasionally, they dance in pairs and cross-fertilize. *Volvox* colonies include two cell types, numerous somatic cells forming the sphere and a smaller number of reproductive cells contained within the sphere. Given its transitional lifestyle, as an organism capable of both single and multicellular living, *Volvox* has become a model organism for studying the evolution of multicellularity. The molecular and developmental changes involved in this particular transition to multicellularity are relatively few and took place early during the evolution of *Volvox*, about 200 million years ago.^([7](#17_notes.xhtml#chapter6-7))

*V*. *carteri* is one of 22,000 or so species of green algae, which range from single-celled flagellates to large multicellular seaweeds and kelp. Green algae as a group are abundant, providing primary food sources for many fresh- and saltwater organisms, and contributing significantly to the global oxygen supply. This is also the group whose ancestors gave rise to oxygen-producing land plants. The role of lineage merger is pervasive in the oxygenation of Earth’s biosphere, with multiple independent merger or joining events between cyanobacteria and various lineages of green algae, red algae, land plants, and glaucophytes (see [figure 2.5](#09_ch2.xhtml#fig2-5)).

### Land Plants

Quaking aspen (*Populus tremuloides*) is the most widely distributed native tree species in North America, ranging from Alaska and northeastern Canada south through the Rocky Mountains and into Mexico. Like all photosynthesizers, quaking aspen trees release oxygen as a by-product, separating oxygen from the carbon in carbon dioxide molecules. The amount of oxygen released by trees varies greatly depending on tree species, individual size, and environment, but rough estimates are that the oxygen consumption of one adult human is offset by the activity of roughly 10 to 20 trees.
