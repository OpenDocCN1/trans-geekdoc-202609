
But for many, this context is less than obvious. Most of us are buffered from natural environments, relying on technology for food, shelter, medicines, and travel. Our basic survival skills, stripped of technology, have atrophied, and we live in areas and densities that preclude reliance on hunting and gathering food. Considering environmental health for a nonhuman can help us see more clearly the lengthy coevolution, the current connections, and the large role that other species play in healthy, favorable environments for any species of concern. So, let’s consider the magnificent peregrine falcon. It lives off the land, in a way our species once did but no longer can. Having been a falconer, this species is among my favorites and directly familiar.

## Lives in Nature

Peregrines need lots of other species to eat. Though they specialize on small songbirds, more than a fifth of all 10,000 extant bird species have been recorded as peregrine prey somewhere across their global range, varying from hummingbirds to great gray owls, helping, involuntarily, to sustain peregrines as a species ([figure 6.1](#14_ch6.xhtml#fig6-1)). This is also true for the smaller component of bats, voles, shrews, hares, and other small mammals, as well as reptiles, amphibians, and insects, on which they feed where conditions suit.

Peregrines also need whoever and whatever it is that supports their supporters. Thus, a peregrine pair raising their young on the windswept coastal cliffs of northern California require not only hundreds of individual violet-green swallows, cliff swallows, orange-crowned warblers, least sandpipers, and cinnamon teal to feed their brood, they also need the feeding relationships and the species that support all of their diverse prey. If we follow the feeding relationships for just the cinnamon teal among their prey, we find that peregrines, via the cinnamon teal, also rely on insects such as damselflies, dragonflies, caddisflies, beetles, and midges and dozens of species of marine and freshwater snails, slugs, and clams, as well as many plant species whose parts are eaten by cinnamon teal, including pondweeds, bulrushes, sedges, and salt grasses.

Reliance on these species, which compose the food of one of the peregrine’s food species, is still only a sliver of the food web, which includes more examples of what the eaten eat, as with dragonflies eating gnats eating rotten fruits. And there is also reliance on decomposer species providing soil nutrients for plants. If we consider just the decomposer bacteria making nutrients newly available for the bulrushes in the teal’s diet, we can find hundreds of millions of individual bacteria representing hundreds of species, in just a handful of pond muck.

To expand the reach of these feeding relationships, multiply these local relationships for one prey species, the cinnamon teal, to the thousands of other peregrine prey species, not just in California during the breeding season but throughout the peregrine’s annual range, from Alaska to Tasmania. This gives an idea of the number and diversity of the peregrine falcon’s current supporting relationships.

And change is a constant. Declines in abundance of the peregrine’s prey species and declines in those species supporting their prey can and do influence the distribution, reproduction, and overall health of peregrine populations. Similarly, the story of the peregrine’s decline and recovery shows the impacts of environmental toxins moving through their web of feeding relationships. Human application of pesticides with toxic chemicals (DDT and DDE) starting in the 1940s sought to enhance crop yields in the Americas. But after poisoning insects, the toxins moved into insect-eating songbirds, and from there into songbird-eating peregrines. In peregrines the toxins hampered calcium metabolism, yielding thin eggshells, lower reproductive success, and local extinction of breeding populations in the 1960s–1980s. Recovery followed reduction in pesticide use, protection of remaining populations, and reintroduction of captive-bred peregrines.

Our feeding relationships were once as rich in biological diversity as those of peregrines. Although this may still be the case for a few hunter-gatherer human populations, humans are mostly reliant, instead, on technology and a small number of domesticated plants and animals. Our experiences and our awareness of other species are diminished. This feeds the perception that we are somehow above nature and no longer in need of biological diversity to sustain our lives. It also lets us think we are the alpha and omega of life on Earth. But these views are mistaken, as I will argue below.

Just as we needed to clarify what we meant by human health in the previous chapter, we need to address here what is meant by environmental health. For many agencies and individuals concerned with environmental health, including the World Health Organization, the role of environment in human health is considered primarily from the standpoint of human exposure to chemical toxins, radiation, and pathogens. Most environmental health regulations adopted by government agencies attempt to minimize exposure to these environmental dangers. But there is more to consider regarding environmental health than human exposures.

A better conception for environmental health, in better alignment with evolution’s new narrative, is the capacity for the environment to sustain humans and other life-forms. In this view, environmental health varies over time, as it is linked to the ability of humans and other species to coevolve and adapt to change. This is still consistent with use of the term by public health professionals, and it adds the relationships among humans and other life-forms as key components for health. These relationships among species play out globally, across evolutionary time scales ranging from the present back to life’s origins 3.8 billion years ago, and they include all species regardless of size, lifestyle, or distribution.

Yes, there are a relatively small number of species having direct and utmost importance to human health. However, I justify the broad addition of all other species as recognition that our knowledge of the indirect effects of any particular species on others is incomplete, and that those species with direct influence will change over time and by geographic location. The better conception above is still, admittedly, human-centric. The least biased or speciesist conception would not be centered on healthy environments for humans alone. A nonspeciesist view of environmental health is laudable, though not widely embraced. Perhaps the exploration below may help convince readers of all life’s inherent value, individually and collectively.
