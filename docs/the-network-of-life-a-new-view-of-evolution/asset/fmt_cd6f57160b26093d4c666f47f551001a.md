
# Technology Extends Human Capabilities

At this point, our technology surpasses our brain’s innate abilities alone in many ways. Technology extends our senses in seeing over 13 billion light years away to the furthest known galaxy from Earth. We can detect the movements of electrons, which are 1/1000th the size of protons, which are about 1/25 trillionth of an inch in diameter. Our machines have vastly greater memory and capacity for gathering and analyzing information, boosting our brains in problem solving and prediction.

## Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning

Using computers to simulate human intelligence in gaining and applying knowledge is deemed **artificial intelligence (AI)**. AI is pervasive in the daily lives of hundreds of millions of people. It is central to most commercial computing and online activity. It’s used in formulating answers to complex questions with `Chat GPT`, web-search algorithms like `Google`, recommendation systems as in `Facebook` and `Netflix`, targeted advertising, language translation, speech and face recognition, automated asset trading, self-driving cars, weather prediction, navigation, matchmaking, and gaming.

**Machine learning** is a subset of AI in which computers are programmed to use data recursively in improving their analyses. For example, machine learning is at work in identifying junk email and is most effective when given feedback to boost learning. More generally, machine learning provides cycles of reprogramming and improving software based on its own performance.

## The AI Effect and Future Trajectory

Experts agree AI’s capacity for rationality and analysis will surpass the intelligence of our brightest individuals, if it hasn’t happened already. Indeed this has been in progress for quite a while, resulting in something called the **AI effect**. This is when tasks once considered to require intelligence are no longer viewed that way, after AI has solved and automated the job. As cognitive scientist Douglas Hofstadter notes, AI is whatever hasn’t been done yet. Perhaps this is just face-saving, and our way of maintaining our role in the process of solving various problems. Though we may move the goalposts for what qualifies as intelligence, the era of augmenting our brains with software and hardware is making fast progress.

`Chat GPT4`, freely available to anyone with an internet connection, now has human-level intelligence and could outcompete humans for many jobs. It can write and debug computer code faster than we can, as well as ace standardized tests, including writing essays, for evaluating prospective law, business, and medical students. Many AI experts are urging a pause in further development of “unpredictable black-box models with emergent capabilities,” asking whether we should rush to develop “nonhuman minds that might eventually outnumber, outsmart, obsolete and replace us.”^[11] The pause could be used to work out and implement safety protocols that are audited and overseen by independent experts. Efforts to pause or stop technology development have been implemented or attempted with human cloning, human sex-cell editing (as discussed above), and gain-of-function research targeting pathogens; however, those technologies are harder to access than open-access AI apps. Whether or not a pause happens in development of powerful AI systems, and it seems unenforceable and hence unlikely, we should be proactive in seeking and applying wisdom rather than reactive. It will take years to develop the requisite regulatory and governance systems for AI, and in the meantime our existential risk appears real but enigmatic. AI has been compared to technologies of fire, the internet, and nuclear fission for its likely impacts on human lives and society. There have been negative and positive consequences for those, and I suppose the same will be true for AI. It can’t be undeveloped.

## Limitations of Brain Replication

Recreating the brain or parts of it with engineered physical devices may contribute to advances, but this approach is handicapped by the legacy of evolutionary history in brain design. This includes its construction using proteins encoded by DNAs. Our brains have brought us far, but natural selection did not optimize them for the kinds of analyses and rationality we often need and that advanced computation can provide.

## Near-Term Benefits: Transportation and Virtual Assistants

In the near term, safe self-driving cars, trucks, and buses can be expected to reduce the 1.2 million deaths globally from vehicle accidents each year. A corresponding shift from personal cars to autonomous ride sharing in urban areas can reduce costs of transportation for many, while reducing traffic congestion and space lost to parking. Current virtual assistants like `Siri` and `Alexa` are error prone (me: “Alexa, call the police!” Alexa: “Shuffling songs by the Police.”), but improving. Future virtual assistants with enhanced background knowledge and detailed information about their users’ circumstances will be significantly better in managing and integrating calendars, travel bookings, purchases, payments, taxes, investment decisions, healthy meals, relevant unbiased news, and communication with friends and family. If these features were well implemented in a system that could be trusted (no small feat), individuals could be empowered to act more effectively in a complicated and sometimes adverse world.

## AI in Health Care

AI is flowing in the heart of health care. This is based on the growth and use of large datasets in training computer programs to improve themselves and their inferences. This is machine learning. Progress can most readily be seen, perhaps, in those medical specialties that rely on images for making diagnoses. There is an expanding core of comparative imaging data, from `MRIs` (magnetic resonance imaging), `CTs` (computed tomography), `EKGs` (electrocardiograms), ultrasounds, and more. Computers can learn about patterns within the images and link them to the diagnoses that were made by professionals, reviewing hundreds of thousands and eventually millions of patient cases, with a relatively small amount of human oversight. It is still early days for machine learning on medical images, but, in the words of cardiologist and author Eric Topol, this technology has the potential to “diagnose some types of skin cancer as well as or perhaps even better than board-certified dermatologists, to identify specific heart-rhythm abnormalities like cardiologists, to interpret medical scans or pathology slides as well as senior, highly qualified radiologists and pathologists, respectively, to diagnose various eye diseases as well as ophthalmologists, and to predict suicide better than mental health professionals.”^[12]

Of course, AI will influence more in medicine than just pattern recognition. There is great potential to learn from linking whole genome sequences for millions of patients with particular symptoms and diseases, along with the outcomes of particular treatments. AI-driven computer programs don’t get tired or distracted from overworking. None of this is to say that physicians can or will be replaced. Quite the opposite should be true. As Topol notes, AI computers can support a more humane and empathic medicine, where physicians and other health-care workers have more time for in-person and empathic patient care.
