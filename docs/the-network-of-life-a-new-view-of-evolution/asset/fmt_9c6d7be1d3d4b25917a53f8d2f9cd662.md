
## Life-Spans

Many people are aware that average human life-spans are increasing. Fewer recognize that the maximum age, roughly 125 years, has not changed much since the end of the Stone Age 5,000 years ago.^[6] What has changed is life expectancy, the average time that an individual lives, and the frequency with which some reach milestones such as 100 years of age (Figure 8.2). Longer life expectancy stems from greatly reduced infant mortality, improved hygiene, and public health measures, plus advances in medicine. These are the result, in turn, of improved knowledge and technology regarding healthy lifestyles, genetic markers and monitoring for early warning about diseases, and improvements in medicine. Regarding diet, recent research confirms well-known advice for a longer, healthier life. Restrict calories without malnutrition. Stop eating before feeling stuffed. Eat less often. Fast for 12–16 hours on occasion. Hippocrates knew much of this 2,400 years ago, though knowledge is not application.

Exciting new research into the aging process (senescence), shows it to be driven by a changing balance of DNA damage and repair processes in our bodies over time (Figure 8.3).^[7] The DNAs in all of our cells naturally experience damage over time, as they are copied and exposed to toxins, carcinogens, and radiation. Our cells have impressive innate abilities to repair those damaged DNAs. However, when the rate of damage exceeds the rate of repair, and it does sooner or later, the damage accumulates. This damage contributes to overall senescence, characterized by diminishing levels of DNA repair and reduced capacity to eliminate abnormal cells, part of an important process called apoptosis, or programmed cell death. The outcomes of these problems in DNA repair and apoptosis are increases in the illnesses of old age: cancer, dementia, heart disease, respiratory disease, and others.

Let’s consider a few current research projects on the mechanisms and possible control of aging. Sirtuins are a family of proteins that influence important cell functions. These include DNA repair, healthy inflammation in response to infection, and apoptosis, noted above as the process of eliminating abnormal cells. When apoptosis fails, aging, cancerous, and virus-infected cells can proliferate. Because of their important functions, damage to sirtuins or excessive use and stress on them can accelerate aging. It is not yet clear how to apply this knowledge to slow the aging process in humans. However, stimulation of sirtuin production in mice has led to clear improvements in DNA repair, memory, and physical endurance, which are rough proxies for aging.

mTOR is another group of proteins implicated in aging. Similar to the sirtuins, mTOR also functions in DNA repair and digestion of old proteins in humans. But when mTOR is overexpressed and overly active, its usual functions can be compromised. Studies of nonmammals have found that reducing activity of some of the same proteins—called TOR without the “m” for mammalian—leads to a longer life-span in yeast, roundworms, and fruit flies. In mice, an inhibitor of mTOR, rapamycin, also increases life-span. Trials of a rapamycin-like drug in humans resulted in improvements in immune-system function and fewer infections, but possible effects on longevity are still unknown.^[8]

Natural biological stresses can damage cells causing unhealthy inflammation, failure to replicate, and, as with sirtuins and mTOR, resistance to apoptosis and the destruction of abnormal cells. Research is underway on drugs that can target and eliminate damaged cells, known as senolytics. Trials in mice have demonstrated an ability for these drugs to reverse several features of aging. Preliminary trials in humans show promise.^[9] Based on progress to date, the next few decades are likely to see progress in the knowledge and technologies that reduce DNA damage, enhance DNA repair, and, thereby, slow the processes of aging.

There are many concerns related to increasing average longevity and human population growth. Will the additional years be lived with good health and a sound mind? How will societies, families, and economies adapt to increasing numbers of supercentenarians, the term for those reaching 110 years of age? How will this impact our already heavy consumption of natural resources and the ensuing environmental problems? Will humans stop trying to extend average longevity with good health? I don’t think so. The desire to extend lives will not dissipate as long as people love and care about their families, friends, and others. Further, the scientific challenge of slowing the aging process is irresistible, and, at least incrementally, within reach. We’ve already made good progress (see Figure 8.2), and that is prior to current understanding of the molecular mechanisms of DNA damage and repair.

Aging and its associated physical and mental decline are, or at least still appear, inevitable. But its pace can be slowed and we seem bound to find the molecular mechanisms responsible. As the saying goes, death is easy, its living that’s hard. Humans are drawn to doing hard things. Often with technology.

### Extending Our Brains

The brain’s ability to coordinate our body functions and movements is impressive. So are our species’ intellectual accomplishments rooted in abstract thought, such as developing the technologies at the heart of this discussion. These accomplishments distinguish our species from the others. However, is your brain optimal? Do you have flawless recall of information and events? Are your judgements unbiased and wise? Are your thoughts reliably creative or well focused? I’m guessing the answers are no. Our brains serve us well in many ways, but as products of evolution they are constrained by our inheritance from early vertebrates, by selection for success in prior environments, and by the materials at hand.

We struggle with the brain’s limits. We forget. We ignore and daydream. We misinterpret and deny. We hope irrationally rather than plan. Our biases, filtered by emotions and personality, are as fascinating as they are humbling.^[10] The gambler’s fallacy describes our bias in supposing that future possibilities reflect past events. For example, if you lose nine coin tosses in a row, you feel more than 50 percent likely to win the tenth. You shouldn’t. With groupthink we make irrational decisions to avoid conflict. Sometimes—well, let’s say often—the less we know about a subject, the greater our certainty in our own views about it. This is the Dunning-Kreuger effect. Perhaps most insidious, confirmation bias denotes our tendency to seek and magnify evidence supporting our beliefs and to ignore discordant facts.
