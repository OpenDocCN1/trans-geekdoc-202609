
# 共生起源的发现

Schwendener presented his findings to the Swiss Naturalists Club in 1867, saying that lichens are colonies, composed of thousands of individuals of algae and one or more controlling fungus. He compared the fungal and algal components to spider and prey, respectively, with the fungus stimulating the algae trapped in its net of filaments rather than feeding on them. His audience was deeply skeptical. Some experts said Schwendener’s imagination was overactive. Others just called him a simpleton. No one could produce lichens by combining algae and fungi.

Andreas Schimper ([figure 3.3](#10_ch3.xhtml#fig3-3)), another young botanist, was also compelled to question orthodoxy. Peering through his microscope in 1882, the 27-year-old watched an array of tiny green spheres reproduce as they floated within a single isolated plant cell. One sphere become two, over and over. He coined their name “chloroplastiden,” “chloroplasts” in English, for their green pigment, chlorophyll. Schimper puzzled over the evolution of such complexity, noting that the chloroplasts might be mistaken for organisms in their own right, if not for their confinement within plant cells. He published a manuscript in 1883 pointing out the strong resemblance between dividing chloroplasts and free-living, dividing cyanobacteria. Schimper casually stated in a footnote that if plant reproductive cells contained chloroplasts, that would support the preposterous idea that green plants originated as a symbiotic relationship between chloroplasts and some other organism. Though a few other botanists noticed the same similarity between chloroplasts and some bacteria, his speculation was ignored. No symbioses (species living together) among plants and other organisms were known that might lend the idea a bit of plausibility. Schimper did not pursue the idea either, and carried on with his studies of chloroplasts and plant ecology.

Konstantin Mereschkowski, a Russian biologist, did pick up on Schimper’s wild idea about 20 years later. He gathered evidence supporting a much more detailed scenario for the evolution of complex cells, in which both chloroplasts and mitochondria arose via symbiotic relationships, in publications between 1905 and 1918. He was well aware of the power of symbioses, being a student of lichens as well as hydras, small freshwater invertebrates that often live together with algae. Unlike Schwendener and Schimper, Mereschkowski explicitly rejected the Darwinian notion that natural selection could explain all or even most biological novelty, and this predisposed him to seeing the evolution of cells as a process of symbioses instead of selection on small, incremental variations among individual organisms.

But the idea of symbiotic origins for chloroplasts and mitochondria within cells was in direct conflict with the accepted mid-twentieth-century view that chromosomes in cell nuclei were the primary if not sole source of heritable materials for organisms. The view that chloroplasts and mitochondria evolved gradually from materials already within cell nuclei was a much better fit with Darwinian natural selection as catalyzing evolutionary change. Evolution was generally not viewed as advancing by symbioses as outlined by Schwendener, Schimper, and Mereschkowski. Mereschkowski in particular felt himself marginalized by the scientific community. He was a deeply troubled person and eventually took his own life.

Today we can look back and see that key features of the lineage-joining phenomena investigated and described by Schwendener, Schimper, Mereschkowski, Griffith, Linnaeus, and many other pioneers have been not only confirmed but greatly expanded. After Schwendener’s presentation at the Swiss Naturalists Club in 1867, scientists agreed that compelling proof of lichens’ dual nature could be provided if lichens could be produced experimentally from mixtures of fungi and algae in the lab. After many failures, a group led by German botanist Christian Ernst Stahl in 1877 found the right conditions and was able to grow lichens from fungal and algal components. Fast-forwarding to the present, some lichens are now known to include a dozen or more partner species of algae and fungi. The dual nature of lichen evolution, first glimpsed in the mid-1800s, has now expanded to house a veritable community of species.

Obligate symbioses among formerly independent species have been discovered across many life-forms. Invertebrate animals such as corals and jellyfish are reliant on algal symbionts. Symbioses with nitrogen-fixing bacteria are common within the roots of many plant species and key for the plant’s survival. Fungal structures called mycorrhiza stem from symbiotic relationships between fungi and plants, and they increase the ability of plant roots to absorb soil nutrients. These symbioses have been found in over 90 percent of all plant families examined, including some fossil plants about 400 million years old. Symbioses are frequently seen between insects and bacteria that assist in digestion of food and defense against pathogens. In discoveries that will surprise many, our own immune systems, as well as those of all multicellular organisms, were shaped by symbioses with viruses. Symbioses take many forms, ranging from obligate to occasional, and with or without genetic integration. Their evolutionary consequences, however, are deep and widespread across species.
