
Although humans are knowledgeable, we often misunderstand the context while interacting with one another, and we often interpret similar sentences or words entirely differently. We can examine this scenario using an elementary example sentence, "Call me a taxi, please." One can interpret the sentences in many ways, and the sentence is ambiguous. The meaning can be understood in the following ways.
- Call the person as a taxi
- Book a taxi to the person

Another example of an ambiguous sentence is "I saw a man on a hill with a telescope." This sentence can be interpreted in many ways, such as
- A man is standing on the hill, and I saw him with a telescope.
- A man is standing on the hill, which has a telescope.
- I was standing on a hill and saw a man using my telescope.
- I was standing on a hill and saw a man having a telescope.
- There was a man on a hill, and I saw him something with my telescope.

These examples illustrate that language processing is not "deterministic," which means the same language or sentence can have multiple interpretations, and something convenient for one person can be inconvenient or not suitable for another person. Interpretations and suggestion can be varied from entity to entity. Since there is such ambiguity, we can consider Natural Language Processing (NLP) as a non-deterministic approach. Since Natural Language Processing (NLP) is non-deterministic, it can be used to create a highly intelligent machine capable of understanding how humans think and interpret in various contexts.

## 4.2. Types of Natural Language Processing
There are two different classifications in Natural Language Processing; they are Rule-based Natural Language Processing and Statistical Natural Language Processing

### 4.2.1. Rule-based Natural Language Processing
Rule-based approaches are the first developed techniques to process Natural Language. Since these techniques are here for so long, they are proven to work very well. Some of the examples for Rule-based NLP are Context-Free Grammar (`CFG`) and Regular expressions. This technique uses common sense reasoning to operate or consider the "fill in the blanks" method to solve problems. The problems with Rule-based NLP are the performance degradation occurring in generalized scenarios, and the approach takes much time and requires manual efforts.

### 4.2.2. Statistical Natural Language Processing
Statistical Natural Language Processing operates on large amounts of data and tries to find solutions from it. In Statistical NLP, Machine learning algorithms are used to train Natural Language Processing models. ## 4.3. Components of Natural Language Processing (NLP)
There are different stages in natural language processing, as shown in the below figure 4.1., they are Lexical Analysis, Syntactic Analysis, Semantic Analysis, Discourse Analysis, and Pragmatic Analysis. We will discuss each of them one by one.
![](Image00333.jpg)

### 4.3.1. Lexical Analysis
Lexical analysis is otherwise called structure analysis. Here, we analyze the structure of the sentence. Lexicon of a language means the collection of words and phrases in a language. The lexical analysis phase divides the whole input text into paragraphs, sentences, and words. It involves identifying and analyzing words' structure and is hence known as structure analysis.

### 4.3.2. Syntactic Analysis
Syntactic analysis is otherwise called parsing; in this stage, the words (output of lexical analysis phone) are analyzed for grammar and are arranged in a fashion that shows the relationship among the words. Consider an example sentence, "The shop goes to the garden," does not get accepted by English syntactic analyzer since it does not show any relationship between words.

### 4.3.3. Semantic Analysis
Semantic analysis tries to find the accurate meaning for the words and analyses the text's meaningfulness. In this stage, make sure that sentences such as "hot ice-cream" do not go through the Semantic checking phase.

### 4.3.4. Discourse Integration
Discourse integration takes into consideration the context of the text. It considers the meaning of the sentence before it ends. Consider the example, "He came to office." In this case, "he" must be referenced in the preceding sentence.

### 4.3.5. Pragmatic Analysis
The pragmatic analysis is the last stage of Natural Language Processing. The overall communication and interpretation of language are carried out in the Pragmatic analysis stage. It aims to develop meaningful use of language in various situations.

## 4.4. Stemming
Stemming is a procedure in Natural Language Processing used to normalize words. In languages, a single word can have multiple forms depending upon the context used. Let us consider an example word "carry," The word "carry" is a verb, but it can take many other forms such as "carries," "carried," "carrying," and so on, related to its context. During the lexical analysis phase, the input text or speech is tokenized, and the interpreter considers these input words as different words even though they convey the same meaning. Since Natural Language Processing aims to understand the meaning of content for different contexts, we use it to resolve this problem. The stemming procedure normalizes the word by truncating the word to its stem word. Let us see one example, the words "carries," "carried," "carrying" will be reduced to "carri", and all these words are referred to by the stem word "carri" only. That is the token used for all the word forms is "carri". The stemmed output "carri" does not carry any meaning or included in the dictionary. Stemming may not give us a dictionary, grammatical word for a particular set of words. There are different types of Stemming, such as `Porter's Stemmer`, `Snowball Stemmer`, `Lovin's Stemmer`, and many more. One advantage of Snowball Stemmer is that it supports a wide variety of languages.

## 4.5. Lemmatization
Lemmatization operates similarly to stemming and tries to find a similar base "stem" for a given word. However, Lemmatization differs from stemming; Lemmatization finds a dictionary word as a base "stem" instead of truncating the original word. Lemmatization is slower than stemming because stemming does not consider the context or meaning of the stemmed word. Lemmatization is slower but more accurate compared to stemming. The choice of stemming or lemmatization depends on the requirement and project goal. If a project needs faster responses, then stemming may be used, but If higher accuracy is crucial, then the best option is Lemmatization. Lemmatization takes into account Part of Speech (`POS`) values, The process may produce different outputs for different values of `POS`. We generally have four options for `POS`, such as `Verb(v)`, `Noun(n)`, `Adjective(a)` and `Adverb()`

Python Program- Showing the difference between Stemming and Lemmatization
```
import nltk
nltk.download('wordnet')
from nltk.stem import PorterStemmer
from nltk.stem import WordNetLemmatizer
lemmatizer = WordNetLemmatizer()
print(lemmatizer.lemmatize('carrying'))
stemmer = PorterStemmer()
print(stemmer.stem('carrying'))
```
Output:
```
Carrying
Carri
```

## 4.6. Part of Speech Tagging (PoS tagging)
Let us discuss that we need Part of Speech (`PoS`). Consider the simple question, “can you help me with the can?” The word “can” can have several meanings depending on the context and `PoS`. The first occurred, “can” is used as a question word for question formation. The second “can” at the end of the sentence is intended to denote a container or bucket. If you look at the Part of Speech (`PoS`), the first “can” is a verb, while the second “can” is a noun. Giving the word a specific meaning helps the program to handle it correctly in both semantic and syntactic analysis, and hence `PoS` tagging is essential for syntactic and semantic analysis to solve sentences with several semantic meanings.

Python Program
```
import nltk
nltk.download('averaged_perceptron_tagger')
tagging = nltk.pos_tag(["carrying","carried"])
print(tagging)
```
Output:
```
[('carrying', 'VBG'), ('carried', 'VBD')]
```
