
## 4. CASE STUDY : SOURCE IDENTIFICATION IN A STREET

The here-proposed case study refers to the detection of a polluting vehicle stopped somewhere on a boulevard with its motor turned on. It is assumed that stationary conditions of the pollution distribution are obtained under constant wind velocities. We could also consider a situation where another point-wise source such as a gas leak emits in a stationary regime.

### 4.1 Parameters

The training database is built by building the set of parameters,

{p_k = [A_{s,k}, x_{s,k}, σ_{disp k}^2, c_k] ; 1 ≤ k ≤ n_train} , (13)

with [A_s, x_s, σ_disp^2, c_k] ∈ [10^{-2},4] × [0.1,0.9] × [50,2000] × [-10^{-3},10^{-3}] generated by a Sobol sequence beginning from its 10001^{st} term, with units [g km^{-3} s^{-1}, km, km^{-2}, km s^{-1}] respectively. The validation set is created in the same manner with n_test samples, choosing [A_s, x_s, σ_disp^2, c] ∈ [5 × 10^{-2},3] × [0.1,0.9] × [70,1500] × [-10^{-3},10^{-3}], the sequence starts at its 101^{st} term. Source position is purposely generated so that x_s ∈ [0.1 km,0.9 km] to avoid generating sources which would be located too close to the domain boundaries, that could lead to a non-physical solution of our dispersion model given by (1). Our database contains n_train = 15000 training samples, whilst validation is applied on n_test = 200 samples. As a metric of performance, the Relative Root-Square Error (RRSE) is chosen, defined by,

RRSE = ||q̂ - q_T||_2 / ||q_T||_2 , (14)

where (q_T, q̂) ∈ ℝ^{N_x}^2 are respectively the real source profile and its estimate. To facilitate the convergence of the variational method, the initial guess is given by

θ_k^b = [0, arg max_x(u_k^m), 0] , 1 ≤ k ≤ n_test; (15)

meaning that the initial guessed source position is located at the largest measured concentration. The initial guess is used both in the cost function and as initialization of the L-BFGS-B descent method.

The best neural network architecture has been obtained after some trials. Hence the chosen NN contains 2 fully-connected hidden layers of 50 neurons each, with the tanh activation function. The optimization is carried out using the L-BFGS-B algorithm. L_2 regularization is set at a low value of 10^{-12}. The machine learning approach is built using keras and tensorflow libraries under Python.

### 4.2 Comparison without noise

Experiment with 12 sensors: Simulations are performed without noise to determine how NN performs compared to the 3D-Var. Several very aberrant estimates returned by the NN (certainly due to non physical conditions in the training process) and bad estimates from 3D-Var (due to non convergence of the BFGS algorithm) are excluded: 16 out of 200, leading to the study of 184 estimates.

![](img/97952697a39a4e1dbafa6b892d1afef5_3_0.png)

Fig. 2. Example of source reconstruction : left, with a neural network; right, with a variational method.

Based on this experiment, the neural network is able to estimate a source with an RRSE in the same order of magnitude as with the standard 3D-Var, with RRSE mean and standard deviation of μ_RRSE^NN = 0.22 and σ_RRSE^NN = 0.12 and μ_RRSE^var = 0.31 and σ_RRSE^var = 0.21 for NN and 3D-Var respectively. Overall the Neural Network performs slightly better. This is probably due to the fact that the variational approach is a non convex optimization problem. Therefore, some local minima may lead to near optimal solutions with significant differences in the parameter values.

The RRSE obtained with the machine learning and variational approaches follow a log-normal law confirmed by the use of a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p-value = 0.39 > 0.05 and 0.44 > 0.05 respectively). The distributions of RRSE are shown in Fig. 3.

The machine learning approach is data-driven and thus does not contain any a priori information on the physical model whereas the variational approach relies on the knowledge of the physics and on a good guess as given by (15). We notice that the RRSE metric is sensitive to a slight error of estimated position. For example in the case of the 65^{th} sample of validation illustrated in Fig. 2, the neural network estimates well the magnitude and the dispersion, but a slight error of position leads to an RRSE^NN = 0.16 for the neural network against RRSE^var = 0.04 for the variational approach respectively.

Overall, without noise and using 12 sensors in total (10 regularly spaced in the domain and 2 that measure the boundary conditions), the machine learning approach performs slightly better than the variational approach with respect to the RRSE metric. The NN sometimes returns non-physical estimates such as negative sources or non-Gaussian sources. Fig. 4 shows that the performance of the two methods are similar.

Experiment with 50 sensors: Simulations with 50 sensors are carried out to observe a sensitivity of the estimations towards measurement density. Several non-physical estimates returned by the neural network or by the variational approach are excluded from the study: 9 out of 200, leading to the study of 191 estimates. Both methods return similar results as previously, with mean and standard deviation of μ_RRSE^NN = 0.13 and σ_RRSE^NN = 0.08 compared to μ_RRSE^var = 0.24 and σ_RRSE^var = 0.15 for NN and 3D-Var respectively.

This means that the error decreased by about a half for the NN and by one third for 3D-Var compared to using 12 sensors. Again the Neural Network performs better than 3D-Var. As with 12 sensors, RRSE distributions follow log-normal law with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p-value = 0.19 > 0.05 for the NN and p-value = 0.90 > 0.05 for 3D-Var). Overall, source reconstruction using 50 noiseless sensors (48 regularly spaced in the domain and 2 which measure the boundary conditions) achieves 30 to 50% improvements compared to the case with 12 noiseless sensors. Fig. 4 shows that the performance of the neural network is clearly better than the 3D-Var, its distribution being narrower and closer to 0 on RRSE axis. On the other hand, both methods improve with the use of 50 sensors instead of 12.

In this experiment, a gaussian noise ε_s^obs is applied to each sample s (1 ≤ s ≤ n_test or n_train) of data-set of concentration profiles given by the N_s sensors following the procedure described in 2.2.

Thus the gaussian noise of a set of measurements has a constant variance, which depends on the maximum of the noiseless-measured concentration profile. This hypothesis is reasonably conservative because the lower measured values of a concentration profile will be relatively noisier than its maximum value. The neural network is trained here with a noisy concentration dataset.

The initial guess error used in 3D-Var was modified from σ^b = 1 × 10^6 to σ^b = 5 × 10^4 to help to make the problem more convex.

Experiment with 12 sensors: Simulations are performed with noise in the same way as before. Several non-physical estimates returned by the NN or 3D-Var are excluded from the results: 9 out of 200, leading to a panel of 191 estimates. Both methods have similar performance in the presence of noise with mean and standard deviation of μ_RRSE^NN = 0.48 and σ_RRSE^NN = 0.26 using the NN and μ_RRSE^var = 0.52 and σ_RRSE^var = 0.23 using 3D-Var. Fig. 6 shows that the distribution of RRSE of both methods are close to each other.

Experiment with 50 sensors: With 50 sensors, 48 regularly spaced and two measured concentration values at boundaries, the results improve compared to the use of 12 sensors. This leads to mean and standard deviation metrics of μ_RRSE^NN = 0.40 and σ_RRSE^NN = 0.21 using NN and μ_RRSE^var = 0.45 and σ_RRSE^var = 0.22 using 3D-Var. Thus μ_RRSE^NN and μ_RRSE^var decreased by 17 and 13 % respectively with the use of 50 sensors instead of 12. Hence the Neural Network performs slightly better than the variational approach. However it can be seen in Fig. 5 that the distribution of errors are still fairly close between the two methods. Again, error distributions are proven to follow a log-normal law.
