
## Source Estimation: Variational Method Versus Machine Learning Applied to Urban Air Pollution\*

**R. Lopez-Ferber**\*, **S. Leirens**\*, **D. Georges**\*\*

\* Univ. Grenoble Alpes, CEA, Leti, F-38000 Grenoble, France
\*\* Univ. Grenoble Alpes, CNRS, Grenoble INP (Institute of Engineering and Management), GIPSA-lab, F-38000 Grenoble, France

\* This work is supported by The French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission

**Abstract:** Source detection is a research field of growing interest due to environmental concerns about air quality in populated areas. We developed a data-driven approach based on a multilayer perceptron neural network and inspired by previous works on road traffic estimation. We then compared it to a classical variational method (3D-Var) on a one-dimensional and stationary model of pollution concentration. We tested source reconstruction with datasets of synthetic measurements generated by 12 and 50 sensors with and without noise. Both methods appear to be reasonably robust to measurement noise and the neural network performs slightly better compared to the classical 3D-Var method.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Today public policies are more and more interested in topics related to environmental risk assessment and air quality monitoring, where the detection of pollutant sources plays a key role. Source reconstruction has been widely used in the domain of civilian and military nuclear. For example, the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty adopted in 1996 by the United Nations has encouraged researchers to develop methods of source detection using a global network of radionuclide sensor measurements to monitor compliance with this treaty. In this context, Pudykiewicz (1998) has applied the adjoint method to retrieve source parameters such as location, time and amount of release of radionuclides. Other works dealt with source rate estimation of the damaged Fukushima power plant during its accident in 2011. Winiarek et al. (2014) used a merged Bayesian and variational estimation method to estimate, with known uncertainty, this source rate.

On the other hand, there is a growing interest for the estimation of pollution sources in urban areas (e.g. Mons et al. (2017), Kumar et al. (2015)). In the context of a continuous point-wise source of pollution in urban environment, Kumar et al. (2015) used an adjoint method combined with a CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) model to retrieve source rate and source position in a stationary context. Ensemble methods are also cited in the literature. Mons et al. (2017) used an ensemble-based variational approach (EnVar) with an accurate but very computationally-expensive CFD model to estimate the diameter and position of a puddle of a volatile pollutant in a stationary and urban context.

To the best of our knowledge, machine learning has not been widely used for source detection so far. Recently, a machine learning approach has been applied to solve an inverse problem dealing with road traffic estimation (Georges (2020)). This approach has inspired the present work, where a feed-forward Neural Network (NN) is used with a few hidden layers. This data-driven method avoids any use of calculus of variations such as in variational methods and so avoids calling a dispersion model inside an optimisation framework. Moreover, NNs are not computationally expensive once the training is performed, compared to variational methods, especially those based on complex CFD models. NN training is indeed performed off-line, i.e. before the exploitation phase. This paper proposes a method to reconstruct a source of pollution in urban area in the case of a one-dimensional domain and stationary conditions to consider firstly pollutant dispersion in isolated streets. The machine learning approach is compared with a classical model-based variational approach (3D-Var) which is usually considered as a reference in terms of estimation accuracy.

The paper is now organized as follows: Section 2 presents the pollutant transport model and its finite-dimensional approximation. It also justifies the choices of wind velocity and diffusion coefficient used in the model. Section 3 details the proposed machine learning method and briefly presents the variational approach. Numerical simulation results are given in Section 4 whilst concluding remarks are discussed in Section 5.

**Outputs:** $\Theta \in \mathbb{R}^{n_s \times 3}$ is a tensor containing $n_s$ different vectors of source parameters, where each line of $\Theta$ is of the form $\Theta_k=[\theta_k(1),\theta_k(2),\theta_k(3)]$, $1 \leq k \leq n_s$. To avoid biases in the training process caused by the difference of magnitude of each source parameter, source parameters need to be scaled to the same order of magnitude. To perform this, we build a scaled tensor $\tilde{\Theta}$ where each parameter is scaled using the transformation,

$$\tilde{\theta}(j)=\frac{\theta(j)-\min(\Theta(j))}{\max(\Theta(j))}, \qquad 1\leq j\leq 3. \tag{7}$$

Thus the model returns an estimation of the adimensional parameters as an output. A rescaling is applied to the output estimate using $\min(\Theta(j))$ and $\max(\Theta(j))$ obtained during the training process.

**Training Data:** $n_{\mathrm{train}} \in \mathbb{N}$ simulations of pollutant dispersion with various wind speeds $c$ and source parameters $\theta$ have to be performed to generate a training and a validation set. To cover all the realistic domain of possible training samples, wind speeds and training source parameters are generated by using a Sobol pseudo-random sequence with low discrepancy property. Low-discrepancy sequences are known to ensure “a good filling of the domain” in the sense of the best approximate computation of function integrals for a given number of samples (see Niederreiter (1988)).

### 3.2 Variational approach

The variational method relies on the minimization of the least-squares error between output predictions based on a known model and measurement data. These kinds of methods are widely used and considered as a standard way to estimate parameters given some measurement especially with the use of an adjoint model (Georges (2019) ). Here, we propose to apply a variational method to a stationary problem where $u$ can be expressed as an explicit function of $\theta$ thanks to the finite-difference model, thus avoiding the explicit use of an adjoint model. The 3D-Var cost function, which consists of minimizing the output prediction error in the least-squares sense, is chosen. A regularisation term (see Tikhonov (1977)) depending on an initial guess, is added such that the entire cost function is given by,

$$\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(\theta-\theta^b)^T\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\theta-\theta^b)+\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{F}(\theta)-u^m)^T\mathbf{R}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(\theta)-u^m), \tag{8}$$

where $u^m$ denotes the vector of concentration measurements provided by the sensors and $\theta^b$ refers to an initial guess, with

$$\mathcal{F}(\theta)=\mathcal{H}\circ\mathcal{M}(\theta). \tag{9}$$

$\mathcal{F}$ denotes the forward operator which is a composition of the observation operator $\mathcal{H}$ with $\mathcal{M}$ given by (1), where $\mathcal{H}$, given by (5), maps the concentration returned by the model on the whole domain to the space of the measurements. Matrices $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{R}$ respectively denote the covariance matrices of the uncertain initial guess and measurement noise. We assume here that the noises are uncorrelated so that $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{R}$ are diagonal and with equal variances. $\sigma^b$ and $\sigma$ are the standard deviations associated with initial guess and observations.

The data-assimilation problem

$$\min_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta) \tag{10}$$

is infinite-dimensional. In this paper, the choice is made to solve a direct finite-dimensional version of this problem, based on the finite-dimensional approximation of model (1) given by (3), that is

$$\min_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_a(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(\theta-\theta^b)^T\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\theta-\theta^b)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{H}_a\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{B}_1q(\theta)+\mathbf{B}_2\mathbf{C})-u^m\right\|_{\mathbf{R}^{-1}}^2. \tag{11}$$

where $\mathbf{H}_a$ is the matrix obtained from the approximation of the $N_s$-sensor integral operator $\mathcal{H}$ given by (5), based on a trapezoidal method. The solution of this non-convex problem requires the computation of the gradient of $\mathcal{J}_a(\theta)$ w.r.t. $\theta$, that is

$$\nabla\mathcal{J}_a(\theta)=\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\theta-\theta^b)+\left(\mathbf{H}_a\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_1\frac{\partial q}{\partial\theta}(\theta)\right)^T\mathbf{R}^{-1}(\mathbf{H}_a u-u^m). \tag{12}$$

with $u$ obtained with (3). Here, the solution of optimization problem (11) is carried out by using a quasi-Newton descent method based on the L-BFGS-B algorithm (see Byrd et al. (1995)) and gradient (12).
