Besides practical problems associated with having a high-resolution camera inside or in an external loop parallel to a crystallization vessel, a range of challenges related to the analysis of captured images may hinder gaining usable information from them. Some of the common challenges in computer vision are illumination, rotation, and deformation, overlap or occlusion, background clutter, and interclass variation,^20 many of which are common in crystallization especially in images captured *in situ*.

Using imaging to monitor a crystallization process requires (1) high-quality images that provide information on the shape and texture of the particles and (2) robust image analysis to extract useful information from each image by translating the image to qualitative or quantitative data for subsequent decisions. The first is possible thanks to recent advancements in PAT development. There are different commercial or custom-built instruments used in the literature for *in situ* imaging in crystallization processes, such as EasyViewer, Blaze, and EZprobe.^11,12,21 As an example, the Mettler-Toledo particle view and measurement (PVM) probe, EasyViewer 100, used in this study is capable of capturing high-resolution images (1500 by 1500 pixels) as frequently as one image per second. There are also numerous studies by the crystallization community using a variety of image analysis methods to analyze slurry images. However, most of these are based on traditional methods. These typically start with a series of image modification and enhancement steps and use edge detection algorithms, such as the Sobel operator, to detect and characterize single objects in an image.^22–24 The main issue with this approach is that dense slurries (or dense images) with significant particle overlap cannot be parsed into individual objects. This is partially why many of the reports in the literature use either external loops and a flow cell or offline imaging to acquire analyzable images from a crystal slurry. Modern computer vision is mainly based on data-driven models and deep learning in the form of convolutional neural networks (CNNs).^{25,26} In short, in a typical deep learning-based image analysis method, a series of filters (convolutional layers) are applied to an input image (possibly after some image modification or enhancement steps), and the results are a series of feature maps that highlight certain characteristics of the input image. These feature maps then can be used by fully connected layers for different purposes such as classification. Finally, each filter and other layers’ parameters are optimized during the training step to achieve the best performance. Naturally, similar to other deep learning-based models, training these networks requires substantial amounts of data, especially for deeper networks which may possess millions of parameters.^26 Modern *in situ* imaging probes can capture thousands of images per hour of process time, providing a significant amount of data that can be used for training these networks. There are several well-known CNN architectures that are pretrained on standard image data sets and are capable of extracting different low- and high-level image features. These pretrained networks can be fine-tuned for specific applications using a limited amount of training data (i.e., transfer learning). Examples of using these novel deep learning-based models in the crystallization community are few. Recently, Chen et al.^27 used a regional-CNN (R-CNN) to detect and characterize the length of needle-like L-glutamic acid crystals. Gao et al.^12 used a mask R-CNN for crystal object segmentation to monitor the formation of two different polymorphs, α- and β-forms, of L-glutamic acid. Mane et al.^16 also used a mask R-CNN approach with RetinaNet as the supporting network to characterize the crystal size distribution in NaCl–ethanol–water antisolvent crystallization. These studies use deep learning-based approaches and provide quantitative data about the progress of a crystallization process. However, they either still deal with dilute slurries with a low solid density and limited object overlap in the captured images or rely on external loops to generate analyzable images. Neural networks as regression models are also used for shape characterization of plate-like crystals based on dual-projection images^28 or to extract information about solution and solid-phase concentrations from Raman and IR spectra in different crystallization processes.^29,30

In the present work, we develop a computer vision model, based on CNNs, that can be combined with high-throughput *in situ* imaging using a PVM probe for live monitoring of crystallization processes. The focus is on a system where *in situ* images contain highly overlapped objects and traditional approaches proved inadequate. The goal is specifically to develop a tool for automated process monitoring that can (1) examine *in situ* images taken using a PVM probe to monitor the state of the slurry, (2) detect and communicate the potential presence of undesired crystals in the slurry, and (3) estimate the location of the undesired object for the operator for further inspection and taking proper control action.
