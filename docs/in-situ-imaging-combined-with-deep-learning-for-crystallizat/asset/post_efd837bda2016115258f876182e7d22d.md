On the time scale of a typical crystallization process (hundreds of seconds), the longer response time of ResNet-50 does not, in fact, limit its application and still enables taking control-related actions. An appropriate control action in this system may be adjusting the acyl donor (PGME) flow rate into the vessel to reduce the PG concentration and dissolve its crystals. In other crystallization systems, an action might be a temperature or pH cycle to dissolve the undesired particles.

With the backbone CNNs trained for classification of the two crystal types for each system (pH swing or enzymatically made PG), we evaluate the performance of the image analysis model by calling it on a set of new images. These images were acquired in separate experiments and were not used in the training process of the CNNs (neither as training, validation, or test data). Also, note that the CNNs were trained using relevant crops from the in situ images for each class and not the raw image as a whole. As mentioned, we take a sliding window-like approach to analyze different regions in each image by passing them through the CNN. Regions with a high score in the Bad class, regions that are very likely to contain a PG crystal, are then flagged and reported. Using the sliding window approach to study an image is actually less common compared to other more recent methods in modern computer vision such as EdgeBox35 or Selective Search.34 However, in our case, the goal is to build a tool for identifying crystals with known characteristics. For example, in PG formation by an enzymatic reaction, the typical size and shape of the undesired PG particles are known by performing preliminary experiments. Figures 7 and 8 show the results of applying the image analysis model based on ResNet-18 to a series of example images from each system with two different cutoffs used for classification. In each image, the model successfully identifies the region which corresponds to particles with characteristics of PG. Results for the model based on ResNet-50 are very similar and are presented in Figures S.3 and S.4 in Supporting Information.

Besides the training parameters, classification threshold, and possible preprocessing steps to increase the quality of in situ images, another important hyperparameter in this model is the sliding window size. Size of the sliding window should be chosen considering the system under study and the characteristics of the specific crystals. For example, for PG crystals made by the pH swing method (Figure 3), crystals are relatively larger than those produced by the enzymatic route. Naturally, the sliding window grid used for that system is chosen such that it has larger candidate boxes (100–125 μm compared to 60–70 μm). A good rule of thumb for selecting the sliding window size range is to use the average size of the bounding boxes drawn during the training data preparation. Other methods such as building an image pyramid for detecting instances of an object with different scales are common in computer vision.37 However, considering that crystals’ characteristics are known in our case, we have limited our analysis to each image’s original scale.

In some cases (such as panel c in Figure 7), several overlapping or neighboring regions are reported, which is a result of the sliding window grid and the overlap threshold used for NMS. Sliding window size and the threshold used in NMS are hyperparameters that can be adjusted for specific cases to reduce the number of overlapping regions; nevertheless, the main goal of locating the PG particle is achieved. Another observation is that depending on the classification threshold used some regions might or might not be flagged. Classification threshold in Figures 7 and 8 refers to the class Bad score above which the model flags the corresponding region. The choice of the classification threshold may be any number in the range of 0.5–1 (below 0.5 being assigned to the other class), and it directly impacts the false-positive and false-negative rates. For example, in Figure 7 panel b, using the value of 0.8 leads to a false-positive in the center of the image. On the other hand, using the value of 0.9 leads to a false-negative in panel f. This threshold is an important hyperparameter in the model and should be chosen based on the specific process at hand and the degree of acceptable risk in it.

To further test the accuracy and limit of detection of the image analysis model, experiments were conducted with a gradually increasing number of PG crystals. In addition to the limit of detection, insights into model performance, image acquisition, and slurry behavior are gained by performing experiments where PG crystals are added to a standard CEX slurry in a highly controlled manner. Sufficiently large crystals (75–225 μm) were used such that a predictable number of crystals were added with each aliquot. As expected, during the experiment, the number of images flagged as containing PG increased as the PG solid concentration increased. Eventually, there are a sufficient number of PG crystals in the vessel such that they are detected in almost every image. A schematic representation of this experiment is shown in Figure 9.

As can be seen, two types of crystals (CEX and PG) are present in the vessel. Depending on the number density of PG crystals and the solution volume observable by the probe, zero to dozens of PG crystals may be detected through the probe window. Figure 10 shows the percentage of frames in which the image analysis model detected at least one PG crystal as a function of PG crystal mass in a CEX slurry. At each PG solid concentration, the percentage is calculated based on analyzing 100 frames, from which 50 frames were randomly sampled 30 times to calculate the standard errors. By analyzing the images captured from the slurry, the model recognizes the increase in the mass of the PG crystals and detects their presence (flagging the corresponding image) more frequently. However, as was seen before in Figure 7, the model also reports false-positives, images of pure CEX slurry flagged as containing PG, especially when a lower classification threshold is used (in this case, about 5% of images are falsely flagged for classification threshold S* = 0.8, and 0–1% for S* = 0.9).

The number of PG crystals, N_PG, can be estimated from the added mass and their average size (see Supporting Information). The solution volume visible to the PVM probe, V_samplingbox is estimated as the area of the probe window multiplied by a focal range (~1000 × 1000 × 100 μm³). Completely out-of-focus crystals cannot be analyzed with high accuracy and will not receive high scores from the classification network. The probability of a captured frame containing at least one PG particle is then equal to 1 minus the probability of it containing none (derivation of eq 2 is presented in Supporting Information).

Simulations confirm that the PG observations in the described system should mimic an exponential saturation curve (similar to Figure 10). However, the apparent similarity between the theoretical calculations and the experimental points should not be taken as a sign of a perfect image analysis model. In the analysis of each frame using the model, there might be PG crystals that are not detected by the computer, or there might be some false-positive cases. The significance of these simulations is that they confirm the overall shape of the experimental curve. Figure 11 also highlights the necessity of using premade PG crystals with relatively large mean size in the experiments such as that depicted in Figure 10.

It is also possible to train a second regression neural network for refining the reported bounding box coordinates similar to, for example, that of the fast R-CNN.36 However, taking that approach requires adjustments in previous parts of the algorithm presented here and does not add significant information as to whether or not phenylglycine has nucleated in the vessel.
