Besides practical problems associated with having a high-resolution camera inside or in an external loop parallel to a crystallization vessel, a range of challenges related to the analysis of captured images may hinder gaining usable information from them. Some of the common challenges in computer vision are illumination, rotation, and deformation, overlap or occlusion, background clutter, and interclass variation,<sup>20</sup> many of which are common in crystallization especially in images captured *in situ*.

Using imaging to monitor a crystallization process requires (1) high-quality images that provide information on the shape and texture of the particles and (2) robust image analysis to extract useful information from each image by translating the image to qualitative or quantitative data for subsequent decisions. The first is possible thanks to recent advancements in PAT development. There are different commercial or custom-built instruments used in the literature for *in situ* imaging in crystallization processes, such as EasyViewer, Blaze, and EZprobe.<sup>11,12,21</sup> As an example, the Mettler-Toledo particle view and measurement (PVM) probe, EasyViewer 100, used in this study is capable of capturing high-resolution images (1500 by 1500 pixels) as frequently as one image per second. There are also numerous studies by the crystallization community using a variety of image analysis methods to analyze slurry images. However, most of these are based on traditional methods. These typically start with a series of image modification and enhancement steps and use edge detection algorithms, such as the Sobel operator, to detect and characterize single objects in an image.<sup>22–24</sup> The main issue with this approach is that dense slurries (or dense images) with significant particle overlap cannot be parsed into individual objects. This is partially why many of the reports in the literature use either external loops and a flow cell or offline imaging to acquire analyzable images from a crystal slurry. Another issue with the traditional approaches is that the developed algorithms typically have several hyperparameters that may significantly impact the image analysis results. This is an issue especially when the goal is to quantify the crystal size distribution which might be impacted by the choice of hyperparameters (a simple example is the impact of dilation and erosion operations on the estimated size of a particle).

Modern computer vision is mainly based on data-driven models and deep learning in the form of convolutional neural networks (CNNs).<sup>25,26</sup> In short, in a typical deep learning-based image analysis method, a series of filters (convolutional layers) are applied to an input image (possibly after some image modification or enhancement steps), and the results are a series of feature maps that highlight certain characteristics of the input image. These feature maps then can be used by fully connected layers for different purposes such as classification. Finally, each filter and other layers’ parameters are optimized during the training step to achieve the best performance. Naturally, similar to other deep learning-based models, training these networks requires substantial amounts of data, especially for deeper networks which may possess millions of parameters.<sup>26</sup> Modern *in situ* imaging probes can capture thousands of images per hour of process time, providing a significant amount of data that can be used for training these networks. There are several well-known CNN architectures that are pretrained on standard image data sets and are capable of extracting different low- and high-level image features. These pretrained networks can be fine-tuned for specific applications using a limited amount of training data (i.e., transfer learning). Examples of using these novel deep learning-based models in the crystallization community are few. Recently, Chen et al.<sup>27</sup> used a regional-CNN (R-CNN) to detect and characterize the length of needle-like L-glutamic acid crystals. Gao et al.<sup>12</sup> used a mask R-CNN for crystal object segmentation to monitor the formation of two different polymorphs, α- and β-forms, of L-glutamic acid. Mane et al.<sup>16</sup> also used a mask R-CNN approach with RetinaNet as the supporting network to characterize the crystal size distribution in NaCl–ethanol–water antisolvent crystallization. These studies use deep learning-based approaches and provide quantitative data about the progress of a crystallization process. However, they either still deal with dilute slurries with a low solid density and limited object overlap in the captured images or rely on external loops to generate analyzable images. Neural networks as regression models are also used for shape characterization of plate-like crystals based on dual-projection images<sup>28</sup> or to extract information about solution and solid-phase concentrations from Raman and IR spectra in different crystallization processes.<sup>29,30</sup>

In the present work, we develop a computer vision model, based on CNNs, that can be combined with high-throughput *in situ* imaging using a PVM probe for live monitoring of crystallization processes. The focus is on a system where *in situ* images contain highly overlapped objects and traditional approaches proved inadequate. The goal is specifically to develop a tool for automated process monitoring that can (1) examine *in situ* images taken using a PVM probe to monitor the state of the slurry, (2) detect and communicate the potential presence of undesired crystals in the slurry, and (3) estimate the location of the undesired object for the operator for further inspection and taking proper control action. Particularly, we focus on the application of such a tool in continuous pharmaceutical manufacturing processes where robust monitoring of the slurry is essential for the production of a pure and high-quality product that meets set quality-attribute standards. We take the production of cephalexin (CEX, a beta-lactam antibiotic with a large annual production rate of >5,000 tons per year<sup>31</sup>) as an example where a byproduct of the reaction may crystallize and contaminate the product in the event of deviation from the process set point.<sup>32</sup>

## 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Crystallization Experiments

Industrial processes for cephalexin production typically use an enzymatic reaction between an activated acyl donor such as phenylglycine methyl ester (PGME) and the beta-lactam nucleus 7-aminodesacetoxycephalosporanic acid (7-ADCA).<sup>31,32</sup> The biocatalyst used in this process is typically penicillin G acylase (PGA), which catalyzes the primary reaction of producing cephalexin as well as two hydrolysis side reactions shown in eq 1. In the reactive crystallization process, conditions are set such that cephalexin is crystallized in the reactor to produce CEX crystals, which protects the cephalexin from biocatalytic hydrolysis.<sup>32</sup> However, the byproduct phenylglycine (PG) has a low solubility in water (~30–35 mM at 25 °C and pH = 5.5–7.5<sup>33</sup>) and may crystallize and contaminate the product if generated in excess.

```
PGME + 7-ADCA
  → MeOH + CEX
  → CEX·H₂O (crystal)

PGME + H2O → MeOH + PG → PG (crystal)

CEX + H2O → 7-ADCA + PG → PG (crystal)    (1)
```

For the production of CEX crystals, pH manipulations were used to generate the required supersaturation (solubility of cephalexin in water as a function of pH value is presented in Figure S1 in Supporting Information). A typical crystallization experiment started with dissolving a sufficient amount of CEX solids (RIA, Hanover, NJ) in 200 mL of deionized water at pH 7.5 to prepare a saturated solution. After saturation, the resulting solution was filtered to remove any undissolved solids. The solution was then transferred to a jacketed crystallization vessel that maintained a temperature of 25 °C and was mixed using a pitched blade stainless-steel impeller (with four 45° blades and 1 cm off-bottom clearance) at 250 rpm. Crystallization was started by adding 0.05 g of ground seed crystals and adding 2 M HCl to acidify the solution and generate supersaturation. The pH value was lowered from the initial 7.5 to 5.5 over a period of 2–3 h using a Mettler-Toledo SP-50 dosing unit. The slurry was mixed for 3–5 h after the pH manipulation and until no significant change in the solution turbidity (measured using the EasyViewer probe) was detected. PG crystals were produced in two sets of experiments. In the first, PG was crystallized using the same pH swing method described above; initial and final pH values ranged from an initial pH of 9 to a final pH of 5 (PG solubility in water as a function of pH is provided in Figure S1 in Supporting Information). The crystals were filtered, dried at room temperature, and later sieved to remove large agglomerates (particles >300 μm). In the second set, PG was formed under conditions mimicking that of the enzymatic synthesis. The PGA biocatalyst (obtained from Centrient, Netherlands) at a concentration of about 2 μM was added to a CEX slurry saturated with PG at pH = 7. The solution was then given sufficient time for the enzyme to hydrolyze the cephalexin and produce sufficient PG supersaturation for PG crystallization to occur (5–8 h). PG particles formed by the first method (pH swing) had a larger mean size which helps to quantify their number density in a solution more accurately. PG particles formed by the second method (enzymatically) had a smaller mean size but more accurately represent the shape and characteristics of the byproduct particles formed during the enzymatic synthesis of cephalexin.
