Alternatively, the data in Figure 12 can be analyzed from the viewpoint of detection time. In some applications, it might be required to detect the presence of the undesired particle, in this case, PG, in the shortest time possible. Note that detecting even a single PG particle is sufficient for this purpose. The detection time depends on the time it takes to analyze each frame and the amount of the impurity. Figure 12b shows the probability of detecting the PG particles’ presence in the system as a function of time observing the slurry, at three different PG solid concentrations. Observation time corresponds to the time that is required to capture images and analyzing them. At the low solid density, 0.8 mg/mL, a short observation time (e.g., only one frame taken and processed) might not lead to detecting the PG presence, and the slurry needs to be observed for a longer time using the model. It is only after 60 s that it is certain that the model can detect the PG presence. Naturally, this time significantly shortens at higher PG concentration. As mentioned, this analysis directly depends on the number of undesired particles in the system. For cases with a small mean size, even a small mass leads to immediate detection of the presence of PG by analyzing only a few frames. As a final note, during a crystallization process, undesired crystals (be a byproduct as in the case of PG in CEX crystallization or an undesired polymorph as in the case of, e.g., piroxicam crystallization¹) may form if the process deviates from its appropriate setpoint and trajectory. Detecting this

deviation as soon as possible is important, so proper control actions (such as adjustment of pumps or a temperature cycle) can be taken. Unfortunately, in the early stages of nucleation and growth, particle sizes are likely to be relatively small. Microscope resolution therefore puts a limit on the applicability of the image analysis for process monitoring. Clearly, using direct imaging as a robust PAT for process fault detection requires sufficiently large crystals such that they can be visually detected. In some specific cases, nucleation and growth of the undesired particles may lead to some changes in the slurry, such as a sudden change in solution turbidity, but whether that can be used as a robust monitoring tool is probably highly case-dependent.

# 4. CONCLUSIONS

An image analysis model, based on deep learning and CNNs, was developed for the classification of complex in situ images from a CEX crystal slurry. Provided sufficient training data, the model can detect the presence of an undesired crystal (e.g., a byproduct, which in this case was phenylglycine, or an undesired polymorph) in the slurry by processing different regions of the raw in situ microscope images. The two networks used as the classification backbone, ResNet-18 and ResNet-50, showed high classification accuracy and were capable of distinguishing between different crystal types. However, considering the very distinct visual features of CEX and PG, other well-known deep, or even shallow, CNNs may also be capable of performing the classification task. The proposed approach can be extended to other crystallization systems where the detection of undesired crystals is critical to producing an acceptable product. Typical challenges of computer vision, such as overlapping objects which is found in high suspension density slurries, can complicate this task. Note that the analysis of neat images from a processed slurry (such as dilution) in a highly controlled setup remains the best and most robust method for observing and measuring crystals in a process. However, the approach proposed here enables the use of PVM probes and in situ imaging as a practical and direct approach to monitoring a crystal slurry in a large-scale process in real-time.
