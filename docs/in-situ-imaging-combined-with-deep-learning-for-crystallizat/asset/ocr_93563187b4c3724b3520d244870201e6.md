For example, for PG crystals made by the pH swing method (Figure 3), crystals are relatively larger than those produced by the enzymatic route. Naturally, the sliding window grid used for that system is chosen such that it has larger candidate boxes (100–125 μm compared to 60–70 μm). A good rule of thumb for selecting the sliding window size range is to use the average size of the bounding boxes drawn during the training data preparation. Other methods such as building an image pyramid for detecting instances of an object with different scales are common in computer vision.37 However, considering that crystals’ characteristics are known in our case, we have limited our analysis to each image’s original scale.

In some cases (such as panel c in Figure 7), several overlapping or neighboring regions are reported, which is a result of the sliding window grid and the overlap threshold used for NMS. Sliding window size and the threshold used in NMS are hyperparameters that can be adjusted for specific cases to reduce the number of overlapping regions; nevertheless, the main goal of locating the PG particle is achieved. Another observation is that depending on the classification threshold used some regions might or might not be flagged. Classification threshold in Figures 7 and 8 refers to the class Bad score above which the model flags the corresponding region. The choice of the classification threshold may be any number in the range of 0.5–1 (below 0.5 being assigned to the other class), and it directly impacts the false-positive and false-negative rates. For example, in Figure 7 panel b, using the value of 0.8 leads to a false-positive in the center of the image. On the other hand, using the value of 0.9 leads to a false-negative in panel f. This threshold is an important hyperparameter in the model and should be chosen based on the specific process at hand and the degree of acceptable risk in it.

To further test the accuracy and limit of detection of the image analysis model, experiments were conducted with a gradually increasing number of PG crystals. In addition to the limit of detection, insights into model performance, image acquisition, and slurry behavior are gained by performing experiments where PG crystals are added to a standard CEX slurry in a highly controlled manner. Sufficiently large crystals (75–225 μm) were used such that a predictable number of crystals were added with each aliquot. As expected, during the experiment, the number of images flagged as containing PG increased as the PG solid concentration increased.
