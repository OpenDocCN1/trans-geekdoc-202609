Once the filter is defined, it is a simple matter to generate the filtered vectors. This can be done in a parallel fashion using the same set of Chebyshev vectors:

```
$$\eta_l \equiv F(H|E_l)q_0 = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} f_k(E_l)q_k \quad l=1, 2, \dots, L; \quad [75]$$
```

Where $E_l$ are chosen in the spectral window of interest ($[E_{lower}, E_{upper}]$) and $\mathbf{q}_k$ are generated recursively using Eq. [56] or directly via the damped Chebyshev recursion (Eq. [59]). With a sufficiently large $L$, these vectors span the local eigenspace and the eigenpairs in the energy range can be obtained by solving the following small generalized eigenproblem:

```
$HB = SBE \quad [76]$
```

where the Hamiltonian and overlap matrices are given in terms of the filtered vectors:

```
$H_{lm} = \mathbf{\eta}_l^T H \mathbf{\eta}_m \quad \quad S_{lm} = \mathbf{\eta}_l^T \mathbf{\eta}_m \quad [77]$
```

We note that the spectral method can be regarded as a special case of FD with $L=1$, for the uncertainty principle dictates that the spectral resolution is inversely proportional to the propagation length. In FD, the spectral resolution is enhanced beyond the uncertainty principle, because of the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian in the subspace by multiple filtered vectors.

In practice, the filtering energies $(E_i)$ are often chosen as the Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature points in the range $[E_{lower}, E_{upper}]$ for a particular K. Both K and L are often treated as convergence parameters. Generally, FD works well for well-separated eigenvalues and those near the spectral extrema. The better convergence of the extremal eigenvalues can be readily understood because the cosine mapping affords more interpolation points near both ends of the spectrum (see Figure 4). As expected, the resolution of closely spaced eigenvalues requires further recursion. Mandelstam and Taylor proposed the following estimate of the number of recursion steps:$^{154}$

```
$K \sim 2\rho\Delta H \quad [78]$
```

where $\rho$ is the local density of states and $\Delta H$ is the spectral range.

The Lanczos algorithm, the dependence of the convergence rate on the spectral range is one of great importance. Thus, one often places a premium on minimizing $\Delta H$ in FD and other recursive diagonalization. Other caveats of FD include eigenvectors that have small overlaps with the initial vector and identifying spurious eigenvalues because of the linear dependence of the filtered vectors. Hence, careful convergence tests are essential.

The scaling laws of FD are dominated by the recursion because the diagonalization of small matrices is relatively inexpensive. However, because one must store multiple filtered vectors along the recursion, FD could be a burden for large systems.

### 317 Low-Storage Filter-Diagonalization

The FD method can be further extended to a low-storage version, often denoted as LSDF. We Neuhauser first showed that the matrix elements in Eq. [77] can be obtained directly from the time autocorrelation function, without explicitly resorting to the filtered vectors.$^{147}$ In doing so, the construction and storage of the filtered vectors can be avoided, which is advantageous when only eigenvalues are of interest. Mandelshahm and Taylor extended the idea to the delta filter based on the Chebyshev propagation, and their derived analytical equations for calculating the S and H matrices from the correlation function.$^{154,136}$ Chen and Guo further developed several efficient schemes to construct the matrix elements for general filters.$^{158,162}$

To illustrate for the delta filter (Eq. [62]), we note that the matrix elements can be written as

```
$S_{ij} = \sum_{k=0}^K \sum_{k'=0}^K (2-\delta_k)(2-\delta_{k'}) \cos\theta_j \cos\theta_{i'} q_k^T q_{k'} \quad [79]$
```

```
$H_{ij} = \sum_{k=0}^K \sum_{k'=0}^K (2-\delta_k)(2-\delta_{k'}) \cos\theta_j \cos\theta_{i'} q_k^T H q_{k'} \quad [80]$
```

where $E_i = \cos\theta_i$, an important observation is that the last terms in Eqs. [79] and [80] are related to the autocorrelation function ($C_k$):

```
$q_k^T q_{k'} = (C_{k+k'} + C_{k-k'})/2 \quad [81]$
```

```
$q_k^T H q_{k'} = (C_{k+k'+1} + C_{k-k'+1} + C_{k-k'-1})/4 \quad [82]$
```

where the double sums of Eq. [69] is used. In addition, the double sums in Eqs. [79] and [80] can be reduced analytically to a single sum,$^{154}$ or evaluated via FFT.$^{162}$ The calculation of the matrix elements can be carried out in quadruple precision to minimize errors. The final formulas for computing these matrix elements can be found in the original publications.$^{154}$ One can also compute any frequency from its overlap with the initial vector:

```
$b_n = \sum_{k=0}^K (2-\delta_k) \cos\theta_l q_k \quad [83]$
```

```
$\zeta_n = \sum_{k=0}^K (2-\delta_k) \cos\theta_l B_l \quad [84]$
```

The convergence of the eigenvalues obtained can be checked by varying L and K. For practical purposes, L is typically in the range of 100. The error of a particular eigenvalue can be estimated from $\|(H - E_n)b_n\|$, where $\|H r_n\|$ can also be expressed in terms of the autocorrelation function.

The LSDF method essentially the same memory requirement as the Lanczos algorithm. Likewise, the CPU scaling is linear and dominated by the recursion (because the numerical costs for solving the problem are usually much smaller). In addition, the global nature of the propagator allows one to determine eigenvalues in multiple spectral windows, where the grid $E_l (l=1,2,\dots,L)$ has to be defined a priori in the original FD. Of course, a much longer recursion might be needed for spectral windows in the interior of the spectrum.

Most LSFD applications to date are based on the autocorrelation function obtained by propagating a single vector. However, it has been pointed out that the use of cross-correlation functions may be beneficial in determining eigenvalues with LSFD, particularly for cases with high levels of degeneracy or deteriorating signals. This can be achieved by propagating multiple initial vectors and computing the cross-correlation matrix at every step, similar to the block-Lanczos algorithm discussed above. The Hamiltonian and overlap matrices in Eq. [76] can be obtained in a similar fashion, albeit with a much larger dimension because of the block nature of the correlation functions. The benefits of cross-correlation functions are obvious because they have higher information content than the autocorrelation function.

We note that the Chebyshev recursion-based LSFD can be used to extract frequencies from a signal in the following form:

```
C_k = \sum_n d_n e^{-iktE_n} \quad [85]$
```

by assuming the signal corresponds, implicitly, to a quantum system in which the explicit form of the Hamiltonian is of no importance. Specifically, the signal in Eq. [85] can be considered as a correlation function over a discrete time window with a step of τ. This strategy is successful for classic and semi-classical dynamics, and for NMR signal processing. In addition, several signal processing methods related to LSFD have been proposed by various authors. Interested readers are referred to an excellent review by Mandelstham.
