## Free Volume, Structural Order, and Entropy

The macroscopic properties of a material are related intimately to interactions between particles, be they atoms, molecules, or colloids. For example, the pressure of low-density vapor can be accurately described by virial expansion, whereas its transport coefficients can be estimated from kinetic theory.

However, the conceptual picture for liquids, glasses and other forms of soft matter is considerably more complex. The average structures in these materials are too dense (i.e., too correlated), on one hand, to be amenable to a "dilute gas" description, and at the same time too disordered to be suitable for the unit-cell framework of crystals. As a result, progress in the theory of liquids and glasses (and of the glass transition itself) has been helped by enough gases. Nonetheless, major advances have occurred in the past 20 years, which have been fueled by the development of (1) efficient algorithms for simulating the liquid state, and (2) faster computers to accelerate the exploration of these systems across broader length and time scales.

In a sense, many advances in understanding the liquid and glassy states may be directly attributed to the ability of computer simulations to provide precise information on the "trinity" of relevant material properties (structure, thermodynamics, and dynamics) for a variety of model systems. This new information is allowing for stringent tests of the most pervasive ideas about the microscopic origins of the characteristic behaviors of the liquid and glassy states. This chapter focuses on three central concepts (structural ordering, free volume, and entropy) that continue to gain traction in this area largely because they offer promising, albeit still empirical, means for connecting static or thermodynamic properties to dynamics. For each concept, basic definitions are presented and some relevant computational strategies are employed. We discuss the rationale for their continued use, but we also mention some of their practical shortcomings. Finally, examples are given where the computational application of these concepts has led to new physical insights. This chapter is not meant to serve as a general review of the phenomenology of the liquid and glassy states because several excellent reviews and papers on this topic are already available. Rather, we provide a compact and useful starting point for both novice graduate students and experts alike who are interested in contributing to an improved understanding of this important class of material systems.
