This is similar to the parameter introduced by Chao and Hardwick,$50}$ only rescued in such a way that the average value of $q$ varies between 0 (for completely uncorrelated systems) and 1 (for a perfect tetrahedral network). Note that this metric provides local, when averaged over all particles, global measures of the tetrahedral order. The so-called tetrahedrality parameter$^{51}$ of Naberukhin, Voloshin and Medvedev is another metric that can be used to detect/quantify tetrahedral order.

The competition between directional attractions and short-range repulsions in tetrahedral liquids, such as water or silica, give rise to highly nontrivial physical and structural properties. Examples include expansion upon cooling at constant pressure$^{51,52}$ and increased mobility upon isothermal compression at sufficiently low temperatures$^{53-56}$ Given the prominence of tetrahedral fluids in scientific phenomena and industrial processes, a deeper understanding of the relationship between macroscopic thermodynamic and kinetic properties and the molecular-organization of these fluids has long been desired.

In a recent study, Errington and Debenedetti$^{29}$ investigated the connection between measures of structural order and macroscopic properties of stable, super-cooled, and stretched liquid water using the extended simple point charge (SPC/E) model$^{57}$ They could relate well-known macroscopically observed thermodynamic and kinetic anomalies to microscopic structural anomalies$^{29}$ The use of an order map may gain fruitful.
