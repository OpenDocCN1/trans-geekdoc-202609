In what follows, we illustrate the importance of this point by considering the effect of interfacial symmetry on static and kinetic friction.

Commensurate surfaces have a tendency to exhibit much larger static friction than incommensurate surfaces. This tendency can be understood through the following example. Imagine two egg cartons on top of one another. If the cartons are perfectly aligned, the peaks of one will sit in a valley on the other, and a large force will be required to simultaneously lift all the peaks out of the valleys to initiate motion. By rotation, on the other hand, if the cartons are brought out of registry, e.g., only a few of the peaks and valleys will be aligned and less energy will be required to initiate motion. This scenario happens if the cartons are separated by eggs, which introduce new peaks and valleys with the same periodicity as the underlying carton. The rationale behind this simple example applies to surfaces that are composed of atoms and may be separated by other atoms and molecules. In this case, the periodicity of the surface atoms defines the peaks and valleys, and any confined atoms and molecules adapt to this periodicity.

It may come as a surprise to some that two commensurate surfaces stand finite forces if they are separated by a fluid.${51}$ But one has to keep in mind that breaking translational invariance automatically avoids the potential of mean force $F$. From the symmetry breaking, commensurate walls can be pinned even by an ideal gas embedded between them.${32}$ The reason is that $F$ scales linearly with the area of contact. In the thermodynamic limit, the energy barrier for the slider to move by one lattice constant becomes infinitely high so that the motion cannot be thermally activated, and static friction becomes finite. No such agreement applies when the surfaces do not share a common period.

The kinetic friction $F_k$ is also affected by symmetry. If two surfaces are separated by one atomic layer only, $F_k$ may actually be reduced because of commensuration, although static friction is increased.${25}$ The strikingly different behavior for commensurate and incommensurate surfaces is demonstrated in Figure 6.

Therefore, whenever we introduce symmetries into our systems, we risk observing behavior that is inconsistent with that observed when these symmetries are absent. Because opposing surfaces are almost always incommensurate unless they are prepared specifically, it will be important to avoid symmetries in simulations as much as possible. Unfortunately, it will be difficult to make two surfaces commensurate in simulations, particularly when the interface is composed of two identical crystalline surfaces.
