# General Density Functional Theory

The basic variable in density functional theory (DFT)$^{22}$ is the electron density $n(\vec{r})$. In the usual implementation of DFT, the density is calculated from the occupied single-particle wave functions $\psi_i(\vec{r})$ of an auxiliary system of noninteracting electrons

$n(\vec{r}) = \sum_i \theta(\varepsilon_F - E_i)|\psi_i(\vec{r})|^2$ [22]$$

and the orbitals $\psi_i(\vec{r})$ are obtained by solving the Kohn–Sham equations$^{23}$

$(-\frac{\nabla^2}{2} + V_{KS}(\vec{r}))\psi_i(\vec{r}) = \varepsilon_i\psi_i(\vec{r})$ [23]$$

written in atomic units. The symbol $\theta$ in Eq. [22] is the step function, which ensures that all orbitals with energies $\varepsilon_i$ below the Fermi level $\varepsilon_F$ are occupied

and all orbitals with energies above $\varepsilon_F$ are empty. The Fermi level is determined by the normalization condition

$$\int n(\vec{r})d^3r = N \quad [24]$$

where $N$ is the number of electrons. The effective Kohn–Sham potential $V_{ks}(\vec{r})$ appearing in Eq. [23] is the sum of several distinct contributions:

$$\V_{ks}(\vec{r}) = V_{ext}(\vec{r}) + V_{H}(\vec{r}) + V_{sc}(\vec{r}) \quad [25]$$

The external potential $V_{ext}(\vec{r})$ contains the nuclear or ionic contributions and possible external field contributions. The Hartree level $V_H(\vec{r})$ is the classic electrostatic potential of the electron

$$\V_{H}(\vec{r}) = - \int \frac{n(\vec{r'})}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}d^3\vec{r'} \quad [26]$$$

and $V_{sc}(\vec{r})$ is the exchange-correlation potential. Exchange between the electrons originates from the antisymmetric character of the many-electron wave function of a identical fermionic particles: Two electrons cannot occupy the same single-particle state (characterized by orbital and spin quantum numbers) simultaneously. This effect has the consequence of building a hole, called the Fermi hole, around an electron that excludes the presence of other electrons of the same spin orientation ($\uparrow$ or $\downarrow$, in the usual notation for the $z$ component). Additionally, there are Coulombic correlations between the instantaneous positions of the electrons because these are charged particles that repel each other. Because of this repulsion, two electrons cannot approach one another too closely, independent of their spin orientation. The combined effect of the Fermi and Coulomb correlations can be described as an exchange–correlation hole built around each electron. In practice $V_{sc}(\vec{r})$ is calculated, using its definition in DFT, as the functional derivative of an exchange–correlation energy functional $E_{sc}[n]$,

$$\V_{sc}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\delta E_{sc}[n]}{\delta n(\vec{r})} \quad [27]$$$

The local density approximation (LDA)$^{24}$ is often used to calculate $E_{sc}[n]$ and $V_{sc}(\vec{r})$. The LDA uses as input the exchange–correlation energy of an electron gas of constant density. In a homogeneous system the exchange energy per particle is known exactly and it has the expression

$$\e_x(n_0) = - \frac{3}{4}(\frac{3}{\pi})^{1/3}n_0^{1/3} \quad [28]$$$

$n_0$ is the constant density of the system. The exchange energy of an inhomogeneous system with density $n(\vec{r})$ is then approximated by assuming that Eq. [28] is valid; that is,

$E_x[n] = -\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{3}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\int n(\vec{r})^{4/3}d^3r \quad [29]$$

Performing the functional derivative of Eq. [29] (see Eq. [27]) leads to

$V_x^{LDA}(\vec{r}) = -\left(\frac{3}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}n(\vec{r})^{1/3} \quad [30]$$$

An expression for the correlation energy per particle $e_c(n_0)$ of a homogeneous electron gas does not exist, but good approximations to that nevertheless exist.$^{24}$ Also, nearly exact correlation energies have been obtained numerically for different densities.$^{25}$ and the results have been parametrized as useful functions $e_c(n).^{26}$ The corresponding LDA correlation

$V^{LDA}(\vec{r}) = \left.\frac{d[ne_c(n)]}{dn}\right]_{n=n(\vec{r})} \quad [31]$$$

is immediately obtained. In summary, in the LDA, $V^{LDA}(\vec{r})$ at point in space is assumed to be equal to the exchange-correlation potential in a homogeneous electron gas with "constant" density $n=n(\vec{r})$, precisely equal to the local density $n(\vec{r})$ at that point. The LDA works in practice better than expected, and this success is rooted in the fulfillment of several formal properties of the exchange $E_{sc}[n]$ and in subtle cancellations. Substantial improvements have been obtained with the generalized gradient approximations (GGAs)

$$E_{xc}^{GGA}[n] = \int E_{xc}^{GGA}(n(\vec{r}),\nabla n(\vec{r}))d^3r \quad [32]$$$

include $\nabla n(\vec{r})$, or even higher order gradients of the electron density, in the exchange-correlation energy functional.$^{27-29}$

term. Von Barth and Hedin formulated DFT for spin-polarized cases. The basic variable in this case is the $2 \times 2$ spin-density matrix $\rho_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r})$, defined as

$$\rho_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r}) = \sum_i \theta(\varepsilon_F - \varepsilon_i) \Psi_{i^*\alpha}(\vec{r})\Psi_{i\beta}(\vec{r}) \quad [33]$$$

where the notation $\chi$ includes both the position $\vec{r}$ and the spin, $\alpha = +1/2$ or $\alpha = -1/2$. The $2 \times 2$ matrix $\rho_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r})$ is hermitian and defined at point $\vec{r}$. The spin density of this matrix

$$n(\vec{r}) = \text{Tr}\rho_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r}) = n_+(\vec{r}) + n_-(\vec{r}) \quad [34]$$$

$n-,(\vec{r}) = \rho++(\vec{r}) = \rho_-(\vec{r})$ are the diagonal terms. To quantify the magnetic effects, one can define the magnetization density $\vec{m}(\vec{r})$ such that

$$\rho_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{2}n(\vec{r})I + \frac{1}{2}\vec{m}(\vec{r}) \cdot \sigma \quad [35]$$$

where $I$ is the $2 \times 2$ unit matrix and $\sigma = (\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z)$, with $\sigma_x, \sigma_y$, and $\sigma_z$ define the $2 \times 2$ Pauli spinors. $n(\vec{r})$ and $\vec{m}(\vec{r})$ form an alternative representation of $\rho_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r})$. The one-particle representation is now based on two-component spinors (when adding spin to the spatial orbitals, the two components, $+$ and $-$ of the spinor correspond to the two projections of the spin, up $\uparrow$ and down $\downarrow$, along a quantization axis)

$$\Psi(\vec{r}) = \begin{bmatrix} \Psi_+(\vec{r}) \\ \Psi_-(\vec{r}) \end{bmatrix} \quad [36]$$

The purpose of spin-polarized DFT is to describe the system (molecule, cluster, ...) with an auxiliary noninteracting system of one-particle spinors $\{\Psi_1, \dots, \Psi_N\}$. The ground state density matrix of this noninteracting system

$$\rho_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{r}) = \sum_i \theta(\varepsilon_F - \varepsilon_i) \Psi_{i^*\alpha}(\vec{r})\Psi_{i\beta}(\vec{r}) \quad [37]$$$

should be equal to that of the interacting system. In terms of the one-particle spin-orbitals

$$n(\vec{r}) = \sum_i \theta(\varepsilon_F - \varepsilon_i) \Psi_i^*(\vec{r})\Psi_i(\vec{r}) \quad [38]$$$

$$\vec{m}(\vec{r}) = \sum_i \theta(\varepsilon_F - \varepsilon_i) \Psi_i^*(\vec{r})\sigma\Psi_i(\vec{r}) \quad [39]$$$
