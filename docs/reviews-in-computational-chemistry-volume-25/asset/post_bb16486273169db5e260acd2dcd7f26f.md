## APPLICATIONS

### Bound States and Spectroscopy

The Lanczos algorithm has traditionally been an efficient way to extract the lowest few eigenvalues of a sparse matrix. However, many researchers have come to realize that it is equally powerful for mapping out large portions of, or even entire, bound-state spectra of polyatomic molecules. The equally powerful Chebyshev FD method, particularly the low-storage version, has also been used successfully for extracting bound-state spectra. Typical recursive diagonalization starts with an arbitrary initial vector, which is often chosen to minimize the possibility of missing certain eigenfunctions. The recursion, which is the most numerically costly part of the calculation, generates the correlation function or the tridiagonal matrix. The final step involves the diagonalization of a small generalized problem or a tridiagonal matrix. Some sorting and convergence testing might be needed to remove spurious eigenvalues. Several approaches exist, as discussed.

The recursive solution of the ro-vibrational Schrödinger equation not only gives the eigenfunctions that form the spectrum but also additional information about the intermolecular coupling and dynamics. A significant question is how the energy injected in a particular vibrational mode is dispersed in a polyatomic molecule.⁵⁷,²⁸³⁻²⁸⁶ Experimentally, the intramolecular vibrational energy redistribution (IVR) can be investigated by overtone spectroscopy and stimulated emission pumping. A theoretical understanding of such problems often requires knowing the vibrational energy spectrum in regions with very high spectral density and the corresponding spectral intensities. Such problems are ideally suited for the methods described in this review. In particular, both the position and the intensities of the spectral lines can be obtained by efficient recursive methods such as RGGM and SLP without resorting to the explicit calculations of the eigenvectors.¹108-115}

An important problem in molecular spectroscopy is the assignment of vibrational states, assuming the system is in the regular regime, which is trivial to do if the eigenvectors are known. However, it has been shown that even without the eigenvectors, important information about the eigenvectors can be extracted to an unambiguous assignment of quantum numbers. Two strategies have been proposed and demonstrated to accomplish this assignment. The first is based on a perturbative scheme to compute the expectation values of the vibrational operator for a particular mode,$¹²¹¹ as described above. Such an operator could be chosen as the one-dimensional vibrational Hamiltonian ((1/2μ)δ^2/∂R^2+(k/2)(R-R_0)^2) or the squared displacement from equilibrium (R-R_0)^2, which is amenable to both the Lanczos algorithm and the Chebyshev method LSFD, which is especially efficient for assigning normal quantum number near the minimum. The second strategy takes advantage of the SLP by computing the overlaps of the eigenvectors with a set of prescribed target functions. For example, the target functions could be chosen to be Gaussian functions at large R values, which have large overlaps with highly excited local-mode states.¹103,117,198} This latter approach is particularly effective in "fishing out" eigenvectors with well-defined characteristics.

### Reaction Dynamics

The dynamics of a scattering event can be described by the causal operator.${²⁸⁷,²⁸⁸}

$$G^+ = \frac{1}{EI-H+iE} \quad [98]$$

$\epsilon$ is an infinitesimally small number that can be interpreted as the absorbing boundary condition.¹¹³⁹,²⁸⁹} In particular, the S-matrix element for a transition from the initial (i) to final (f) state at energy $E$ is given by:

$$S_{f\to i}(E) = \frac{i}{2\pi a_i(E)a_f^*(E)}(\chi_f|G^+|\chi_i) \quad [99]$$

where $a_i$ and $a_f$ are the energy amplitudes of the initial and final wave packets, respectively. The S-matrix elements can be computed using the time-dependent wave packet theory that expands the Green operator in terms of the time propagator. As discussed above, the Chebyshev propagator bears many similarities with the time propagator, and a similar expansion (Eq. [70]) can be used.¹²⁵,¹²⁶ The Chebyshev propagation is superior to time propagation because the action of the propagator on a vector can be calculated exactly with no approximation and a real algorithm is possible when the damped Chebyshev recursion¹⁴⁰,¹⁴¹ is used. Studies of reaction dynamics using the Chebyshev propagator have been advocated by several authors.

Unlike the bound-state calculations where a random initial vector is commonly used, calculating the S-matrix element in Eq. [99] involves a well-defined initial vector. Such an initial vector consists of a translational wave packet and an eigenstate for the internal degrees of freedom, placed in the dissociation asymptote. Because each recursion produces a single column of the S-matrix, many recursions might be needed to obtain the entire matrix.
