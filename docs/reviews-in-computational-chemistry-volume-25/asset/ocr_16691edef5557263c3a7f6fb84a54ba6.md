Eq [19] ensures that the thermodynamic distribution of Eq. [20] is the suggestion (long-time) limit of the Markov chain generated by Eq. [18]. It does not specify the transition rates unlike, however. Let us write them in the following way:

```
$W(x \rightarrow x') = W_0(x \rightarrow x')W_T(x \rightarrow x')\quad [22]$
```

where $W_0$ is the probability suggesting $x'$ as the next state, to suggest a certain MC move, and $W_T$ is the thermal acceptance probability chosen to fulfill Eq. [19]. This requires the suggestion probabilities to be reversible

```
$W_0(x \rightarrow x') = W_0(x' \rightarrow x)\quad [23]$
```

Only a few choices for $W_T$ exist in the literature, i.e., Metropolis rates, Glauber rates, or heat-bath,$^{1}$ that is an unlimited variety of choices for $W_0$, and this is the great advantage of the MC method. Some choices for $W_0$ result in physically reasonable dynamics (in general, local moves selecting a monomer and moving it to a randomly chosen direction by a small distance), but all reversible choices lead to the correct equilibrium distribution of states. One can therefore invent MC moves targeted at overcoming the main physical barriers leading to slow equilibration of a model system.

The two main sources for slow relaxation in polymers are entanglement effects and the glass transition. The first is entropic in origin, whereas the second—at least in chemically realistic polymer models—primarily entropic. We write the relaxation time in the melt as

```
$\tau_c(T, N) = \tau_{mes}(T)N^x\quad [24]$
```

where $	au_{mes}$ is a mesoscopic scale. The chain length dependence over from $x = 2$ for Rouse behavior to $x = 3.4$ for repeating chains.$^{12}$ Every simulation method that performs configuration changes typical for the mesoscopic scale $	au_{mes}$, i.e., local rearrangements, leads to a relaxation of the large-scale structure of the polymer in the melt only after $O(N^x)$ such configuration changes.
