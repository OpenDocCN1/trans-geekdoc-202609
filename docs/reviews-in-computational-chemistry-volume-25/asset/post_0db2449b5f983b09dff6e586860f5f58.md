Four compounds containing metal–metal quadrupole bonds, the [M$_2$(CH$_3$)$_6$] ions where M = Cr, Mo, W and n = 4, 4, 4, 2, have also been studied theoretically$^{44}$ using the CASPT2 method employed in Re$_2$Cl$_6$. The molecular structure of these compounds has been determined, and the energy of the $\delta \rightarrow \delta^*$ transition. The high negative charges on the Cr, Mo, and W complexes lead to the successful modeling of the ground-state structures, which is a problem that has been addressed by the explicit inclusion of four Li^+$ ions in these calculations. The ground-state geometries of the complexes and $\delta \rightarrow \delta^*$ transition are in excellent agreement with Re, but only one satisfactory agreement for Mo, Cr, and W.

The primary goal of this study$^{44}$ was to provide a theoretical understanding of the apparently linear relationship between metal–metal bond length and $\delta \rightarrow \delta^*$ excitation energy for the octamethyldimetallates of Re, Cr, Mo, and W. As we demonstrated, these seemingly anionic systems represent a surprising, largely because of the difficulty in modeling systems (without electronegative ligands) that have negative charges. Nevertheless, with the CASPT2 method with Li^+$ counters, one can model the ground-state geometries of the complexes in a satisfactory way. This multiconfigurational approach, which is critical for the calculation of excited-state energies of the complexes, does a fairly good job of modeling trends in the $\delta \rightarrow \delta^*$ excitation energy with the metal–metal bond length, although the accuracy is such that we are not yet able to explain fully the linear relationship discovered by Satteltberger and Fackler. Progress on these systems will require better ways to accommodate the highly negative charges, which are in general difficult to describe, because of the intrinsic problem of the localization of the negative charges. These efforts are ongoing.

# THE Cr-Cr MULTIPLE BOND

The chromium atom has a ground state with six unpaired electrons ($3d^54s, ^7S$). Forming a bond between two Cr atoms could, in principle, result in a heptuple bond, is it not surprising that the chromium dimer has become a challenging test for various theoretical approaches to chemical bonding. All existing quantum chemical methods have been used. The results are widely varying in quality (see Ref. 46 for references to some of these studies). It was not without Roos the CASSCF/CASPT2 approach to Cr2 that a picture of the bonding was achieved.$^{46}$ This study resulted in a bond energy ($D_0$) of 1.65 eV, a bond distance of 1.66 Å, and an $\omega_\pi$ value of 413 cm$^{-1}$ (experimental values are $1.53 \pm 0.06$ eV,$^{47}$ 1.68 Å$^{48}$ and 452 cm$^{-1}$, respectively)$^{49}$.

Do the two atoms form a hexuple? Roos gave the following observations of the bonding and antibonding orbitals: $4d_\sigma.1.90$, $3d_\sigma.0.10$, $3d_\sigma.1.77$, $3d_\sigma.0.23$, $3d_\pi.3.62$, $3d_\pi.0.38$, $3d_\delta.3.16$, and $3d_\delta.0.84$, yielding a total effective order of 4.46. The $\delta$ bond is weak and could be considered as intermediate between a “true” chemical bond and antiferromagnetically coupled electrons. The chromium dimer could then be described as a qualitatively bonded system with the $\delta$ electrons localized on the separate atoms and coupled in a way to give a total spin of zero.

The difficulty in forming all $3d$ and $4s$ orbitals arises from the large difference in Cr–Cr distance such that the $3d$ orbitals reach an effective bonding order, the $4s$ orbitals are already far up on the repulsive part of their potential curve, a behavior that explains why the bond energy is so small despite the high bond order. The difference in order decreases for heavier atoms. The $5s$ orbital of Mo is even more similar in size to the $4d$ orbital. Even more pronounced is the effect for W, where the relativistic contraction of the $6s$ orbital and the corresponding expansion of the $5d$ orbital make them very similar in size. The result is a much stronger bond for $W_2$ with a bond energy above 5 eV and an effective bond of $5.19$.^{50}$ The occupation numbers of the bonding orbitals are never smaller than 1.8, which is a value that is the highest bond order among any of the Periodic Table.

Another feature of these compounds is their temperature-dependent paramagnetism, explained by the existence of lowly excited triplet states, which arises from a shift of the weakly coupled δ electron spin.⁵⁵ A general picture of the Cr–Cr multiple bond emerges from these studies. Not unexpectedly, fully developed bonds are formed by the 3σ and 3π orbitals, whereas the 3δ orbitals are only weakly coupled. The notion of a hexuple bond in the Cr₂ system, a quintuple bond in ArCrAr, and a quadruple bond in the Cr(II)–Cr(II) complexes is therefore an exaggeration. The situation is different for the corresponding compounds containing the heavier atoms Mo and W, where more fully developed bonds can be expected in all three cases.⁵⁶
