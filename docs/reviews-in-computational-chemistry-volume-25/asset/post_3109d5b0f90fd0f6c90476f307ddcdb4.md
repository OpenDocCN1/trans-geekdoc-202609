Numerically, resonance states can be considered eigenstates of a complex-symmetric Hamiltonian with a negative imaginary potential $H = H - iV^{137-139}$ This approach is related to the more complex scaling method in which the dissociative coordinate is rotated into the complex plane.$^{208,209}$ The diagonalization of the complex-symmetric Hamiltonian matrix yields eigenvalues in the form of $E - i\Gamma/2$, where $E$ and $\Gamma$ represent the position and width of the resonance, respectively. In the limit of isolated resonances, the lifetime of the resonance is given by $1/\Gamma$. Like the real-symmetric case, the size of the matrix limits the applicability of direct diagonalization methods.

The application of the Chebyshev recursion to complex-symmetric problems is restricted because Chebyshev polynomials may diverge outside the real axis. Nevertheless, eigenvalues of a complex-symmetric matrix that are close to the real axis can be obtained using the FD method based on the damped Chebyshev recursion. It has been shown that the use of multiple cross-correlation functions may be beneficial.

Because of the damping in the Chebyshev recursion, however, the formulas for the autocorrelation function (Eq. [67] and [68]) do not hold any. Consequently, one might have to compute all correlation values from the original definition ([64]), which would result in numerical inefficiency. Nevertheless, it has been shown by Li and Guo that the doubled autocorrelation according to Eqs. [67] and [68] can still be used to calculate the positions and the widths of narrow resonances accurately with LSFD based on the damped Chebyshev recursion, even though the errors can be large for broad resonances. This observation can be rationalized by fact that damping in the asymptotic regions does not significantly affect narrow resonances because they are largely localized in the interaction region. Numerically, the doubling cuts the computational cost by half and the savings can be significant for large systems. A formal discussion about the validity of the doubling scheme has been given by Neumaier and Mandelstam.

## PROPAGATION OF WAVE PACKETS AND DENSITY MATRICES

The solution of the Schrödinger equation

$i\frac{\partial}{\Psi t}\Psi(t) = \hat{H}\Psi(t)$ [94]

constitutes the propagation of a wave packet in the evolution operator $e^{-Ht}$. As discussed, **H** can be very large and sparse. As a result, many techniques introduced before can be used to approximate the time propagator. We emphasize that if the eigenstates are all known, the time propagation can be performed analytically with minimal effort. However, it is often unnecessary to resolve the eigenpairs. Interpolation works well, particularly for relatively short time the time propagator may be approximated by a Chebyshev expansion:

$e^{-iHt} = \sum_{k=0} (2-\delta_{k0})(-i)^k J_k(\mathbf{H})$ [95]

where $J_k$ are the Bessel functions of the first kind and the spectral range of $\mathbf{H}$ is assumed to be normalized. Other orthogonal polynomials can also be used to approximate the time propagator^{125–127,220–225}. The Lanczos algorithm can also be used to approximate a short-time propagator. The short Lanczos (SIL) method of Park and Light constructs a small set of Lanczos vectors^{226}, which can be summarized by Eq. [96]:

$e^{-iH\Delta t} \approx QE^{-\sum\sigma_k\Delta t}Z^{\dagger}Q^{\dagger}$ [96]

where $Q$ and $Z$ are the matrices that tridiagonalize and diagonalize $T$, respectively. Because the time is short, only a few Lanczos vectors are needed to approximate the propagator. Even if the eigenvalues in Eq. [96] might not be converged, they are sufficient for an interpolation for a short time step. For time-dependent Hamiltonians, one can reformulate the problem with the $(t,t')$ scheme^{227} in which the time is treated as an extra degree of freedom. The techniques developed for stationary problems can be applied in a straight-forward manner. Applications of recursive methods to laser-driven dynamics have been reported by several authors^{99,228–230}.

By analogy the propagation of a density matrix, which corresponds to the solution of the Liouville–von Neumann equation^{231}:

$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \hat{L}\rho$ [97]

requires calculating the Liouville–von Neumann propagator($e^{-\hat{L}t}$). The Liouville-operator($\hat{L}$) is typically complex nonsymmetric and much larger than the Hamiltonian because the density matrix ($\hat{\rho}$) is a rank-2 tensor. The corresponding eigenvalues are generally complex, and diagonalization is not always possible.

The most successful strategy for approximating the operator is to interpolate it with polynomial operators. To this end, Newton and Faber polynomials have been suggested to globally approximate the propagator^{126,127,225,232–234} as in Eq. [95].
