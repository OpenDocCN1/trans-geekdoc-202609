The net area of this intimate contact is called the real area of contact $A_{real}$. It is assumed that plastic flow occurs at most microscopic points of contact, so that the normal, local pressures correspond to the hardness $\sigma_h$ of the softer of the two materials that are in contact. The (maximum) shear pressure is given by the yield strength $\sigma_y$ of the same material. The net load $F_L$ and the net force $F_s$ follow by integrating $\sigma_h$ and $\sigma_y$ over the real area of contact $A_{real}$. That is, $L = \sigma_h A_{real}$ and $F_s = \sigma_y A_{real}$. Hence, the plastic deformation results in the following (static) friction coefficient:

```
$\mu_s = \sigma_y / \sigma_h$
```

where $\mu_s$ is defined as the ratio of $F_s$ and $L$. Although this explanation for a linear relationship between friction and load has been used extensively in the literature, Bowden and Tabor, who originally suggested this, we were aware of the limitations of their model and only apply it to contacts (bare)$^{12}$. Two important objections exist to the claim that plastic deformation is a dominant friction mechanism. First, friction between two solids does not typically depend solely on the mechanical properties of the two materials in contact but on the properties of these materials and the lubricant between them. Second, calculations of typical surface profiles show that plastic flow would occur at only a very small fraction of the total number of contact points, and hence, unlike that plastic deformation contributes significantly to friction in real engineering contracts$^{13}$.

So far, we have not considered lubricants intentionally such as oils, or unintentionally in the form of contaminants as airborne hydrocarbons. It is known that adsorbed molecules can alter the behavior of sliding dramatically as long as these molecules remain at the microscopic points of contact$^{14,15}$. From an engineering point of view, such molecules prevent surfaces from making intimate mechanical contact, reducing plastic deformation and wear. However, they also prevent surfaces from becoming superliquid. Under sliding conditions where a sufficient quantity of lubricant exists, one or two layers of lubricant remain in the contact, where they solidify because of the typically high pressures at the microscopic scale. In this regime, one generally talks about boundary lubrication, and because the interactions between lubricant particles are relatively weak, the adsorbed atoms and molecules will try to optimize their interactions with the confining walls. This can lock the surfaces geometrically, as illustrated schematically in Figure 4, and when sliding one surface relative to another, an energy barrier has to be overcome, which generates a static friction force.
