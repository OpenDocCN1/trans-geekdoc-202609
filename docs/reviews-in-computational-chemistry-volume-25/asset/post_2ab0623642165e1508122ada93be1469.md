It has clearly demonstrated by experiments as well as simulations that the glass transition phenomenon is associated with an increase in dynamic heterogeneity in the motion of the glass-forming moieties. Using computer simulations of simple liquids,$^{186,187}$ it was suggested that the non-Gaussianity of the van-Hove correlation functions for tagged particle motion can identify fast-moving particles. Typically, the van Hove function $G_s(r,t)$ from the simulation develops a tail when compared with the Gaussian approximation $G_s^c(r,t)$ having the same second moment, i.e., having the mean-square displacement of the molecules. The fraction of molecules with displacements beyond the crossing point $r^*$ defined by $G_s(r,t)=G_s^c(r,t)$ as fast-moving particles yields around 6% of particles. This definition of fast-moving and variants thereof have been applied to simulations of the bead-spring polymer model$^{188–190}$

When one studies the clustering properties of these fast particles, one finds that the clusters are typically ramified, string-like objects. The average age of the clusters as a function of time lag from a starting configuration has a peak in the $\beta$ regime, where the non-Gaussianity of the van Hove function is maximum. At this point, the particles break out of their cages. In the average cluster size is a consequence of the identification of the particles as being faster than predicted by the Gaussian behavior. A dynamic correlation length (defined as the average mean-square size of the cluster) increases only from $\xi\sim 2.5\sigma$ to $\xi\sim 3.1$ approaching $T_c$, where $\sigma$ is the Lennard–Jones radius of the bead-spring monomers. This small increase is compatible with the experimental finding that the typical size of a fast relaxing molecules at temperatures below $T_c$ is only a few nanometers, which translates into between 3 and 10 Lennard–Jones radii.

The new qualitative insight obtained from the cluster analysis is the string-like character of fast particles, which means that these particles tend to follow each other along their paths of motion. However, only short scale correlation of this motion exists. There is also no observable tendency for the bead-spring monomers to move along their chain to take up the position of their bonded neighbor. In contrast, for PB, a clear tendency exists for a monomer to replace its bonded neighbor on the average time scale of a torsional transition.$191$
