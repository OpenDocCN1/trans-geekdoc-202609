However, real engineering contacts rarely contain commensurate surfaces; indeed, it is exceedingly difficult to purposely devise such systems. Moreover, the frictional properties of systems that contain commensurate surfaces differ significantly from those of systems with incommensurate surfaces, surfaces that do not share a common periodicity in a systematic fashion. Thus, to obtain results from simulations, one must take care to ensure that the surfaces being studied do not contain commensurate surfaces or other symmetries. The design of realistic systems is further complicated by the presence of defects, surface curvature and roughness, lubricant molecules, and contaminants, all of which must be treated appropriately. For instance, preventing lubricant molecules from squeezing out of contacts, or even neglecting atomic-scale surface roughness, may lead to erroneous results in simulations. In some cases, it is even necessary to consider the chemical reactivity of the lubricant molecules.

In this chapter, we discuss how to perform meaningful simulations by avoiding the potential pitfalls that were mentioned above. In the next section, some theoretical aspects of friction between solids will be explained. Then an overview of algorithms that have been used in the simulation of tribological phenomena is provided. # THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

Everyday experience indicates that a finite threshold force, namely the static friction force $F_s$ must be overcome to initiate lateral motion of one solid body relative to another. Experience also indicates that a finite minimum force, namely the kinetic friction force $F_k$, must be applied in order to keep a solid body moving at a constant velocity. In plain terms, we must extend a certain amount of energy to overcome friction if we want to move an object and keep it. This contrasts with the situation encountered when one pulls a solid through a fluid medium where there is no such threshold, and instead, one needs to overcome frictional forces that are linear in the final velocity $v_f$. In the sliding regime, the applied force is less than required to initiate sliding and corresponds to the kinetic friction force $F_k$.
