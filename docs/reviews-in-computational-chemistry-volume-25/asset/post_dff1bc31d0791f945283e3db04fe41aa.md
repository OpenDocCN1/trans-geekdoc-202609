We have studied the structure and dynamics of the water environment on a uranyl ion using such an approach (the nonempirical model potential, NEMO, method), which has been developed during the last 15 years.$85,86}$ It has been used primarily to systems like liquid water and water clusters, liquid formaldehyde and acetonitrile, and the solvation of organic molecules and inorganic ions in water. A recent review$^{85}$ by Engkvist contains references on specific applications.

The interaction between uranyl and a water molecule has been studied using accurate quantum methods.${87}$ The information has been used to fit a NEMO potential, which is then used to evaluate other structural and dynamical properties of the system. Multiconfigurational wave function calculations were performed to generate pair potentials between uranyl and water. The quantum chemical energies were used to fit parameters in a polarizable force field with an added charge term. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed for the uranyl ion up to 400 water molecules. The results showed a uranyl ion with five water coordinated in the equatorial plane. The U-water distance is 2.40 Å which is close to the experimental estimates. A coordination shell starts at 4.7 Å from the uranium atom. Exchange of waters between the first and second solvation shell is found to occur through a path intermediate between association and interchange. This study is the first fully ab determination of the solvation of the uranyl ion in water.

## THE ACTINIDE–ACTINIDE CHEMICAL BOND

After studying actinide-actinide molecules, the next question that one tries to answer is if it possible to form bonds between actinide atoms and, if so, what is the nature of these bonds? Experimentally, there is some evidence of such bonds in the gas phase and in a low-temperature matrix. The uranium diatomic molecule $U_2$ was detected in the gas phase in 1974.${88}$ The dissociation energy was estimated to be $50\pm5$ kcal/mol. Andrews and co-workers found both $U_2$ and Th molecule isolation spectroscopy.${89}$ Both molecules were also found in the gas phase using vapor labeling of a solid uranium or thorium target.${90}$ Small molecules containing $U_2$ as a central unit were also reported, for example, $H_2U_3^{91}$ and $UO_2^{88}$. Not much was known theoretically about the nature of the chemical bond between actinides before the study of $U_2$ by Gagliardi and Roos.${86}$ The molecule was studied theoretically in 1990,$92}$ but the methods used were not enough to allow for a conclusive characterization of the chemical bond.

Is it possible to say something about the bonding pattern of a molecule like $U_2$ based on qualitative arguments? Before undertaking the study of the diuranium molecules, some systems containing a transition metal and a uranium atom were studied, for example, $UAu_4$ and $UAu_6$ molecules and $NIr^{4,93,94}$. The ground state of the uranium atom is $(5f)^3(6d)^1(7s)^2$, $^5L_6$ with four unpaired electrons that can form a radical bond. The double occupancy of the $7s$ orbital, however, prevents the unpaired orbitals from coming to contact with the bonds. On the other hand, a valence state with six unpaired electrons is 0.77 eV above the ground level: $(5f)^3(6d)^2(7s)^2$, $^7\mathcal{M}_6$. A hexaple bond could form in principle if it is strong enough to overcome the needed atomic promotion energy of 1.54 eV. However, there is one obstacle to form the bond. The $7s$ and $6d$ orbitals can expected to overlap more strongly than the $5f$ orbitals. In particular, the $5f$ orbitals, which are occupied in the free atom, will have little overlap. Thus, there must be a transfer of electrons from $5f$ to $6d$ to form a strong bond. As we see, this competition between the atomic configuration and the ideal molecule determines the electronic structure of the uranium dimer. To proceed further with the analysis, one needs to perform explicit calculations, and such calculations were done using a basis set ANO type, with inclusion of scalar relativistic effects, ANN-RCC, of the size: $9s8p65d2fg1lb^6$. As pointed out, 13 active orbitals on each atom are involved in the bonding ($5f, 6d, 7s$). This would yield an active space of 26 orbitals with 12 active orbitals, an impossible calculation, so the number of trial calculations were performed using smaller active spaces. The results had one important feature: They showed that a triple bond was formed involving $7\sigma_g$ and $6d\pi$ orbitals. The occupation numbers of these orbitals were close to two with small occupation of the corresponding bonding. It was therefore decided to leave these orbitals inactive in the CAS wave function and to remove the antibonding counterparts $7\sigma_g$ and $6d\pi$. This approximation should work well near the equilibrium bond, but prevents the calculation of full curves. With six electrons and six MOs removed from the active space, one is left with 6 electrons in 20 orbitals, a calculation that could be performed easily. Several calculations were done with different space and spin of the wave function. The resulting ground state was found to be a triplet with all six electrons having parallel spin, and the orbital momentum was high with $\Lambda = 11$. Spin-orbit calculations showed that the spin and orbital momenta coincided to form an $\Omega = 8$. The final label of ground state was $^7O_8$. The main terms of the configurational wave function were found to be $\Psi(S=3,\Lambda=11)=0.782(7\sigma_g)^2(6d\pi_u)^4(6d\sigma_g)(6d\delta_u)(5f\delta_u)(5f\pi_u)(5f\Phi_u)+0.596(7\sigma_g)^2(6d\pi_u)^4(6d\sigma_g)(6d\delta_u)(5f\delta_u)(5f\pi_u)(5f\Phi_u)$. This wave function reflects nicely the competition between the preferred atomic state and the most binding situation. We have assumed that the triple bond is fully formed. Also two electrons exist in $6d$-dominated bonds, $6d\sigma$ and $6d\delta$. The remaining MOs are dominated by $5f$. Two weak bonds (one $\delta$ and one $\pi$) are formed from $5f\delta$ and $5f\pi$ orbitals. Note that there is substantial occupation of the corresponding antibonding orbitals. Finally, $5f\phi$ orbitals remain atomic and do not contribute to the bonding (equal occupation of bonding and antibonding combinations). Formally, a quintuple bond is formed, but the large occupation of some antibonding orbitals reduce the effective bond order closer to four than five. Because of the weak bonding of $5f$ orbitals, the effective bond order of U is not five but closer to four. It is interesting to note the occupation of the different atomic valence on each uranium atom. There are $7s$, $0.94$, $6d$, $2.59$, and $5f$, $2.4$. Compare with the population in the lowest atomic level with $7s$ only: $7s$, $1.00$, $6d$, $2.00$, and $5f$, $3.00$. We see a transfer of $0.6$ electrons from $5f$ to $6d$, which allows the molecule to use the better bonding of $6d$ orbitals compared with $5f$.

The calculations give a bond length of $2.43$ Å and a bond energy of $35$ kcal/mol, including the effects of spin-orbit coupling. An experimental value of $50\pm5$ kcal/mol was reported in 1974.${88}$

Is it possible that other actinides also form dimers? We already noticed that $Th_2$ has been detected in the gas phase and in a matrix. We have studied this and the dimers of Ac and Pa.${95}$ Some major findings are reported here.

We present in Table 3 the excitation energies needed to produce a valence state with all orbitals singly occupied. The largest excitation energy is for Ac. The price to pay for forming a triple bond between two Ac atoms is $2.28$ eV, for Th, $1.28$ eV is needed, which, in principle, form a quadruple bond. Note that in these cases only $7s$ and $6d$ orbitals are involved. For Pa, $1.67$ eV is needed, which allows for the possibility of a quintuple bond. The uranium case was already described above where we saw that, despite six unpaired atomic orbitals, only a quintuple bond is formed with an effective bond order closer to four than five.

It is the competition between the needed atomic promotion energy and the strength of the bond that will determine the electronic structure.
