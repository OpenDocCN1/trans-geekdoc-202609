Based on the discussion in earlier sections of this chapter, one may expect atomically flat incommensurate surfaces to be superlubric. The first suggests that ultra-low friction may be possible based on simulations of copper surfaces$^{6,7}$. Furthermore, Ni(100)/(100) interfaces discussed in the previous section showed very low friction when the surfaces were atomically flat and misoriented (see the data for the atomically flat system between 30° and 60° in Figure 21). In general, however, it is reasonable to assume that bare metals are not good candidates for superlubric materials because they are vulnerable to a variety of energy dissipation mechanisms such as dislocation formation, plastic deformation, and wear.

Layered materials such as graphite and MoS$_2$ have been the focus of much attention in terms of superfluidity. These systems are expected to exhibit low friction because the coupling between atoms within a given sheet is much stronger than between layers. As a result, the sheets will remain relatively rigid under sliding conditions, which will minimize energy dissipation and lead to ultra-low friction. A major problem associated with layered materials is that they tend to rub away, leading to debris formation, which increases friction. Indeed, in first experimental studies to demonstrate superlubricity, it was found that MoS$_2$ exhibited ultra-low friction to the onset of wear$^{87}$.

Carbon nanotubes have also received a great deal of attention within the context of low-friction materials. These structures are consistent with rolled graphite sheets and either solitary tubes (single-walled nanotubes) or several concentric tubes of increasing size (multiwalled nanotubes). In multiwalled nanotubes, the interactions between atoms within the tubes are much stronger than those between the atoms in two neighboring tubes, which opens up the possibility of ultra-low friction. As such, it has been suggested that multiwalled nanotubes could be used as molecular-level bearings$^{88}$ and springs$^{89,90}$ as well as gigahertz nano-oscillators$^{91}$.
