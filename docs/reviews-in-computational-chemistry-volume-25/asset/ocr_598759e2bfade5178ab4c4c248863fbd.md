The convergence of the eigenvalues obtained can be checked by varying L and K. For practical purposes, L is typically LSFD in the range of 100. The number of recursion step (K) is proportional to the average density of states and the spectral range. The error of a particular eigenvalue can be estimated from its dispersion ||(H - E_n^2)b_n||, where ||H^n r_n|| = η^n H^n r can also be expressed in terms of the autocorrelation function.

The LSFD method essentially the same memory requirement as the Lanczos algorithm. Likewise, the CPU scaling is linear and dominated by the recursion (because the numerical costs for solving the generalized problem are usually much smaller). In addition, the energy global nature of the propagator allows one to determine the eigenvalues in multiple spectral windows, where the grid E_l(l=1,2,...,L) has to be defined a priori in the original FD. Of course, a much longer Chebyshev recursion might be needed for spectral windows in the interior of the spectrum. The relative merits of LSFD and the Lanczos algorithm will be discussed.

Most LSFD applications to date are based on the autocorrelation function obtained by propagating a single vector. However, it has been pointed out that the use of cross-correlation functions may be beneficial in determining the eigenvalues with LSFD, particularly for cases with high levels of degeneracy or deteriorating signals. This can be achieved by propagating multiple initial vectors and computing the cross-correlation matrix at every step, similar to the block-Lanczos algorithm discussed above. The Hamiltonian and overlap matrices in Eq. [76] can be obtained in a similar fashion, albeit with a much larger dimension because of the block nature of the correlation functions. The benefits of cross-correlation functions are obvious because they have higher information content than the autocorrelation function.

We note that the Chebyshev recursion-based LSFD can be used to extract frequencies from a signal in the following form:

```
C_k = \sum_n d_n e^{-iktE_n} [85]
```

by assuming the signal corresponds, implicitly, to a quantum system in which the explicit form of the Hamiltonian is of no importance. Specifically, the signal in Eq. [85] can be considered as a correlation function over a discrete time window with a step of τ. This strategy is successful for classic and semi-classical dynamics, and for NMR signal processing. In addition, several signal processing methods related to LSFD have been proposed by various authors. Interested readers are referred to an excellent review by Mandelstham.
