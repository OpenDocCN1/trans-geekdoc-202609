When integrating the equations of motion, it is important not to impose the shear only at the boundaries because this would break translational invariance. An alternative to Lees-Edwards boundary conditions is the formalism put forth by Parrinello and Rahman for simulation of solids under constant stress. They described the positions of particles by dimensionless coordinates $r_n$, where $r_n$ can take the value $0 \le r_n < 1$ in the central image. Periodic images of a given particle are generated by adding or subtracting images from the individual components of $r$.

The real coordinates of $R$ are obtained by multiplying $r$ with the matrix $b$ that contains the vector spanning the simulation cell. In the two-dimensional (2-D) example, this could read as follows:

$$R_{\alpha} = \sum_{\beta} b_{\alpha\beta}r_{\beta}$$

$$b = \begin{bmatrix} L_x & 0 \\ 0 & L_y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \end{bmatrix}$$

In this approach, the potential energy $V$ is a function of reduced coordinates and the $b$-matrix. For the kinetic energy, one would only be interested in the motion of the particle relative to the distorted geometry so that suitable Lagrange $L_0$ for the system would read as follows:

$$L_0 = \sum_{\alpha} \frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}(\sum_{\beta} b_{\alpha\beta}\dot{r}_{\beta})^2 - V(b, \dot{r})$$

in which the $b$ matrix may be time dependent. Through this Lagrangian, it is straightforward to derive the Newtonian equations for the reduced coordinates, which can then be solved by any number of integration schemes. One advantage of the scheme outlined in Eqs. [28] to [30] is that it is relatively easy to allow for fluctuations of the size of the central cell. This scheme is described further below.

## Geometric and Topological Constraints

An important aspect of the methods described in the preceding section is that $L_x$ and $L_y$ can be time dependent. As we will show in this section, flexibility allows the simulation cell to fluctuate independently along different spatial dimensions during the simulation. This capability is useful in simulating systems such as self-assembled monolayers under shear. Care must be taken when allowing for this additional flexibility because, for some systems, e.g., simple fluids under shear, there is no particular reason why $L_x$ and $L_y$ should be chosen to be independent of one another. PS and PB usually phase separate at low temperatures; however, because of their chemical connectivity, block copolymers cannot mix on a macroscopic scale. When lamellar structures are formed, it is necessary to ensure that the dimensions of the lamellae are commensurate with the intrinsic periodicity of the lamellae. This prevents unintentionally subjecting the system to artificial pressure as a result of the geometric constraints. Subjecting the system to a predetermined pressure, or stress, in a controlled manner can be achieved by allowing the system to fluctuate to “solid directions,” which are introduced in Figure 14. For these directions, it would be appropriate to employ the usual techniques to maintain constant stress.$^{52,53}$

Let us consider the three-dimensional case and work within the Parrinello-Rahman framework. A general three-dimensional $h$ matrix of Eq. [31] will be considered:

$$b = \begin{bmatrix} L_{xx} & 0 & L_{xy} & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & L_{yy} & L_{yz} & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ L_{xz} & L_{yz} & L_{zz} & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

It is now possible to couple fluctuating geometries not only to constant isotropic pressure but to non-isotropic stresses as well. The description of these approaches is beyond the scope of the current tutorial, but it can be found in the literature.$^{52,53}$

It is often sensible to choose $M_{cell}$ such that the simulation cell adjusts to the external pressure, which simplifies Eq. [32] to:

$$T_{cell} = \sum_{\alpha\beta} \frac{1}{2}M_{cell}L_{\alpha\beta}^2$$

where $p$ is an isotropic pressure and $h$ is the volume of the simulation cell. The Lagrangian $L$ for Lees-Edwards boundary conditions combined with Parrinello-Rahman now reads as:

$$L = L_0 + T_{cell} - p \det h$$

When studying systems with mixed "fluid" and "solid" directions, it is important to keep in mind that each solid direction should be bathed and fluid directions need to be scaled isotopically or constrained to a constant value. Allowing two fluid directions to fluctuate independently from one another allows the simulation cell to become flat like a pancake, which we would like to avoid.
