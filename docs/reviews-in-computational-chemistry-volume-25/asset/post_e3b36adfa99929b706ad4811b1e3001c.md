This assumption breaks down close to $T_{c}$. MCT has been augmented by coupling to mass currents, which are sometimes termed "inclusion of processes," but the extension of the theory to temperatures below $T_{c}$ or even down to $T_{c}$ has not been successful.$101$ Also, the theory is not often applied to experimental density fluctuations (observed by neutron scattering) but instead dielectric relaxation or to NMR experiments. These techniques probe reorientational motion of anisotropic molecules, whereas the MCT equation describes a scalar quantity. Using MCT results to compare with dielectric or NMR experiments forces one to assume a direct coupling of orientational correlations with density fluctuations exists. The different orientational correlation functions and the question to what extent they directly couple to the density fluctuations have been extended in like the standard MCT picture.$104-108$

Of the available experimental techniques, the various neutron scattering methods mostly measure structural relaxation. However, their dynamic range is limited and several experimental setups have to be combined to obtain information on polymer relaxation from the picosecond scale to the longest time accessible in neutron spin echo experiments (~sim 100 ns depending on momentum transfer), with all the experimental correction and normalization issues involved in matching results from different experiments. To our knowledge, the reconstruction of a single relaxation curve $S(q,t)$ out of experimental information for the different frequency and time windows has not been tried. simulation studies of the glass transition will provide us with the most detailed information of the structural relaxation processes.$109$

Before we examine in detail more the dynamics of a super-cooled melt of coarse-grained grains and of PB systems, let us first come the structure of these two glass-forming systems. Structure is obtained experimentally from either the neutron or the X-ray structure factors. The melt (or liquid) structure factor is given as$110$

$$S_{m}(q) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{n,m} b_{n}(q)b_{m}(q)e^{i\vec{q}\cdot(\vec{r}_{n}-\vec{r}_{m})} [59]$$

Here the angular brackets indicate thermal as well as isotropic averages, and the sum runs over all M scattering centers in the melt. The quantities $b_n(q)$ are the scattering factors of different scatterers in the sample. For X-ray scattering, momentum transfer $q$ dependence of form factor of electronic clouds must be taken into account. For neutron scattering, form factors reduce to $q$-independent scattering lengths, $b_n(q)=b_n$. Neutron scattering studies of the melt structure are typically performed on deuterated samples, i.e., where all H atoms replaced by D atoms, for deuterium and carbon atoms, coherent scattering (they have the same coherent lengths, whereas hydrogen atoms scatter incoently). X-ray scattering is most sensitive to positional correlations of heavy atoms in the sample with their associated clouds. Performing neutron scattering on deuterated chain in protracted matrix (or vice versa; both provide the same contrast) gives the single-chain structure,

$$S_{ch}(q) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n,m}^N b_n b_m \langle e^{iq\cdot(\hat{r}_{n}-\hat{r}_{m})} \rangle [60]$$

where the sum runs only over all monomers of a single chain. When we think of situations involving bead-spring models, all scatterers can be assigned the same scattering lengths [that are absorbed into arbitrary units for $S(q)$], and for united atom like the one used for PB, we can consider scattering from the united atoms in the same way. This simplifies the scattering functions of [59] and [60] to be

$$S_m(q) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{n,m}^M \langle e^{iq\cdot(\hat{r}_{n}-\hat{r}_{m})} \rangle [61]$$

and

$$S_{ch}(q) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n,m}^N \langle e^{iq\cdot(\hat{r}_{n}-\hat{r}_{m})} \rangle [62]$$

The structure factors are Fourier transforms of radial pair-distribution functions for the complete melt or single chain, respectively,

$$S_m(q) = 1 + 4\pi \int_0^{\infty} r^2 g_m(r) \frac{\sin(qr)}{qr} dr [63]$$

$$S_{ch}(q) = 1 + 4\pi(N-1) \int_0^{\infty} r^2 g_{ch}(r) \frac{\sin(qr)}{qr} dr [64]$$

The behavior of the bead-spring model is different from PB. The melt structure factor and the single-chain structure factor depicted in Figure 7 only start to agree at the third peak in the melt structure factor. For smaller momentum transfer, they oscillate with the same wavelength but with a phase shift. The intramolecular structure factor has a minimum preceding the amorphous halo and maximum shifted slightly with respect to, but still within, the amorphous halo. For the chemically realistic united chain, we observe a shoulder in the intramolecular structure factor at the position of the amorphous halo and a first minimum where the melt structure factor also has its first minimum.
