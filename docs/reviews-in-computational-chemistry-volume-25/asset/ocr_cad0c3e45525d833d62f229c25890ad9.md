This wave function reflects nicely the competition between the preferred atomic state and the most optimal binding situation. We have assumed that the triple bond is fully formed. Also two electrons exist in $6d$-dominated sigma bonds, $6d\sigma$ and $6d\delta$. The remaining MOs are dominated by $5f$. Two weak bonds (one $\delta$ and one $\pi$) are formed from $5f\delta$ and $5f\pi$ orbitals. Note that there is substantial occupation of the corresponding antibonding orbitals. Finally, the $5f\phi$ orbitals remain atomic and do not contribute to the bonding (equal occupation of the bonding and antibonding combinations). Formally, a quintuple bond is formed, but the large occupation of some antibonding orbitals reduce the effective bond order closer to four than five. Because of the weak bonding of the $5f$ orbitals, the effective bond order of U is not five but closer to four. It is interesting to note the occupation of the different atomic valence orbitals on each uranium atom. There are $7s$, $0.94$, $6d$, $2.59$, and $5f$, $2.4$. Compare with the population in the lowest atomic level with $7s$ only: $7s$, $1.00$, $6d$, $2.00$, and $5f$, $3.00$. We see a transfer of $0.6$ $electrons from $5f$ to $6d$, which allows the molecule to use the better bonding of the $6d$ orbitals compared with $5f$.

The calculations give a bond energy of $2.43\text{\AA}$ and a bond energy of $35$ $kcal/mol$, including the effects of spin-orbit coupling. An experimental value of $50\pm5$ $kcal/mol$ was reported in $1974.^{88}$

Is it possible that other actinides also form dimers? We already noticed that $Th2$ has been detected in the gas phase and in a gas matrix. We have studied this and the dimers of Ac and Pa.$^{95}$ Some major findings are reported here.

We present in Table 3 the excitation energies needed to produce a valence state with all orbitals singly occupied. The largest excitation energy is for Ac. The price to pay for forming a triple bond between two Ac atoms is $2.28$ $ev$, for Th, $1.28$ $ev$ is needed, which, in principle, form a quadrangle bond. Note that in these cases only $7s$ and $6d$ orbitals are involved. For Pa, $1.67$ $ev$ is needed, which allows for the possibility of a quintuple bond. The uranium case was already described above where we saw that, despite six unpaired atomic orbitals, only a quintuple bond is formed with an effective bond order closer to four than five.

It is the competition between the needed atomic promotion energy and the strength of the bond that will determine the electronic structure.
