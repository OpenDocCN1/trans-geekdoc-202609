Gas-phase results provide insight into the reaction pathways for isolated HE molecules; however, the absence of the condensed-phase environment is better to affect reaction strongly. Some key questions related to condensed-phase decomposition are as follows: (1) How does the temperature and pressure affect the reaction pathways? (2) Are there temperature or pressure-induced phase-transitions that play a role in the reaction pathways that may occur? (3) What happens to the reaction profiles in a shock-induced detonation? These questions can be answered with condensed-phase simulations, but such simulations would require large-scale chemical systems considering thousands of atoms. We here present results of condensed-phase atomic simulations, which are pushing the envelope toward reaching the required goal.

One possible non-molecular phase that may be a supersonic solid. Superionic solids exhibit exceptionally high ionic conductivity, where one ion type diffuses through a crystalline lattice of the remaining types. In this unique phase of matter, chemical bonds are breaking and reforming rapidly. Since their discovery in 1836, a fundamental understanding of supersonic conductors has been one of the major challenges in condensed matter physics$^{63}$. In general, it has been difficult to create a simple set of rules governing superionic phases. Studies have been limited mostly to metal-based compounds, such as AgI and PbF$_2^{66}$. However, the existence of superionic phases of hydrogen-bonded compounds has been theorized previously$^{64,65}$.

Recent experimental and computational results indicate the presence of a high-pressure triple point in the H$_2$O phase diagram$^{66-68}$ including a so-called superionic solid with fast diffusion$^{68,69}$. Goldman et al. described the emergence of symmetric hydrogen bonding in superionic water at 2000 K and 95 GPa$^{69}$. In symmetric hydrogen bonding, the intramolecular X–H bond becomes identical to the intermolecular X–H bond, where X is an electronegative element.
