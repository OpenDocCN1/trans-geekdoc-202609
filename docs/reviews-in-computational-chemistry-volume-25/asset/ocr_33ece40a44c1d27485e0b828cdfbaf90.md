Even if one takes the view that the only influence the short-window (ns) of MD simulation is to shift the apparent glass transition temperature $T_g$ (MD) upward in comparison with that of experiment $T_g$ (expt), one still has to assess the amount of such upward shift.

Practitioners of MD simulation studying glass fluids often compare MD results on glass transition temperatures with experimental data,${82-84}$ ignoring this systematic difference between $T_g$ (MD) and $T_g$ (expt). For “strong” glass formers like molten $SiO_2$, this issue has been studied carefully. In the range $10^{12}$ K/s < $10^{15}$ K/s, which is many orders of magnitude higher than in the experiments,$86}$

A very strong dependence of $T_g$ (MD) on the cooling rate $\Gamma$ was found, in the case of $SiO_2$ as in the range $\approx 1450$ K is more than 1000 K! Because of the much steeper variation of the structural relaxation time $	au$ with $T$ near $T_g$ (expt), one does not expect drastic effects for fragile glass formers like most polymers, and indeed for simulations,$^{82-84}$ it was found that $T_g$ (MD) agreed with $T_g$ (expt) reasonably well.

One reason for ignoring the cooling rate dependence of the simulated Tg$ in these publications is because MD simulations of realistic models are generally too consuming for a systematic study of different cooling rates (at one exception, see Ref. 87), especially when one takes into account that for each cooling rate one average over several independent cooling runs for each will be described. To evaluate cooling rate dependence, one best uses a coarse-grained model, as done for the bead-string model suggested in Refs. 88 and 89. In the latter part, the melting temperature of the bead-string model was determined to be $T=0.76$ in Lennard-Jones units. Upon cooling at a fixed rate in a MD simulation at constant temperature and pressure (NpT) employing a Nosé–Hoover thermostat and an Andersen barostat,$75,77}$ one observes the temperature-dependent specific values shown in Figure 5. In the top panel, the temperature dependence of the specific value is shown for a cooling rate of $\Gamma = 52.083 \cdot 10^{-6}$. Straight lines fit in the melt and in the glassy phase assume a constant coefficient in both phases. The intersection point between the straight lines defines the glass transition temperature $T_g(\Gamma)$ for this cooling rate. In the lower panel, the curves are shown for different cooling rates. The melt curve does not depend on the cooling rate, but the glass curves show a systematic shift, although the uncertainty of the data, slope of the curves (the thermal expansion coefficient) is independent of the cooling rate also in the glass. It is obvious from the plot that a small (but systematic) variation in $T_g(\Gamma)$ exists in the range of cooling rates that was accessible in the simulation $(3.3 \cdot 10^{-6} < \Gamma < 8.3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ in Lennard-Jones units).
