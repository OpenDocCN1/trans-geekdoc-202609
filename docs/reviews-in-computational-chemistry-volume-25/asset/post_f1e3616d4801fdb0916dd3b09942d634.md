## SIMPLE EXPLANATION OF THE DECAY OF THE MAGNETIC MOMENTS WITH CLUSTER SIZE

Simple models can explain the general decay of the magnetic moment as the cluster size increases,$^6$ but they cannot explain the fine details. Neglecting the contribution of the $sp$ electrons and using the Friedel model, in which the $d$ electrons form a rectangular band,$7$ the local density of electronic states (LDOS) with spin $\sigma$ (that is, $\uparrow$ or $\downarrow$) at site $i$ can be expressed as$^8$

$$D_{i\sigma}(\varepsilon) = \frac{5}{W_i} \text{for } \frac{W_i}{2} < \varepsilon - \varepsilon_{d\sigma} < \frac{W_i}{2} [3]$$

$\varepsilon_{d\sigma}$ is the energy of the center of the $\sigma$ spin sub-band and $W_i$ is the local band width (assumed to be equal for $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$ spins). The tight binding theory (see the next section) gives a scaling relation$^9$ in which $W_i$ is proportional to the square root of the local atomic coordination number $Z_i$

$$W_i = W_b(Z_i/Z_b)^{1/2} [4]$$

where $W_b$ and $Z_b$ are the corresponding quantities for the bulk solid. If the band splitting $\Delta = |\varepsilon_{d\uparrow} - \varepsilon_{d\downarrow}|$ caused by the exchange interaction is assumed equal to the splitting in the bulk, the local moment

$$\mu_i = \int_{-\infty}^{\varepsilon_F}(D_{if}(\varepsilon) - D_{ij}(\varepsilon))d\varepsilon [5]$$

becomes

$$\mu_i = \frac{Z_b}{Z_i}^{1/2} \mu_b \text{ if } Z_i \geq Z_c$$
$$\mu_i = \mu_{\text{dim}} \text{ otherwise} [6]$$

Here $\varepsilon_F$ is Fermi energy, that is, the energy of the highest occupied; $Z_c$ is a limiting atomic coordination number below which the local magnetic moment of atom adopts the value $\mu_{\text{dim}}$ of the dimer, and one can choose $Z_c=5$ in Ni.$^9$ The average magnetic moment $\mu_N = (1/N)\sum_{i=1}^N \mu_i$ depends sensitively on the ratio between the number of surface atoms and the bulk-like atoms. Surface atoms have small values of $Z_c$ and large values of $\mu_i$, whereas the internal atoms have $Z_i=Z_b$ and $\mu_i=\mu_b$. In the case of small clusters, most atoms are on the surface and $\mu$ is large. But as the cluster size increases, the fraction of surface atoms decreases and $\mu$ also decreases. Assuming magnetic moments $\mu_s$ for the surface atoms and $\mu_b$ for bulk atoms, Jensen and Bennmann$^{10}$ calculated the average magnetic moment of the cluster using Eq. [7]

$$\mu = \mu_b + (\mu_s - \mu_b)N^{-1/3} [7]$$

This formula shows a smooth decrease of $\tilde{\mu}$ toward the bulk magnetic moment with increasing N. However, the experimental results graphed in Figure 1 indicate that the variation of $\tilde{\mu}$ with N has a more complex, oscillatory behavior. Its explanation requires a detailed consideration of the geometry of the cluster and a better treatment of its electronic structure.

## TIGHT BINDING METHOD

### Tight Binding Approximation for the $d$ Electrons

The orbitals of the $d$ states in clusters of the $3d, 4d,$ and $5d$ transition elements (or in the bulk metals) are fairly localized on the atoms as compared with the $sp$ values of comparable energy. Consequently, the $d$ states are not much perturbed by the cluster potential, and the $d$ orbitals of one atom do not strongly overlap with the $d$ orbitals of other atoms. Intraatomic $d-d$ correlations tend to give a fixed integral number of $d$ electrons in each atom $d$-shell. However, the small interatomic $d-d$ overlap terms and $s-d$ hybridization introduce interatomic charge fluctuations in each $d$ shell. In fact, a $d$ orbital contribution to the conductivity of the metals and to the low temperature specific heat is obtained only by starting with an extended description of the $d$ electrons.

The partially filled $d$ band of the transition metals... are described by the tight binding method. The basic concepts are:

- (1) The $d$ states are a linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO).
- (2) The $d$ orbitals are localized on atoms.
- (3) Only the first nearest neighbors are retained.
