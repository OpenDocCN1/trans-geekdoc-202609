A purely thermodynamic treatment of detonation ignores the important question of reaction time scales. The finite time scale of reaction leads to strong deviations in detonation velocities from values based on the Chapman-Jouguet theory.$^{16}$ The kinetics of even simple molecules under high-pressure conditions is not well understood.

High-pressure experiments provide insight into chemical reactivity under extreme conditions. For instance, chemical equilibrium analysis of shocked hydrocarbons predicts the formation of condensed carbon and molecular hydrogen.$^{17}$ Similar mechanisms are at play when detonating energetic materials form condensed carbon.$^{10}$ Diamond anvil experiments have been used to determine the equation of state of methanol under high pressures.$^{18}$ We can use a thermodynamic model to estimate the amount of methanol formed under detonation conditions.$^{19}$

Despite the importance of chemical kinetic rates, chemical equilibrium is often reached when energetic materials react. As discussed, this is a useful working approximation, although it has not been established through direct measurement. Chemical equilibrium can be reached rapidly under high-temperature (up to 6000 K) conditions produced by detonating energetic materials.$^{20}$ We begin our by examining thermodynamic cycle applied to explosive detonation. This is a current research topic because high explosives produce detonation products at extreme pressures and temperatures: up to 40 GPa and 6000 K. These conditions make it difficult to probe chemical speciation. Relatively little is known about the equations of state under these conditions. Nonetheless, shock experiments on a wide range of materials have generated sufficient information to allow reliable thermodynamic modeling to proceed.

One of the attractive features of thermodynamic modeling is that it requires very little information regarding the unreacted energetic material; elemental composition, density, and heat of formation of the material are the only information needed. As elemental composition is known once the material is specified, only density and heat of formation need to be predicted.

The C-J detonation theory¹15 implies that the performance of an explosion is determined by thermodynamic states, the C-J state, and the connected expansion region, as illustrated in Figure 1. As detonation processes are rapid, there is insufficient time for thermal conduction during expansion, which implies that the expansion from the C-J state lies on an adiabat dE = -pdV. The adiabatic expansion of the detonation products releases energy in the PV work and heat. Subsequent turbulent mixing of the detonation products in air surrounding the energetic material leads to combustion processes that release more energy.

Thermochemical codes use thermodynamics to calculate states illustrated in Figures 1 and 2, and thus, produce explosive performance. The allowed thermodynamic states behind a shock are intersections of the Rayleigh line (expressing conservation of mass and momentum) and the shock Hugoniot (expressing conservation of energy). The C–J theory assumes a stable detonation occurs when the Rayleigh line is tangent to the shock Hugoniot, as shown of tangency in Figure 2.

This point can be determined, assuming that the equation of state P = P(V,E) of the products is known. The chemical composition of the products changes with the thermodynamic state, so thermochemical codes solve for state variables and chemical concentrations simultaneously. This problem is relatively straightforward, given that the equation of state (EOS) of the fluid and solid products are known.

This is a current research topic because high explosives produce detonation products at extreme pressures and temperatures: up to 40 GPa and 6000 K. These conditions make it difficult to probe chemical speciation. Relatively little is known about the equations of state under these conditions. Nonetheless, shock experiments on a wide range of materials have generated sufficient information to allow reliable thermodynamic modeling to proceed.

One of the attractive features of thermodynamic modeling is that it requires very little information regarding the unreacted energetic material; elemental composition, density, and heat of formation of the material are the only information needed. As elemental composition is known once the material is specified, only density and heat of formation need to be predicted.

The C-J detonation theory¹15 implies that the performance of an explosion is determined by thermodynamic states, the C-J state, and the connected expansion region, as illustrated in Figure 1. As detonation processes are rapid, there is insufficient time for thermal conduction during expansion, which implies that the expansion from the C-J state lies on an adiabat dE = -pdV. The adiabatic expansion of the detonation products releases energy in the PV work and heat. Subsequent turbulent mixing of the detonation products in air surrounding the energetic material leads to combustion processes that release more energy.

Thermochemical codes use thermodynamics to calculate states illustrated in Figures 1 and 2, and thus, produce explosive performance. The allowed thermodynamic states behind a shock are intersections of the Rayleigh line (expressing conservation of mass and momentum) and the shock Hugoniot (expressing conservation of energy). The C–J theory assumes a stable detonation occurs when the Rayleigh line is tangent to the shock Hugoniot, as shown of tangency in Figure 2.

This point can be determined, assuming that the equation of state P = P(V,E) of the products is known. The chemical composition of the products changes with the thermodynamic state, so thermochemical codes solve for state variables and chemical concentrations simultaneously. This problem is relatively straightforward, given that the equation of state (EOS) of the fluid and solid products are known.

One of the most difficult parts of this problem is describing the EOS of the fluid components accurately; Because of its simplicity, the Becker-Kistiakowski-Wilson (BKW)^21 EOS is used in many applications involving energetic materials. Numerous parameter sets have been proposed for the BKW EOS,22–25 Koury and Sours have critically reviewed these sets by comparing their predictions to a database of detonation tests. They concluded that the BKW EOS does not model the detonation of a copper-lined charge adequately. The BKWC parameter set^26 confirms this partially through multivariate parameterization techniques. However, the BKWC parameter set is not reliable when applied to explosives that are very high in hydrogen content.

It has long recognized that the validity of the BKW EOS is questionable.^12 This is particularly important when designing new materials that may have unusual elemental compositions. Efforts to develop better EOSs have been largely based on the concept of potential models. With potential models, molecules interact via idealized spherical particles. Statistical mechanics is employed to calculate the EOS of the interacting mixture of effective particles. Most often, the exponential-6 (exp-6) potential is used for the pair interactions:

$V(r) = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha-6} \exp\left[-\alpha\left(\frac{r}{r_m}\right)^m\right] \quad [1]$

Here, r is the distance between particles, r_m is the minimum of the potential well, ε is the well depth, and α is the softness of the potential well. The Jacobs-Coerwthwaite-Zwissler (JCZ3) EOS was the first successful model based on a pair potential that was applied to detonation.^27.
