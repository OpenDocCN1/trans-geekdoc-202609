# STRUCTURAL ORDERING MAPS

A useful concept for relating structural order to material properties is the ordering map$^{30,31}$, which projects the possible states of a system onto the plane of its structural order metrics. Maps provide a broad representation of the evolution of structural order along equilibrium as well as nonequilibrium paths. An ordering map for a monatomic hard-sphere system$^{30,31}$ is shown in Figure 2. The two relevant forms of order, bond-orientational and translational order, serve as the two axes of the plot. The bond-orientational order is presented as $Q = Q_6/Q_6^{fc}$ (see Eq. [5]); i.e., this is the raw quantity of $Q_6$ normalized by its value for a perfect fcc crystal. The translational order $\tau$ is given by Eq. [1], and measures the similarity of the average particle separations in the fluid with that of a perfect fcc crystal. Defined in this manner, both structural order metrics take values between zero (ideal randomness) and unity (perfect crystalline) in the hard-sphere system.

Inspection of the ordering map allows one to readily quantify the type and degree of order displayed by the equilibrium and glassy states of a material. One important point is that the glasses of the hard-sphere system occupy a different region of order space than does the equilibrium fluid, even though both are nominally "disordered."$^{30}$ The hard-sphere system captures the basic ordering patterns of many atomic and molecular fluids, this finding contradicts the traditional notion that glass structure should be considered simply "liquid-like."
```
$q=1-\frac{3}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sum_{k=j+1}\left(\cos \psi_{jk}\right)^{2} [6]$
```

provides an effective means for quantifying tetrahedral order. Where, $\psi_{jk}$ is the angle formed by bonds joining a molecule of interest and two of its neighbors. This is similar to the parameter introduced by Chao and Hardwick,$50}$ only in such way that the average value of $q$ varies between 0 (for uncorrelated systems) and 1 (for perfect tetrahedral network). Note that this metric provides local, when averaged over all particles, global measures of the tetrahedral order. The so-called tetrahedrality parameter$^{51}$ of Rabuukhin, Voloshin and Medvedev is another metric that can be used to detect tetrahedral order.

Although the bond-orientational metrics defined above have proven useful for identifying crystalline$^{45$ they are inadequate for detecting order in systems like face-centered cubic, body-centered cubic, simple cubic, and hexagonally-packed lattices. The competition between directional attractions and short-range repulsions in tetrahedral liquids, such as water or silica, give rise to nontrivial physical and structural properties. Examples include expansion upon cooling at constant pressure$^{51,52}$ and increased mobility upon isothermal compression at sufficiently low temperatures$^{53-56}$
