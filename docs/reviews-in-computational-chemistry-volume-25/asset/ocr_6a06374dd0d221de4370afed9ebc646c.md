Humans excel at some types of task: We can recognize the face of a friend almost instantly, even though the computations in the brain required to do so are complex. On the other hand, humans are not very adept at mathematical tasks: Although a computer could cube root 373234824812999 in milliseconds, few people could do this without the aid of pencil and paper, let alone manage it in less than a second.

Conventional “linear” computer programs can readily outrun the human brain on numerical calculations, but computer scientists recognized decades ago that, the human mind is not engineered to cube root 15-digit numbers. They reasoned that, if it were possible to create a computer program that functioned in the same way as a human brain, the software might be able to emulate the learning abilities of humans and therefore to be at least as good as humans at pattern recognition, but without the irritating propensity of humans to forget the important stuff and remember the trivial.

The artificial network, or ANN, was an attempt to create such software. The power of the method is reflected in its use in image recognition and classification tasks. Neural networks are useful when one wishes to identify patterns or trends that are not readily apparent in data, whether the data consists of images or purely numerical data, and are also useful in answering “what if” questions, in other words, in making predictions. The range of possible questions is very large, “What would happen in this nuclear power station if the control rods were raised by 20%?” to “Would this affluent pay back £150,000 if we made a mortgage offer to him?” An additional advantage of neural networks is that, once fully prepared through suitable training, they are fast in execution, which makes them valuable in real-time applications such as process control.
