# 294 Recursive Solutions to Large Eigenproblems

After K steps of recursion, the Lanczos vectors can be arranged into an N × K matrix Q, which reduces the Hamiltonian matrix to a tridiagonal form:

```
Q^T HQ = T^{(K)} \quad [30]
```

where

```
Q^T Q = I or q_k^T q_k = \delta_{kk} \quad [31]
```

and the tridiagonal matrix is symmetric and has the following form:

```
T^{(K)} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_1 & \beta_1 & & \ \beta_1 & \alpha_2 & \ddots & \ & & \ddots & \beta_{K-1} \ & & \beta_{K-1} & \alpha_K \end{pmatrix} \quad [32]
```

In fact, the Lanczos reduction was originally proposed as a tridiagonalization scheme, predating the Givens and Householder methods. Unlike the latter, however, the Lanczos method is recursive. This means that the dimensionality of the T matrix is determined by the number of steps of the Lanczos recursion (K), which is much smaller than the dimensionality of the Hamiltonian matrix (N) in real calculations.

Finally, the conversion of the tridiagonal matrix to a diagonal matrix yields the approximate eigenvalues of H.$^{20}$ In particular

```
Z^{-1}T^{(K)}Z = E^{(K)} \quad [33]
```

where Z = $(z_1^{(K)}, z_2^{(K)}, \dots, z_K^{(K)})$ is an orthogonal matrix that contains the eigenvectors of T as its columns. In other words,

```
T^{(K)}z_n^{(K)} = \epsilon_n^{(K)}z_n^{(K)} \quad [34]
```

As discussed, this step is considered to be straightforward and relatively inexpensive.

The recursive diagonalization method outlined in Eqs. [25] to [34] is depicted in Figure 2 and referred to as the Lanczos algorithm. If the Hamiltonian matrix H has N non-degenerate eigenvalues, it is easy to imagine that in exact arithmetic, the Lanczos recursion terminates when K=N because the Lanczos vectors span the eigenspace of H. However, it is important to point out that some converge much earlier than the entire eigenspace is being generated.$^{20,27}$ In fact, this is the beauty of a recursive method, which generates converged eigenvalues rather than waiting until all of them are obtained.
