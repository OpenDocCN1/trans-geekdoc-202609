These differences were demonstrated by considering the change in the friction force as a system proceeds from stick-slip behavior to smooth sliding. In the stick-slip regime, the friction force is dominated by static friction, whereas kinetic friction plays the major role in the smooth sliding regime. The simulations showed that an abrupt change in the friction force under a transition from stick-slip to smooth sliding, which is consistent with vanishing kinetic friction as the sliding velocity goes to zero. That study was extended by considering higher concentrations of confined atoms or molecules, 1-D, 2-D, and three-dimensional (3-D) surfaces, and the internal structure of the confined molecules.¹82 In what follows, we will focus on the effect of the concentration of confined particles. The relevant results are summarized in Figure 18 and show that the dependence of the friction coefficient over the concentration of lubricant molecules differs for systems with commensurate and incommensurate walls. For incommensurate systems, it was found that the magnitude of the friction coefficient μ was relatively insensitive to the concentration of confined molecules for coverages between 0.25 and 2.5 monolayers. Furthermore, μ is not highly dependent on v₀ and remained finite as the sliding velocity went to zero. This behavior is caused by the ability of first-order instabilities to occur within incommensurate systems.
