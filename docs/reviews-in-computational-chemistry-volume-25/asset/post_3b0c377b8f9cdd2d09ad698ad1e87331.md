
## Introduction of s and p Electrons

Elements like carbon or silicon have only s and p electrons in the valence shell. Of course, s and p electrons are also present in the transition elements. Their contribution to the electronic and magnetic properties of transition metal clusters is discussed later in this chapter. In particular their effect can be taken into account in the tight binding method by a simple extension of the ideas presented above. For this purpose, the basis of atomic orbitals has to be extended by adding s and p orbitals. The new basis contains s, p_x, p_y, p_z atomic orbitals in addition to $d_{xy}, d_{xz}, d_{yz}, d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}$ orbitals. It was also pointed out in the previous section that the overlap integrals of the atomic orbitals across neighboring sites can be neglected as a first approximation. To overcome this limitation, an often applied improvement is to substitute the original basis set of atomic orbitals with an orthogonal basis. This orthogonalization can be performed using the method introduced by Löwdin.$^{13}$

$$\Psi_{ij}=(S_{i'j''})^{-1/2}\Phi_{i'j'}$$

preserve the symmetry properties of the original set. S in Eq. [14] is the overlap matrix $S_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=\langle\Phi_{i\alpha}|\Phi_{j\beta}\rangle$, and the index $\alpha$ identifies the different atomic orbitals ($\alpha$ generalizes the index $m$ used above). Consequently, the integrals in Eqs. [10] and [11] become integrals between Löwdin orbitals.

A key approximation that makes the TB calculations simple and practical is to replace the $\Delta$ and $t$ integrals of Eqs. [10] and [11] by parameters depending only on the interatomic distance $|R_i-R_j|$ and the symmetry of the orbitals involved.

## Formulation of the Tight Binding Method in the Notation of Second Quantization

Recent application of the TB method to transition metal clusters offers use of a convenient formulation in the language of second quantization.$^{14}$ In this formalism, the TB Hamiltonian in the unrestricted Hartree–Fock approximation can be written as a sum of diagonal and nondiagonal terms$^{15}$

$$H=\sum_{i,\alpha,\sigma}\hat{\epsilon}_{i\alpha\sigma}\hat{n}_{i\alpha\sigma}+\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\sigma'}t_{ij}^{\alpha\beta\sigma\sigma'}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\alpha\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\beta\sigma'}$$

In this expression, $\hat{c}_{i\alpha\sigma}$ is an operator representing the creation of an electron with spin $\sigma(\sigma=\uparrow$ or $\downarrow)$ and orbital state $\alpha$ at site $i$, $\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\alpha\sigma}$ is the corresponding annihilation operator, and the operator $\hat{n}_{i\alpha\sigma}=\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\alpha\sigma}\hat{c}_{i\alpha\sigma}$ appearing in the diagonal terms are the so-called number operator. As indicated, $\alpha(\beta) \equiv s, p_x, p_y, p_z, d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}, d_{x^2}, d_{z^2}$. The hopping integrals $t_{ij}^{\beta}$ between orbitals $\alpha$ and $\beta$ at equilibrium are assumed to be independent of spin and are well fitted to reproduce the first-principles band structure of the metal at the observed lattice constant. The variation of the hopping integrals with the interatomic distance $R_{ij} = |R_i - R_j|$ is often assumed to follow power law $(R_0/R_{ij})^{l+1}$, where $R_0$ is equilibrium-distance in the bulk solid and $l$ are angular momentum of the two orbitals $\alpha$ and $\beta$, involved in hopping. An exponential decay is sometimes used instead of power law.

The spin-dependent diagonal terms $\varepsilon_{ij\sigma'}$ contain all of the many-body contributions. In a mean field approximation, these environment-dependent energy levels can be written as

$$\varepsilon_{ij\sigma'} = e_{ij}^0 + \sum_{\beta\sigma'} U_{ij\beta'} \Delta v_{ij'} + \sum_{j} \frac{e^2}{R_{ij}} \Delta v_j + Z_\sigma$$

where $e_{ij}^0$ are reference energies. These energies could be atomic levels, but most orbital of paramagnetic metal are taken as reference energies. The second term gives the shifts of the energies caused by intraatomic interactions. The intraatomic Coulomb integrals

$$U_{ij\sigma\sigma'} = \int \frac{\psi_{i\sigma}(r)\psi_{j\sigma'}(r)}{|r-r'|} \psi_{j\sigma'}(r')\psi_{i\sigma}(r') d^3rd^3r'$$

give the electrostatic interaction between two charges corresponding to the orbitals $|i\sigma\rangle$ and $|j\sigma'\rangle$ on the same atom. The quantity $\Delta v_{ij\beta} = v_{i\beta} - v_{\beta}$, where $v_{i\beta}$ is average occupation of spin-orbital $|i\beta\rangle$ and $v_{\beta}$ is corresponding occupation in bulk metal. The intraatomic Coulomb integrals $U_{ij\beta}$ can be expressed in terms of more convenient quantities, the effective integrals $U_{ij\beta} = (U_{i\beta} + U_{j\beta})/2$ and the exchange integrals $J_{ij\beta} = U_{i\beta} - U_{j\beta}$. Then, the intraatomic term of Eq. [16] splits into two contributions

$$\sum_{\beta\sigma'} U_{ij\beta'} \Delta v_{ij\beta} = \sum_{\beta} U_{ij\beta} \Delta v_{ij\beta} + \sum_{\sigma} \frac{J_{ij\beta}}{2} \mu_{\beta}$$

where $\Delta v_{ij\beta} = v_{i\beta} + v_{j\beta}$, $\mu_{\beta} = v_{i\beta} - v_{j\beta}$, and $\sigma_{\sigma}$ is sign function $(z_1 = -1, z_2 = +1)$. The first term $U_{i\beta}$ refers to the Coulomb interaction between electrons with opposite spin and $U_{j\beta}$ to the interaction between electrons with the same spin. The first contribution in Eq. [18] arises from the change in electronic occupation of the orbital $|i\beta\rangle$ and the second contribution from the change of the magnetization (spin polarization) of that orbital.

## Magnetic Properties of Atomic Clusters of the Transition Elements

Because of the different local environments of the atoms in a cluster, charge transfer between noneequivalent sites can occur. However, the direct Coulomb repulsion tends to suppress charge redistribution among atoms and to approximate local neutrality (i.e., $\Delta v$ is small). The direct and exchange integrals, $U_{ij}$ and $J_{\beta ij}$, are usually parametrized. The difference between $s-s$, $s-p$, and $p-p$ direct Coulomb integrals is neglected by writing $U_{sp} = U_{pp}$, and it is assumed that $U_{sd} = U_{dd}$. The ratio $U_{sd} : U_{dd}$ of the magnitudes of $U_{sd}$, $U_{dd}$, and $U_{dd}$ can be taken from Hartree-Fock calculations. The absolute value of $U_{dd}$ can be estimated by independent method, from instance, atomic spectroscopic data. Typical values for $U_{sd} : U_{dd}$ ratios are $0.32 : 0.42 : 1$ for Fe and $U_{dd} = 5.40$ eV$^{15}$ The direct Coulomb integral between $d$ electrons, $U_{dd}$, determines $U_{sd}$ and $U_{sd}$. The magnetic properties of clusters are not very sensitive to the precision of $U_{dd}$ because charge transfer $\Delta v$ is typically small. In most cases, all exchange integrals involving $s$ and $p$ electrons are neglected and the $d$ exchange integral $J$ is determined to reproduce the bulk magnetic moment. Typical $J$ for Cr, Fe, Ni, and Co are between 0.5 and 1.0 eV$^{15,17,18}$

The third term in Eq. [16] represents the Coulomb shifts resulting from electronic charge transfer between atoms. The quantity $\Delta v_j = v_j - v_j^0$, where $v_j = \sum_{\beta}(\langle n_{\beta j}\rangle+\langle n_{\beta j}\rangle)$ is the total electronic charge on atom $j$ and $v_j^0$ is the reference value. In Eq. [16], the Coulomb integrals

$$V_{i\alpha j\beta} = \int \psi_{i\alpha}(r)\psi_{j\beta}(r)\frac{1}{|r-r'|}\psi_{j\beta}(r')\psi_{i\alpha}(r')d^3rd^3r'$$

have been approximated as $V_{ij} = e^2/R_{ij}$. Finally, the last term in Eq. [16] takes into account the energy level corrections from nonorthogonality effects$^{15,19}$ and from the crystal field potential of the neighboring atoms$^8$ which are approximately proportional to the local atomic coordination $Z_i$. The constants $\Omega_{ij}(\alpha=s,p,d)$ are orbital-dependent and can be obtained from the difference between the energy levels (that is, excluding Coulomb shifts) of the isolated atom and the bulk. These constants can have different signs for $s-p$ orbitals, for instance, V et al.$^{15}$ obtained $\Omega_s = 0.31$ eV, $\Omega_p = 0.48$ eV, and $\Omega_d = -0.10$ eV for Fe, which means the overlap dominates the orbital shifts for $s$ and $p$ electrons, where the dominant contribution for more localized $d$ electrons is the negative crystal field shift. One can also model, through this term, the energy levels rising from changes in the bond length associated with a lowering of the coordination number$^{8,15}$

The spin-dependent local electronic occupations $\hat{n}_{i\sigma}$ and the local magnetic moments $\mu_i = \sum_{\sigma}(\langle\hat{n}_{i\sigma}\rangle - \langle\hat{n}_{i\bar{\sigma}}\rangle)$ are self-consistently determined.
