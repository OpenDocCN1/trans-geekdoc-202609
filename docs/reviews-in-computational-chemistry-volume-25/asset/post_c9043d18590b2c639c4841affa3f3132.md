Recently, a modified zeroth-order Hamiltonian has been suggested by Ghigo and coworkers$^{20}$ to accomplish this, which removes the systematic error and considerably implies both dissociation and excitation energies. Equation [5] can be written approximately as an interpolation between the two extreme cases

$$F_{pp} = \frac{1}{2}(D_{pp}(IP)_p + (2 - D_{pp})(EA)_p) [6]$$

where $D_{pp}$ is the diagonal of the one-particle density matrix for orbital $p$. The formula is correct for $D_{pp} = 0$ and 2 for a singly occupied shell. Now assume that when one excites an orbital, its energy to be replaced by $-EA$. This is achieved by adding a shift to Eq[6].

$$\sigma_{p}^{(EA)} = \frac{1}{2}D_{pp}(IP)_p - (EA)_p [7]$$

Contrarily, if one excites out of this orbital, its energy to be replaced by $-IP$. The corresponding shift is

$$\sigma_{p}^{(IP)} = \frac{1}{2}(2 - D_{pp})(IP)_p - (EA)_p [8]$$

The definitions of $(IP)_p$ and $(EA)_p$ are not straightforward. Therefore, $(IP)_p - (EA)_p$ was replaced with an average shift parameter $\epsilon$. The two shifts are

$$\sigma_{p}^{(EA)} = \frac{1}{2}D_{pp}\epsilon [9]$$

$$\sigma_{p}^{(IP)} = \frac{1}{2}(2 - D_{pp})\epsilon [10]$$

A large number of tests showed that a value of 0.25 for $\epsilon$ was optimal. The mean error in the dissociation for 49 diatomic molecules was reduced from 0.2 eV to 0.1 eV. Using an average $\epsilon$ was particularly impressive for tri-bonded molecules: The average error for $N_2$, $P_2$, and $As_2$ was reduced from 0.45 eV to less than 0.15 eV. Similar absolute improvements were obtained for excitation and ionization energies.$^{20}$

Perturbation theory like MP2 or CASPT should be used only when the perturbation is small. Orbitals that give rise to large coefficients for the states in Eq. [1] should be included in the active space.

## The Multiconfigurational Approach (257-258) 

Large coefficients in the first-order wave function are the result of small zeroth-order energy differences between the CAS reference state and one or more of the excited functions. We call these functions intruders. If the interaction term $V_{ij}$ is small, one can remove the intruder using a level shift technique that does not affect the contributions for others.$^{21-23}$

The reference (zeroth-order) function in the CASPT2 method is a predicted CASSCF wave function. The coefficients in the CAS are thus fixed and not affected by the perturbation operator. This choice of the reference function often works well when the other solutions to the CAS Hamiltonian are well separated in energy, but there may be a problem when two or more electronic states of the same symmetry are close in energy. Such situations one then expect the dynamic correlation to also affect the reference function. This problem can be handled by extending the perturbation method to include electronic states that are close in energy. This extension, called the *Multi-State* CASPT2 method, has been implemented by Finley and coworkers.$^{24}$ We will briefly summarize the main aspects of the *Multi-State* CASPT2 method.

Assume several CAS wave functions, $\Psi_i, i=1, N$, obtained in an average calculation. The resulting (single state) CASPT2 functions are $\chi_i, i=1, N$. The functions $\Psi_i + \chi_i$ are used as basis functions in a “variational” calculation where all terms higher than second order are neglected. The corresponding effective Hamiltonian has the elements

$$(H)_{ij} = \delta_{ij}E_i + \langle\Psi_i|H|\chi_j\rangle$$

where $E_i$ is the CASSCF energy. This Hamiltonian is not symmetric, and in practice, a symmetrized version is used, which may cause problems if the density of states is large, so it is then advisable to extend the active space. For transition metal compounds, one can expect the dynamic correlation to also affect the reference function.

## Relativity

Nonrelativistic quantum chemistry has been discussed so far. But transition metal (starting already from first row) and actinide compounds cannot be treated theoretically without a detailed account of relativity. Can this be done with enough accuracy for chemical applications? Much work has been done in recent years to develop a relativistic treatment. The multiconfigurational approach for wave functions is difficult and would, if pursued, lead to lengthy and complex calculations, which allow only small molecules. It is possible, however, to transform the four-component operator to a two-component form where one analyzes only the most important of these terms. The most widely used transformation of this type leads to the Douglas-Kess-Hamiltonian.$27,28}$

The DKH Hamiltonian can be divided into a scalar part and a spin-orbit part. The scalar part includes the mass-velocity term and the potential close to the nucleus such that the relativistic singularity of the orbital is removed. The effect on energies is similar to that of the Darwin term, but the resulting operator is variationally stable. This part of the relativistic corrections can be easily included in a nonrelativistic treatment. Usually, only contributions to the one-electron Hamiltonian are included. For larger atoms, the scalar effects will be dominant and calculations can be safely performed by adding this term to the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian. The scalar DKH Hamiltonian has been implemented recently into the CASSCF/CASPT2 version of the multiconfigurational approach by Roos and Malqvist.$29}$

The scalar terms are only one part of the DKH Hamiltonian. Here is also a true two-component term that has the spin-orbit interaction and is therefore difficult to implement for molecular systems. However, in 1996, an effective one-electron Fock-type spin-orbit Hamiltonian was suggested by Hess and coworkers$^{30}$ that significantly simplifies the algorithm for calculation of spin-orbit matrix elements. Two-electron terms are treated as screening corrections of the dominant one-electron terms, at least for heavy elements. The atomic mean field integrals (AMFI) method is used, which, based on the short-range behavior of the spin-orbit interaction, avoids the calculation of multi-center one-electron spin-orbit integrals and reduces the integral evaluation to individual atoms, taking advantage of full spherical symmetry. The approach reduces the computational effort drastically but leads to a negligible loss of accuracy compared with, e.g., basis or correlation limitations as shown by Christensen et al.$^{31}$

The treatment of the spin-orbit part of the DKH Hamiltonian within the AMFI scheme is based on the assumption that the strongest effects of SOC arise from the interaction of electronic states that are close in energy. For these states, independent CASSCF/CASPT2 calculations are performed. The resulting CASSCF wave functions are then used as basis functions for the calculation of the spin-orbit coupling. The diagonal elements of the spin-orbit Hamiltonian can be modified to account for dynamic correlation effects on the energy by, for example, replacing the CASSCF energies with CASPT2 energies.
