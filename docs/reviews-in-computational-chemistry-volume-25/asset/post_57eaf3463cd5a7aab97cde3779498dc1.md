$V_{bond}(r_{ij}) = \frac{1}{2}k_{bond}(r_{ij}-r_{ij}^0)^2 \quad [13]$

```

$r_{ij}^0$ is an equilibrium bond length and angle, whereas $k_{bond}$, $k_{bend}$, $k_{ijkl}(n)$, and $k_{ijkl}^{soop}$ are the bond, bend, torsion, and out-of-plane bending force constants, respectively. The indices indicate which (bonded) atoms are involved in the interaction. Combined with the nonbonded parameters $q_i$, $\epsilon$, and $\sigma$, constitute the classical force field for a particular polymer.

Although there are standard force fields in the literature like AMBER,¹ OPLS-AA,² COMPASS,³ CHARMM,⁴ and PCFF⁵⁵ to name a few (see Refs. 24-28), one will typically find that they are only qualitative or at best a semi-quantitative representation of a polymer might want to study. The quantitative modeling of a given polymeric material has to start from high-level quantum chemistry calculations as the source of molecular information for force field validation and parameterization. Such calculations are not yet possible on large molecules that are representative of polymer repeat units and for oligomers. These calculations can provide the molecular geometry, partial charges, polarizabilities, and the conformational energy surface needed for accurate prediction of structural, thermodynamic, and dynamic properties of polymers. A general procedure for deriving quantum chemistry-based potentials can be found in the literature.²⁹,³¹,³² The intermolecular dispersion actions also can, to a certain extent, be determined from these calculations.

For nonpolar, simple hydrocarbon chains that we will discuss later we can employ a simple force field of the:

```
$V(\{\vec{r}\}) = \sum_{i} V(L_i) + \sum_{j} V(\theta_j) + \sum_{k} V(\Phi_k) + \sum_{n,m} V_{nb}(r_{nm}) \quad [16]$

where the sum runs over all bonds, bends, torsions, and nonbonded interacting atoms. One often does not treat the hydrogen atoms explicitly in simulations of hydrocarbons but instead unites them with the carbon atoms to which they are bound to “united atoms.” This approximation not only reduces the number of force centers for the calculation of the nonbonded interactions, but it also removes the highest frequency oscillations ($C-H$ bond length and $H-C-H$ and $H-C-C$ bond angles) from the model. This approximation works well when one wants to study structure and relaxation properties in amorphous polymers without any specific local interactions (i.e., strong electrostatic interactions or hydrogen bonding). In the latter cases, internal degrees of freedom of the united atoms and a model for their interaction may be added, but no reliable ways exist so far to determine the parameters entering such a description quantitatively for a given polymer, one generally loses the ability to obtain quantitative predictions using such models. A final approximation employed in large-scale polymer simulations is to neglect the $C-C$ bond oscillations and to perform the simulation with constrained bond lengths.³34. The approximations discussed in this paragraph are motivated by computational expediency. However, they reflect our understanding of the relevant physical processes that must be included in the computer simulation to obtain a quantitative reproduction of the structure and dynamics of a realistic polymer. Such approximations are imposed on us by the huge spread of relaxation times one has to cover in the simulation, which range from local relaxations to conformational changes of entangled chains requiring substantial computational efforts when one is striving to perform simulations in thermodynamic equilibrium. The simulation studies of dynamic processes are generally conducted using molecular dynamics (MD) methods. Equilibrating the starting configurations for these studies, however can profit from the Monte Carlo (MC) techniques where moves generating global conformational rearrangements are included.³35

# COARSE-GRAINED MODELS

Coarse-grained models neglect the chemical detail of a specific chain and include only excluded volume and topology (chain connectivity) as the properties determining universal behavior. They can be formulated for the continuum (off-lattice) as well as for a lattice. For all coarse-grained models, the repeat unit or monomer represents a section of chemically realistic chain. MD techniques are employed to study dynamics with off-lattice models, whereas MC techniques are used for the lattice models and for efficient equilibration of the continuum models. A tutorial on coarse-grained modeling can be found in this book series.³43

# Coarse-Grained Models of the Bead-Spring Type

These models retain the form of the nonbonded interaction used in chemically realistic modeling, i.e., they either an Lennard-Jones potential or of the exponential-6 type. The repulsive parts of potentials generate the necessary local excluded volume, whereas the attractive long-range parts can be used by varying solvent quality to dilute or semi-dilute solutions and to generate a reasonable equation-of-state behavior for polymeric models.

The inclusion of chain connectivity prevents polymers from crossing one another in the course of a computer simulation. In bead-spring polymers, this typically means that one has to limit the maximal (or typical) extension of a spring connecting the beads that represent the models monomers along the chain. This process is most often performed using the so-finely extensible, nonlinear elastic (FENE) type potentials⁴⁴ of Eq. [17]:

$U_F(l) = \frac{1}{2}kt_{max}\ln[1-(l/l_{max})^2] \quad 0 \leq l \leq l_{max}$

but also harmonic spring lengths with a length cut-off⁴⁵ or very force constants⁴⁶. Beyond this bond length potential, one may typically include a bending energy term to reduce local flexibility. Because the bending energy and geometry on this length scale do not derive from chemical hybridization, one typically takes the equilibrium bond angle to be $180^{\circ}$. Dihedral energy terms are generally not included in coarse-grained models. The chains are treated at mesoscopic scales as freely rotating.
