# Load-Dependency of Friction

Many macroscopic systems show an almost linear relationship between friction $F$ and load $L$

$F = \mu L$

[3] where the friction coefficient $\mu$ does not depend on the apparent area of contact. This linear dependence, which is routinely taught in introductory physics classes, is referred to as Amonton's law.¹ The origin of this relationship is the subject of a great deal of controversy.

Initial attempts to explain Amontons's law focused on the nature in which surface asperities slide past one another. The basic argument put forward was, that if asperity protrudes from the surface at some angle $	hetatheta$, force required to move past asperity is $F = Ltan(\theta)$. When averages over all asperities on a surface, $tan(\theta)$ will attain some constant value and the friction will be proportional to the load. Although this explanation seems to account for Amonton's law at first glance, it was quickly pointed out, that an asperity reaches the top of another, it will slide down the other side and the applied energy will be recovered. As, in a statistical sense, the average value of $	heta$ will be constant irrespective of the sliding direction, there is no reason to anticipate any net loss of energy, and friction should essentially be nonexistent in such a scenario. In fact, similar arguments have been used to support the possibility of low-friction situations, such as superlubricity.
