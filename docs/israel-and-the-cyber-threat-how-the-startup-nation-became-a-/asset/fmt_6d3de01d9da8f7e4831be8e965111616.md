
Important figures in Israel shared the US concerns, part of a deep and at times rancorous debate among top policymakers that played out both behind the scenes and, remarkably, on the front pages of the nation’s newspapers. The head of the Mossad, Meir Dagan, was the most vociferous opponent of a military strike among Israel’s defense chiefs, but the IDF chief of staff and the head of the ISA were similarly reticent, without at least implicit US approval.[^(120)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1220) Dagan feared that a military strike would postpone the Iranian program by no more than a matter of months, reinforce Iran’s determination to acquire nuclear weapons, push it to bury the program so far underground that it would become essentially impregnable, and end up actually accelerating the weapons development process. An attack, he later told an interviewer, “would unite the Iranian people behind the project and enable (Supreme Leader) Khamenai to say that he must get a bomb in order to defend himself against Israel. Bombing would be the stupidest thing we could do.” Dagan thus shared Obama’s hope that Olympic Games would achieve the desired delay in the Iranian program, without the condemnation, retribution, and escalation that would have almost certainly followed a kinetic attack.[^(121)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1221)

Prime Minister Netanyahu and Defense Minister Ehud Barak reportedly sought cabinet approval for an attack on at least three occasions, in 2010, 2011, and 2012, but apparently failed to muster the necessary majority when faced with the united opposition of virtually the entire defense leadership. Toward the end, Barak himself seems to have backed away from his earlier support for an attack and it is not inconceivable that Netanyahu, who had successfully forced other difficult positions through the cabinet but is known for military caution, may have done so as well, his strident public posture notwithstanding.[^(122)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1222)

The NSA and CIA were the primary players in Olympic Games on the US side, the Mossad and Unit 8200 in Israel.[^(123)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1223) The cooperative effort was not without tensions, both over tactics and strategy. In one case in 2012, Iran uncovered a series of cyber attacks on its oil industry, which the United States publicly blamed on Israel.[^(124)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1224) The overall outcome of the joint effort was, nevertheless, an unprecedented cyber campaign, starting with Flame, followed by Duqu, and culminating in Stuxnet.[^(125)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1225)

### Flame

—was a highly complex cyber intelligence operation designed to collect information about Iran’s nuclear program and a precursor to the centrifuge sabotage program conducted under Stuxnet. Flame may have started as early as December 2006 and may have lasted for over five years, continuing to gather intelligence in support of Stuxnet even after the sabotage program had begun.[^(126)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1226)

Flame was able to take control over computers and smartphones and use their built-in microphones and cameras to record audio and video, take screenshots of specific applications, log keyboard activity, capture network traffic, and send and receive commands and data. Dressed up to look like a legitimate Microsoft update, it was actually a backdoor Trojan horse.[^(127)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1227) Likely introduced via a USB stick, once the initial computer had been infected, Flame was able to spread across a local network without further instructions. To reduce the risks of discovery, it was also able to determine the type of antivirus software installed on a computer and modify itself accordingly.[^(128)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1228) Flame did not, however, have the ability to replicate itself automatically[^(129)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1229) and could only infect computers that had been knowingly targeted.[^(130)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1230) Flame was modular, meaning that it could be programed for a variety of different uses and new uses could be added over time.[^(131)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1231)

In addition to the nuclear program, Flame targeted computers at Iran’s Ministry of Petroleum, National Iranian Oil Company, Iranian Offshore Oil Company, Central Bank, and Ministry of Culture.[^(132)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1232) Although Flame focused on Iran, it also appears to have targeted thousands of computers across the Middle East and North Africa.[^(133)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1233)

Flame was discovered in 2012, following what some have claimed may have been an independent Israeli cyber attack, conducted without the United States, during a period in which the negotiations with Iran had stalled.[^(134)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1234) Whatever the case, once discovered, Flame issued a preprogrammed “kill” command, designed to remove all traces of the infection from the targeted computers.[^(135)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1235) Despite its discovery, Flame is considered to have been one of the most advanced cyber attacks to date.[^(136)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1236)

### Duqu

—like Flame, was an espionage tool and a further precursor to Stuxnet,[^(137)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1237) but appears to have been more narrowly focused on a smaller number of targets.[^(138)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1238) Apparently first deployed in 2010, Duqu shared many features with Stuxnet,[^(139)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1239) including similar command and control protocols and architecture,[^(140)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1240) but was designed solely for purposes of intelligence collection, not sabotage.[^(141)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1241) Duqu lacked the ability to self-replicate, so that each targeted computer had to be infected individually,[^(142)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1242) but was capable of downloading additional modules and functionality.[^(143)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1243)

When infected documents were opened, Duqu utilized a zero-day exploit found in Microsoft Word to insert Trojan horses into targeted systems.[^(144)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1244) Duqu stole design documents, digital certificates, private keys (central to cryptography), and passwords, captured keystrokes and screenshots, and more. In preparation for Stuxnet, Duqu specifically sought out information about the industrial control systems[^(145)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1245) used in Iran’s nuclear program. Once discovered in 2012, the server Duqu operated from was quickly shut down, and it largely removed itself from infected computers.[^(146)](#29_Notes.xhtml#oso-9780197677711-miscMatter-11-note-1246)
