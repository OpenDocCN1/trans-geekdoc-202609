
Additionally, with such numerous measurable models, it is difficult for the client to figure out which one is the most suitable. As referenced in [21], the current models are not sufficient, and the measurable models can't be good for some mind-boggling cases in genuine applications.

### 10.1.3 Spatial-and-Temporal Closeness-Based Methodologies

The spatial-and-temporal similarity-based techniques are based on the assumption that there are strong correlations between sensor information and their neighbors (spatial similarity), as well as their histories (temporal comparability). Krishnamachari and Iyengar have exploited the structural and temporal relationship of multiple sensors [22]. Jeffrey _et al._ tried to manipulate both temporal and spatial relationships to fix inaccurate reports [23]. Their techniques agree that all details within each spatial and temporal granule are uniform. The shortcoming recognition systems consider any high-value edge worth outputting as damaged and suggested a non-parametric outlier detection (NPOD) model for sensor data [24]. This system diffusely detects irregularities by testing the nearest neighbors of each sensor. Information distributions are measured by component volume function, and multifaceted variations are identified by testing heterogeneous readings. The system generates clusters of sensor readings and distinguishes variations by measuring the variance of the sensor to its neighbors. There are also some limits on this technique:
- The spatial similarity theory may not be legitimate in all cases; sensor relationships are affected by different variables, including sensor organization, the general environment, and target dynamics.
- The temporal closeness assumption may fail in some cases. Sensor reliability may decrease over time, e.g., the sensors may be damaged in harsh environments or run out of power [25].

## 10.2 Feature Recovery Methodologies

Feature recovery methods identify defective data by contrasting different features. These techniques first attempt several data features such as natural type, correlation degree, and temporal patterns, and then build classifiers to identify various kinds of flaws. As Ni established some regular features, including system features, context features, and data features [26]. They consolidated various features to characterize and distinguish regularly observed deficiencies. Ni and Pottie deployed sensors to detect the presence of arsenic in groundwater [27]. A Fault Remediation System (FRS) is developed to identify faults and recommend solutions using rule-based strategies and static thresholds for water pressure and other domain-specific features. Authors proposed a Pattern Growth Graph (PGG)-based technique to distinguish variations and filter noise across evolving clinical streams [28]. The concept of wave pattern is proposed to capture significant information in clinical data evolution and represent them concisely. Variations are identified by a wave pattern matching algorithm, and significant data changes are distinguished from noise. Authors proposed a two-phase approach to discovering inconsistencies in complex datasets [29]. The algorithm uses an efficient deterministic space partition to eliminate obvious normal occurrences and produces a small set of anomaly candidates, then checks each candidate with density-based models to determine the final outcomes. Feature-based methodologies generally perform better than other techniques, yet they are more domain-specific. Such techniques require users to provide detailed context information and carefully define defective records. Versatility and adaptiveness are serious issues preventing their application in a broader scope of CPS.

## 10.3 CPS vs. IT Systems

In our view, four key contrasts make big data modeling and analysis for CPS different from similar issues in the general-purpose computing space. The first two are:
- The tight connection of computing components with the physical world through feedback control loops.
- A thorough engineering lifecycle for critical CPS compared to standard software engineering practice for IT applications, where specialists cannot always rely on continuous software updates to fix prior issues.

These directly lead to a formalized model-based design paradigm [30, 31] exemplified by a variety of engineering tools, such as Simulink/Stateflow, Ptolemy, etc. Third, CPS frameworks exhibit many more operating modes compared to IT systems. We can consider a web service operating in different modes depending on traffic patterns, for example, day versus night, but often there are few such modes. With few operating modes, we can combine spatial data and “brute force” search to determine state transition points and learn separate models. However, this approach will not scale to numerous discrete states that are difficult to identify, just like the case for complex CPS [32]. Fourth, we also expect the role and interpretation of analysis to be different in CPS. Typically, when working with IT systems data, we can achieve good generalization with standard AI methods by expanding the state space. For example, rather than using only the number of request arrivals, we can also use its first and second derivatives as features. This may help us identify different operational modes. However, with the paradigm shift to low-cost, ubiquitous sensing, such approaches become craft—how many and which factors to analyze? What do different changes (for example, derivatives, logarithms, etc.) represent? Ad hoc analysis approaches are unlikely to significantly impact the CPS domain due to the need and entrenched custom of model-based structure and development. Because of these differences, we believe that big data modeling and analysis for CPS warrants further independent research.

### 10.4.1 Establishing Conscious Computation and Information Systems

The formal definition of cognitive integration and device administration (CACN) was given in [35]. Creating context-aware computing may be regarded as a CACN operating at higher access management levels. The data collected by sensors for their unique service shall be regarded as original data, which have been legitimately gathered from the environment as data is generated, without further processing. With raw data alone, it is challenging to analyze and interpret them, let alone the massive data generated by large-scale sensor deployment. By providing valid and efficiently understandable data, sensors need to participate in the careful registration process; that is, sensors need to store processed significant data, otherwise called “context data”, which is easily interpretable [36, 37]. A reference comparing raw sensor data and context data is insulin readings obtained by biomedical sensors on patient assemblies, which are regarded as original data. At the stage where these measurements are processed and represented as the person's blood glucose level, the dataset is indicated. The presence of the context (QoC) metric will be used to assess relevance, reliability, quality, and contextual structure [38]. Managing careful registration to protect, plan, delete, and update can be accomplished by applications themselves, using libraries and toolboxes, or middleware platforms [39]. Context data can be further classified into primary context and secondary context, which provides information on how the data was acquired. For example, reading RFID tags directly from various production parts in mechanical plants is considered primary context, while obtaining the same data from the plant's database is referred to as secondary context [40, 41].
