Early-onset CAD is defined as younger individuals (age <55 in men and <65 in women), while late-onset CAD is defined as older individuals (age ≥55 in men and ≥65 in women). 26 These analyses aimed to explore potential differences between subgroups, and the performance of the different classification models was compared across different subgroups. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the population with normal coronary arteries and including only patients with coronary plaques, or by excluding CACS of zero and including only those with a CACS greater than zero. All analyses and calculations were performed with R (version 4.4.2). The detailed libraries and packages are listed in the Supplemental Appendix. A 2-tailed P value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS

### Comparison of Baseline Characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the entire cohort. A total of 8431 patients were included (mean age, 54.73±10.21 years; 48.2% were male; 50.9% with symptomatic chest pain, with 319 MACE (3.8%) were recorded, including 34 deaths, 8 cases of AMI, 244 coronary revascularizations (202 percutaneous coronary interventions and 42 coronary artery bypass grafting), and 54 strokes, during a median follow-up of 3.68 years (interquartile range, 2.65–4.27 years). The most common cardiac symptom was atypical angina (44.6%), followed by typical chest pain (6.3%). In the CCTA examination, 76.4% of patients had normal coronary arteries, 11.1% had nonobstructive plaques, 7.7% had obstructive CAD (stenosis ≥50%) in 1 vessel, 3.0% had obstructive CAD in 2 vessels, and 1.7% had obstructive CAD in 3 vessels or the left main artery. The average total plaque burden for all patients was 6.08% (SD ±12.74%), with a noncalcified plaque burden of 5.78% (SD ±12.02%), a fibrous plaque burden of 5.04% (SD ±10.56%), a lipid plaque burden of 0.73% (SD ±1.90%), and a calcified plaque burden of 0.30% (SD ±1.63%; Table 2).

The baseline characteristics and outcomes of the training set (n=5901, 70%) and testing set (n=2530, 30%) are also detailed in Table 1. The median 10-year risk of CAD calculated by the pooled cohort equation risk score was 10.0% (interquartile range, 4.9–19.6). The cardiovascular risk factors, pooled cohort equation risk score, and MACE events in both groups were very similar, with no significant differences. The distribution of plaque burden was generally similar between the 2 cohorts (Table S3).

### Comparison of Baseline and Coronary Features Between the MACE and Non-MACE Groups

In the training set, the MACE group was significantly older than the non-MACE group (60.66±9.49 versus 54.65±10.08 years; P<0.001). The MACE group had a higher proportion of males (66.8%) and had a higher prevalence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, except for family history of CAD, body mass index, and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, compared with the non-MACE group (Table S4). The MACE group also had a higher incidence of obstructive CAD, greater stenosis rates, and a higher prevalence of high-risk plaques. In coronary artery quantitative analysis, the MACE group displayed significant differences in PVs and plaque burden across different plaque subtypes (all P<0.001). These results were similar in the testing cohort (Table 2).

### Feature Selection and Modeling Training

Using all 48 CCTA candidate variables without feature elimination, the XGBoost model exhibited superior predictive performance on both training and testing sets, achieving a C-index of 0.918 to 0.925 and an AUC of 0.905 to 0.925, thereby outperforming 5 other ML survival analysis algorithms. The comparison of different ML performance is summarized in Table 3; Table S5. The global interpretability of the XGBoost model and its top 20 most impactful features is illustrated using Shapley additive explanation value plots (Figure 1A and 1B). These key features were confirmed by clinical and imaging experts to be consistent with clinical relevance and underlying pathophysiology. A recursive feature elimination process identified 6 most important variables; the performance of the simplified final model is also detailed in Table 3; Table S5, also with XGBoost chosen as the best model. In both the training and testing cohorts, all 6 CCTA features were independently associated with a higher MACE risk, independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors and CACS (Table S6).

### Model Performance Comparison

In the training cohort, the simplified final model using 6 CCTA features (model 3) predicted MACE with an ROC and AUC of 0.903 (95% CI, 0.872–0.933). In contrast, the clinical risk factors model (model 1) had an ROC AUC of 0.788 (95% CI, 0.740–0.837), and the combined clinical risk factors+CACS model (model 2) showed an ROC AUC of 0.830 (95% CI, 0.787–0.873). Thus, the clinical+CACS was only slightly superior to the clinical model (ΔAUC, 0.042 for model 2 versus model 1; P=0.100), the CCTA model performed significantly better than the clinical model and the combined clinical+CACS model (ΔAUC, 0.115 for model 3 versus model 1; ΔAUC, 0.073 for model 3 versus model 2; P<0.001 for both). When CCTA features were added to the clinical model (model 4) and the clinical+CACS model (model 5), the ROC AUC improved to 0.915 (95% CI, 0.885–0.944) and 0.924 (95% CI, 0.897–0.950; P<0.001 for both; Figure 2A; Table S7).

After recalibrating all models, we tested their discrimination performance in the testing cohort. The simplified final model using CCTA features (model 3) predicted MACE events with an ROC and AUC of 0.899 (0.839–0.959). In contrast, the clinical risk factors model (model 1) had an ROC AUC of 0.708 (95% CI, 0.610–0.806), and the combined clinical risk factors+CACS model (model 2) showed an ROC AUC of 0.753 (95% CI, 0.662–0.844). Thus, the clinical+CACS was only slightly superior to the clinical model (ΔAUC, 0.045 for model 2 versus model 1; P=0.308). The CCTA model performed significantly better than the clinical model and the combined clinical+CACS model (ΔAUC, 0.191 for model 3 versus model 1; ΔAUC, 0.146 for model 3 versus model 2; P<0.001 for both). When CCTA features were added to the clinical model (model 4) and the clinical+CACS model (model 5), the ROC AUC improved to 0.895 (95% CI, 0.842–0.947) and 0.905 (95% CI, 0.844–0.966; P<0.001 for both; Figure 2B; Table 4).

The trend observed in the C-index parallels that of the time-dependent AUC. These findings indicate that the CCTA model exhibits a superior discriminative ability in predicting cardiovascular events, with its performance validated in the testing cohort.
