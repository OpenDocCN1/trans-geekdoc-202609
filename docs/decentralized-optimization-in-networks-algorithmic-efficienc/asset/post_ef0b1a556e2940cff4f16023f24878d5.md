
**Definition 3.1.** Given the closed convex set $\Omega_0 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p$, we designate $P_{\Omega_0}(x) = \arg\min_{s \in \Omega_0} \|x - s\|$ as the projection operator, where both $x$ and $s$ belong to the space $\mathbb{R}^p$.

Based on Definition 3.1, it is evident that for any pair of vectors, namely $\mu$ and $v$, the subsequent inequality consistently holds true:

$$\|P_{\Omega_0}(\mu) - P_{\Omega_0}(v)\| \leq \|\mu - v\|, \qquad \text{(3.4)}$$

and the optimal solution, denoted as $x^*$, is ensured to fulfill the subsequent correlation:

$$s^* = P_{\Omega_0}(s^* - \eta_0 \nabla f(s^*)), \qquad \text{(3.5)}$$

where $s^*$ represents the result of projecting the optimal solution $x^*$ of problem (3.1), while $\eta_0$ denotes a fixed positive step-size. From this, we can deduce that $s^* = x^*$.

### 3.2.1 The difference and relation with multi-objective optimization

Here, it is important to note the distinction and correlation between problem (3.1) and multi-objective optimization problems.

- **(i) Difference:** Presented as a mono-objective optimization problem, the decentralized optimization problem in (3.1) aims to minimize the global function outlined in the same equation, and it possesses a solitary optimal solution based on Assumption 3.1 (illustrated on the left side of Fig. 3.1). In contrast to the problem stated in (3.1), multi-objective optimization problems strive to simultaneously minimize numerous objective functions expressed as $f(x) = (f^1(x), f^2(x), \dots, f^m(x))^\mathrm{T} (\mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^m)$ [44–46]. These problems yield a collection of solutions known as Pareto optimal solutions (displayed in the center of Fig. 3.1). Additionally, multi-objective optimization problems emphasize the Pareto frontier (depicted on the right side of Fig. 3.1), a concept not addressed in this work.

- **(ii) Relation:** As discussed in numerous studies [43–46], a multi-objective optimization problem can be transformed into a mono-objective optimization problem, similar to the one addressed in this work, namely problem (3.1). For instance, the goal programming method [47] and the weighted sum technique [48] have been introduced to facilitate this conversion. Furthermore, from the viewpoint of objective and local functions, distinct objective functions can be assigned to different nodes, serving as local functions. Subsequently, the decision-maker's preferences can be expressed through the topological connections between these nodes [43,49]. By adopting this methodology, we can utilize a row-stochastic matrix (representing an undirected network) in lieu of a doubly stochastic matrix, enabling us to solve multi-objective optimization problems effectively.

In conclusion, it bears mentioning that the primary objective of this undertaking is to effectively tackle the (mono-objective) decentralized optimization problem denoted by (3.1), which holds significant relevance in numerous engineering scenarios. However, it is important to clarify that the focus of this endeavor does not encompass multi-objective optimization problems. We intend to address multi-objective optimization problems via a decentralized optimization algorithm in our forthcoming projects.

## 3.3 Algorithm construction

To decrease the margin of error introduced by the stochastic gradient algorithm, which can enhance computational efficiency, prior scholars have suggested the variance-reduction method (VR) [20–22]. To minimize the inaccuracy stemming from inadequate agreement among various nodes, past researchers have introduced a gradient tracking method (GT) [50,51]. Leveraging these two strategies, certain scholars have put forward the GT-VR algorithmic structure, noted for its high computational efficiency and precise linear convergence speed, particularly when the step-size is adequately small, as exemplified in [19]. To address optimization problems confined to a closed convex set, the researchers in [26] employed the projection operator to devise an algorithm called GT-CDGD. However, there is still considerable room for enhancing its computational efficiency. In order to enhance this efficiency, we integrate the projection operator with the variance-reduction method, presenting a new projected decentralized variance-reduction algorithm (P-DVR) tailored for constrained decentralized optimization problems. The iterative process at node i is exhibited below, with \(\eta > 0\) representing a fixed step-size:

```
\(x_{k+1}^i = P_{\Omega_0}[\sum_{j=1}^m a^{ij} x_k^j] - \eta y_k^i,\) (3.6a)

\(s_{k+1}^i = P_{\Omega_0}[x_{k+1}^i],\) (3.6b)

\(y_{k+1}^i = \sum_{j=1}^m a^{ij} y_k^j + g^i(s_{k+1}^i) - g^i(s_k^i).\) (3.6c)
```

In Algorithm 1, each node \(i\) maintains a table of component gradients denoted as \(\{ \nabla f^{i,j}(t_k^{i,j}) \}_{j=1}^{n^i}\) for the purpose of computing the gradient estimator \(g^i(s_k^i)\). Here, \(t_k^{i,j}\) represents the most recent iterative state pertaining to the \(j\)th data point prior to

```
Algorithm 1 A Projected Decentralized Variance-Reduction Algorithm: for node \( i \in \mathcal{V} \).

1: **Initialization:** Set \( x_0^i = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^p \), \( s_0^i = P_{\Omega_0}(x_0^i) \), \( t_0^{i,j} = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^p \), \( g^i(s_0^i) = \nabla f(s_0^i) \in \mathbb{R}^p \), \( y_0^i = g^i(s_0^i) \in \mathbb{R}^p \), and \( t_0^i = [\nabla f^{i,1}(s_0^i) \dots \nabla f^{i,n^i}(s_0^i)]^\text{T} \), where \( p \) is the dimension of \( x \).
2: for \( k = 1, 2, \dots \) do
3: Update the state \( x_k^i \) via (3.6a).
4: Compute the auxiliary variable \( s_k^i \) via (3.6b).
5: Choose \( \chi_k^i \) uniformly and randomly from \( \{1, \dots, n^i\} \).
6: Compute the local gradient estimator \( g^i(s_k^i) = \nabla f^{i, \chi_k^i}(s_k^i) - \nabla f^{i, \chi_k^i}(t_k^{i, \chi_k^i}) + \frac{1}{n^i} \sum_{j=1}^{n^i} \nabla f^{i,j}(t_k^{i,j}) \).
7: If \( j = \chi_k^i \), then \( t_k^j = s_k^i \), else \( t_k^j = t_k^{i, \chi_k^i} \).
8: Compute the local gradient tracker \( y_k^i \) via (3.6c).
9: **end for**
```

iteration \( k \), while \( \nabla f^{i,j}(t_k^{i,j}) \) signifies the corresponding latest component gradient. At node \( i \), the \( \chi_k^i \)th component gradient, specifically \( \nabla f^{i, \chi_k^i}(t_k^{i, \chi_k^i}) \), is chosen uniformly and at random from this gradient table during each iteration \( k \). Following this selection, the local gradient estimate is then derived by

```
\( g^i(s_k^i) = \nabla f^{i, \chi_k^i}(s_k^i) - \nabla f^{i, \chi_k^i}(t_k^{i, \chi_k^i}) + \frac{1}{n^i} \sum_{j=1}^{n^i} \nabla f^{i,j}(t_k^{i,j}) \).
```

Upon calculating \( g^i(s_k^i) \), all gradient table entries remain unaltered, with the exception of updating the \( \chi_k^i \)th entry to \( \nabla f^{i, \chi_k^i}(s_k^i) \).

> **Remark 3.1.** For large-scale data application scenarios, our work stands out due to its enhanced computational efficiency, an aspect that is noticeably absent in [26]. The problems encountered in our research can be summarized as follows: 
> - (i) Achieving a convergence rate comparable to the existing unconstrained algorithm [19] is demanding, primarily because we have incorporated the projection operator to accommodate the closed convex set constraint. 
> - (ii) The theoretical analysis of our novel algorithm is more complex than that of an unconstrained optimization algorithm. This complexity arises from the introduction of the variance-reduction technique, which we have employed to elevate computational efficiency.

## 3.4 Convergence analysis

To facilitate analysis, we define \( x_k = [x_k^1, \dots, x_k^m]^\text{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p} \), \( s_k = [s_k^1, \dots, s_k^m]^\text{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p} \), \( y_k = [y_k^1, \dots, y_k^m]^\text{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p} \), \( \nabla F(s_k) = [\nabla f^1(s_k^1), \dots, \nabla f^m(s_k^m)]^\text{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p} \),

$g(s_k) = [g^1(s_k^1), \dots, g^m(s_k^m)]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}, \bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T x_k \in \mathbb{R}^p, \bar{y}_k = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T y_k \in \mathbb{R}^p, \nabla \bar{F}(s_k) = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T \nabla F(s_k) \in \mathbb{R}^p, \bar{s}_k = P_{\Omega_0}(\bar{x}_k) \in \mathbb{R}^p, \Omega = \Omega_0^m, \text{ and } P_{\Omega_0}(\bar{x}_k) = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T P_{\Omega}(\mathbf{1}_m \bar{x}_k) \in \mathbb{R}^p$. The condensed representation of the iterative steps involved in the P-DVR algorithm, denoted by expressions (3.6a)–(3.6c), is presented below:

$x_{k+1} = P_{\Omega}[Ax_k] - \eta y_k, \quad (3.7a)$
$s_{k+1} = P_{\Omega}[x_{k+1}], \quad (3.7b)$
$y_{k+1} = Ay_k + g(s_{k+1}) - g(s_k). \quad (3.7c)$

Provided with the initial value $x_0$, based on the condensed iterative procedure outlined in (3.7) and taking into account that $A^\infty = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m \mathbf{1}_m^T$, we obtain

$A^\infty x_{k+1} = \bar{x}_{k+1} = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T P_{\Omega}(Ax_k) - \eta \bar{y}_k, \quad (3.8a)$
$A^\infty y_{k+1} = \bar{y}_{k+1} = \bar{y}_k + \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T g(s_{k+1}) - \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T g(s_k) = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T g(s_{k+1}) = \bar{g}(s_{k+1}), \quad (3.8b)$
$\mathbb{E}[\bar{y}_{k+1}] = \frac{1}{m} \mathbf{1}_m^T \nabla F(s_{k+1}), \quad (3.8c)$
$\bar{s}_k = P_{\Omega_0}[\bar{x}_k]. \quad (3.8d)$

To depict the impact of the projection operator at the optimal solution, we introduce the subsequent variables based on (3.5) to elucidate the characteristics of $x^*$ as the decision vector $x$ attains $x^*$:

\(\begin{cases} x^* = P_{\Omega_0}(x^*) - \eta \nabla f(P_{\Omega_0}(x^*)), \\ s^* = P_{\Omega_0}(x^*), \\ x^* = s^* - \eta \nabla f(s^*). \end{cases}\)
