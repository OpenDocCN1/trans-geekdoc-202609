
$$\le n\mathbb{E}[||\sum_{i=1}^n \|y_{i,k}\|^2 + 2\sum_{i=1}^n \langle y_{i,k}, \frac{g_{i,k}}{y_{i,k}} \rangle + \sum_{i=1}^n \|\frac{g_{i,k}}{y_{i,k}}\|^2 ||]$$

$$\le 2n^2 m b_{\max}^2 + n^2 \theta^2 L^2 + 2n \theta L \sqrt{2m} b_{\max} = M_2.$$ (5.27)

Since $\alpha_k = \alpha_0 / \sqrt{k+1}$, one has $(1/\alpha_k) < 2\sqrt{K}/\alpha_0$ and $\sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{k-1} \le \sum_{k=1}^K (\alpha_0 / \sqrt{k}) \le 2\alpha_0 \sqrt{K}$. Therefore based on Eqs. (5.19) and (5.25)-(5.27), we can conclude that

$$\phi_1 \le (M_2 \alpha_0 + \frac{2h(x^*)}{\alpha_0}) \sqrt{K} + M_3,$$ (5.28)

where

$$M_3 = M_1 + \mathcal{H}_{\Omega}^h(-\bar{z}_1, \alpha_0) + \langle \bar{z}_1, x^* \rangle.$$

Alternatively, we proceed to examine $\phi_2$. Based on Assumption 5.2, we consequently obtain

$$\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i=1}^n (f_{i,k}(x_{j,k}) - f_{i,k}(x_{i,k})) + \langle g_{i,k}, x_{i,k} - \bar{x}_k \rangle]$$

$$\le \mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i=1}^n L \|x_{j,k} - x_{i,k}\| + L \|x_{i,k} - \bar{x}_k\|]$$

$$\le \mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K 3nL \max_{i \in \mathcal{V}} \|x_{i,k} - \bar{x}_k\|].$$ (5.29)

Based on Lemma 5.3, it arrives at

$$\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K 3nL \max_{i \in \mathcal{V}} \|x_{i,k} - \bar{x}_k\|]$$

$$= 3nL \mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K \max_{i \in \mathcal{V}} \|\Pi_{\Omega}^h(z_{i,k}, \alpha_{k-1}) - \Pi_{\Omega}^h(\bar{z}_k, \alpha_{k-1})\|]$$

$$\le 3nL \mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{k-1} \max_{i \in \mathcal{V}} \|z_{i,k} - \bar{z}_k\|]$$

$$= 3nL \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{k-1} \mathbb{E}[\max_{i \in \mathcal{V}} \|z_{i,k} - \bar{z}_k\|].$$ (5.30)

Hence, in accordance with (5.30) and Lemma 5.2, we can obtain

$$\phi_2 \le 3nL \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{k-1} \zeta_k \le M_4 \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{k-1} \le 2M_4 \alpha_0 \sqrt{K},$$ (5.31)

where

$$M_4 = 3nL\big(\frac{nC\theta L + \sqrt{2m}b_{\max}}{1 - \varpi} + C \sum_{i=1}^n ||z_{i,1}|| + 2\theta L\big).$$

According to (5.28) and (5.31), it yields

$$\bar{R}_{j,K} \le \phi_1 + \phi_2 \le (M_2\alpha_0 + \frac{2h(x^*)}{\alpha_0} + 2M_4\alpha_0)\sqrt{K} + M_3,$$

which completes the proof.

### Remark 5.5.
According to Theorem 5.1, it is evident that Algorithm 1 is capable of attaining the anticipated regret at a sublinear rate of $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{K})$. This signifies that every node will progressively drive its expected regret towards zero as the time horizon $K$ increases, indicating that $\lim_{K\to\infty} \bar{R}_{j,K}/K$ approaches 0. Furthermore, Theorem 5.1 also implies that the magnitude of $b_{\max}$ has a direct impact on the precision of optimization achieved by Algorithm 1, assuming constant system parameters. Specifically, as $b_{\max}$ rises (indicating a lower privacy level $\epsilon_k$ at a set sensitivity, as will be elaborated in the subsequent subsection), the algorithm's optimization accuracy diminishes, given unchanging system parameters.

### Remark 5.6.
It appears that the zero-sum correlated perturbation approach mentioned in references [45,55] could potentially be integrated into Algorithm 1, replacing the differential privacy method, to enhance the algorithm's efficiency while maintaining privacy. Nevertheless, this integration is not straightforward. Algorithm 1 operates within a directed network and utilizes row-stochastic matrices, complicating the introduction of noises in a zero-sum correlated perturbation scheme, as these noises must inherently satisfy the network's noise balance. Furthermore, the privacy-preserving analysis presented in [45,55] relies on stronger assumptions regarding network topology, rendering it less resilient compared to the differential privacy analysis detailed in the subsequent subsection.

## 5.4.3 Differential privacy analysis

In this subsection, our main objective is to demonstrate that Algorithm 1 can attain $\epsilon$-differential privacy. Imagine a scenario where certain hostile nodes are actively monitoring sensitive data exchanged among nodes and deliberately attempting to deduce their privacy. Differential privacy's core aim is to conceal the delicate information of nodes, thus rendering hostile nodes unable to discern the authentic node data. Additionally, according to Definition 5.3, sensitivity gauges the amount of noise introduced by assessing the effect of a single node's data within Algorithm 1's dataset. Therefore to ensure $\epsilon$-differential privacy, we estimate the necessary noise level by limiting the sensitivity of our proposed algorithm.

### Lemma 5.5.
Based on Assumptions 5.1 and 5.2, $\Delta_k$ is bounded by

$$\Delta_k \le 2L\theta\sqrt{m},$$

where $\theta$ originates from Lemma 5.1, while $m$ denotes the dimensionality of the variable $z_i$ for each $i$ belonging to the set $\mathcal{V}$.

Proof. Based on Definition 5.3, there are two neighboring datasets, specifically, $U = \bigcup_{k=1}^K U_k$ and $U' = \bigcup_{k=1}^K U'_k$, in which $U_k$ has the ability to shape and define each local loss function $f_{i,k}$ for all $i$ in the set $\mathcal{V}$. At any given time $k$, $U_k$ and $U'_k$ can be seen as the inputs for the algorithm denoted as $\mathcal{B}$. Consequently, $z'_{i,k}$ and $z_{i,k}$ can be viewed as the respective outputs from executing $\mathcal{B}(U')$ and $\mathcal{B}(U)$. It is important to note that the information exchanged between nodes, when intercepted by hostile nodes, remains unchanged, meaning $\tilde{z}_{i,k}$ equals $\tilde{z}'_{i,k}$. Therefore when coupled with Eq. (5.7), we can deduce the following inferences:

$$||z_{i,k+1} - z'_{i,k+1}||_1 \le || \frac{g_{i,k}}{y_{i,i,k}} ||_1 + || \frac{g'_{i,k}}{y_{i,i,k}} ||_1$$

$$\le \theta\sqrt{m} (||g_{i,k}|| + ||g'_{i,k}||)$$

$$\le 2L\theta\sqrt{m},$$ (5.32)

where the first inequality employs the attribute of 1-norm. Based on the flexible characteristic of the neighboring datasets $U_k$ and $U'_k$, Definition 5.3 implies that

$$\Delta_k = \sup_{U_k, U'_k: Adj(U_k, U'_k)} ||\mathcal{B}(U_k) - \mathcal{B}(U'_k)||_1 \le 2L\theta\sqrt{m},$$

which arrives at the desired result.

Lemma 5.5 primarily demonstrates that the sensitivity of Algorithm 1 is influenced by the dimension $m$ of the variable $z_{i,k}, y_{i,i,k}$, and the parameter $L$. The theorem presented below establishes that Algorithm 1 ensures $\epsilon$-differential privacy.

### Theorem 5.2.
Bear in mind that $\gamma_{i,k}$ belongs to $\mathbb{R}^m$ for all $i$ in the set $\mathcal{V}$ and $k$ ranging from 1 to $K$, as shown in Eq. (5.6). This equation also involves the pertinent parameter $b_k$, defined as $\Delta_k/\epsilon_k$, with $\epsilon_k$ having a positive value. Given the conditions stated in Assumptions 5.1 and 5.2, Algorithm 1 ensures $\epsilon_k$-differential privacy. Additionally, Algorithm 1 maintains $\hat{\epsilon}$-differential privacy across the entire time span of $K$, where $\hat{\epsilon} = \sum_{k=1}^K (\Delta_k / b_k)$.

Proof. Let us introduce the notation $z_k$ as the transpose of the vector $[z_{1,k}, ..., z_{n,k}]$ belonging to $\mathbb{R}^{nm}$, and similarly, let $z'_k$ represent the transpose of $[z'_{1,k}, ..., z'_{n,k}]$, which also lies in $\mathbb{R}^{nm}$. Drawing from Definition 5.3, we can deduce that

$$||z_k - z'_k||_1 \le \Delta_k.$$ (5.33)

Observing that both $z_k$ and $z'_k$ belong to the space $\mathbb{R}^{nm}$, and invoking the characteristics of the 1-norm, we can infer that

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{t=1}^m |z_{i,k}(t) - z'_{i,k}(t)| = ||z_k - z'_k||_1 \le \Delta_k,$$

where $z'_{i,k}(t)$ and $z_{i,k}(t)$ represent the $t$th element of $z'_{i,k}$ and $z_{i,k}$, respectively. It is worth noting that the observations made by malicious nodes during the executions $\mathcal{B}(U'_k)$ and $\mathcal{B}(U_k)$ are identical, meaning $\tilde{z}_{i,k}$ equals $\tilde{z}'_{i,k}$. Subsequently, aligning with the reasoning presented in Proposition 1 of reference [23], it suffices to show that

$$\prod_{i=1}^n \prod_{t=1}^m \frac{[\tilde{z}_{i,k}(t) - z_{i,k}(t)]}{[\tilde{z}'_{i,k}(t) - z'_{i,k}(t)]}$$

$$\le \exp(\frac{||z'_{i,k} - z_{i,k}||_1}{b_k}) \le \exp(\frac{\Delta_k}{b_k}) = e^{\epsilon_k}.$$

Additionally, it can be inferred that

$$\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U) \in S] = \prod_{k=1}^K \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U_k) \in S].$$

Hence, it can be concluded that

$$\prod_{k=1}^K \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U_k) \in S] \le \prod_{k=1}^K \exp(\frac{\Delta_k}{b_k}) \cdot \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U'_k) \in S],$$

which according to $\hat{\epsilon} = \sum_{k=1}^K (\Delta_k / b_k)$ shows that

$$\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U) \in S] \le \exp(\hat{\epsilon}) \cdot \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U') \in S].$$

Based on Definition 5.2, Theorem 5.2 is derived.

Drawing from Theorem 5.2, it becomes evident that both the parameter $b_k$ and sensitivity $\Delta_k$ play a role in determining the degree of differential privacy. Specifically, $\Delta_k$ exhibits a direct correlation with privacy levels, whereas $b_k$ demonstrates an inverse correlation with privacy at any given time $k$. It is worth noting that adjusting the sensitivity $\Delta_k$ poses challenges, primarily because it is solely influenced by the dimension $m$ of the vectors $z_{i,k}$ and $y_{ii,k}$, as well as the Lipschitz parameter $L$. Consequently, careful consideration must be given to selecting the parameter $b_k$ to ensure the attainment of our desired $\epsilon_k$-differential privacy. Furthermore, Theorem 5.2 clarifies that the cumulative parameter $\hat{\epsilon}$ represents the summation of individual $\epsilon_k$ values across $K$ iterations. This implies that as iterations progress, the level of privacy gradually diminishes. Intuitively, the disclosure of more sensitive information becomes inevitable after $K$ iterations in comparison to a single iteration at any specific time $k$, thereby elevating the risk of potential privacy breaches.
