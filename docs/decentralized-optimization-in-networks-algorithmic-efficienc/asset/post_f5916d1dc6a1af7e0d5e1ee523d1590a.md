
Theorem 6.1 demonstrates that Algorithm 1 is capable of attaining an expected regret that diminishes at a sublinear rate, specifically $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})$. This signifies that, as the time horizon $T$ progresses, each node will gradually drive its expected regret towards zero, ensuring that the average regret per time step, denoted as $\bar{R}_T^j / T$, converges to zero in the limit as $T$ approaches infinity.

**Remark 6.6.** The upper limit on regret, as stated in Theorem 6.1, is contingent upon various factors, including the parameter $b_{\max}$, the constrained magnitude of subgradients, and the dimensionality of nodes' decisions. Notably, the presence of $b_{\max}$ is confined to select components within $O_1$. Additionally, it is crucial to highlight that $b_t^1$ exhibits an inverse relationship with the privacy-preservation level. Consequently, these specified elements of $O_1$ can be interpreted as convergence penalties. Fundamentally, Theorem 6.1 elegantly captures the inherent tension between optimizing for accuracy and preserving privacy, highlighting a clear tradeoff between the two objectives.

### 6.4.3 Differential privacy analysis

In this subsection, our primary objective is to establish that Algorithm 1 is capable of achieving $\epsilon$-differential privacy. To this end, we initially present a pivotal lemma of the sensitivity $\Delta_t$ of Algorithm 1, which serves as a cornerstone for our differential privacy analysis. Subsequently, we rigorously demonstrate that Algorithm 1 effectively ensures $\epsilon$-differential privacy.

As nodes within the network engage in communication and coordination, there exists a risk of collusion among malicious nodes, aiming to intercept local subgradients containing vital information from honest nodes. The core objective of differential privacy is to obfuscate sensitive node data, rendering it impossible for malicious actors to discern genuine node information. Furthermore, by Definition 6.3, we can ascertain that the extent of noise injected is determined by placing an upper bound on the sensitivity of Algorithm 1.

**Lemma 6.5.** Given the validity that Assumptions 6.1 and 6.2 hold. The sensitivity of Algorithm 1 is bounded by

$$\Delta_t \le 2m^{\frac{3}{2}} K \omega,$$

where $\omega$ and $K$ are given in Lemma 6.2.

**Proof.** According to Definition 6.3, we have two neighboring datasets, specifically $U = \bigcup_{t=1}^T U_t$ and $\hat{U} = \bigcup_{t=1}^T \hat{U}_t$, where $U_t$ plays a pivotal role in shaping each local loss function $f_t^i$ for $i \in \mathcal{V}$. At any given time $t$, $U_t$, and $\hat{U}_t$ serve as inputs to the algorithm $\mathcal{B}$, generating outputs $\hat{q}_t^i$ and $q_t^i$, respectively, corresponding to the executions of $\mathcal{B}(\hat{U}_t)$ and $\mathcal{B}(U_t)$. It is crucial to observe that the intercepted information exchanged between nodes by malicious entities remains consistent, denoted as $\hat{q}_t^i = \hat{q}_t^i$. Consequently, incorporating (6.12), we arrive at the subsequent conclusions:

```
\|q_{t+1}^i - \hat{q}_{t+1}^i\|_1 \le \|\frac{\hat{g}_t^i}{\nu_{t+1}^i}\|_1 + \|\frac{\hat{g}_t^i}{\hat{\nu}_{t+1}^i}\|_1
\le \omega\sqrt{m}(\|\hat{g}_t^i\| + \|\hat{\hat{g}}_t^i\|)
\le 2Km^{\frac{3}{2}}\omega, (6.35)
```

where the second inequality utilizes the characteristic of the 1-norm. Drawing from Definition 6.3 and considering the arbitrary nature of the neighboring datasets $U_t$ and $\hat{U}_t$, one arrives at the conclusion that

```
\Delta_t = \sup_{U_t, \hat{U}_t : \text{Adj}(U_t, \hat{U}_t)} \|q_t^i - \hat{q}_t^i\|_1 \le 2Km^{\frac{3}{2}}\omega,
```

which deduces the desired result.

Lemma 6.5 underscores the fact that two crucial factors, specifically the dimensionality $m$ of the variable $q_t^i$ and the parameter $K$, along with the variable $v_{t+1}^i$, contribute to shaping the sensitivity of Algorithm 1. The subsequent theorem underscores the capability of Algorithm 1 to ensure $\epsilon$-differential privacy.

**Theorem 6.2.** Recall the sequence $\{o_t^i\}_{t=1}^T$ defined in (6.8), involving the pertinent parameter $c_t = \frac{\delta_t}{\gamma_t^i}$, with the condition that $\gamma_t > 0$. Given the validity of Assumptions 6.1 and 6.2, Algorithm 1 ensures $\gamma_t$-differential privacy at each time step. Additionally, Algorithm 1 maintains $\bar{\gamma}$-differential privacy across the entire time span $T$, where $\bar{\gamma} = \sum_{t=1}^T \left( \frac{\delta_t}{c_t} \right)$.

**Proof.** Define $q_t = [q_t^1, ..., q_t^n]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{nm}$ and $\hat{q}_t = [\hat{q}_t^1, ..., \hat{q}_t^n]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{nm}$. Utilizing Definition 6.3, we can draw the conclusion that

```
\|q_t - \hat{q}_t\|_1 \le \Delta_t. (6.36)
```

Observing that both $q_t$ and $\hat{q}_t$ reside within the space $\mathbb{R}^{nm}$, leveraging the characteristic of the 1-norm, we can infer that

```
\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{y=1}^m |q_t^i(y) - \hat{q}_t^i(y)| = \|q_t - \hat{q}_t\|_1 \le \Delta_t, (6.37)
```

where the $y$th elements of $q_t^i$ and $\hat{q}_t^i$ are individually referred to as $q_t^i(y)$ and $\hat{q}_t^i(y)$, respectively. It is noteworthy that the observations made by the malicious nodes regarding the executions $\mathcal{B}(\hat{U}'_t)$ and $\mathcal{B}(U_t)$ are identical, implying $\tilde{q}^i_t \equiv \hat{q}^i_t$. Building upon the rationale presented in Proposition 1 of [29], it suffices to establish that

```
\prod_{i=1}^n \prod_{y=1}^m \frac{[\tilde{q}^i_t(y) - q^i_t(y)]}{[\hat{q}^i_t(y) - q^i_t(y)]} \le \prod_{i=1}^n \prod_{y=1}^m \exp(\frac{|q^i_t(y) - \hat{q}^i_t(y)|}{b_t}) = \exp(\frac{||\hat{q}_t - q_t||_1}{b_t}) \le \exp(\frac{\Delta_t}{b_t}) = e^{\epsilon_t}. \quad (6.38)
```

In addition, one yields that

```
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U) \in S] = \prod^T_{t=1} \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U_t) \in S]. \quad (6.39)
```

Thus it deduces that

```
\prod^T_{t=1} \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U_t) \in S] \le \prod^T_{t=1} \exp(\frac{\Delta_t}{b_t}) \cdot \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\hat{U}_t) \in S], \quad (6.40)
```

which based on $\hat{\epsilon} = \sum^T_{t=1} (\Delta_t / b_t)$ derives that

```
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U) \in S] \le \exp(\hat{\epsilon}) \cdot \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\hat{U}) \in S].
```

Pursuant to Definition 6.2, the conclusions of Theorem 6.2 are derived.

According to Theorem 6.2, it becomes evident that the parameter $b_t$ and the sensitivity $\Delta_t$ play pivotal roles in determining the privacy level. Specifically, $\Delta_t$ exhibits a direct proportionality with the privacy level, whereas $b_t$ inversely correlates with it at a given time $t$. Lemma 6.5 underscores the intricacy of adjusting $\Delta_t$, thus necessitating a meticulous selection of $b_t$ to ensure the desired $\epsilon_t$-differential privacy. Furthermore, Theorem 6.2 clarifies that the cumulative privacy budget $\hat{\epsilon}$ is the summation of $\epsilon_t$ across $T$ iterations. This intuitively suggests that as compared to a solitary iteration at time $T$, performing $T$ iterations reveals more sensitive information, thereby amplifying the risk of private data exposure.

**Remark 6.7.** Indeed, Algorithm 1 harbors potential for performance enhancement, particularly in enhancing communication and computational efficiency. Drawing inspiration from recent advancements cited in [2,55], an event-based triggering mechanism can be integrated into Algorithm 1 to optimize communication efficiency. This approach entails that information exchange between network nodes occurs selectively, rather than continuously, with each node dynamically deciding the optimal instant to disseminate its current estimate to neighboring nodes based on a pre-defined triggering criterion. This strategy minimizes communication overhead and leads to heightened communication efficiency. Furthermore, given the significance of stochastic optimization in contemporary online learning frameworks, we can leverage this concept in decentralized online learning scenarios. Typically, each node in such settings is required to compute a comprehensive gradient locally for its entire dataset, which can be computationally prohibitive for large sample sizes. Consequently, we propose substituting traditional deterministic gradients with stochastic gradients, as outlined in [53,54], within Algorithm 1. This modification aims to bolster computational efficiency. Both of these strategies represent valuable avenues for optimizing Algorithm 1 and will be subjects of future in-depth investigations.
