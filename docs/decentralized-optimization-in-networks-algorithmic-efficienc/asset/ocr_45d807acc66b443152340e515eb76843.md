# 6.2.3 Differential privacy

Indeed, within a decentralized network architecture, not all nodes adhere to the norm, with some potentially exhibiting malicious intentions. As these rogue nodes abide by the revised protocols, they actively scrutinize the sensitive inter-node communications, deliberately attempting to deduce the private information of other nodes. Even though each node is restricted to acquiring only the functional value and exchanging localized estimates within its immediate vicinity, as documented in [50], the threat of privacy breaches persists, as evidenced by model inversion attacks [51]. Consequently, to safeguard the local loss functions of participating nodes, the differential privacy framework is embraced. Proceeding further, we will delve into pertinent definitions of differential privacy, as outlined in [43].

**Definition 6.1** (Adjacency). Consider two distinct data collections, denoted as $U = \{u_1, ..., u_n\}$ and $\hat{U} = \{\hat{u}_1, ..., \hat{u}_n\}$. The datasets $U$ and $\hat{U}$ are deemed adjacent if there exists a specific index $i_0$ within the set $\mathcal{V}$ such that the corresponding elements differ, _i.e.,_ $u_{i_0} \neq \hat{u}_{i_0}$, while all other elements indexed by $j$ in the subset $\mathcal{V}_0 = \{j | j \in \mathcal{V}, j \neq i_0\}$ remain identical, meaning $u_j = \hat{u}_j$ for all $j \in \mathcal{V}_0$.

Stated differently, the datasets $U$ and $\hat{U}$ are considered adjacent if there exists a single discrepancy among their respective elements. Based on Definition 6.1, we now proceed to articulate the concept of differential privacy.

**Definition 6.2** (Differential Privacy). For any two neighboring datasets $U$ and $\hat{U}$, provided that

$\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U) \in S] \leq \exp(\epsilon) \cdot \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\hat{U}) \in S],$ (6.4)

then the algorithm $\mathcal{B}$ is endowed with $\epsilon$-differential privacy, where $\epsilon > 0$ represents the degree of privacy protection. Here, $S$ denotes an arbitrary subset of the output space $\text{Range}(\mathcal{B})$, which comprises the entire collection of potential results yielded by the algorithm $\mathcal{B}$. 

Utilizing the inequality (6.4), it becomes evident that any minimal alterations to the private dataset will have negligible impact on the outcome of the randomized algorithm $\mathcal{B}$. Furthermore, a decreased value of $\epsilon$ signifies a heightened degree of privacy safeguarding for the nodes. Consequently, to achieve the utmost privacy protection, $\epsilon$ should be minimized through the introduction of noise with substantial variance, albeit potentially compromising the algorithm’s precision. Let us designate $\text{Adj}(U_t, \hat{U}_t)$ as the adjacency relation between the neighboring datasets $U_t$ and $\hat{U}_t$ at time $t$. Based on this, we proceed to define the concept of sensitivity as follows.

**Definition 6.3** (Sensitivity). The level of responsiveness or susceptibility of the randomized algorithm $\mathcal{B}$ at the specific instant $t$ can be articulated as

$\Delta_t = \sup_{U_t, U'_t: \text{Adj}(U_t, \hat{U}_t)} ||\mathcal{B}(U_t) - \mathcal{B}(\hat{U}_t)||_1.$
