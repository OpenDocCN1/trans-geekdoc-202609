
### 5.1.2 Main contributions

- (i) We introduce an efficient differentially private decentralized dual averaging algorithm, achieved by integrating the gradient rescaling tactic into the decentralized dual subgradient averaging method, which employs Laplace noise perturbation. In contrast to the current cloud-based differential privacy techniques referenced in [43,46], our proposed algorithm facilitates a simpler decentralized implementation as it eliminates the need for an additional central hub.

- (ii) Unlike the methods described in references [27–31], which necessitate starting from zero initial conditions, our algorithm permits dual variables to begin with any initial state. This flexibility and robustness eliminate the need for nodes in the network to coordinate beforehand. Additionally, contrasting with the technique outlined in reference [23], our algorithm utilizes dual subgradient averaging, offering a fresh viewpoint on decentralized online optimization challenges with constraints.

- (iii) When compared to the privacy safeguarding methods [44,45], our algorithm, utilizing row-stochastic weights, can be applied to a wider range of practical and general network topologies. Additionally, our algorithm can address imbalances caused by directed networks by employing a gradient rescaling tactic and is broader in scope than the method described in [27], which relies on prior knowledge of the entire network to compute the eigenvectors of the weight matrix beforehand.

- (iv) Our proposed algorithm attains an expected regret of O(√K) (K denotes the time horizon), which is on par with non-privacy-focused algorithms referenced in [27,29–31]. Additionally, we conducted a thorough analysis of the ε-differential privacy aspect of our algorithm (ε represents the privacy level), thereby exposing the balance between optimization precision and privacy protection. Lastly, our investigations also demonstrated that our algorithm exhibits comparable performance even in the presence of limited communication delays within the network.

## 5.2 Problem description

In this section, we outline a decentralized online optimization challenge with constraints. Following that, the fundamentals of differential privacy are introduced.

### 5.2.1 Problem descriptions

Consider a directed network \(\mathcal{G} = \{\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}\}\) composed of \(n\) nodes, where \(\mathcal{V} = \{1, 2, ..., n\}\) and \(\mathcal{E} \subseteq \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{V}\) represents the connections. Within this graph, node \(j\) can acquire data from the vertices in its adjacent set \(\mathcal{N}_j^{in} = \{i \in \mathcal{V} | (i, j) \in \mathcal{E}\}\). Assume that every node in the directed graph has access only to the data communicated by its incoming neighbors. This data is relayed through a dependable communication link, unaffected by delays, lost packets, or network congestion. Given these conditions, each vertex can use the information obtained from its incoming neighbors via the link to efficiently perform its iterations. Furthermore, each node \(i \in \mathcal{V}\) is associated with a collection of local convex cost functions \(\{f_{i,k}\}_{k=1}^K\), whose precise shapes are not predetermined but are determined as node \(i\) makes decisions at each instance \(k\). Under these circumstances, the objective of the nodes within the graph, at every instance \(k \in \{1, \cdots, K\}\), is to collectively optimize the subsequent minimization problem:

$$\min f_k(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n f_{i,k}(x), \quad \text{s.t. } x \in \Omega, \quad (5.1)$$

where \(\Omega\) is a non-empty, closed, and convex subset of \(\mathbb{R}^m\), and let \(x\) represent a global decision vector belonging to \(\mathbb{R}^m\).

In this study, like previous investigations [12,15,23,28], we utilize regret as a measure to assess the effectiveness of our proposed algorithm. Due to the stochastic nature of the algorithm being examined (introducing randomized noise to obscure the true state of nodes for privacy reasons), we employ the concept of pseudo-individual-regret for node \(j \in \mathcal{V}\) across the timeframe \(K\), as referenced in [12,23,28]:

$$\bar{R}_{j,K} = \mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i=1}^n f_{i,k}(x_{j,k})] - \mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i=1}^n f_{i,k}(x^*)], \quad (5.2)$$

where \(x_{j,k}, j \in \{1, \cdots, n\}\) represents the local variable for \(x\) at time instant \(k\). Furthermore, the optimal decision over the time horizon \(K\) is denoted as \(x^*\), which is obtained by \(\arg\min_{x \in \Omega} \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i=1}^n f_{i,k}(x)\).

Our primary objective in this context is to devise an efficient decentralized online optimization algorithm that not only ensures sublinear regret with respect to \(K\) as expressed in (5.2), specifically satisfying the condition \(\lim_{K \to \infty} \bar{R}_{j,K} / K = 0\) for all \(j \in \mathcal{V}\), but also fully preserves the privacy of the participating nodes. Furthermore, we operate under a set of presuppositions outlined in [32,47].

**Assumption 5.1.** a) \(\mathcal{G}\) remains constant and possesses a robust connectivity; b) The matrix \(A\), belonging to \(\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\), is row-stochastic, satisfying the condition that the sum of its elements in each row equals 1, that is, \(\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} = 1\) for every \(i\) in the set \(\mathcal{V}\).

**Assumption 5.2.** a) The maximum possible diameter of \(\Omega\) is bounded above by a positive number \(M\); b) The subgradient \(g_{i,k}(x)\) possesses \(L\)-Lipschitz continuity, where \(L\) is a positive constant. This implies that the norm of \(g_{i,k}(x)\) is always less than or equal to \(L\), regardless of the values of \(k\) (as long as it is greater than or equal to 1), \(i\) (belonging to the set \(\mathcal{V}\)), and \(x\) (within the domain \(\Omega\)).

**Remark 5.1.** Assumption 5.1 a) assumes that every node within the directed network $\mathcal{G}$ has the ability to directly gather information from their incoming neighbors or indirectly through other nodes in the network. Assumption 5.1 b) imposes further limitations on the weighted matrix $A$, which outlines how each node impacts its out-going neighbors. To elaborate, $A$ is presumed to be row-stochastic, indicating that the total of each row in $A$ equals 1. This assumption is extensively adopted in numerous studies [23,32,47,50] dealing with directed networks and holds significant importance for the subsequent analysis of the algorithm’s convergence. It is worth mentioning that formulating a weighted matrix $A$ adherent to Assumption 5.1 b) can be relatively straightforward, given that each node is aware of its in-degree, as referenced in [47,50].

### 5.2.2 Differential privacy

The scholars in [48] undertook a comprehensive examination of differential privacy, a concept that guarantees the confidentiality of delicate data while permitting participating nodes to divulge their personal details. In this section, we harness a differential privacy approach to protect the individual loss function of every node. The ensuing section introduces the definition of differential privacy.

**Definition 5.1.** Given two datasets, denoted as $U = \{u_1, ..., u_n\}$ and $U' = \{u'_1, ..., u'_n\}$, we say that $U$ and $U'$ are denominated as adjacent if for an $i_0 \in \mathcal{V}$ and $\forall j \neq i_0, u_{i_0} \neq u'_{i_0}$ and $u_j = u'_j$ are satisfied.

In other words, if there is a sole difference in one data point between the two datasets, $U$ and $U'$, then $U$ and $U'$ are considered adjacent. Based on Definition 5.1, differential privacy is subsequently defined as follows.

**Definition 5.2.** Think about two neighboring datasets, namely $U$ and $U'$. If
\[\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U) \in S] \leq \exp(\epsilon) \cdot \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U') \in S],\]
(5.3)

then the randomized algorithm denoted as $\mathcal{B}$ qualifies as $\epsilon$-differential privacy. Here, Range($\mathcal{B}$) signifies the comprehensive collection of potential outputs generated by the algorithm $\mathcal{B}$, while $S$ represents any subset within Range($\mathcal{B}$). Additionally, the privacy level is represented by the parameter $\epsilon > 0$.

Based on inequality (5.3), it becomes evident that slight alterations in the private dataset exert minimal influence on the ultimate output of the randomized algorithm $\mathcal{B}$. Consequently, a hostile node can only acquire limited insights about other nodes. Here, $\epsilon$ signifies the degree of privacy, where a lower value of $\epsilon$ corresponds to enhanced privacy safeguards. To this end, it is advisable to minimize $\epsilon$ by introducing noise with significant variance, albeit potentially impacting the effectiveness of $\mathcal{B}$. Thus determining the privacy level, represented by $\epsilon$, involves balancing the desired privacy with the performance of $\mathcal{B}$. Let Adj($U_k, U'_k$) represent the adjacency relationship between the neighboring datasets $U_k$ and $U'_k$ at time $k$. Subsequently, we proceed to define sensitivity as follows.
