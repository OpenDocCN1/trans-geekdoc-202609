## 6.2 Problem description

In this section, we delve into the intricacies of a decentralized approach to online constrained optimization, outlining the essence of the issue. Following this, we offer a foundational exposition of the concept of differential privacy.

### 6.2.1 Problem description

Given that each node \(i\), where \(i\) ranges from 1 to \(n\), is associated with a collection of local convex loss functions, denoted as \(\{f_t^i\}\) for \(t\) spanning from 1 to \(T\), and without specifying their exact formulations, the collective objective of the nodes interconnected in the network, at each instant \(t\) within the period 1 to \(T\), is to jointly tackle the minimization of the subsequent optimization task spanning the entire time horizon \(T\):

\[
\min_{x \in \Omega} \sum_{t=1}^T f_t(x), \quad f_t(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n f_t^i(x), \tag{6.1}
\]

where \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m\) is characterized as being non-empty, closed, and convex. Within this set, \(x\) represents a global decision vector. Now, let us envision a scenario involving online and bandit feedback mechanisms, where each node \(i\) is only permitted to inquire about the value of its loss function \(f_t^i\) after making a decision \(x_t^i\) at any given time $t$. Put simply, the precise formulations of these local loss functions remain concealed from each node. Furthermore, no node has direct access to the global loss function $f_t : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}$.

The assessment of online optimization algorithms’ efficacy inherently necessitates the consideration of regret as a pivotal measure. Herein, we introduce a traditional, individualized notion of regret of node \( j \in \{1, \cdots, n\} \), as detailed in [13]:

\( R_T^j = \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i=1}^n f_t^i(x_t^j) - \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i=1}^n f_t^i(x^*), \)

where \( x_t^j, j \in \{1, \cdots, n\} \) represents the localized variable corresponding to \( x \) at time \( t \) and the optimal decision over time horizon \( T \) is denoted by \( x^* = \arg\min_{x \in \Omega} \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i=1}^n f_t^i(x) \). Our current investigation involves incorporating random perturbations into the actual states of nodes to safeguard the privacy of participating entities. Consequently, attaining a precise, deterministic form of regret akin to the conventional regret defined in (6.2) becomes impractical. Instead, we resort to a regret metric that quantifies the discrepancy between the expected cost incurred by node \( j \)’s decisions \( x_t^j \) and the optimal cost across \( T \), as outlined in [10,25]:

\( \bar{R}_T^j = \mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i=1}^n f_t^i(x_t^j)] - \mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i=1}^n f_t^i(x^*)]. \)

The current focal point revolves around devising a potent decentralized dual averaging algorithm of the zeroth-order nature, ensuring that not merely does the regret expressed in (6.3) exhibit a sublinear trend concerning \( T \), specifically, satisfying the condition \( \lim_{T \to \infty} \bar{R}_T^j / T = 0, j \in \mathcal{V} \), but also guarantees robust safeguarding of the privacy rights of the participating nodes.

**Remark 6.1.** There exist two compelling motivations for contemplating bandit feedback. Firstly, numerous methods are devoid of gradient information accessibility, as highlighted in [30]. Secondly, calculating the function’s value proves significantly more straightforward than estimating its gradient, as evidenced by [32]. Fundamentally, bandit-based online optimization constitutes a gradient-free (or zeroth-order) approach to tackling online optimization challenges.

### 6.2.2 Communication network

The interaction between nodes can be envisioned as a dynamically evolving directed network, labeled \( \mathcal{G}_t = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}_t) \), comprising a set of nodes \( \mathcal{V} = \{1, 2, \dots, n\} \) and a time-dependent set of edges \( \mathcal{E}_t \subseteq \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{V} \) for all \( t \ge 1 \). If node \( j \) can immediately send data to node \( i \) at time \( t \), then the edge \( (j, i) \) belongs to \( \mathcal{E}_t \), with \( j \) considered an inbound neighbor of \( i \) and vice versa, \( i \) being an outbound neighbor of \( j \). The collection of inbound neighbors for node \( i \) at time \( t \) is denoted by \( \mathcal{N}_{t,\text{in}}^i = \{j \in \mathcal{V} | (j, i) \in \mathcal{E}_t\} \), and similarly, the set of outbound neighbors \( \mathcal{N}_{t,\text{out}}^i \) is defined. The network is deemed unbalanced at time $t$ if the number of inbound connections for node $i$ differs from its outbound connections. The weighted adjacency matrix of $\mathcal{G}_t$ is represented as $C_t = [c_t^{ij}] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, where $c_t^{ij} > 0$ if $(j, i) \in \mathcal{E}_t$, and $c_t^{ij} = 0$ otherwise. Assuming that each node in these directed networks can solely access the information conveyed by its inbound neighbors at any given time $t$, the information transfer occurs through a reliable channel, free from delays, packet losses, or congestion. Consequently, each node can leverage the information received from its inbound neighbors in the channel at time $t$ to perform its iterative updates accordingly. Furthermore, we adopt the following assumptions, as outlined in [30,33].

**Assumption 6.1.**
- a) There exists a positive integer $B$ where the union of consecutive networks from $\mathcal{G}_t$ to $\mathcal{G}_{t+B-1}$, with $t \ge 1$, forms a strongly connected graph;
- b) At any given time $t$, the matrix $C_t$ belonging to the set $\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is column-stochastic, meaning that its transpose $C_t^T$ satisfies the condition $C_t^T \mathbf{1}_n = \mathbf{1}_n$, where $\mathbf{1}_n$ denotes the column vector of ones with length $n$.

**Remark 6.2.** Assumption 6.1(a) signifies that within any consecutive period of $B$ periods, every node can access information, either directly or through intermediate channels, about all other nodes in the network. This assumption is broader than mandating the strict connectivity of each individual directed network $\mathcal{G}_t$ for all $t \ge 1$. Furthermore, Assumption 6.1(b) imposes an additional constraint on the weighted matrix $C_t$. Here, the weights embody the actual influence that each node exerts on its various outbound neighbors within the network at time $t$. The column-stochastic property of $C_t$ (signifying that the sum of each column's elements equals 1 at time $t$) plays a pivotal role in the subsequent algorithm design, which is a prevalent hypothesis adopted in numerous studies [39,40,56] concerning time-varying directed networks. Notably, constructing $C_t$ that adheres to Assumption 6.1(b) is straightforward when each node is aware of its outbound degree. For instance, we can assign $c_t^{ij} = \frac{1}{d_{t,\text{out}}^j + 1}$ if $j$ is an inbound neighbor of $i$ at time $t$, and $c_t^{ij} = 0$ otherwise, where $d_{t,\text{out}}^j$ denotes the outbound degree of node $j$ at time $t$, and $| \cdot |$ represents the number of elements in a set.

**Assumption 6.2.**
- a) It holds that there are two distinct positive constants, denoted as $d$ and $D$, satisfying $d < D$, for which the relationship $d \cdot \mathbb{B}^m \subset \Omega \subset D \cdot \mathbb{B}^m$ is valid;
- b) Each individual local loss function, denoted as $f_t^i(x)$, exhibits $K$-Lipschitz continuity within the domain $\Omega$. This implies that the absolute difference between $f_t^i(a)$ and $f_t^i(b)$ is bounded by $K$ times the distance between $a$ and $b$, formally stated as $| f_t^i(a) - f_t^i(b) | \le K \cdot | a - b |$, for all $a, b \in \Omega$ and for every $i$ belonging to the set $\mathcal{V}$.

**Remark 6.3.** Assumption 6.2, which imposes additional constraints on both the feasible region $\Omega$ and the localized cost function $f_t^i(x)$, is a widely encountered premise in the exploration of decentralized optimization problems under constraints. It serves as a pivotal foundation for guaranteeing the subsequent analysis of convergence about the introduced algorithmic approach. Furthermore, Assumption 6.2(b) implies that the subgradient $g_t^i(x)$ associated with $f_t^i(x)$ is confined within a bound within $\Omega$, specifically, for all $t \ge 1$, there exists a positive constant $K$ such that the norm of $g_t^i(x)$ does not exceed $K$ for any $x$ in $\Omega$ and $i$ belonging to the set $\mathcal{V}$.
