**Remark 5.1.** Assumption 5.1 a) assumes that every node within the directed network $\mathcal{G}$ has the ability to directly gather information from their incoming neighbors or indirectly through other nodes in the network. Assumption 5.1 b) imposes further limitations on the weighted matrix $A$, which outlines how each node impacts its out-going neighbors. To elaborate, $A$ is presumed to be row-stochastic, indicating that the total of each row in $A$ equals 1. This assumption is extensively adopted in numerous studies [23,32,47,50] dealing with directed networks and holds significant importance for the subsequent analysis of the algorithm’s convergence. It is worth mentioning that formulating a weighted matrix $A$ adherent to Assumption 5.1 b) can be relatively straightforward, given that each node is aware of its in-degree, as referenced in [47,50].

# ## 5.2.2 Differential privacy

The scholars in [48] undertook a comprehensive examination of differential privacy, a concept that guarantees the confidentiality of delicate data while permitting participating nodes to divulge their personal details. In this section, we harness a differential privacy approach to protect the individual loss function of every node. The ensuing section introduces the definition of differential privacy.

**Definition 5.1.** Given two datasets, denoted as $U = \{u_1, ..., u_n\}$ and $U' = \{u'_1, ..., u'_n\}$, we say that $U$ and $U'$ are denominated as adjacent if for an $i_0 \in \mathcal{V}$ and $\forall j \neq i, u_{i_0} \neq u'_{i_0}$ and $u_j = u'_j$ are satisfied.

In other words, if there is a sole difference in one data point between the two datasets, $U$ and $U'$, then $U$ and $U'$ are considered adjacent. Based on Definition 5.1, differential privacy is subsequently defined as follows.

**Definition 5.2.** Think about two neighboring datasets, namely $U$ and $U'$. If
\[\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U) \in S] \leq \exp(\epsilon) \cdot \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(U') \in S],\]
(5.3)

then the randomized algorithm denoted as $\mathcal{B}$ qualifies as $\epsilon$-differential privacy. Here, Range($\mathcal{B}$) signifies the comprehensive collection of potential outputs generated by the algorithm $\mathcal{B}$, while $S$ represents any subset within Range($\mathcal{B}$). Additionally, the privacy level is represented by the parameter $\epsilon > 0$.

Based on inequality (5.3), it becomes evident that slight alterations in the private dataset exert minimal influence on the ultimate output of the randomized algorithm $\mathcal{B}$. Consequently, a hostile node can only acquire limited insights about other nodes. Here, $\epsilon$ signifies the degree of privacy, where a lower value of $\epsilon$ corresponds to enhanced privacy safeguards. To this end, it is advisable to minimize $\epsilon$ by introducing noise with significant variance, albeit potentially impacting the effectiveness of $\mathcal{B}$. Thus determining the privacy level, represented by $\epsilon$, involves balancing the desired privacy with the performance of $\mathcal{B}$.
