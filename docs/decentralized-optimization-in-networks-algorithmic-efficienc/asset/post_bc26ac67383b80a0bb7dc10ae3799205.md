
\(\begin{equation*} G_\eta = \left[ \begin{array}{ c c c c } \frac{1+\sigma_2^2}{2} & 0 & 0 & \frac{2\eta^2\beta^2}{1-\sigma_2^2} \\ \frac{4\beta^2\eta}{\lambda} + 2\sigma_2 + \frac{2\sigma_2}{\eta\lambda} & 1 - \frac{\eta\lambda}{2} & \frac{4\eta^2\beta^2}{m} & 0 \\ \frac{2}{n_{\min}} & \frac{2}{n_{\min}} & 1 - \frac{1}{n_{\max}} & 0 \\ \frac{194}{1-\sigma_2^2} & \frac{128}{1-\sigma_2^2} & \frac{22}{1-\sigma_2^2} & \frac{3+\sigma_2^2}{4} \end{array} \right] . \end{equation*}\)

Drawing upon Lemma 3.4, in order to assess the convergence of the P-DVR algorithm, we aim to ascertain the range of \(\eta\) that guarantees \(\rho(G_\eta)\) is smaller than 1. In order to reach this objective, we may confine every component of the positive vector \(\epsilon = [\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3, \epsilon_4]^T\) to ascertain the scope of \(\eta\), thereby ensuring that the subsequent correlation holds true:

\(\begin{equation} G_\eta \epsilon \le (1 - \frac{\eta\lambda}{4})\epsilon . \tag{3.40} \end{equation}\)

To facilitate analysis, we initially provide a sufficiently small value for \(\omega\) such that \(\omega \le \eta\). Subsequently, inequality (3.40) can be reformulated as

\(\begin{equation} \frac{\lambda\eta}{4} + \frac{2\eta^2\beta^2}{1-\sigma_2^2} \frac{\epsilon_4}{\epsilon_1} \le \frac{1-\sigma_2^2}{2}, \tag{3.41} \end{equation}\)

\(\begin{equation} \frac{4\eta\beta^2}{m} \epsilon_3 \le \frac{\lambda}{4}\epsilon_2 - (\frac{4\beta^2}{\lambda} + \frac{2\sigma_2}{\omega} + \frac{2\sigma_2}{\omega^2\lambda})\epsilon_1, \tag{3.42} \end{equation}\)

\(\begin{equation} \frac{\eta\lambda}{4} \le \frac{1}{n_{\max}} - \frac{2}{n_{\min}} \frac{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_3} - \frac{2}{n_{\min}} \frac{\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_3}, \tag{3.43} \end{equation}\)

\(\begin{equation} \frac{\eta\lambda}{4} \le \frac{1-\sigma_2^2}{4} - \frac{194}{1-\sigma_2^2} \frac{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_4} - \frac{128}{1-\sigma_2^2} \frac{\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_4} - \frac{22}{1-\sigma_2^2} \frac{\epsilon_3}{\epsilon_4}. \tag{3.44} \end{equation}\)

Clearly, the inequalities denoted by (3.42)-(3.44) suggest that the magnitude of \(\eta\) limits the right-hand side of these inequalities, all of which are greater than zero. From this insight, it becomes apparent that the boundaries for \(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3, \epsilon_4\) can be established independently of \(\eta\). We then proceed to determine the specific values for \(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3, \epsilon_4\) by exploiting the positivity of the right-hand side of the inequalities labeled (3.42)-(3.44).

Drawing upon the positivity aspect inherent in inequality (3.42), we are able to assign a discretionary value to \(\epsilon_1\) and derive the subsequent conclusion:

\(\begin{equation} \epsilon_2 \ge 20(\frac{\beta^2}{\lambda^2} + \frac{\sigma_2}{\omega^2\lambda^2})\epsilon_1. \tag{3.45} \end{equation}\)

Likewise, (3.43) enables us to infer that

\(\begin{equation} \epsilon_3 \ge \frac{3n_{\max}}{n_{\min}}(\epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2). \tag{3.46} \end{equation}\)

Subsequently, utilizing the inequality denoted by (3.44), it can be determined that

\(\epsilon_4 \ge \frac{32}{(1 - \sigma_2^2)^2}(194\epsilon_1 + 128\epsilon_2 + 22\epsilon_3).\quad (3.47)\)

Next, we utilize the value range \(\epsilon = [\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3, \epsilon_4]\) to confine the magnitude of \(\eta\) in inequalities labeled (3.41)-(3.44). Based on inequality (3.42), we determine that

\(\eta \le \eta_1 = \frac{m\lambda}{4\beta^2\epsilon_3}(\frac{\beta^2}{\lambda^2} + \frac{\sigma_2}{\omega^2\lambda^2})\epsilon_1.\quad (3.48)\)

Furthermore, in accordance with the inequality denoted by (3.41), it is evident that \(\eta\) fulfills

\(\eta \le \eta_2 = \frac{2(1 - \sigma_2^2)^2\epsilon_1}{\lambda(1 - \sigma_2^2) + 8\beta\epsilon_4}.\quad (3.49)\)

Subsequently, to ensure that inequality (3.43) remains valid, we simply adjust \(\eta\) to satisfy

\(\eta \le \eta_3 = \frac{4}{3\lambda n_{\max}}.\quad (3.50)\)

Lastly, to guarantee the validity of inequality (3.44), we adjust \(\eta\) accordingly to meet

\(\eta \le \eta_4 = \frac{7(1 - \sigma_2^2)}{8\lambda}.\quad (3.51)\)

Upon compiling the results denoted by (3.48)-(3.51), we arrive at the subsequent conclusion: (3.39) is valid provided that \(\epsilon = [\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3, \epsilon_4]\) fulfills the conditions outlined in inequalities (3.45)-(3.47), and \(\eta\) satisfies

\(0 < \eta \le \bar{\eta} := \min\{\frac{m\lambda}{4\beta^2\epsilon_3}(\frac{\beta^2}{\lambda^2} + \frac{\sigma_2}{\omega^2\lambda^2})\epsilon_1, \frac{4}{3\lambda n_{\max}}, \frac{2(1 - \sigma_2^2)^2\epsilon_1}{\lambda(1 - \sigma_2^2) + 8\beta\epsilon_4} \}.\)

Based on Lemma 3.4, it is understood that \(\rho(G_\eta) \le 1 - \frac{\lambda\eta}{4}\). When \(\eta = \bar{\eta}\), it follows that

\(\rho(G_\eta) \le 1 - \min\{\frac{m}{16\beta^2\epsilon_3}(\beta^2 + \frac{\sigma_2}{\omega^2})\epsilon_1, \frac{1}{3n_{\max}}, \frac{\lambda(1 - \sigma_2^2)^2\epsilon_1}{2\lambda(1 - \sigma_2^2) + 16\beta\epsilon_4} \},\)

which completes the proof.

**Theorem 3.2** (Complexity of P-DVR). Provided that Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 are valid, the intricacy of P-DVR, which pertains to the necessary repetitions to attain an error margin of \(\epsilon\), is

\(\mathcal{O}(\max\{\frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_1}}, \frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_2}}, \frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_3}}\}\log \frac{c}{\epsilon}),\)

where \(a_1 = \frac{m}{\beta^2 \epsilon_3}(\beta^2 + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{\omega^2})\epsilon_1\), \(a_2 = \frac{1}{n_{\max}}\), and \(a_3 = \frac{\lambda(1-\sigma_2^2)^2 \epsilon_1}{\lambda(1-\sigma_2^2)+8\beta\epsilon_4}\). It is evident that the result of \(\max\{\frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_1}}, \frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_2}}, \frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_3}}\}\) and \(c\) remain unchanged, hence the intricacy can be condensed to \(\mathcal{O}(\log \frac{1}{\epsilon})\).

Proof. To simplify our analysis, we define \(a_1 = \frac{m}{\beta^2 \epsilon_3}(\beta^2 + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{\omega^2})\epsilon_1\), \(a_2 = \frac{1}{n_{\max}}\), and \(a_3 = \frac{\lambda(1-\sigma_2^2)^2 \epsilon_1}{\lambda(1-\sigma_2^2)+8\beta\epsilon_4}\). Given a convergence error denoted as \(\epsilon\), we formulate the subsequent inequality based on Theorem 3.1:

\(\mathbb{E}[\|x_k^i - x^*\|^2] \le c(1 - \min\{a_1, a_2, a_3\})^k \le \epsilon.\)

By applying logarithmic operations to the middle and right sides of the aforementioned inequality, we derive the subsequent equation:

\(k \ge \frac{-\log \frac{\epsilon}{c}}{-\log(1 - \min\{a_1, a_2, a_3\})}.\)

Further simplifying the expression leads to a more comprehensible equation as follows:

\(k \ge \max\{\frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_1}}, \frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_2}}, \frac{1}{\log \frac{1}{1-a_3}}\} \log \frac{c}{\epsilon}.\)

The proof is completed.

**Remark 3.2.** The algorithm incorporating gradient tracking has already facilitated a linear convergence speed, as referenced in [34,35,37,40]. Nevertheless, in comparison, the P-DVR algorithm possesses distinct benefits: (i) Due to the inclusion of a projection operator, it is apt for addressing constrained optimization problems; (ii) Thanks to the variance-reduction strategy, it exhibits computational efficiency.

## 3.5 Numerical simulations

To validate the efficacy of the P-DVR algorithm, we conducted numerical simulation experiments involving logistic regression models and acoustic source localization problems.

### 3.5.1 Decentralized logistic regression experiments

In this subsection, the P-DVR algorithm is employed to address a binary classification problem using logistic regression. Assume that a communication network consisting of \(m\) nodes is established, with each node housing \(n\) data points. The logistic regression binary classification problem can be formulated as follows:

\(\min \ f(x), \ \text{s.t.} \ x \in \Omega_0,\)

where $f(x) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m f^i(x)$ denotes the global function. Furthermore, $f^i(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n f^{i,j}(x)$ signifies the local function allocated to node $i$. The component function is expressed as $f^{i,j}(x) = \log[1 + e^{-(x^\top \tau_{ij}) \varphi_{ij}}] + \frac{\alpha}{2} \|x\|_2^2$, while $\Omega_0$ stands for the closed convex set. Notably, $\tau_{ij}$ symbolizes the feature vector corresponding to the $j$th data entry at the $i$th node. Additionally, $\varphi_{ij}$ represents the related binary label, and $\alpha$ serves as a regularization parameter, aimed at preventing over-fitting.

In this experiment, we utilized the COVTYPE [54], MNIST [55], and BREAST [54] datasets to assess the convergence speed and processing efficiency of the P-DVR algorithm. It is important to mention that we standardized all three datasets and randomly picked two categories of data, specifically class -1 and class 1, from each dataset. Regarding the amount of data chosen for training and testing, we drew 8000 and 100 entries from the COVTYPE dataset, respectively; 1000 and 100 entries from the MNIST dataset, respectively; and 440 and 100 entries from the BREAST dataset, respectively. It is worth clarifying that the reason for selecting varying data volumes across datasets stems from practical considerations: the limited memory capacity of our experimental equipment precluded loading excessive amounts of high-dimensional data, such as the MNIST dataset, or due to the inherently small data size within certain datasets, as in the case of the BREAST dataset.

We conducted simulations with 20 nodes, evenly distributing the aforementioned datasets among them. We set the parameter $\alpha$ to 0.01 and established the closed convex set constraint as $\Omega_0 = [-1, 5]^p$ (where $p$ represents the dataset’s dimension). The step-size was carefully adjusted to ensure optimal performance. Utilizing Metropolis rules, we generated a doubly stochastic matrix linked to an undirected geometric graph. For evaluation, we calculated the squared error using the formula $\|x_k - 1_m x^{*\top}\|^2 / \|x_0 - 1_m x^{*\top}\|^2$, where $x^* \in \mathbb{R}^p$ determined via a centralized algorithm, and $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}$ signifies the decision variable during the $k$th network iteration.
