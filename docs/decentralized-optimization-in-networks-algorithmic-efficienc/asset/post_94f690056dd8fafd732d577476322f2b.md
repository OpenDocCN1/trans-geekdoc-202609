

## 5.5 Generalizing the algorithm design

In this section, we examine the effectiveness of Algorithm 1 under conditions of limited transmission delays.

### 5.5.1 Bounded communication delays

Consider that nodes in the network may encounter communication delays which are arbitrary but uniformly bounded during the reception of information sent by its in-neighbors. To elaborate, let us denote \(\vartheta_{ij}, (j, i) \in \mathcal{E}\), as a previously unknown delay incurred when information is relayed from node \(j\) to node \(i\). Subsequently, we postulate the following assumptions concerning these communication delays.

**Assumption 5.3.** The delays in communication, denoted as \(\vartheta_{ij}\), are consistently limited across all pairs \((j, i)\) belonging to the set \(\mathcal{E}\). This means that there exists a positive integer \(\vartheta\) such that

\[
0 \leq \vartheta_{ij} \leq \vartheta, \forall (j, i) \in \mathcal{E} .
\]

Furthermore, every node has its own variable without any delay, implying that \(\vartheta_{ii}\) equals zero for all \(i\) belonging to the set \(\mathcal{V}\).

**Remark 5.7.** Actually, it is not an unrealistic presumption, as supported by references [50,51], to postulate that the communication delays encountered in every connection are not fixed constants but are consistently limited. Moreover, to accommodate varying (yet bounded) delays in the connection at different times, we can utilize time stamping to store information up to the maximum constant delay in the worst-case scenario (that is, transforming fluctuating delays into constants for easier processing), as outlined in [52]. This simplifies the analytical process.

It is important to observe that the updates of \(y_{i,k}\) and \(z_{i,k}\) in Algorithm 1 depend on data from the incoming neighbors of each node \(i\) belonging to the set \(\mathcal{V}\). Conversely, the updates of \(x_{i,k}\) do not necessitate any additional information exchange and are executed solely within the local environment. Therefore in the presence of communication delays, the updates of \(y_{i,k}\) and \(z_{i,k}\) in Algorithm 1 undergo

\[
z_{i,k+1} = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} \bar{z}_{j,k-\vartheta_{ij}} + \frac{g_{i,k}}{y_{i,k}},
\]

\[
y_{i,k+1} = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} y_{j,k-\vartheta_{ij}}. \tag{5.38}
\]

The simplified representation of Algorithm 1, incorporating communication delays, is presented in Algorithm 2.

### Algorithm 2 A differentially private decentralized dual averaging algorithm with delays – from the viewpoint of $i \in \mathcal{V}$.

- 1: **Require:** $\alpha_0 > 0, z_{i,1} \in \mathbb{R}^m, x_{i,1} = \Pi_\Omega^h(z_{i,1}, \alpha_0), \text{and } y_{i,1} = e_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
- 2: **Delays:** Every communication link $(j, i)$ belonging to the set $\mathcal{E}$ within the network $\mathcal{G}$ experiences random yet consistently limited communication delays denoted as $\vartheta_{ij}$ for all $j$ that are part of $\mathcal{N}_i^{\text{in}}$.
- 3: **Set** $k = 1$.
- 4: **while** $k \le K$ **do**
- 5: Generate noise $\gamma_{i,k} \sim \mathcal{L}(0, b_k)$.
- 6: Perturb $z_{i,k}$ by $\gamma_{i,k}$ and obtain $\tilde{z}_{i,k}$ via (5.6).
- 7: Transmit $A_{ji}y_{i,k}$ and $A_{ji}\tilde{z}_{i,k}$ to node $j \in \mathcal{N}_i^{\text{out}}$; Receive $A_{ij}y_{j,k-\vartheta_{ij}}$ and $A_{ij}\tilde{z}_{j,k-\vartheta_{ij}}$ from node $j \in \mathcal{N}_i^{\text{in}}$.

### 5.5.2 Main results

The theorem below will demonstrate that Algorithm 2 is capable of not only achieving the anticipated regret, but also guaranteeing $\hat{\epsilon}$-differential privacy in the presence of a communication delay in every connection of the directed networks.

Theorem 5.3. Taking into account the sequence \(\{x_{i,k}\}_{k=1}^K\) produced by Algorithm 2, and based on Assumptions 5.1–5.3, we can deduce the subsequent conclusions:

- **(a)** Provided that \(\gamma_{i,k}\), where \(i \in \mathcal{V}\), satisfies the conditions stated in Theorem 5.2, Algorithm 2 ensures $\hat{\epsilon}$-differential privacy across \(K\) iterations.
- **(b)** Provided that \(\alpha_k\) fulfills the criteria outlined in Theorem 5.2, Algorithm 2 attains a regret of order \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{K})\) over the set \(\Omega\).

Proof. It is worth mentioning that Algorithm 2 represents an advancement of Algorithm 1, incorporating the consideration of communication delays. In this context, Theorem 5.3 can be validated through a two-step process, as referenced in [27]. Initially, by employing an expanded directed network, Algorithm 2 (incorporating communication delays) can be converted into an algorithm devoid of delays. Following this transformation, Section 5.4 aids in the assessment of the derived delay-free algorithm’s convergence and privacy-preservation abilities.

Bear in mind that, without delays, our algorithm relies on a communication network denoted as \(\mathcal{G}\) and represented by a row-stochastic matrix \(A\). However, in scenarios involving delays, our intention is to expand \(\mathcal{G}\) into a virtual directed network, denoted as \(\hat{\mathcal{G}}\) and represented by a different row-stochastic matrix \(\hat{A}\). Specifically, we introduce delay nodes (virtual), designated as \(d_{ji}\), along the edge \((i, j)\) of the original network \(\mathcal{G}\). Following this, we construct a delay chain composed of directed edges, namely, \((i, d_1), (d_1, d_2), \dots, (d_{ji}, j)\), and redirect all data from node \(i\) through this chain to reach node \(j\). Furthermore, the cumulative number of delay nodes within the virtual network is designated as $d_{sum}$, calculated as $\sum_{(i,j)\in \mathcal{E}} d_{ji} = d_{sum}$. Let $h$ belong to the set $\{n + 1, ..., n + d_{sum}\}$, representing the delay nodes within the entire $\hat{\mathcal{G}}$. Subsequently, for determining the weights of the expanded network $\hat{\mathcal{G}}$, we establish the following guidelines:

- 1) In the event that a node $i$ belonging to the set $\mathcal{V}$ forwards its data to the initial delay node, denoted as $d_1$, or when information is exchanged between delay nodes, the weight values $\hat{A}_{hi}$ and $\hat{A}_{(h+1)h}$ are both set to one. Specifically, $\hat{A}_{hi}$ equals 1, and $\hat{A}_{(h+1)h}$ also equals 1.

- 2) When the final delay node forwards the message to a node $j$ that belongs to the set $\mathcal{V}$, the weight $\hat{A}_{jh}$ corresponds to the weight in matrix $A$ connecting nodes $i$ and $j$. In other words, $\hat{A}_{jh}$ equals $A_{ji}$.

Based on the definitions of $\hat{A}$ and $A$, we can infer that, given Assumption 5.3, both matrices share analogous characteristics. Consequently, the outcome of Theorem 5.3 can be validated in an analogous manner to the approach outlined in Section 5.4. $\square$

### 5.5.3 Other directions

Algorithm 1 provides a hopeful launching pad for tackling the present obstacles in this domain. However, additional investigations are required to completely enhance the algorithm's efficiency. Below, we outline several research avenues that have the potential to further refine the efficiency of Algorithm 1, reserving the pertinent theoretical exploration for subsequent studies.

- (i) Communication efficiency: Taking into account that Algorithm 1 necessitates every node in the network to engage in communication with its incoming neighbors for each instance of the assigned online learning assignment, it becomes evident that this communication approach demonstrates less resilience and possesses a comparatively subpar communication efficiency. Inspired by recent studies referenced in [2,21,53], we have the opportunity to incorporate asynchronous communication tactics into the algorithm. This integration serves to alleviate the communication burden, thereby achieving enhanced communication efficiency.

- (ii) Computation efficiency: Real-world online learning implementations, whether in the medical or military domain, inherently entail the handling of extensive datasets. This poses significant challenges to the computational capabilities of the algorithms, especially considering the finite processing power of actual hardware. In this scenario, taking cues from references [57,58], we may utilize stochastic gradients as an alternative to the deterministic gradient used in Algorithm 1. Such a replacement notably lightens the computational load on individual nodes, without compromising the algorithm's convergence properties.

- (iii) General constraints: Furthermore, by employing problem transformation techniques referenced in [3], Algorithm 1 possesses the capability to address widespread constrained (inequality) optimization challenges within directed networks.
