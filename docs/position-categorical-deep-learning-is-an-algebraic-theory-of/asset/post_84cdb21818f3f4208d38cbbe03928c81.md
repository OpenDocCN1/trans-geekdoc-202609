## C.1. Permutation-equivariant Learning on Graphs

leading to graph neural networks (Veličković, 2023).

![](img/92dd63b8e10fefd3f3f227bf65a30b83_15_0.png)

In this case:

- The group $G = \Sigma_n$ is the permutation group of $n$ elements,
- The carrier object for the algebras (scalar) node features $R^n$, and potentially, adjacency matrices $R^{n \times n}$,
- The structure map for the first algebra, $P_{X,A} : \Sigma_n \times R^n \times R^{n \times n} \rightarrow R^{n \times n}$, executes the permutation: $P_{X,A}(\sigma, X, A) = (P(\sigma)X, P(\sigma)AP(\sigma)^T)$, where $P(\sigma)$ is the permutation matrix specified by $\sigma$.
- The structure map for the second algebra, $P_X$, still executes the permutation, but only the node features. The reduction is not strictly necessary, but it is the standard when designing graph neural networks—as they are often assumed not to modify their underlying computational graph. We deliberately omit this reduction here to illustrate how our framework can handle neural networks transitioning across different algebras.

## C.2. Rotation-equivariant Learning on Spheres

leading to the first layer of spherical CNNs (Cohen et al., 2018).

![](img/92dd63b8e10fefd3f3f227bf65a30b83_15_1.png)

In this case:

- The group $G = SO(3)$ is the special orthogonal group of 3D rotations.
- The carrier object for the first algebra is $S^2 \rightarrow R$; (scalar) data defined over the sphere. Note that in practice, this will be desirable, so be written to represent it using matrices.
- The structure map for the first algebra, $p_{SO(3)} : SO(3) \times (S^2 \rightarrow R) \rightarrow (S^2 \rightarrow R)$, executes a 3D rotation on the spherical data, as follows: $p_{SO(3)}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \psi) = \phi$, such that $\phi(x) = R(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)x$, where $R(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ is the rotation matrix specified by the ZYZ-Euler angles, (this inverse) rotation is applied to points $x \in S^2$ on the sphere.
- The carrier object for the second algebra is $SO(3) \rightarrow R$; (scalar) data defined over rotations. Once again, this will be quite desirable in practice.
- The structure map for the second algebra, $p_{SO(3)} : SO(3) \times (SO(3) \rightarrow R) \rightarrow (SO(3) \rightarrow R)$ now executes a 3D rotation over the rotation-matrix data, as follows: $p_{SO(3)}(\alpha', \beta', \gamma'), \psi) = \phi$ such that $\phi(x) = R(\alpha', \beta', \gamma')^{-1}R(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)x$.

leading to G-CNNs (Cohen & Wiley, 2016), as well as the subsequent layers of spherical CNNs (Cohen et al., 2018).

```
$G \times (G \rightarrow \mathbb{R}) \xrightarrow{G \times f} G \times (G \rightarrow \mathbb{R})$$
$$

$$
nG \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \xrightarrow{f} G \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$
```

In this case:

- The group G is also the carrier of the carrier objects (G $\rightarrow R$).
- Both algebras' map follows the execution of the regular representation of G, $A_G : G \times (G \rightarrow R) \rightarrow (G \rightarrow R)$, by composition, as follows $A_G(g,\psi)(h) = \psi(g^{-1}h)$.

## D. Lawvere Theories and Syntax

de Haan et al. (202a) expanded the theory of equivariant layers in neural networks using the abstraction of natural transformations of functions. In this section, we will explain how to understand morphisms of monads, and in the semantics of algebras in these terms.

Definition 1.1. If C is a category, a presheaf on C is a functor $C^{op} \rightarrow Set$. The category whose objects are presheaves and morphisms are natural transformations is denoted P(C).

Fix a monad T on Set and $C_T$ denote its category of algebras and homomorphisms.

Given an algebra $A \in C_T$, the way to interpret A as a functor is via the Yoneda embedding $C_T \rightarrow P(C_T)$, which identifies A with the presheaf $[-, A]$. It is a standard result that the Yoneda functor is faithful, which means that we can identify algebras with morphisms as presheaves by the definition of limits and colimits.

Furthermore, these presheaves have a special property. If $\lim j$ is a small colimit in C, then $[\lim j, A] = [\lim j, A]$, essentially to add to the class of all, and 0 to the reader of empty.

Definition D.2. If C is a category and J is a class of small colimits in C, then a J-continuous presheaf on C is a presheaf $F \in P(C)$ such that $F(\lim j) = \lim F(j)$ for all $j \in J$. The corresponding full subcategory of PSh(C) is denoted PSh(C), or simply PSh(C) in the case that J is the class of all small colimits. We will use $[ \ ]$ to refer to the class of all colroducts, and 0 to the reader of empty.

It turns out that C has a nice property: it is strongly compact, meaning that every continuous presheaf is representable as above by an object of $C$. In other words, we have the identification $C = PSh(C)$.

However, it is impractical to work with the entire category C. When possible, we want to reason in terms of a more tractable vocabulary. This will provide us with syntax for monads.

Definition D.3. The Kleisli category $K_T$ is the full subcategory of $C$. The finitary Kleisli category $K_T^F$ is $K_T$. The unary Kleisli category $K_T^U$ is $K_T$.

$C_T \supset K_T \supset K_T^F \supset K_T^U$
