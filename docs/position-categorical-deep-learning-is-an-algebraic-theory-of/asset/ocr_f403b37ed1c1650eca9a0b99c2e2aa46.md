# ## Abstract

We present our position on the elusive quest for a general-purpose framework for specifying and studying deep learning architectures. Our intuition is that the keys map so far a coherent bridge between specifications which must satisfy and their implementations. Focusing on such a bridge, we propose to apply category theory—precisely, the universal algebra of morphisms valued in a 2-category of programs—as a theory largely subsuming these flavours of neural network design. We show that our theory recovers constraints by geometric deep learning, as well as implementations of many architectures from the diverse landscape of neural networks, such as RNNs. We also illustrate how our theory enables many standard constructs in computer science and automata.

# ## 1.1 Our Opinion

It is our opinion that ample attention has already been given to both the top-down and bottom-up approaches in isolation, and there hasn't been sufficiently expressive to address them simultaneously. If we want guiding framework for deep learning, this needs to change. To support our opinion, we perform a few efforts on both sides of the divide.

One of the most successful examples of the tradeoff framework is geometric deep learning (Bronstein et al., 2021), GDL, which uses a group- and representation-theoretic perspective to describe neural networks via symmetry preserving constraints. The actual realisations of such layers are derived by solving constraints.

GDL proved to be powerful: allowing, e.g., to construct convolutional layers as an exact solution to translation invariance in grids (Fukushima et al., 1983; LeCun et al., 1998), and passing message and self-attention mechanisms to perform learning over graphs (Mayer et al., 2017; Vasikopoulos et al., 2017). It also extends to exotic domains such as spheres (Coben et al., 2018; de Haan et al., 2020) and geometric constraints (Fuchs et al., 2020). While this covers many architectures of interest, GDL also has inescapable constraints.

Firstly, usability of GDL to implement architectures correlates directly with how easy it is to resolve equation constraints.
