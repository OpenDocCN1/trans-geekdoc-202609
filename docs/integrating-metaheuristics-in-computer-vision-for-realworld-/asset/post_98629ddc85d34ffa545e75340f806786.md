Common natural polymers used in bioprinting include gelatin, alginate, collagen, hyaluronic acid (HA), chitosan, and agarose. On the other hand, synthetic polymers, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) and hydrogel, have similar chemical and mechanical properties to natural polymers. Hydrogels have the ability to absorb a significant amount of biological fluids and can mimic live tissues due to their soft and rubbery appearance when swelled. Hydrogels can be an essential component of tissue transplants because of their exceptional capacity to permit nutrient and oxygen transport while providing sufficient immunological isolation [16]. Additionally, synthetic polymers typically outperform natural ones in terms of their mechanical qualities. Contrary to natural polymers, synthetic polymers likewise offer few or no niches for cellular functions. Natural polymers, on the other hand, have great cell viability, improved cell–cell connections, and functional tissues.

Bioprinting requires precise and organized placement of relevant biomaterials onto cells to create highly mimetic natural tissues. The microenvironment surrounding bioprinted cells must replicate physiological conditions found in vivo to enable the cells to assume their in vivo roles. For tissue creation, a variety of cell types is necessary to perform different functions. Accurate positioning of cells in their respective locations or simultaneous seeding of different cell types in the same or different hydrogels is possible when printing various cell types to create complex tissues [17]. Additionally, researchers have explored directly seeding scaffolds with stem cells, and stem cell differentiation can be induced through bioink additives, culture conditions or stress during the printing process. Undifferentiated stem cells can also serve as a means to prevent graft rejection following tissue transplantation, as they do not provoke an immune response.

Furthermore, during tissue fabrication, various biomolecules, referred to as factors, the printed constructs can be used to simultaneously control the proliferation, differentiation, and migration of cells [18]. These factors include enzymes, growth factors, polynucleotides, polypeptides, and polysaccharides.

## 3.4 Applications for 3D Printing in Cells

The adaptability and remarkable tissue engineering resolution of 3D bioprinting has sparked high hopes among scientists. This technology has already been used to produce various tissue types, such as skin, cartilage, bone, cardiovascular, liver, and neuronal tissues.

### 3.4.1 Blood Vessels

For patients searching for a substitute organ for their damaged or missing body parts, tissue engineering holds enormous promise for creating an organ that is physiologically relevant and functionally active. The circulatory system, in particular, is crucial for the survival of organs. To develop functioning bioprinted vascular systems, researchers have investigated a variety of methods [19]. For instance, a team of researchers printed a microvascular structure in 2016 utilizing human microvascular endothelial cells, fibrin bioink, and a modified commercially available inkjet bioprinter. Vasculature bioprinting's main challenge continues to be cell survival. Thermal inkjet bioprinters offer the best compromise between cell survival and printing effectiveness, nevertheless. A successful crosslinking method is essential for optimizing tissues’ physical qualities, cell survival, and speeding up the printing process. The right kind of hydrogel can help with greater cell spreading and proliferation in addition to increasing cell viability. Another study team was able to create extracellular matrix that was decellularized and thick prevascularized tissues with layers of cells (dECM). The tissue also had many, differently sized branches of the vasculature [20]. The researchers also stated that even after 48 hours under in vitro circumstances, cell viability and structural integrity was preserved.

A bioink made by combining photo-crosslinkable gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) with a variety of cell types to approximate the makeup of native vascular cells produced high cell survival (>85%) in vitro in a recent work [21]. These bioprinted vascular structures were merged with host circulation and were shown to be functional. Additionally, subcutaneous transplantation in vivo showed that the printed vascular structures included red blood cells. This positive study showed the potential of using bioprinted vasculature in clinical settings. The dimensions of the bioprinted vasculature continue to be crucial for its effective operation in addition to cell viability. A team of researchers developed a totally biological self-assembly technique for scaffold-free small dimension (outside diameter: 0.9 to 2.5 mm) vascular reconstruction in 2009 [22]. This technique was executed through a fast prototyping bioprinting process.

Aortic valve bioprinting employing hydrogels based on polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) and sodium alginate was also reported by another group [23]. The inner diameter of these scaffolds ranged from 12 to 22 mm, and pig aortic valve interstitial cells adhered to them and moved through them. Sacrificial filaments, such as Pluronic F127, are a novel type of filament being developed by several research facilities that can be utilized to create fluidic channels. Such filaments could help to speed up printing in addition to helping to create vascular patterns [24].

### 3.4.2 Liver

Liver fibrosis poses a significant medical challenge as it involves a complex and progressive interplay between hepatocytes and nonparenchymal cells (NPCs) over time. Replicating the complexity of the liver in both in vitro and in vivo models is challenging [25]. However, a liver tissue model was fabricated using primary human hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and hepatic stellate cells through bioprinting to mimic essential fibrogenic features, making it one of the earliest of its kind. This model demonstrated the precise and automated inclusion of these cells in the bioprinting process. In a newly released study, the same group altered the culture conditions, causing collagen to gradually build up in the tissues over a prolonged exposure period. When Kupffer cells were included in the experiment, the TGF-1 and methotrexate-induced fibrogenic response to cytokine and pharmacological stimuli improved [26].

Another study team demonstrated a proof-of-concept for bioprinting a liver organ using a mixture of human stellate cells and HepaRG cells [26]. The expression of tight junctions and markers of general metabolism (glucose, lactate, and lactate dehydrogenase), as well as the expression of albumin and cytochrome P450 3A4 was observed that they remained stable when cultivated under static conditions in the liver that was created using a stereolithographic printing method. In accordance with a previously released study, a team of researchers developed a hydrogel model of an air sac that mimics the lung and delivers oxygen to the surrounding blood vessels [27]. These translucent lung air sac hydrogel models have a short manufacturing time and an effective composition that includes intravascular 3D fluid mixers as two of their main advantages. A rodent model of chronic liver injury was also used to implant structured hydrogel constructions containing liver cells, demonstrating the potential practical significance of these cutting-edge biomaterials.

### 3.4.3 Cartilage

Because cartilage tissue lacks blood vessels and nerves (aneural), it differs from other tissues (avascular). They mostly get the nutrition from synovial fluid [28]. The attempts at cartilage healing are rarely particularly successful because of these usual variances. Additionally, cartilage lesions do not heal naturally, leading to persistent problems that might lower a patient's quality of life. Similar to surgical techniques, tissue engineering techniques for cartilage repair also fell short of creating new tissue with characteristics and similarities to native cartilage. By using inkjet bioprinting, it is possible to produce tissues that closely resemble original tissues and heal cartilage without causing further harm. To fix cartilage abnormalities, a team of researchers 3D-printed human articular chondrocytes mixed with bioink. These chondrocytes had greater cell viability (up to 90%) and were equally dispersed throughout the hydrogel [29]. Furthermore, a stronger adhesion between the printed cartilage implant and the surrounding tissues as well as an increase in extracellular matrix production between the hydrogel scaffold and the host cartilage tissue were noted. The ear cartilage was successfully printed by another research team and implanted subcutaneously in naked mice. It is interesting to note that after 2 months, the build matured and developed new cartilage throughout the entire construct. The relevance of direct cartilage healing using bioprinting technology is illustrated by the aforementioned examples.

The regeneration of cartilage uses a variety of scaffolds and printers. Using a multiprinter-head system, a chondrocyte-encapsulated alginate hydrogel and polycaprolactone was printed with excellent biocompatibility. Inkjet printing and an electrospinning device were also used in a method for creating cartilage [30]. It was discovered that rabbit chondrocyte inkjet printing and electrospun PCL fibers may be used to produce a five-layer tissue construct. It was discovered that the chondrocytes grew well inside the printed constructs. Studies have also mentioned printing auricular cartilage and meniscus constructions using alginate and nanocellulose.

### 3.4.4 Muscle

Common musculoskeletal injuries can leave a patient weak and severely impair their ability to carry out daily activities. If left untreated, significant muscle injuries can cause chronic disability in addition to the production of scars [31]. There are various benefits to 3D bioprinting for muscle restoration.

The muscle component was formed using hydrogel-based bioink made of thermoplastic polyurethane and C2C12 myoblasts, while the tendon component was created using poly(-caprolactone) with NIH/3T3 cells.
