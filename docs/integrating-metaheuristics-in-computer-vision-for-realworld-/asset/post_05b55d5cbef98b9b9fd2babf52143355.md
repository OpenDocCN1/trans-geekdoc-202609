A brief comparison of all the models used to detect exoplanets can be seen in the Table 9.2. These comparisons include comparing the f1-score, recall, accuracy, and precision.

The second model is used to determine if an exoplanet is a gas giant, a Neptune-like planet, a super-earth, or a terrestrial planet. This dataset contains 16 characteristics over 18 columns and a total of 2507 exoplanets. The dataset's correlation matrix is shown in Figure 9.18. The correlation matrix reveals that the planet-star radius ratio and transit depth, which measures the drop in brightness of a star caused by an exoplanet transiting it, have a strong positive link. This indicates that when the size of the planet rises in relation to the star, the depth of the transit signal likewise grows, indicating that the exoplanet is greater in size. This is consistent with the plots shown above.

The feature importance plot for the exoplanet type prediction model indicates that the planetary radius is the most relevant feature (see Figure 9.19). With a relative importance of roughly 0.3, this variable is more influential than other features in predicting the model. With a relative importance of about 0.15, the transit depth is the second-most significant feature. With relative importance of less than 0.05, the remaining attributes are considered to be of relatively modest significance. These findings imply that the most important characteristics in determining the type of an exoplanet are the planetary radius and the depth of the transit.

Similar to the first model, this model also makes use of a number of machine learning methods. The confusion matrices for the decision tree classifier, random forest classifier, Naive Bayes, KNN, and logistic regression models are shown in Figures 9.20 to 9.24.
