Typically, the filters are tiny squares of pixels that glide over the image and execute a dot product operation on the image pixels. The feature maps that result emphasize various patterns and elements in the image, such as edges, corners, and textures. Following the convolutional layer, a pooling layer is often used to lower the spatial resolution of the feature maps and boost the network's processing efficiency. The pooling procedure pools the values of nearby pixels in the feature maps and produces a smaller feature map. A CNN's fully connected layers are identical to those found in a typical neural network and are used to categorize the input picture based on the learnt attributes. The output of the last fully connected layer is fed into a softmax activation function, which generates a probability distribution across the many potential classes of the input picture.

CNNs are taught via supervised learning, in which the network is given a large number of labelled training pictures and is tuned to minimize the gap between its predicted outputs and the real labels. This is usually accomplished with the use of a loss function, such as cross-entropy, and an optimization procedure, such as stochastic gradient descent. CNNs have shown cutting-edge performance in a variety of computer vision applications, including image classification, object identification, segmentation, and more.

# c. Viola-Jones Algorithm

The Viola–Jones method is a common computer vision technique for recognizing objects in photos and movies, particularly faces. It was introduced in 2001 by Paul Viola and Michael Jones and has since become one of the most extensively used face identification algorithms. The Viola–Jones algorithm is a form of Haar cascade classifier, which means it detects objects in images by using a succession of predefined characteristics. In the instance of face detection, the algorithm looks for patterns of human-facial traits, such as the location and form of the eyes, nose, and mouth. The program divides the picture into tiny subwindows and examines each one for the existence of the item.

It identifies potential regions of the picture that may include a face using a collection of pretrained Haar-like features, which are rectangular patterns of pixel intensities. These characteristics are easy to calculate and efficient, making them excellent for real-time applications. The Viola–Jones approach then applies a series of classifiers to the candidate regions, gradually eliminating false positives and retaining just the regions most likely to contain a face. Each classifier in the cascade is trained on a unique set of characteristics and tuned to reduce the amount of false positives at each level. The Viola–Jones approach also employs an integral picture representation, allowing the features to be efficiently calculated using basic arithmetic operations. As a result, the method is extremely quick and scalable, which is critical for real-time applications such as video surveillance. Overall, the Viola–Jones algorithm is a powerful and frequently used face identification technology with several applications in security, entertainment, and robotics.

# d. Local Binary Patterns:

LBP is a computer vision approach used for texture analysis and object detection. It was initially proposed in 1996 by Ojala, Pietikainen, and Harwood and has since become a popular feature extraction approach due to its simplicity and efficacy. The LBP method generates a binary pattern by comparing the intensity values of a pixel with those of its surrounding pixels.
