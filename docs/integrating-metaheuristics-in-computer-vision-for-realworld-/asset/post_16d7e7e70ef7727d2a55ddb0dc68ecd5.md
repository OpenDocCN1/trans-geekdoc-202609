The term “additive manufacturing” is another name for three-dimensional (3D) printing (AM). It is a method for creating 3D solid models of any shape from digital data. Medical 3D printing has long been a pipe dream in terms of science. Hull CW submitted the initial 3D printing patent in the 1980s. A 3D print of any object can be produced by adding successive layers of foundation material on top of one another [2]. Since then, 3D printing has had an impact on a number of industries, including manufacturing, engineering, and medicine.

In recent years, the idea of tissue regeneration, which was once regarded as a buzzword, has gained significant popularity. The emergence of new terminologies like tissue engineering (TE) and regenerative medicine (RM) can be attributed to this trend. Tissue engineering is a field that combines biological principles and engineering techniques, and it focuses on the replacement of damaged tissue. The scaffolds manufactured by the 3D bioprinting method are sturdy enough to physically support newly created tissue while also supporting cell contact [3]. It has been discovered that including stem cells in 3D bioprinted scaffolds has excellent potential for producing replacement tissues.

The 3D printing technique typically makes use of powder, plastics, metals, liquids, or even living cells. This technique is highly adaptable to specific requirements because the components can be integrated or deposited onto a substrate. The procedure is precise, economical, and repeatable, allowing it to dependably enable the creation of customized tissues. Additionally, it offers the chance for academics, engineers, and physicians to work together to create a real product from a digital file that was downloaded and transferred over the internet [4].

The use of 3D bioprinting has become indispensable in the medical field, it can be used to produce various types of tissues and organs for both humans and animals, as well as surgical implants and anatomical models that serve diagnostic and educational purposes. The objective of this study is to present a comprehensive review of the use of 3D printing technology in the field of medicine, including a discussion of current research trends and their impact on the healthcare industry.

## 3.2 Bioprinting in Three Dimensions (3D)

A printed model must be finished in five technical processes. Finding out the anatomy of the target area is the first step. Subsequently, the structural data is subjected to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) scanning to generate the 3D geometry. The resulting file is prepared for actual printing. Next, the right 3D printer and components are carefully chosen. To build layered slices of the model, additional processing is applied to the file. The printer is then fed these portions to print them. The initial layer is printed initially, followed by the addition of more layers until the entire model is formed.

## 3.3 3D Printing Types

The printing industry has undergone significant advancements over time, resulting in the adoption of various 3D printing techniques in research and commercial applications [5]. Some of the well-known 3D printing techniques include fused deposition modeling (FDM), stereolithography, microextrusion, inkjet printing, selective laser sintering (SLS), laser beam melting, electron beam melting, digital laser printing (DLP), and polyjet printing [6]. Furthermore, bioprinting systems utilize various methods, such as radio/microwave, acoustic (ultrasound-based), pneumatic techniques, and electrohydrodynamic (EHD), to produce tissues and organs. Inkjet printing, laser-aided printing, extrusion printing, and stereolithography are some of the commonly used bioprinting techniques, and the subsequent sections provide brief explanations of each method.

### 3.3.1 Inkjet Bioprinting

One of the first AM techniques was the introduction of inkjet printing [7]. In simpler terms, the ink passes through a compressed chamber, which then releases droplets from a pore (Figure 3.2a), the ink is being reacted. Inkjet droplet squeezing now uses three basic methods: thermal, piezoelectric, and electrostatic. The two that are used the most frequently for structure manufacturing are thermal and piezoelectric [8]. Because of the intense pressure of air bubbles that are created as a result of localized heating, thermal inkjet printers expel ink drops from the nozzles. The three main benefits of inkjet printing are its high speed, availability, and affordability. On the other hand, the main drawbacks are reliance on low viscosity bioink and lack of accuracy in droplet size and positioning. Thermal inkjet printers can clog easily as well.

### 3.3.2 Microextrusion Bioprinting

Extrusion bioprinting involves the extrusion of a viscous bioink through a small opening, resulting in a continuous cylindrical line that is printed onto a substrate. Extrusion bioprinting is classified into two types, namely mechanical or pneumatic, depending on the type of pneumatic or mechanical screw plungers employed to dispense the bioink (Figure 3.2b). Extrusion bioprinting systems can accommodate bioinks with different viscosities, and the extrusion speed, process, and deposit location can all be modified as needed [9]. Direct-writing systems or bioplotting are based on extrusion bioprinting, and they can use cells, cell-encapsulated matrices, and polymers as materials.

### 3.3.3 Laser-Assisted Printing

Laser-assisted bioprinters are based on the laser direct writing technique and primarily use photopolymerization technology for printing biomaterials. This method enables the printing of a diverse range of cells while preserving their viability [10]. A pulsed laser source directs a focus tool towards a metallic ribbon film that absorbs laser energy and a receiving substrate (see Figure 3.2c). While printing, the laser focuses on the top glass layer of the ribbon, which is coated with a thin layer of gold and titanium nanoparticles. As the layer evaporates, bubbles form at the bottom, which release the suspended bioink droplets onto the receiving substrate. Laser-assisted printing is not restricted by nozzles, making it suitable for use with a broad range of materials like hydrogels, ceramics, epoxy-based photoresist SU-8, cell-encapsulating materials, and cells [11]. The technique offers several advantages, including high precision, the ability to print high viscosity materials, and high cell densities. However, it is associated with high costs and a lengthy process.

Extrusion bioprinting boasts several advantages, such as the capacity to handle high cell densities, accommodate multiple printer heads for intricate printing requirements using diverse bioinks, and process high viscosity bioinks with ease. On the other hand, one significant drawback of the extrusion bioprinting procedure is the breakdown of cell structure when passing through the tiny printer head.

### 3.3.4 Stereolithography

The process of bioprinting with laser assistance is comparable to stereolithography. By layer-by-layer selectively solidifying the photosensitive polymer with UV light, stereolithography printing creates intricate structures. The organ structure is converted into a stereolithography file that corresponds to MRI and CT scan images (Figure 3.2d). After being divided into thin layers, the structure can then be printed one layer at a time. To create a 3D-printed structure in stereolithography, the respective top layers are overlaid once the lower layer has solidified due to light [12].

The two main benefits of stereolithography are improved fabrication precision and quick printing. On the other hand, some of the significant limitations of stereolithography include the use of intense UV light, the requirement of prolonged postprocessing procedures, and the limited availability of compatible materials [13].

### 3.3.5 3D Bioprinting Materials and Cells

To ensure that bioprinted materials do not require additional surgeries for implant removal, it is crucial that noncytotoxic, printable, and biodegradable biomaterials are used. Furthermore, the printed material must be strong enough to support and nurture the cells that are seeded. Additionally, the biomaterials used for printing should encourage cell adhesion, maturation, migration, proliferation, and differentiation [14].
