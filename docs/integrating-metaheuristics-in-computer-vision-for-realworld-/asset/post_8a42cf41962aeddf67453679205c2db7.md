
Several different optimization methods exist for linear, quadratic, and convex optimization. These methods include the interior-point approach, the quasi-Newton method, the trust-region method, and the simplex method.

## 13.2.2 Gradient-Based Approaches

Gradient-based techniques are among the most popular classical optimization methods. They are first-order methods (iterative methods) that only exploit information on the objective function and its gradient (subgradient). In simple terms, gradient-based methods generate a new solution $x_{k+1}$ at step k by iteratively incrementing the previous value $x_k$ in accordance with the gradient of the objective function $h(x)$.

$x_{k+1} = x_k + \Delta x_k$

$\Delta x_k = \epsilon g(\nabla h, x^k)$

where the $g(\nabla h, x^k)$ is regarded as the function of the gradient of $h$, and $\epsilon$ represents the step size which varies over the course of an iteration.

The optimization problem's form is determined by the direction of the increment vector $\Delta x_k$. If the vector direction is along the negative gradient ($-\nabla h$) then it will be a minimization problem and if the direction is along a positive gradient then it will be a maximization problem.

In order to minimize $h(x)$, this technique uses an approximation of the cubic polynomial $P(x)$ as

$P(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3$

If the function f(x) is evaluated at four different locations, then the polynomial coefficients $a_0, a_1, a_2$ can be found by simultaneously solving four linear equations. It is still feasible to evaluate the polynomial coefficients if the value of the function and its derivatives are available at two points. The minimum point can be calculated by utilizing the polynomial coefficients after approximating the function with a polynomial.

This search method begins by bracketing the minimum of the function between two points, $x_1$ and $x_2$ so that the following conditions are satisfied.

$f'(x_1) f'(x_2) < 0$

Using $f(x_1), f'(x_1), f(x_2)$ and $f'(x_2)$, the approximate cubic polynomial's minimal value will be determined as follows:

$$\bar{x} = \begin{cases} x_2 & \text{if } \mu < 0 \\ x_2 - \mu(x_2 - x_1) & \text{if } 0 < \mu < 1 \\ x_1 & \text{if } \mu > 1 \end{cases}$$

where

$$\mu = \frac{f''(x_2) + \omega - z}{f'(x_2) - f'(x_1) + 2\omega}$$

$$z = \frac{3(f(x_1) - f(x_2))}{x_2 - x_1} + f'(x_1) + f'(x_2)$$

$$\omega = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{|x_2 - x_1|} + \sqrt{z^2 - f'(x_1)f'(x_2)}$$

## 13.2.3 Gradient-Free Algorithm

Even gradient calculations for large-scale problems can be expensive, and this is particularly true for deep learning problems [25]. In this case, a rough estimate or a partial evaluation of the gradient vector could be helpful. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is one of them. It assesses gradients over a small subset of data sparingly, and the selection of those data may be arbitrary. Optimization algorithms that take cues from nature can benefit from randomization because it improves the diversity and quality of solutions, increases exploration of the search space, and prevents getting stuck in local optima.

## 13.2.4 Nature-Inspired Optimization Algorithms

Alan Turing, the father of heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms, most likely invented them in the 1940s. Then, in the 1960s, evolutionary programming and genetic algorithms were devised [26]. In the 1980s, simulated annealing emerged first [27], followed by Fred Glover's major research on tabu search and meta-heuristics [28, 29]. The 1990s represented a turning point for meta-heuristics and evolutionary computation. Both ACO [2] and PSO [30] were developed in 1995, the former by Marco Dorigo and the latter by James Kennedy and Russell C. Eberhart. After that, R. Storn and K. Price came up with differential evolution (DE) [31] in 1997. D.H. Wolpert and W.G. Macready developed the deep no-free-lunch theorems at the same time [32].

The harmony search [33], the bee algorithm [34], FA [35], CS [36], and BA [37] were all developed rapidly in the first decade of the twenty-first century. More algorithms, such as the flower pollination algorithm, have been published in the research [38], and the research advances continuously, like the grey wolf algorithm [39], arithmetic algorithm [6], Aquila algorithm [5].

In general, nature-inspired algorithms are classified as heuristics or meta-heuristics, though the distinction is minor. In this context, “heuristic” refers to discovering or locating something through hit and trial, whereas “meta” refers to something beyond. Most literature studies will refer to nature-inspired algorithms as metaheuristics.

Among the primary benefits of metaheuristic algorithms, is their ability to rapidly locate accurate and potentially optimal answers. The theory behind these methods is that they should perform relatively well most of the time, but not always because optimal solutions cannot be guaranteed. As a result, there are numerous approaches to designing such algorithms. Over 100 distinct algorithms and variants are thought to exist [40].

In this chapter, we have no plans to evaluate all applicable algorithms. Rather, we concentrate on a handful of algorithms, highlighting their fundamental concepts and characteristics.

## 13.2.5 Genetic Algorithms

GA is likely the most widely recognized example of a nature-inspired algorithm. John Holland [41] created GA, an evolutionary algorithm, which was motivated by the naturalistic Darwinian evolution of biological systems.

An optimization problem's solution vectors are represented as a binary sequence of 1s and 0s known as chromosomes. A pool of potential solutions, represented as chromosomes, serves as the starting point for the algorithm. Each chromosome includes a unique set of genes that serve as the parameters in the optimization problem. The fitness function is then evaluated for each chromosome to ascertain the degree to which the solution meets the problem's objective. Following this, the program uses genetic operators like selection, crossover, and mutation to produce offspring chromosomes [42, 43]. Crossover and mutation introduce new genetic material into a population and create new populations/solutions, while natural selection favors chromosomes with greater fitness. Iterations of this procedure are continued until a stopping criterion is reached, which can be a maximum number of allowed iterations or attaining a sufficient level of fitness. With the aid of this generational repetition, the population evolves and progresses towards an optimal solution and identifies the fittest individuals.

## 13.2.6 Particle Swarm Optimization

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is a metaheuristic optimization technique inspired by the collective intelligence of natural systems like the behavior of bird flocks and fish schools. The fitness function assists the PSO algorithm as it seeks the best possible solution to a problem. The particle population is denoted by $x$, and the fitness function is written as $f(x) = f(x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots, x_n)$. Each particle adjusts its velocity based on the collective and individual flying experiences of its peers. Each particle adjusts its location based on its current positions, speed, and the difference between its best personal best position ($Pbest$) and the swarm's best position ($Gbest$).

The following equation is used to adjust the velocity of each particle:

$S_{t + 1} = wS_t + c_1 r_1(P_{best} - X_t) + c_2 r_2(G_{best} - X_t)$

where $S_t$ = Particle's velocity at a time 't, $X_t$ = Particle's current position at time 't, $P_{best}$ = historical best position of an individual, $G_{best}$ = swarm best position. Both $r_1$ and $r_2$ are random numbers in the range from 0 to 1, while $c_1$ and $c_2$ are acceleration constant values.

## 13.2.7 Ant Colony Optimization

Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) is an optimization algorithm that is inspired by the food-searching habits of ants. It was proposed in 1992 by Marco Dorigo [2]. An artificial ant colony (ACO) is utilized to find the optimal solution to an optimization problem. The ants find a solution by marching through the problem’s search area and leaving pheromones at the path’s boundaries. The pheromone signifies the quality of the solution discovered by the ant. Each ant determines its next move based on the pheromone trail and heuristic information, which evaluates the merits of moving from one location to another. Based on the quality of the solutions discovered by the ants, the pheromone trail is modified. The algorithm keeps on iterating until a stopping criterion, which can be a maximum number of allowed iterations or a minimum required fitness value.

ACO is ideally adapted for discrete optimization problems. For instance, the answer to a routing problem can be encoded as a route or path. When ants explore diverse paths, they leave behind a pheromone trail that diminishes with time. The quality/fitness of the solution is directly linked to the concentration of the Pheromone. The probability of taking a certain path at a junction increases if that path has a higher concentration of pheromones. ACO, like GA, is a hybrid process with numerous versions and applications.

## 13.2.8 Grey Wolf Algorithm

Mirajalili et al. developed a population-based optimization method called Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) [39]. GWO’s appealing aspect is its hierarchical structure, which distinguishes it from other population-based algorithms. The hierarchy’s supremacy is built with the use of a goal known as the objective function which is then subdivided into a fitness function and a cost function, both are needed to calculate the output [44]. The optimum solution is obtained among all the available candidates.

The Grey Wolf algorithm is based on wolf hunting strategies that look for prey (food) collectively and as the name indicates that the algorithm imitates the foraging behavior of wolves. The search was initiated by three wolves: alpha (α), beta (β), and delta (δ). They are all members of the dominant rank and have precedence when making decisions. The omega wolves are an additional dominant rank that does not participate in decision-making. The fitness values of alpha (alpha), beta (beta), and delta (delta) are used to obtain the best possible solution to the optimization problem. The greatest strength of GWO is the ability of its leading wolves to pinpoint the location of their prey and direct omegas for space exploration.
