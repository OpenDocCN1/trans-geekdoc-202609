### 5.2.1.1 IoT in Drug Repositioning

The ability of the Internet of Things (IoT) to revolutionize pharmaceutical production processes, from drug discovery to remote patient access and monitoring, is incomparable. Many leading pharmaceutical businesses from all over the world are gradually implementing IoT technology in their developed plants to achieve optimization and boost development effectiveness. Earlier, data availability and understanding were a substantial difficulty for operational communication because diverse information formats for different procedures in a pharmaceutical production facility existed. By meritoriously connecting the network, equipment, and systems throughout the plant, IoT technologies built on various underlying communication protocols, including as near-field communication (NFC), bluetooth, ultra-wide band, and 5G, empower calibration within a pharmaceutical manufacturing facility. Additionally, by utilizing IoT, pharmaceutical organizations can access real-time data and operations across the entire manufacturing process [9].

The internet of things, cloud computing, social media, and other emerging information technologies are generating a tonne of data that is fueling the expansion of various academic fields. However, due to their enormous variety, the speed at which they are acquired, and their volume, the interpretation of these data is inherently complicated. Large-scale experimental data creation is now possible due to recent technological advancements including high-performance biomedical data-capturing technologies and next-generation sequencing as well as cost reductions. Statistical methods, ML, and, most importantly, models based on biological networks are the foundations of the computational tools currently employed to analyze these data. These computational techniques have already demonstrated excellent potential for bridging the gap between the production and understanding of enormous amounts of data in the health sciences [10]. In the field of life sciences, an IoT-based revolution is only getting started. Pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies are able to spend millions of dollars and swiftly reap the rewards while achieving clinical outcomes. Trials of ingestible pills containing tiny cameras are being conducted to monitor the administration of medications for disorders that cause chronic diseases. Drug research is being sped by new technologies, giving patients hope.

70% of the time required for an FDA approval of a new pharmaceutical concept is spent conducting clinical tests (Food and Drug Administration). These tests must show that the remedy has a high level of efficacy and is secure for usage. Typically, the FDA gives the firm permission to produce the drug solely for the one particular use that was assessed during the studies. IoT can lower the development cost and improve the likelihood that a successful formulation will be selected over less promising ones.

### 5.2.1.2 AI and ML in Drug Repositioning

Nowadays, inventions have no bounds; they have extended the way for the next generations to live a disease-free, modern, and secure life. The death rate has decreased and the average human lifespan has increased because of inventions, particularly in the biomedical sciences. One of them is AI, a turning point in technology. The term AI refers to a class of computing techniques that, when applied to text, image, and sound data, are expected to function similarly to the human brain. A great revolution in AI has been brought about by the recent expansion of extraordinary biomedical data (including genomes, proteomics, metabolomics, drug libraries, and patient care data), the creation of new algorithms, and the expansion of supercomputers. Additionally, AI is employed in the increasingly popular scientific topic of medication design. Eventually, AI in the drug design space aids in determining therapeutic targets and medication efficacy. Since AI is dependable, accessible, and accurate, it has been included in practically all scientific fields, including medication repurposing. The next stage of AI-assisted drug design is drug repositioning. While repositioning a medication, AI is running into some difficulties. Many pharmaceutical companies no longer manufacture new pharmaceuticals due to the high cost and lengthy process associated with conventional drug design. However, this strategy enabled the relevant staff to continue using the existing drug to treat a variety of conditions. All of these are now carried out utilizing multiple components of designed software, making it very simple to estimate the target, medicine, and course of treatment. Since it provides comprehensive knowledge about the gene sequence, protein structure, disease, and framework of the medicine desired, the service provided by docking and repurposing software are unavoidable. It is found that the medication interacts with the target molecule, it can then be developed further and converted from an in-silico platform to an in vitro platform. To predict the efficacy of the medicine on the target, virtual screening is used. In this work, we place particular emphasis on how AI technologies might be applied to the medication development process, particularly for drug repurposing. It offers in-depth knowledge of the resources and web interfaces helpful for AI-based medication repositioning, a directory of emerging biomedical companies embracing AI technology, and some advice for the future for building minds.

In drug repositioning, new uses for already-available medications are found faster and less expensive. Different computational drug repositioning strategies exist. Computational drug repositioning strategies can be loosely categorized into three groups using the COVID-19 pandemic as an example: (i) drug/target network-based models, (ii) structure-based approaches, and (iii) AI approaches. There are two different categories of network-based methods: network-based clustering and network-based propagation [11]. Drug development is the next crucial step after inferring viral structure and their functionalities. Drug discovery is costly and time-consuming, but AI can use neural networks to speed up the process and boost efficiency as shown in Figure 5.2 [12]. Due to their increased data requirements, AI-based networks currently seem less applicable.

The ability to use computational approaches to envision new indications/placements for currently available medications has been made possible by the rapidly developing high-precision in silico techniques/algorithms and steadily expanding computational access to vast amounts of data about clinical research, disease pathways, gene expression profiles, drug target structures, pharmacophores, and other topics [13]. Structure-based drug repositioning (SBDR) is able to screen the repurposable chemical space (RCS) and investigate potential ligand interactions with the target binding site utilising methods like docking, pharmacophore modeling, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, among many other in silico techniques, is significant in and of itself as shown in Table 5.2. Over the past few years, data-driven ML applications have expanded dramatically, and they are now a crucial tool in the early phases of drug discovery. Several factors, including the speedy accumulation of practical experimental data (such as Drug Bank, ChEMBL, PDB, PubChem, and PDBbind), the advent of modern ML algorithms, libraries, and affordably priced computing power, are driving this trend.

ML techniques attempt to learn from past data and foresee qualities rather than immediately computing them based on a physics-based understanding [14]. The drug discovery process has the potential to change as a result of the increasing application of ML and AI to various areas of the discovery process. Drug screening and repurposing, structure-based modelling, an image-based analysis of cells using high-content microscopy, drug safety evaluation, and de novo chemical synthesis are a few examples of applications for AI/ML as shown in Figure 5.3.
