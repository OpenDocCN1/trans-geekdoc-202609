# 3.4.3 Cartilage

Because cartilage tissue lacks blood arteries and nerves (aneural), it differs from other tissues (avascular). They mostly get the nutrition from synovial fluid [28]. The attempts at cartilage healing are rarely particularly successful because of these usual variances. Additionally, cartilage lesions do not heal naturally, leading to persistent problems that might lower a patient's quality of life. Similar to surgical techniques, tissue engineering techniques for cartilage repair also fell short of creating new tissue with characteristics and similarities to native cartilage. By using inkjet bioprinting, it is possible to produce tissues that closely resemble original tissues and heal cartilage without causing further harm. To fix cartilage abnormalities, a team of researchers 3D-printed human articular chondrocytes mixed with bioink. These chondrocytes had greater cell viability (up to 90%) and were equally dispersed throughout the hydrogel [29]. Furthermore, a stronger adhesion between the printed cartilage implant and the surrounding tissues as well as an increase in extracellular matrix production between the hydrogel scaffold and the host cartilage tissue were noted. The ear cartilage was successfully printed by another research team and implanted subcutaneously in naked mice. It is interesting to note that after 2 months, the build matured and developed new cartilage throughout the entire construct. The relevance of direct cartilage healing using bioprinting technology is illustrated by the aforementioned examples.

The regeneration of cartilage uses a variety of scaffolds and printers. Using a multiprinter-head system, a chondrocyte-encapsulated alginate hydrogel and polycaprolactone was printed with excellent biocompatibility. Inkjet printing and an electro spinning device were also used in a method for creating cartilage [30]. It was discovered that rabbit chondrocyte inkjet printing and electrospun PCL fibers may be used to produce a five-layer tissue construct. It was discovered that the chondrocytes grew well inside the printed constructs. Studies have also mentioned printing auricular cartilage and meniscus constructions using alginate and nanocellulose.

# 3.4.4 Muscle

Common musculoskeletal injuries can leave a patient weak and severely impair their ability to carry out daily activities. If left untreated, significant muscle injuries can cause chronic disability in addition to the production of scars [31]. There are various benefits to 3D bio-printing for muscle restoration.

The muscle component was formed using hydrogel-based bioink made of thermoplastic polyurethane and C2C12 myoblasts, while the tendon component was created using poly (-caprolactone) with NIH/3T3 cells.
