# e. HOG:

The HOG is a feature extraction approach in computer vision that is used for object identification and recognition. Dalal and Triggs initially proposed it in 2005, and it has since become a prominent approach in the field of computer vision.

The HOG technique generates a histogram of gradient orientations by examining the gradient orientation and magnitude of each pixel in an image. The picture is divided into tiny cells, the gradient orientation and magnitude of each pixel inside each cell are computed, and the gradient information is aggregated into a histogram of gradient orientations.

The gradient orientations histogram captures the image's local structure and texture and may be utilized as a feature descriptor for item identification and recognition. The HOG technique also includes a normalizing step that makes the descriptor insensitive to variations in lighting and contrast. The HOG technique offers several benefits over other feature extraction methods, including the ability to capture the shape and texture of objects in an image as well as its resistance to changes in scale, orientation, and deformation. It is also computationally efficient and may be used on big datasets as well as in real-time applications. HOG offers wide range of computer vision applications, including pedestrian identification, face detection, and object recognition It is widely utilized in fields like as surveillance, autonomous cars, and robotics, and has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on a variety of benchmark datasets.

# 2.5.1 Facial Recognition

Facial recognition technology is a type of biometric identification system that uses a person's facial features to identify them. This technology has been around for a long time, with the first studies on facial recognition dating back to the 1960s. However, significant technological advancements in the 21st century have resulted in widespread adoption of facial recognition in a variety of industries, including security, law enforcement, and marketing.

Facial recognition technology was primarily used in security systems such as access control and surveillance cameras in the early 2000s. These systems used Eigen face recognition, a technique that involved representing a person's face as a mathematical model and comparing it to a database of known faces. The system would then compare the captured face to those in its database and make a match if the similarity exceeded a certain threshold.

In the early 2000s, the accuracy of facial recognition technology was limited, with false positives and false negatives being relatively common. False positives happen when the system incorrectly identifies someone as a match when they are not, and false negatives happen when the system fails to identify someone who is a match.

In the early 2000s, one of the main challenges of facial recognition technology was the ability to accurately identify faces in a variety of lighting conditions, angles, and poses. To address this, researchers created more sophisticated algorithms capable of recognizing faces even when they were partially obscured or turned away from the camera.
