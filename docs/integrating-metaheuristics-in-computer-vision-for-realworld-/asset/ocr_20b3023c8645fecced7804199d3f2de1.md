# 3.4.7 Neutralization of Neurons

Another clinical setting where 3D bioprinting has enormous potential is neuronal regeneration. Recently, scientists printed NG108-15 cells and pig Schwann cells for brain engineering using a piezoelectric inkjet printer [38]. Despite the high voltage in the printer, the cell viability remained high, and after 7 days of printing, a modest increase in vitality was seen.

The effects of bioprinting on neuronal regeneration or reinnervation have been investigated in a number of in vivo investigations. One example of successful nerve restoration using 3D printing is the production of nerve guiding conduits using photocurable poly- ethylene glycol resin and microstereolithography. A recent investigation demonstrated the creation of conduits capable of encouraging reinnervation across a 3-mm gap in only 3 weeks, producing outcomes comparable to those of an autograft control. Another study described a new nerve guiding scaffold that combines fiber drawing with salt leaching to create microchannels, which allows for better control of microchannel cross-section and porosity, resulting in significant advantages for large-scale processes. In addition, a research team has developed a personalized nerve restoration technique using microextrusion printing [39].

The printed materials used included Alginate/CaCl2/PLGA/PCL/silicone/gelatin. In another study, a semisynthetic hydrogel called Gelatinmethacryloyl containing glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) or nerve growth factor (NGF) was utilized to facilitate effective nerve regeneration in printed scaffolds. Furthermore, a group of researchers produced a conduit that was biocompatible using Schwann cells, agarose, and mouse marrow-derived stem cells. The 3D-printed graft was strengthened by a collagen tube surrounding it and was inserted into a rat sciatic nerve injury model for 40 weeks, leading to the discovery of axon regeneration in the areas surrounding the agarose rods. These findings provide preclinical proof for the use of 3D bioprinting. Recently, a study created bioinks that are filler-free and bioinspired, by selectively blending natural and synthetic polymers to achieve predetermined rheological properties for the matrix. During in vivo experiments, the free-standing tissue constructs loaded with cells were found to be biocompatible and had a low cytotoxic response [40]. The fabrication of soft, autonomous 3D neuronal tissues could have potential advantages for the production of neural tissues for regenerative medicine.
