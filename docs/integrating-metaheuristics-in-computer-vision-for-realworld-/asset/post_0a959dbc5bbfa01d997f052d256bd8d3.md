### Abstract

Bioinspired algorithms have progressed to a new level in the solution of engineering optimization challenges. They have recently piqued the interest of academics due to their ease of use and high performance. These algorithms are primarily employed as global optimization tools, and they have been utilized to solve a variety of optimization engineering challenges. In this regard, a number of algorithms have been formulated taking inspiration from swarm intelligence. Therefore, an effort has been made to highlight a few popular optimization algorithms established. The major goal of this chapter is to present an overview of the most recent advances in swarm intelligence and nature-inspired computation, with a focus on optimization algorithms. The chapter also highlights the strengths and limitations of swarm intelligence and computation. Probable future directions that can address the unresolved issues are made in the chapter for speedy reference for readers.

*Keywords:* Swarm intelligence, optimization, gradient-based algorithms

### 13.1 Introduction

Applications for optimization range widely, from data mining and machine learning to corporate planning and engineering design. Optimization can maximize profit, outcomes, efficiency, effectiveness, and reliability, or minimize energy consumption, costs, journey time, and ecological effects. The real-world forces us to solve a wide variety of design issues with a finite amount of time, money, and other resources, so we have to find the best solutions to a wide range of design challenges while working within a number of difficult constraints.

To adequately represent and define optimization problems, requires mathematical optimization or mathematical programming, encompassing multiple domains including manufacturing, production, supply chain management, inventory control, transportation, business management, artificial intelligence, finance, engineering, mechanics, economics, control theory, and design engineering. Minimizing or maximizing a specified objective function is the primary goal of any optimization problem. The objective function is the quantity (profit, expense, etc.) that is to be optimized.

Simple brute force methods are neither practicable nor desirable for finding optimal solutions, despite the ever-increasing computing capacity of today’s computers. Algorithm efficiency is essential to practically all applications. There are a wide variety of various optimization algorithms, including evolutionary algorithms like evolutionary strategy and genetic algorithm, as well as more conventional approaches like gradient-based algorithms, Tabu search, and the interior-point method [1]. New algorithms like the ant colony optimizer (ACO) [2], particle swarm optimizer (PSO) [3], bat algorithm (BA) [4], Aquila optimizer (AO) [5], and arithmetic optimizer (AO) [6] are all based on ideas from nature. A few other techniques in different fields are discussed in Sharma et al., Mahajan et al., and Singh et al. [7–9].

Every optimization algorithm has some advantages and disadvantages. With the help of this biologically-inspired paradigm, researchers began to explore the cooperative systems in the natural world that they might use to develop nature-inspired algorithms. This collaborative approach has led to the investigation of these algorithms for their beneficial purpose by mitigating their drawbacks [10, 11]. For example: in the Gradient-Based method, the variations are represented by differential equations with space and time gradients. The downside of this method is its reliance on noise, occlusions, and discontinuities. Similarly, the gravitational algorithm (GA)-based method can be difficult to optimize and can be time-consuming to run. Additionally, they may not find the global optimum and can be stuck in local optima. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) has a slow speed of convergence. Grey Wolf Optimization algorithm (GWO) has the disadvantages of slow convergence speed, low solution accuracy, and easily falling into the local optimum. Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) has some shortcomings in the convergence speed and solution accuracy when dealing with a large amount of data. Similarly, arithmetic optimizer (AO) has an inconsistent convergence rate.

### 13.2 Optimization Algorithms

Numerous problems constitute optimization problems. For instance: scheduling problems, traveling salesman problems, control and manufacturing problems, and planning and design performance problems [12]. In computer science, optimization problems can be seen as solutions to problems like data clustering and classification used in fields like artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and data mining (DM). A problem can often have numerous optimal solutions, and optimization is the process of determining which one is best. One or more objectives can be accomplished. Multiple constraints which either can be maximized (hard) or minimized (soft) are subject to optimization problems [13–21]. There are numerous influential factors. The change in their values will affect (improve or deteriorate) the “measurable performance” and the degree of “constraints” violation. It is undoubtedly helpful to formulate optimization questions to obtain some understanding of the issue, but finding the answer is not that much easy. It can be very challenging to solve many optimization problems; e.g. the arithmetic problem known as the traveling sales problem (TSP) involves determining the most efficient route between a set of points and destinations. TSP is known as the non-deterministic polynomial-time problem. Given this, it seems highly improbable that a polynomial-time algorithm exists that solves the TSP in all cases, and it is quite possible that no algorithm exists that solves the problem exactly in all cases. Therefore, approximation and heuristic approaches appear to be the primary choices [22].

### 13.2.1 Mathematical Formulation

Mathematically quoting, an optimization problem is one in which certain variables have to be determined to obtain the best-measured efficiency (objective function) within specified constraints which spans design space vectors, \(x = x_1, x_2, ... x_D\)

Several objective functions must be optimized in a multi-objective optimization problem. Then, these functionalities should either be maximized or decreased based on necessity. Some applications are found in Acharya and Saha and Kumawat et al. [23, 24]. MOPs have more constraints than a single objective function (SOP). Following equations can be used to express an optimization problem in a general form.

```
Maximize/Minimize \(f_n(x) =\) \hfill (13.1)
\(g_j(x) \le 0 \quad j = 1, 2, ... .M\) \hfill (13.2)
\(h_j(x) \le 0 \quad i = 1, 2, ... .N\) \hfill (13.3)
```

Where \(g_j\) and \(h_j\) are known as equality and inequality constraints. It is possible to transform any maximization problem into a minimization problem by just inverting its sign. If \(f_x\), \(g_j\) and \(h_j\) are linear then it turns into a linear programming problem (LP). The simplex method makes it comparatively simple to solve linear programs. However, since the problem functions \(f_x\), \(g_j\) and \(h\) are typically all nonlinear, solving them can be difficult.

If the choice variable \(x_i\) is limited to discrete values, the optimization problem turns into a discrete task. When \(x_i\) admits only integer values, this form of optimization is known as integer programming. A hybrid integer program is generated when some variables are discrete while some are continuous. Programming with integers and programming with mixed integers are challenging combinatorial or discrete optimization problems.
