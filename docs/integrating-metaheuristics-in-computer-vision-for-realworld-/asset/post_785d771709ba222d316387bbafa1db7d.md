## 13.3 Algorithms and Self-Organization

Various methods exist to assess algorithms, depending on the numerous available perspectives. This section will primarily concentrate on algorithmic properties and self-organization.

### 13.3.1 Algorithmic Attributes

Our focus is on the key steps involved in how nature-inspired algorithms work, e.g. search motions and processes, exploration and exploitation, and update of equations or dynamics [49, 50]. They can also be thought of as self-organizing systems [51]. In summary, all algorithms are as follows:

- Every algorithm is population-based, with many agents constituting the population. (e.g., wolves, aquilas, bees, particles, etc.). In any optimization problem, the location of every agent within the design space is regarded as a potential solution. The agents can then interact together based on a specific set of rules, which are typically local laws.

- By modifying the current population’s operators (e.g., mutation, crossover), the population is evolved iteratively. Any perturbation found in the solution of the population is achieved by selecting a random solution, with a fitness-biased selection process. All algorithms are capable of both local exploitation and global exploration.

- Various forms of elitism are combined with the “survival of the fittest” principle to select the best candidate solutions. The fundamental objective is to facilitate the transmission of superior ideas to future generations.

- The candidate’s diversity is higher in the early stages of iterations with increased unpredictability. Diversity will eventually decrease as iterations continue. When all candidates become similar, the system may reach a converged state. A converged candidate contains few strong similarities and organization, but its variety has been reduced to a bare minimum.

## 13.4 Future Research Directions

Although nature-inspired methods are growing more vigorously, they fall back on other methods in developing a theoretical understanding and a proper mathematical framework. A recent study has found a considerable disconnection between practice and theory. For instance, several authors have solved various optimization problems in engineering by using nature-inspired methods, but we still don’t fully grasp why and how they work. We cannot determine if these algorithms will work well for a particular sort of problem, especially novel problems unless we test them. Moreover, the rate of convergence appears to be an enigma. Some methods converge rapidly on certain problems, while others converge slowly.

Compiling all open problems might be a daunting undertaking. In this section, we will draw attention to a handful of unresolved issues in order to stimulate further research.

- 1. A theoretical framework will enable a thorough examination of various strategies to obtain in-depth knowledge of their characteristics and search mechanisms, allowing for more efficient use of these algorithms and possibly the development of more efficient algorithms. Using Markov chains, dynamical systems, and other models, one can conduct theoretical investigations. A multidisciplinary approach is anticipated to be more effective.

- 2. Convergence: It would be quite beneficial to understand the convergence of algorithms and their accurate convergence rate. This enables scholars to determine the conditions and parameter values necessary for a specific algorithm to achieve optimal convergence while avoiding premature convergence. To optimize the rate of convergence, algorithm-dependent parameters can be modified; however, it is unclear how to modify an algorithm in the most effective manner.

- 3. Stability and robustness: While an algorithm’s efficiency is crucial, its stability is as critical. Data noise, the nature of the problem being addressed, and the algorithm’s parameter settings can all affect an algorithm’s stability and robustness. Unstable algorithms are ineffective because they are incapable of generating intelligent, dependable results. Dynamical system theory can be useful for understanding algorithmic stability in most instances; however, certain techniques, such as PSO, can be highly nonlinear, necessitating the use of stability analysis.

- 4. Performance comparison: The literature has numerous algorithms. When different versions are taken into account, there are over a hundred distinct methodologies and types. The validity of comparative studies is contingent on the performance measures employed. Inaccurate performance measurements may result in inaccurate outcomes. It is uncertain which measures are suitable. Currently, precision, success rates, Pareto optimality, computing time, tolerance, means, optimal values, and standard deviations are used for comparison. It is impossible to assess whether these performance measures are reasonable. In-depth investigations based on rigorous mathematics are desperately required.

- 5. Large-scale issues: Any algorithm's main objective is to effectively tackle actual-world issues with thousands or hundreds of thousands of variables. Almost all algorithms presently address smaller-scale toy problems or moderate-sized benchmark problems. It is unknown whether these algorithms can be scaled up to manage major scenarios in the real world. In most instances, simple parallelization might not be sufficient for scaling up. This is still a major hurdle for any approach that aims to tackle issues on a massive scale effectively.
