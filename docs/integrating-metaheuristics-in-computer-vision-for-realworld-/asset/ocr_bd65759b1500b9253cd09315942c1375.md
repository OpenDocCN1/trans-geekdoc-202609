The right kind of hydrogel can help with greater cell spreading and proliferation in addition to increasing cell viability. Another study team was able to create extracellular matrix that was decellularized and thick prevascularized tissues with layers of cells (dECM). The tissue also had many, differently sized branches of the vasculature [20]. The researchers also stated that even after 48 hours under in vitro circumstances, cell viability and structural integrity was preserved.

A bioink made by combining photo-crosslinkable gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) with a variety of cell types to approximate the makeup of native vascular cells produced high cell survival (>85%) _in vitro_ in a recent work [21]. These bioprinted vascular structures were merged with host circulation and were shown to be functional. Additionally, subcutaneous transplantation in vivo showed that the printed vascular structures included red blood cells. This positive study showed the potential of using bioprinted vasculature in clinical settings. The dimensions of the bioprinted vasculature continue to be crucial for its effective operation in addition to cell viability. A team of researchers developed a totally biological self-assembly technique for scaffold-free small dimension (outside diameter: 0.9 to 2.5 mm) vascular reconstruction in 2009 [22]. This technique was executed through a fast prototyping bioprinting process.

Aortic valve bioprinting employing hydrogels based on polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) and sodium alginate was also reported by another group [23]. The inner diameter of these scaffolds ranged from 12 to 22 mm, and pig aortic valve interstitial cells adhered to them and moved through them. Sacrificial filaments, such as Pluronic F127, are a novel type of filament being developed by several research facilities that can be utilized to create fluidic channels. Such filaments could help to speed up printing in addition to helping to create vascular patterns [24].

# 3.4.2 Liver

Liver fibrosis poses a significant medical challenge as it involves a complex and progressive interplay between hepatocytes and nonparenchymal cells (NPCs) over time. Replicating the complexity of the liver in both in vitro and in vivo models is challenging [25]. However, a liver tissue model was fabricated using primary human hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and hepatic stellate cells through bioprinting to mimic essential fibrogenic features, making it one of the earliest of its kind. This model demonstrated the precise and automated inclusion of these cells in the bioprinting process. In a newly released study, the same group altered the culture conditions, causing collagen to gradually build up in the tissues over a prolonged exposure period. When Kupffer cells were included in the experiment, the TGF-1 and methotrexate-induced fibrogenic response to cytokine and pharmacological stimuli improved [26].

Another study team demonstrated a proof-of-concept for bioprinting a liver organ using a mixture of human stellate cells and HepaRG cells [26]. The expression of tight junctions and markers of general metabolism (glucose, lactate, and lactate dehydrogenase), as well as the expression of albumin and cytochrome P4503A4 was observed that they remained stable when cultivated under static conditions in the liver that was created using a stereolithographic printing method.
