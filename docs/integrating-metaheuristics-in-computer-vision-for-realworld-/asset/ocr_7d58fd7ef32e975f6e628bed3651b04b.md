# ## 13.2.5 Genetic Algorithms

GA is likely the most widely recognized example of a nature-inspired algorithm. John Holland [41] created GA, an evolutionary algorithm, which was motivated by the naturalistic Darwinian evolution of biological systems.

An optimization problem's solution vectors are represented as a binary sequence of 1s and 0s known as chromosomes. A pool of potential solutions, represented as chromosomes, serves as the starting point for the algorithm. Each chromosome includes a unique set of genes that serve as the parameters in the optimization problem. The fitness function is then evaluated for each chromosome to ascertain the degree to which the solution meets the problem's objective. Following this, the program uses genetic operators like selection, crossover, and mutation to produce offspring chromosomes [42, 43]. Crossover and mutation introduce new genetic material into a population and create new populations/solutions, while natural selection favors chromosomes with greater fitness. Iterations of this procedure are continued until a stopping criterion is reached, which can be a maximum number of allowed iterations or attaining a sufficient level of fitness. With the aid of this generational repetition, the population evolves and progresses towards an optimal solution and identifies the fittest individuals.

# ## 13.2.6 Particle Swarm Optimization

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is a metaheuristic optimization technique inspired by the collective intelligence of natural systems like the behavior of birds flocks and fish schools. The fitness function assists the PSO algorithm as it seeks the best possible solution to a problem. The particle population is denoted by $x$, and the fitness function is written as $f(x) = f(x_1, x_2, x_3, ... ... x_n)$. Each particle adjusts its velocity based on the collective and individual flying experiences of its peers. Each particle adjusts its location based on its current positions, speed, and the difference between its best personal best position ($Pbest$) and the swarm's best position ($Gbest$).

The following equation is used to adjust the velocity of each particle:

```
$S_{t + 1} = wS_t + c_1 r_1(P_{best} - X_t) + c_2 r_2(G_{best} - X_t)$
```

where $S_t$ = Particle's velocity at a time 't, $X_t$ = Particle's current position at time 't, $P_{best}$ = historical best position of an individual, $G_{best}$ = swarm best position. Both $r_1$ and $r_2$ are random numbers in the range from 0 to 1, while $c_1$ and $c_2$ are acceleration constant values.
