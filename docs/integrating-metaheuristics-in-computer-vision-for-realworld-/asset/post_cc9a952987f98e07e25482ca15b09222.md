## Abstract

Exoplanet prediction using machine learning algorithms has the potential to significantly advance our understanding of these mysterious celestial bodies. In this project, a variety of machine learning algorithms were used, including support vector machines, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbours, random forest, and extreme gradient boosting, to make predictions about the existence of exoplanets and which their type. For this two datasets are used. With one dataset, a model that predicts the existence of an exoplanet was made and with other dataset, a model that predicts the type of an exoplanet was made. The goal was to identify patterns in the data and to make accurate predictions about the existence and characteristics of exoplanets. To evaluate the performance of the different algorithms, a range of metrics were used, including accuracy, precision, and recall. The results showed that these algorithms are highly effective at finding patterns in the data and making accurate predictions about the existence of an exoplanet around a star, with accuracies ranging from 98% to 99%. The second model which predicts the type of exoplanet whether it is a Gas-giant, Terrestrial or other also achieved as satisfying accuracy of 84%. In addition, confusion matrices of various algorithms and a feature importance plot were used to provide a more detailed analysis of the performance of the algorithms and to understand which features or variables were most influential in making predictions. Overall, this exoplanet prediction model has provided a compelling demonstration of the capabilities of machine learning algorithms and the potential for these methods to revolutionize our understanding of the cosmos. The findings from this project have the potential to inspire new discoveries and breakthroughs in exoplanet research, and they demonstrate the power and potential of machine learning for unlocking the secrets of the universe. There are many potential directions for future work in this area, and the potential for continued exploration is exciting.

## 9.1 Introduction

There is perhaps no greater question than “Are we alone”? The only way to answer this and many other questions, such as “How planetary systems are formed and evolved?”; “Is Earth the sole chapter in the tale of life?”; “How common is intelligent life in the universe?” and more, is to explore and study other worlds beyond our solar system [1]. These planets orbiting other stars, blackholes or just travelling across the space are known as Extrasolar planets or Exoplanets. Both scientists and the general public are fascinated with exoplanets, or planets that orbit stars outside of our solar system. Exoplanet discovery has a long history that dates back to the Greeks. But the first known search for exoplanet was by Christian Huygens (1629-1695) in late 17th century [2]. However, the first exoplanet orbiting a sun-like star was not found until the second part of the 20th century. Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz predicted the presence of 51 Pegasi b (see Figure 9.1) using the radial-velocity technique, in 1995. The area of exoplanetary science has flourished since this discovery. With the development of advanced telescopes and methods for finding these far-off planets, the search for exoplanets has significantly increased over the last decade. Over 5,000 exoplanets have been verified as of December 2022, while many more candidates are currently awaiting confirmations [3]. The development of new telescopes and sensors made expressly for detecting exoplanets, including the Kepler spacecraft and the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), has aided in the hunt for exoplanets.
