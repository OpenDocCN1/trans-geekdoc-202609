microenvironment and greater biocompatibility, they often have poor printability [15].
Common natural polymers used in bioprinting include gelatin, alginate, collagen, hyal-
uronic acid (HA), chitosan, and agarose. On the other hand, synthetic polymers, such as
polyethylene glycol (PEG) and hydrogel, have similar chemical and mechanical proper-
ties to natural polymers. Hydrogels have the ability to absorb a significant amount of bio-
logical fluids and can mimic live tissues due to their soft and rubbery appearance when
swelled. Hydrogels can be an essential component of tissue transplants because of their
exceptional capacity to permit nutrient and oxygen transport while providing sufficient
immunological isolation [16]. Additionally, synthetic polymers typically outperform nat-
ural ones in terms of their mechanical qualities. Contrary to natural polymers, synthetic
polymers likewise offer few or no niches for cellular functions. Natural polymers, on the
other hand, have great cell viability, improved cell–cell connections, and functional tissues.
Bioprinting requires precise and organized placement of relevant biomaterials onto cells to
create highly mimetic natural tissues. The microenvironment surrounding bioprinted cells
must replicate physiological conditions found in vivo to enable the cells to assume their in
vivo roles. For tissue creation, a variety of cell types is necessary to perform different func-
tions. Accurate positioning of cells in their respective locations or simultaneous seeding of
different cell types in the same or different hydrogels is possible when printing various cell
types to create complex tissues [17]. Additionally, researchers have explored directly seed-
ing scaffolds with stem cells, and stem cell differentiation can be induced through bioink
additives, culture conditions or stress during the printing process. Undifferentiated stem
cells can also serve as a means to prevent graft rejection following tissue transplantation, as
they do not provoke an immune response.

Furthermore, during tissue fabrication, various biomolecules, referred to as factors, the
printed constructs can be used to simultaneously control the proliferation, differentiation,
and migration of cells [18]. These factors include enzymes, growth factors, polynucleotides,
polypeptides, and polysaccharides.

# 3.4 Applications for 3D Printing in Cells

The adaptability and remarkable tissue engineering resolution of 3D bioprinting has
sparked high hopes among scientists. This technology has already been used to produce
various tissue types, such as skin, cartilage, bone, cardiovascular, liver, and neuronal tissues.
