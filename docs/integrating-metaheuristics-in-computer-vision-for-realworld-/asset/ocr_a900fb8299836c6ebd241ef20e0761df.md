As a result, the drug-repositioning approach, which employs big data to combine fundamental and clinical medicine, is regarded as the best drug-discovery paradigm. Life science databases, which house molecular data like genes and proteins, as well as medical information databases, which compile clinical data on actual patients, are examples of databases.

Big data-driven drug discovery has become an approach that pharmaceutical corporations are increasingly using. To find chemical compounds with desirable properties for oncology drug discovery, numerous medicines now combine cellular screening systems, computational healthcare informatics tools and gene-expression analysis [23]. Using high-performance combination screening technologies and cell-based phenotypic assays, pharmaceutical companies have also combined existing medications to address multiple dysfunctional pathways in a variety of ailments, such as inflammatory, respiratory, metabolic, and viral diseases. The aforementioned statistics showed how big data has become increasingly important in the current drug discovery process. Big data encompasses a wide range of unusually large datasets. In the meantime, data analysis tools and algorithms have advanced quickly, making it much easier to solve big data issues for the paradigm shift towards precision medicine.

# 5.2.1.5.1 Genomics and Proteomics

Biomarkers have the potential to make it possible to predict health outcomes from the viewpoints of physiological processes and symptoms. The rate of success for the development of biomarker-based cancer drugs has increased from just 3.4% to an overall oncology treatment approval rate of 8.3% for 2015. Biomarkers were preceded by genomics, mostly because it is relatively simple to collect gene expression profiling. Additionally, combinations of genomic biomarkers performed better as prognostic indicators than single genomic biomarkers [25]. A combination of 70 genes that predict a bad prognosis with up to 83% accuracy can be shown in sporadic breast cancers as an illustrative case. Proteomics has the potential to be used as biomarkers in addition to genomics [26]. However, proteomics is less likely to be employed for advanced cancer diagnostics to achieve the necessary clinical accuracy due to the continued difficulty of rigorous validation and difficult decoding methods. As a result, relatively few multi-parametric tests have been approved by the FDA under 510(k) procedures to date [27].

# 5.2.1.5.2 Genome-Wide Association Study

An increasing number of studies have established that effective pharmaceutical targets usually include genetic targets, with several examples supporting this claim coming from GWAS. Millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across the genome can be examined simultaneously by GWAS employing a hypothesis-free methodology, enabling a systematic analysis of the impact of particular genetic variants on a given trait.
