## Abstract

Angiogenesis is one of the hallmarks in cancer which is initiated in preneoplastic lesions, from the preexisting vessel. New vasculature supports the tumor growth, progression, invasion, and metastasis. Hypoxia is one of main mediator of angiogenesis, in addition to the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), along with, basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) etc., which are secreted from tumor cells as well as from stromal cells. Ephrin EphRs, angiopoietin Tie, and Delta–Notch signalling pathways have crucial role in angiogenesis regulation. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has been approved various VEGF inhibitors for treatment of advanced cancer. However, acquired therapy resistance limits the benefit of anti-angiogenic therapy. This therapy resistance proved the limited knowledge of angiogenesis in cancer, creating the idea for its further deep understanding. Machine learning or artificial intelligence is one of the recent technologies that involves analytical strategies, which are currently applied to biomarker discovery, gene expression, and image understanding in digital pathology. With the availability of deep learning techniques, researchers are able to easily and accurately quantify morphological structures and are freed up to focus on advancing science. The current review article describing the angiogenesis, its role in cancer, mechanism, in addition to the machine learning approaches in angiogenesis understanding.

Keywords: Angiogenesis, cancer, resistance, deep learning, molecular mechanism

## 16.1 Introduction

Cancer, a pathological condition that resulted due to uncontrolled or unregulated huge cell divisions. The metabolic rate of cancer cells is usually very high compared to their normal healthy cells [1, 2]. Under such conditions microenvironment of the tumor faces oxygen deprivation, low pH, and accumulation of waste or gases [3], while fast-growing tumor requires constant nutrition supply and gaseous exchange to maintain continuous cell divisions [3–5]. It is observed that tumor mass beyond 2 mm in size cannot grow without nutritional support [3]. This requirement of cancer is fulfilled by neovascularization (formation of new vessel). Cancer neovascularization is the formation of new blood vessel from preexisting vessel. The formation of blood vasculature is a rate-limiting step and hence a hallmark step in carcinogenesis [6]. Blood vessel formation is a comprehensive process with multiple steps that involves large-scale interaction between stromal and epithelial cells [6]. Angiogenesis is initiated in the preneoplastic lesion and subsequently support the tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis. Angiogenesis is controlled by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), along with, basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and several other chemokines/cytokines, which are secreted by tumor cells as well as stromal cells [7–9]. In addition, angiopoietin (Ang)–Tie system, Ephrin–Eph pathway, and Delta–Notch signalling pathways have key role in angiogenesis regulation [10]. Based on this observation, various VEGF inhibitors for the treatment of advanced cancer has been approved by FDA [11]. However, due to the acquired resistance against these therapies, effectiveness of therapeutic drugs is limited, thus, creating the idea of further deep knowledge of angiogenesis. Moreover, machine learning or artificial intelligence is a recently developing approach in biomedical field. Various different machine learning or deep learning tools are currently in use for angiogenesis observation, biomarker evaluation, and prognosis calculation [12]. The current review article describing the angiogenesis process, its role in cancer growth and progression, mechanism initiation/regulation, in addition use of machine learning approaches in angiogenesis evaluation and prognosis.

## 16.2 History of Angiogenesis Discovery

The term angiogenesis was first to come into existence in 1787 by a British surgeon John Hunter [13]. Further, a study reported the vascular structure of tumors in the 1860s [14]. In 1907, a vascular network was visualized in human tumor specimens using bismuth oil injections, and intra-arterial injections [15]. Then from 1930 to 1940, several investigations began to determine molecular events in neovascularization. Ide et al. and Algire et al. in the late 1930s and early 1940s observed new vasculature formation “angiogenesis” in the tumor mass [14–17]. Then in 1982, Ziche and Gullino analyzed neoplastic transformation in in vivo and in vitro cancer models [18]. Further, Brem and colleagues noted the formation of leaky capillaries in rabbit iris after human breast neoplastic lesion implementation [19].

## 16.3 Overview of Angiogenesis

Neovascularization is a complex phenomenon that involves multiple interactions between tumor stromal cells and tumor epithelial cells. Angiogenic “switch” is caused by hypoxia which is the primary event and subsequently involves the release of several proangiogenic mediator (e.g., growth factors, chemokines, and cytokines) that stimulate endothelial cell activation. Further events include the release of proteolytic enzymes that degrade ECM (extracellular matrix), migration of endothelial cells through degraded ECM, proliferation of endothelial cells, and eventually capillary tube formation [20]. Following this, endothelial cells get differentiated into tip, stalk and tube cells, in which non-proliferative tip cells expresses Delta-like ligand 4 (DLL4) and present at the front of new vessels. Highly proliferative stalk cells express Notch-1 and form trunk of vessel [21]. Tube cells are non-proliferating cells and contains lumen that gives the final shape to vessel. In addition, pericytes cells, express platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFR-β), and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and cover the vessel from outside [22].

## 16.4 Angiogenesis in Carcinogenesis

Several in vivo and in vitro experimental evidence supported the correlation of aggressive carcinogenesis with increased angiogenesis. For example, in breast carcinoma in situ (CIS), a severe form of dysplasia, an increase in angiogenic density was reported in invasive tumor mass [23]. Further, in the cervix cancer, a remarkable increase in Microvessel density was also seen [24]. Histopathologically, an increase in angiogenesis density was also confirmed in lung and breast metaplasia, dysplasia, and CIS [25, 26]. Moreover, overexpression of VEGF and PDGF has been determined in breast, cervix, and oesophagus carcinoma [27]. Similarly, high levels of MMPs and TIMPs have been observed in tumor lesions as they progress from CIS to invasive carcinoma (Figure 16.1) [28, 29].

## 16.5 Molecular Mechanisms of Angiogenesis Formation

Different multiple kinds of proteins have been identified and characterized as angiogenic activators, such as VEGF, bFGF, angiogenin, TGF-α/β, TNF-α, PDGF, G-CSF, PGF, IL-8, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and epidermal growth factor (EGF) (Figure 16.1) [30]. The primary stimulator for angiogenesis is hypoxia that triggers hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) [31]. HIF-1α operates in association with von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) protein that targets HIF-1α for ubiquitination under normoxic conditions (Figure 16.1) [32]. Paracrine or autocrine release of the above-mentioned factors stimulates endothelial progenitor cells for proliferation. Among all these, an important stimulating factor is a member of vascular endothelial growth factor family. There are different members of this family includes VEGF-A, B, C, and D, and their receptors VEGFR-1, 2, and 3. Because of their crucial role in angiogenesis, FDA has approved many VEGF inhibitors (for example, bevacizumab, sorafenib, dasatinib, regorafenib, and cabozantinib) to treat the advanced stage cancer [33]. Further, the key role of MMPs (matrix metalloproteinase) has also been determined in angiogenesis, that degrade the ECM and form a way for endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and vessel formation. Cyclooxygenase (COX) catalyzes the formation of prostaglandin and thromboxane from arachidonic acid. The role of COX-1 and COX-2 has been evaluated in angiogenesis induction in the co-culture of colon cancer cells and endothelial cells [34]. The activity of COX-1/2 enzyme helps in adherence of cells to ECM, and hence angiogenesis induction [35]. COX-2 also induces the synthesis of VEGF. Angiogenesis also involves Ang-1 and Ang-2 and tyrosine kinase receptor Tie-2 system. Interaction of Ang-1 with their receptor maintain and stabilize mature blood vessels. However, Ang-2 works as an antagonist of Ang-1 stabilizing its action. During carcinogenesis Ang-1 and Ang-2 has increased expression in tumor cells. Although, the expression of Ang-2 is highly overexpressed compared to Ang-1 and Tie2 [36]. In addition, endothelial-1 (ET-1) and its receptors are also involved in cancer angiogenesis regulation. Endothelin-1 along with its receptors ETAR/ETBR showed overexpression in tumor that has been correlated with high levels of VEGF and higher amount of microvessel density [37]. The role of PDGF-β/PDGFR signalling axis has also been determined in establishment, development, and differentiation of blood vessels, which recruit the maturation of pericytes and maturation of microvasculature in the tumor tissue [38]. Moreover, The notch signaling pathway (Notch1 receptors, DLL4, and Jagged-1 ligand) also has regulatory role in vasculature formation through endothelial cell maturation in [39, 40], renal cell carcinoma [41], bladder carcinoma [42], pancreatic carcinoma [43], and prostate carcinoma [44]. Multiple studies also determined the role of epigenetic regulation in angiogenesis. A wide range of angiogenesis initiation and regulation related miRNAs has been profiled in almost all types of human cancers [45]. For instance, miRNAs such as miR-17-92, let-7f, miR-27b, miR-222/221 etc. showed a significant role in the angiogenesis regulation [46].
