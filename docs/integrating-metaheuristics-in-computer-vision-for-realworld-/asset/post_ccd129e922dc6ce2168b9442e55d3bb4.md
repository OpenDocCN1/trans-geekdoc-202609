## 3.4.5 Bone

Bone injuries can be caused by old age, infection, trauma, and malignancy. Allograft or xenograft is a common method of bone repair, but these procedures have their own drawbacks, such as a high likelihood of inadequate or no tissue supply, the need for additional surgery in most cases, and a constant risk of infection and immunological graft rejection [33]. The limits of conventional methods of bone restoration may be solved by tissue engineering techniques like 3D bone printing.

The availability of suitable biomaterials and suitable cell types are two crucial components of any possible biomaterial for printing bone tissue. It has been discovered that hydrogels have promising promise for bone repair. The combination of acrylated RGD, polyethylene glycol dimethacrylate (PEGDMA) hydrogel and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) peptides was utilized to print bone tissue, resulting in strong bone growth after peptide activation. In addition, the osteogenic development of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can be significantly enhanced by adding bioactive glass nanoparticles to specific hydrogels.

Replacing hydrogel with a membrane consisting of a combination of PCL/PLGA/β-TCP has demonstrated the ability to stimulate bone regeneration and substantially enhance the process of osteogenic differentiation [34].

## 3.4.6 Skin

Skin is essential for protecting against the outside environment and for maintaining homeostasis in the body. Although there are numerous conventional skin substitutes that have been created and are already being utilized in clinical settings, these are not appropriate for individualized skin care. The need for frequent replacement of these commercial skin substitutes during therapy raises the total complexity and expense of wound care.

Multicellular 3D constructions could be produced via laser-assisted 3D bioprinting. These structures have keratinocytes acting as bioink and fibroblasts embedded in a collagen matrix [35]. Skin tissue printing has frequently used the droplet-based bioprinting technique. The chitosan-based biomaterials were recently modified to be suitable for functional 3D skin bioprinting, alongside collagen-based biomaterials. Due to a shorter collagen cross-linking time, the chitosan-based technique can overcome poor printability and accelerate the process.

Vascularization, which is critical for tissue survival, remains a significant factor in the success of skin grafting. Using inkjet printing technology, cells can be printed on gels with high cell viability (>90%) [36]. According to researchers, the coprinting of keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in the collagen matrix improved cell survival and wound contraction, thus enhancing the vascularization of skin implants. However, the production of new hair follicles or sweat gland growth in skin grafts remains a challenging task that requires further investigation in the future. There are currently no known effective methods for producing human hair follicles. There is hope that dermal papillae and epidermal cells will work together to produce hair neogenesis, which is necessary for hair follicle formation [37]. In the near future, it might be feasible to create constructs that resemble skin and have veins, nerves, hair follicles, and sweat glands.

## 3.4.7 Regeneration of Neurons

Another clinical setting where 3D bioprinting has enormous potential is neuronal regeneration. Recently, scientists printed NG108-15 cells and pig Schwann cells for brain engineering using a piezoelectric inkjet printer [38]. Despite the high voltage in the printer, the cell viability remained high, and after 7 days of printing, a modest increase in vitality was seen.

The effects of bioprinting on neuronal regeneration or reinnervation have been investigated in a number of in vivo investigations. One example of successful nerve restoration using 3D printing is the production of nerve guiding conduits using photocurable polyethylene glycol resin and microstereolithography. A recent investigation demonstrated the creation of conduits capable of encouraging reinnervation across a 3-mm gap in only 3 weeks, producing outcomes comparable to those of an autograft control. Another study described a new nerve guiding scaffold that combines fiber drawing with salt leaching to create microchannels, which allows for better control of microchannel cross-section and porosity, resulting in significant advantages for large-scale processes. In addition, a research team has developed a personalized nerve restoration technique using microextrusion printing [39].

The printed materials used included Alginate/CaCl2/PLGA/PCL/silicone/gelatin. In another study, a semisynthetic hydrogel called Gelatin methacryloyl containing glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) or nerve growth factor (NGF) was utilized to facilitate effective nerve regeneration in printed scaffolds. Furthermore, a group of researchers produced a conduit that was biocompatible using Schwann cells, agarose, and mouse marrow-derived stem cells. The 3D-printed graft was strengthened by a collagen tube surrounding it and was inserted into a rat sciatic nerve injury model for 40 weeks, leading to the discovery of axon regeneration in the areas surrounding the agarose rods. These findings provide preclinical proof for the use of 3D bioprinting. Recently, a study created bioinks that are filler-free and bioinspired, by selectively blending natural and synthetic polymers to achieve predetermined rheological properties for the matrix. During in vivo experiments, the free-standing tissue constructs loaded with cells were found to be biocompatible and had a low cytotoxic response [40]. The fabrication of soft, autonomous 3D neuronal tissues could have potential advantages for the production of neural tissues for regenerative medicine.

## 3.4.8 Pancreas

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) is currently treated through pancreas islet transplantation, which carries a significant risk of rejection by the immune system. However, the development of a bioprinted pancreas could provide a promising alternative therapy option with a lower rejection rate. This could not only reduce the financial burden and patient wait times but also improve the effectiveness of transplants by combining islet cells with endothelial progenitor cells, regulatory T cells and other similar cells [41].

A Polish research team has successfully created the first 3D-printed model of a vascularized bionic pancreas using a patient's stem cells to derive beta and alpha cells. The pancreatic scaffold, which replicated the pancreatic vascular system, produced positive results in a flow-connected bioreactor.

The internal vascular system of the bioprinted organ matches the prototype, as confirmed by MRI and CT scans. Animal research would be the next step in studying the functionality of a bioprinted pancreas in a living organism. The development of such a technology could benefit the process of producing neural tissues through biofabrication for the purpose of regenerative medicine as well [42].

Recently, researchers successfully created 3D structures with macroporous hydrogel constructions and implanted islet cells using a combination of 3D extrusion bioprinting and islet encapsulation [43]. The construct remained viable and functional throughout the investigation, and insulin generation by the implanted islets continues to occur. This suggests the possibility of producing 3D bioprinted pancreases that could play a crucial role in the clinical management of T1DM.

## 3.5 New Developments

A noteworthy investigation involved the implantation of cocultured human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in porous scaffolds, which led to vascularization and ectopic bone growth. A recent study explored the effects of prevascularization of a calcium phosphate cement (CPC) scaffold on bone regeneration in vivo by coculturing human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived (hiPSC-MSCs) with HUVECs for the first time. The findings demonstrated that coculturing HUVECs with hiPSC-MSCs significantly enhanced bone regeneration [44]. Therefore, the newly developed construct of cocultured HUVECs and hiPSC-MSCs, delivered through CPC scaffolds, holds promise for improving bone and vascular regeneration in orthopedic scenarios.

Researchers conducted an interesting study where they utilized 3D-printed microporous scaffolds to create biological prosthetic ovaries that successfully restored ovarian function in sterile mice [45]. The scaffolds were first seeded with follicles and became highly vascularized. Upon implantation into medically sterilized animals, complete restoration of ovarian function was achieved. This innovative approach proved to be effective as puppies were even born through natural mating and thrived on their mothers’ milk.

The meniscus, a C-shaped cartilage found in the knee joint, plays a crucial role in absorbing stress. However, meniscal tears are common due to degenerative arthritis. In a fascinating study, researchers were able to regenerate a functional knee meniscus in a large animal model (sheep) and followed up for three months [46]. Anatomically accurate 3D-printed scaffolds were used to replace the sheep meniscus. The study emphasized that tissue defects can hinder the regeneration of complex tissues by impacting the survival and development of endogenous cells within the tissue.
