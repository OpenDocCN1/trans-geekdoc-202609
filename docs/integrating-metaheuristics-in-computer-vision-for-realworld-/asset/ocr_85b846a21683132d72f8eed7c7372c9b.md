# 13.2.7 Ant Colony Optimization

Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) is an optimization algorithm that is inspired by the food-searching habits of ants. It was proposed in 1992 by Marco Dorigo [2]. An artificial ant colony (ACO) is utilized to find the optimal solution to an optimization problem. The ants find a solution by marching through the problem’s search area and leaving pheromones at the path’s boundaries. The pheromone signifies the quality of the solution discovered by the ant. Each ant determines its next move based on the pheromone trail and heuristic information, which evaluates the merits of moving from one location to another. Based on the quality of the solutions discovered by the ants, the pheromone trace is modified. The algorithm keeps on iterating until a stopping criterion, which can be a maximum number of allowed iterations or a minimum required fitness value.

ACO is ideally adapted for discrete optimization problems. For instance, the answer to a routing problem can be encoded as a route or path. When ants explore diverse paths, they leave behind a pheromone trail that diminishes with time. The quality/fitness of the solution is directly linked to the concentration of the Pheromone. The probability of taking a certain path at a junction increases if that path has a higher concentration of pheromones. ACO, like GA, is a hybrid process with numerous versions and applications.

# 13.2.8 Grey Wolf Algorithm

Mirajalili et al. developed a population-based optimization method called Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) [39]. GWO’s appealing aspect is its hierarchical structure, which distinguishes it from other population-based algorithms. The hierarchy’s supremacy is built with the use of a goal known as the objective function which is then subdivided into a fitness function and a cost function, both are needed to calculate the output [44]. The optimum solution is obtained among all the available candidates.

The Grey Wolf algorithm is based on wolf hunting strategies that look for prey (food) collectively and as the name indicates that the algorithm imitates the foraging behavior of wolves. The search was initiated by three wolves: alpha (α), beta (β), and delta (δ). They are all members of the dominant rank and have precedence when making decisions. The omega wolves are an additional dominant rank that does not participate in decision-making. The fitness values of alpha (alpha), beta (beta), and delta (delta) are used to obtain the best possible solution to the optimization problem. The greatest strength of GWO is the ability of its leading wolves to pinpoint the location of their prey and direct omegas for space exploration. Wolves often pursue in packs, and their behavior can be broken down into four distinct phases: encircling prey, hunting, seeking for prey (exploration), and attacking prey, (exploitation).
