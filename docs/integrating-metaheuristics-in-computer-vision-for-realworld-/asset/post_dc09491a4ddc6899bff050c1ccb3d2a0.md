### 5.2.1.3 Digital Twin in Drug Repurposing

Different stages regarding process development and advancement in the pharmaceutical manufacturing industry demonstrate the possibility of using Digital Twins to support smart manufacturing. The choice of a manufacturing route and its unit activities can be made much more quickly at the process design stage because of a digital twin’s ability to represent genuine components using a range of models. By removing the need for actual trials, digital twin simulations can be used to research process variability and forecast product quality, productivity, and process parameters, saving time and money [18]. Throughout the operational period, real-time process performance may be monitored and viewed, and the Digital Twins can continually assess the system to provide insights for control and process optimization. Real-time system monitoring and control utilizing ongoing data gathering from equipment, Process Analytical Technology (PAT), finished commodities, intermediate products, and an ongoing global modelling and data analysis platform are some important prerequisites for achieving smart manufacturing with Digital Twin. By utilizing strategies like Continuous Manufacturing (CM), Quality-by-Design (QbD), PAT and flowsheet modelling implementations, the pharmaceutical sector has made some progress in this direction. While some of the tools have been thoroughly studied, Digital Twin integration and development as a whole are still in their early stages [19]. A digital twin is a connection between the physical and digital worlds that consists of three elements: the virtual, the physical, and how the two interact. In the digital world, objects are created in labs with the goal of predicting their structure, simulating their behaviours, and enhancing their performance in the actual world. In the physical world, items are identified by sensors and handled by actuators according to the characteristics, functionality, and interactions with users shown in Figure 5.6.

### 5.2.1.5.1 Genomics and Proteomics

Biomarkers have the potential to make it possible to predict health outcomes from the viewpoints of physiological processes and symptoms. The rate of success for the development of biomarker-based cancer drugs has increased from just 3.4% to an overall oncology treatment approval rate of 8.3% for 2015. Biomarkers were preceded by genomics, mostly because it is relatively simple to collect gene expression profiling. Additionally, combinations of genomic biomarkers performed better as prognostic indicators than single genomic biomarkers [25]. A combination of 70 genes that predict a bad prognosis with up to 83% accuracy can be shown in sporadic breast cancers as an illustrative case. Proteomics has the potential to be used as biomarkers in addition to genomics [26]. However, proteomics is less likely to be employed for advanced cancer diagnostics to achieve the necessary clinical accuracy due to the continued difficulty of rigorous validation and difficult decoding methods. As a result, relatively few multi-parametric tests have been approved by the FDA under 510(k) procedures to date [27].
