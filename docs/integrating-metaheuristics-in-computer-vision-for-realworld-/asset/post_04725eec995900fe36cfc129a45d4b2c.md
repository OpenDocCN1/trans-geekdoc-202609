In this paper, a dataset, dataset1, of the fluxes of about 5000 stars from the NASA’s MIKULSKI [6] archive is utilised, obtained via the Kaggle. There are no null values in this pre-cleaned dataset. The dataset is highly imbalanced, with a 1:1000 ratio between classes 1 (non-exoplanet) and 2 (exoplanet). To address this issue in the exoplanet prediction project, the SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique) [7] was employed to balance the data. SMOTE is a widely used method for oversampling the minority class in a dataset, and it works by generating synthetic samples of the minority class based on the existing minority class samples. By oversampling the minority class, bias towards the majority class can be mitigated and the overall performance of the model improved. In the project, the ratio between the two classes in the dataset was 1:1000, which is highly unbalanced. By using SMOTE to balance the data, the performance of the machine learning algorithms was improved and more accurate predictions about exoplanets were made. The use of SMOTE was a crucial step for prediction in this project. ## 9.2 Background

Astronomy has been actively researching exoplanets, or worlds outside of our solar system, for many years. Technology advancements in recent years have improved our ability to identify exoplanets, enabling us to find thousands of new exoplanets. The transit method, which includes analyzing a star's brightness over time and looking for periodic dips in brightness that might point to the presence of a planet transiting in front of the star, is one of the primary techniques used to find exoplanets. However, it might take a long time and be prone to human errors to manually analyze a lot of data to find these dips. This is where machine learning comes in. One can automate data analysis and discover potential exoplanets more quickly and accurately by employing machine learning techniques. This can aid in the search for undiscovered exoplanets and improve our knowledge of the properties of these distant planets. With the use of machine learning, vast volumes of data can be evaluated rapidly and correctly. Machine learning techniques may be used to examine data from telescopes and other sensors in the context of exoplanet research to find prospective exoplanets and discover more about them. This might perhaps even lead to the discovery of Earth-like planets that might host life and help us better comprehend the diversity of exoplanets in the cosmos. Random Forest Classifiers, neural networks, and Self-Organizing Maps have all been applied to Kepler archived data for exoplanet detection. The employment of several machine-learning models offers an effective framework that can be modified and used to a variety of different large-scale surveys, allowing to reduce the total time spent in the target recognition and ranking phase of exoplanet discovery. Combining the results of many machine-learning techniques may improve forecast accuracy. The fundamental ideas of machine learning were applied to forecast the existence of exoplanets in a report published by IOP science. The Kepler Space Telescope’s light curves were used to evaluate five deep learning models. All models have accuracy levels of more than 98%.

A random forest classifier was selected as the optimal machine learning model to categorize objects of interest in the Kepler Object of Interest table in a paper published by Southern Methodist University. The Random Forest Classifier scored 98% on accuracy in cross-validation. A better than 95% chance exists that 968 candidate observations are exoplanets [11]. In a research published in Digitala Vetenksapliga Arkivet, Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) performance on a labelled data set including time series of the intensity of light from extrasolar stars is compared. In comparison to SVM’s recall of 0.800, CNN’s recall of 1.000 proved to be the best performing approach for the selected setups. With a precision of 0.769 compared to SVM’s 0.571 when the second validation parameter is taken into account, CNN is likewise the top performing algorithm. And in a paper published in The Astronomical Journal, Anne Dattilo and colleagues updated a neural network that had previously been used to recognize exoplanets in the Kepler field in order to be able to recognize exoplanets in several K2 campaigns that exist in a variety of galactic environments. To determine if a certain potential exoplanet signal is created by an exoplanet or a false positive, they developed a convolutional neural network named AstroNet-K2. AstroNet-K2 has a 98% accuracy on the test set for identifying exoplanets and false positives. All of the preceding researches demonstrate the necessity and benefits of using machine learning for astronomy data, particularly to find exoplanets.
