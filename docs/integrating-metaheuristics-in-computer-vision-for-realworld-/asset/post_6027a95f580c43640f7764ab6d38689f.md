A bionic ear that closely resembles a human ear and is composed of a hydrogel matrix containing cells has been successfully 3D printed by another team of researchers. This remarkable ear is capable not only of perceiving stereo sound (different sounds received by the left and right ear) but also radio frequency signals that fall beyond the range of the typical human ear [49]. The ear is equipped with a conductive polymer embedded with silver nanoparticles, which enables it to detect inductive signals from electrodes by cultivating cartilage tissue around an inductive coil antenna.

## 3.6 Progress in India

The medical field has immense potential for 3D printing technology to revolutionize therapeutic care. Despite being in its early stages, some Indian companies are already offering 3D printing services to the medical community. One such company, Pandorum Technologies, has achieved remarkable success in producing artificial 3D bioprinted liver tissues that closely resemble the human liver's function [50]. These tissues can serve as a low-cost model for drug and vaccine research and can also be used to create full-scale 3D-printed organs. Additionally, Pandorum has successfully produced human corneas to help heal corneal lesions without scarring and aid in vision restoration through the bioregeneration process.

Nova Beans, based in Gurugram, provides facial repair surgery and offers 3D-printed models for spinal cord injury surgery, brain aneurysm surgery, and digital dental solutions, with a focus on providing affordable and on-demand solutions for the Indian healthcare market [51]. Similarly, Mumbai-based Anatomis 3D is working on developing 3D-printed heart parts and has published articles on 3D-printed cardiac prototypes and models [52].

In an effort to provide researchers with customizable 3D bioprinting services, Next Big Innovation Laboratories (NBIL) Pvt. Ltd. of Bangalore has created the first customizable 3D bioprinter in India, called “Trivima” [53]. Additionally, NBIL is in the process of developing “Innoskin,” a 3D bioprinted human skin product that uses patented 3D bioprinting technology. Innoskin offers two testing variations, human epidermis (HE) and FT (dermal and epidermal model), and is intended to be used for patients with burns, wounds, and other skin-related conditions.

Finally, a team of researchers from the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) in Delhi, specifically the Department of Textile Technology, has successfully produced 3D bioprinted cartilage that closely resembles the ones naturally found in human knees [54]. This achievement marks the first instance of 3D bioprinted tissue being produced in an Indian laboratory, according to the research team.

## 3.7 Limitation

Despite remarkable progress and successes in 3D bioprinting, there are still several challenges that must be overcome before fully functional bioprinted organs can become a reality. One of the most pressing issues is the slow speed of bioprinting, which needs to be addressed to make the technology commercially viable and scalable. Therefore, the industry needs to identify materials that can facilitate faster printing. Improving the resolution of bioprinted structures is also a challenge that needs to be addressed, along with the need for compatibility with a wider range of biocompatible materials [55]. Higher resolution and better material interaction can provide better control in 3D microenvironments. Artificial biomaterials can help address these challenges by providing a combination of biopolymers that are both biocompatible and provide structural strength.

It is also crucial to ensure that the bioprinted construct has a functional vasculature to enable the transportation of nutrients, oxygen, and waste products. This can be achieved by incorporating vasculature into the initial stages of the build or by adding angiogenic factors to facilitate the formation of vasculature during later stages. However, the development of 3D bioprinting technology may raise concerns about safety, legality, and ethics, which must be addressed before the technology is scaled up for large-scale manufacturing.

## 3.8 A Future Point of View

The medical field can greatly benefit from 3D printing technology, although there are significant challenges that need to be addressed. The potential of 3D-printed organs to alleviate the burden and shortage of organ transplants is immense and has the potential to revolutionize the medical industry. By making organs available sooner and reducing the waiting period, it can also help to lower hospital costs and related expenses. Additionally, the death rate from some chronic diseases may be reduced by having fewer organ shortages and increasing the chances of a successful transplant [56]. The most significant advantage of 3D-printed organs is the possibility of personalized medicine, where transplanted tissues are tailored to meet the needs of individual patients. However, there are still obstacles to overcome, including the high research and manufacturing costs, lack of adequate infrastructure, and higher graft rejection rates. Despite these challenges, technology developments are driving the 3D cell culture market globally and have the potential to transform the field of regenerative medicine.

The advancements in bioprocess engineering, interdisciplinary methodologies, and automated large-scale cell cultures are promising and may lead to the creation of highly precise and low-cost 3D-printed structures that are specific to individual patients in the near future [57]. It may also be possible to use gene editing on a patient’s sick cells or tissues and print gene-edited cells to achieve specific outcomes. By combining different sets of gene-edited tissues, it may be possible to achieve broader endpoints. These unconventional bioprinting techniques, combined with transdisciplinary tools like gene editing, have the potential to bring unprecedented improvements in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

## 3.9 Conclusion

The advent of 3D printing has brought numerous possibilities to the medical industry, but due to the individual clinical requirements of each patient, there is a need for personalization. This has resulted in the emergence of personalized medicine. To prevent any potential misuse of this technology, regulatory agencies should closely monitor and regulate it.
