# 13.3 Algorithms and Self-Organization

Various methods exist to assess algorithms, depending on the numerous available perspectives. This section will primarily concentrate on algorithmic properties and self-organization.

# 13.3.1 Algorithmic Attributes

Our focus is on the key steps involved in how nature-inspired algorithms work, e.g. search motions and processes, exploration and exploitation, and update of equations or dynamics [49, 50]. They can also be thought of as self-organizing systems [51]. In summary, all algorithms are as follows:

+   - Every algorithm is population-based, with many agents constituting the population. (e.g., wolves, aquilas, bees, particles, etc.). In any optimization problem, the location of every agent within the design space is regarded as a potential solution. The agents can then interact together based on a specific set of rules, which are typically local laws.
+   
+   - By modifying the current population’s operators (e.g., mutation, crossover), the population is evolved iteratively. Any perturbation found in the solution of the population is achieved by selecting a random solution, with a fitness-biased selection process. All algorithms are capable of both local exploitation and global exploration.
+   
+   - Various forms of elitism are combined with the “survival of the fittest” principle to select the best candidate solutions. The fundamental objective is to facilitate the transmission of superior ideas to future generations.
+   
+   - The candidate’s diversity is higher in the early stages of iterations with increased unpredictability. Diversity will eventually decrease as iterations continue. When all candidates become similar, the system may reach a converged state. A converged candidate contains few strong similarities and organization, but its variety has been reduced to a bare minimum.
