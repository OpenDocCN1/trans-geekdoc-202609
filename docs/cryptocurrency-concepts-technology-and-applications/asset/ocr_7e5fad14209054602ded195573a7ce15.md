# Table 8.1 A Spectrum of Cryptocurrency Legality

| Level | Type of Permissiveness | Explanation | Country Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Full legal tender | A cryptocurrency (Bitcoin) serves as a full legal tender, equivalent to and exchangeable with the country's issued monetary instrument. | El Salvador |
| 2 | Permissive with regulatory framework | Cryptocurrencies are fully permitted but are generally subject to a regulatory framework involving legal/financial oversight institutions. | U.S., Canada, Australia, |
| 3 | Permissive without specific regulatory framework | Cryptocurrencies are tolerated because they are not considered a part of the ambit of financial supervision, as interpreted by regulatory authorities. | New Zealand, Slovakia, U.K. |
| 4 | Partial permission/partial ban | Cryptocurrencies are allowed in certain ways but not others. Examples include bans on bank processing, on local transactions, on mining, etc. | Indonesia, Cambodia, Russia |
| 5 | Strategic ambiguity | Official regulatory frameworks do not exist, but cryptocurrencies are not explicitly prohibited. This is pursuant to further evolution of the space. | Pakistan |
| 6 | Prohibited | Ownership, exchange, and transactions using cryptocurrencies are entirely prohibited; but perhaps with proposed substitutes (CBDCs). | China (digital yuan), Bolivia, Algeria |

This is the approach taken by the United States, drawing upon a well-formulated strategy that involves the coordination of many different institutions to allow for investor protection and market integrity on the one hand, and financial innovation and user freedom on the other (see Chohan 2022a). This effort to strike a balance between two public values, accountability and freedom, lies at the crux of all mature approaches to citizen-driven technological change (see Chohan 2021a, 2022a). As such, the U.S. approach represents the most sophisticated effort to grapple with the regulatory and legal challenges posed by cryptocurrencies.
