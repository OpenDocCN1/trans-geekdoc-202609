# Recap: Matrix Calculus

## Derivatives of a scalar function w.r.t a vector...

- Yields the gradient vector: $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f = \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \left[ \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_d} \right]^T$
- Example: Quadratic form $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}x^T\mathbf{x} = 2\mathbf{x}$, $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}x^T\mathbf{A}x = 2\mathbf{Ax}$

### Derivatives of a vector-valued function w.r.t a vector...

- Yields a matrix (the Jacobian) $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f = \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} \frac{\partial f_1(x)}{\partial x_1} & \dots & \frac{\partial f_k(x)}{\partial x_1} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_1(x)}{\partial x_d} & \dots & \frac{\partial f_k(x)}{\partial x_d} \end{array} \right]$
- Example: Linear form $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{Ax} = \mathbf{A}^T$

# Recap: Matrix Calculus

Derivatives of a scalar function w.r.t. a matrix...

- is again a matrix
$$
\nabla_W f = \frac{\partial f(W)}{\partial W} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f(W)}{\partial W_{11}} & \dots & \frac{\partial f(W)}{\partial W_{1d}} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f(W)}{\partial W_{k1}} & \dots & \frac{\partial f(W)}{\partial W_{kd}} \end{bmatrix}$$

Derivatives of a vector-valued function w.r.t. a matrix...

- is a 3D tensor!
$$
\nabla_W f = \frac{\partial f(W)}{\partial W} \in \mathcal{R}^{m \times n \times d} \text{with } f: \mathcal{R}^{m \times n} \to \mathcal{R}^d
$$

- Why do we need this?
  - Compute derivative w.r.t weight matrix
  - Likely there will be a simplification of this...
$$\frac{\partial (W x+b)}{\partial W}$$

# Chain rule

With vectors:

$u = u(x), \frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial u(x)}{\partial x} \frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial u}$

- Note: Swapped order
- Special case (scalar -> vector -> scalar):

$u = u(t), \frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial t} = \left(\frac{\partial u(t)}{\partial t}\right)_{1 \times d} \left(\frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial u}\right)_{d \times 1} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{\partial u_i(t)}{\partial t} \frac{\partial g(u)}{\partial u_i}$

# Chain rule with tensors

For weight matrices: $\frac{\partial Wx+b}{\partial W} = \mathcal{T}_{ijd}\text{ with } \mathcal{T}_{[\cdot]d} = \frac{\partial z_d}{\partial W} = \begin{pmatrix} 0^T \\ \vdots \\ x^T \\ \vdots \\ 0^T \end{pmatrix}$ Matrix with zeros, d-th row contains $x^T$

Tensor vector product:

$\mathcal{T}_{ijd}u = \sum_d \mathcal{T}_{[\cdot]d}u_d = \sum_d \begin{bmatrix} x_1u_1 & x_2u_1 & \dots & x_nu_1 \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ x_1u_d & x_2u_d & \dots & x_nu_d \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}^T$

- Which is the outer product!
Hence: $\nabla_W f = \frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial W} = \frac{\partial}{\partial W}(Wx+b)\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial \mathbf{z}} = \frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial \mathbf{z}}\mathbf{x}^T$

# Computational costs

- **Computational cost of forward pass:**
  - $z = Wx + b$
  - Matrix-vector product
  - Roughly one add-multiply operation per weight
- **Computational cost of backward pass:**
  - $\overline{W} = \overline{h}z^T, \overline{h} = W^T\overline{y}$
  - Matrix-vector product + outer product
  - Roughly two add-multiply operation per weight (twice the forward pass)

For a multilayer perceptron, this means the **cost is linear** in the number of **layers**, **quadratic** in the number of **units per layer**.

# Wrap-up for backpropagation

- **Backprop is used to train the overwhelming majority** of neural nets today.
  - Even optimization algorithms fancier than gradient descent (e.g. second-order methods) use backprop to compute the gradients.

- **Despite its practical success, backprop is believed to be neurally implausible.**
  - No evidence for biological signals analogous to error derivatives.
  - Forward & backward weights are tied in backprop.
  - Backprop requires synchronous update (1 forward followed by 1 backward).

- All the biologically plausible alternatives we know learn much more slowly (on computers).

# Stochastic Gradient Descent

- Usually we are minimizing the empirical loss (**batch gradient descent**)
  $$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i} l(x_i; \theta) \quad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \frac{\eta}{n} \sum_{i} \nabla_{\theta}l(x_i; \theta_t)$$
- We do this to approximate the expected loss
$$\mathbb{E}_x [l(\boldsymbol{x}; \theta)] \quad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \mathbb{E}_x [\nabla_{\theta}l(\boldsymbol{x}; \theta_t)]$$

- Use a rougher, cheaper approximation: **stochastic gradient descent**
  $$l(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \theta) \quad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_{\theta}l(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \theta_t)$$
- for random sample i

# Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)

Use only one sample to compute the update

- Does NOT always “descent"
- Iterations are much cheaper
- Requires more iterations
- ... and smaller step sizes

# Stochastic vs. Batch Gradients

- **Blue**: Batch Gradients
- **Red**: Stochastic Gradients

## Rule of thumb:

- Stochastic methods work well far away from optimum
- But struggle to find the exact optimum

## Yet, it is more efficient than batch gradient descent!

- For large datasets, data points will be redundant
- Hence, in batch GD, there is a lot of redundant computation
  - Does not scale to big neural networks with large datasets
- In SGD, we never compute the gradient for the same parameter vector twice
