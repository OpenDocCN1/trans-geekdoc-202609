## Objective for Linear Regression

We want to consider linear functions with multiple inputs

$$f(x_i) = w_0 + \sum_{j} w_j x_{i,j}$$

Our SSE objective now looks like the following

$$SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - (w_0 + \sum_{j} w_j x_{i,j}))^2$$

Can we simplify it using matrices??

## Linear regression models in matrix form

- Equation for the $i$-th sample

$$\hat{y}_i = w_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{D} w_j x_{i,j} = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^T \mathbf{w}, \quad \text{with } \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x_i \end{bmatrix} \text{and } \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ \vdots \\ w_D \end{bmatrix}$$

- Equation for full data set

$$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{y}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \hat{y}_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n^T \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$$

- $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is a vector containing the output for each sample

- $$\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1^T \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n^T \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{is the data-matrix containing a vector of ones as the first column as bias}$$

## Linear regression models in matrix form

- Error vector: $e = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} \hat{y}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \hat{y}_n \end{bmatrix} = y - \hat{y} = y - Xw$

- Sum of squared errors (SSE)
$$SSE = \sum_{i} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \sum_{i} e_i^2 = e^T e = (y - Xw)^T(y - Xw)$$

- We have now written the **SSE completely in matrix form!**

## Deriving Linear Regression

- How do we obtain the optimal $w$? (which minimizes the SSE)

$$w^* = \text{argmin}_w SSE = \text{argmin}_w (y - Xw)^T(y - Xw)$$

![](img/f01928df50903c99ce023dd3c527a33d_84_0.png)

> At a minimal value of a function, its derivative is zero

> i.e., find a $w$ where $\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial w} = \mathbf{0}^T$

## Estimation of w

```
$SSE(\mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})^T(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})$
$= \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y}$
$= \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - 2\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y}$
```

Take the derivative w.r.t $\mathbf{w}$:

```
$\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}SSE(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{w}} \ \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - 2\mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} \}$
```

Setting the gradient to **0** $\mathbf{w}^* =(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$

## Evaluating regression models

### How can we estimate the quality of the model?

- The SSE can take arbitrary values depending on the range of the output
- Make the evaluation invariant to the variance of $y$

R-Square (or $R^2$) determines how much of the total variation in $y$ is explained by the variation in $x$. Mathematically, it can be written as

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{Regression sum of squares}}{\text{Total sum of squares}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{n=1}^N(\hat{y}_n - y_n)^2}{\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n - \bar{y})^2}$$

where $\bar{y}$ is the mean of the outputs. $R^2$ tells how well the regression line approximates the real data points. An $R^2$ of 1 indicates that the regression line perfectly fits the data.

## Discussion

We have now derived our first ML algorithm: **Linear Regression!**

- The solution is called Least Squares solution
- One of the rare cases where we can obtain a closed form solution

**This was only possible because:**

- The cost-function (SSE) is convex for linear $f(x)$
  - There is only one minimum
- The cost function is quadratic in $w$
  - The minimum is easy to obtain

## Linear Functions

So far, we modelled our function $f$ as linear in **x** and **w**

$$f(x) = \tilde{x}^T \mathbf{w}$$

However, this equation can only represent hyper-planes in the D-dimensional input space

![](img/f01928df50903c99ce023dd3c527a33d_90_0.png)

## General Form

In more general writing, we could rewrite it as

$$f(x) = \phi(x)^T w$$

where $\phi(x)$ is a vector-valued function and $\phi_i(x)$ are known as basis functions.

![](img/f01928df50903c99ce023dd3c527a33d_90_0.png)

## Example of Polynomial

$$f(x) = \phi(x)^T w$$

where $\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ \vdots \\ w_D \end{bmatrix}, \phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x \\ \vdots \\ x^D \end{bmatrix}$

## Example of Multiple Regression

$$f(x) = \phi(x)^T w$$

where $\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix}, \phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x \\ x^2 \end{bmatrix}$

## Example of Quadratic Fitting

$$f(x) = \phi(x)^T w$$

where $\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \\ w_2 \\ w_3 \end{bmatrix}, \phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x \\ x^2 \\ x^3 \end{bmatrix}$

## Radial Basis Function (RBF) Features

Popular generic representation:

$$\phi(x) = [\phi_1(x), \dots, \phi_M(x)]^T, \quad \phi_i(x) = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - \mu_i\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

- $\mu_i$: $i^{th}$ center location (fixed)
- $\sigma$: bandwidth
- Typically used as grid for center over input space
- Hard to scale for high-D (we need exponentially many centers)

## Generalized Linear Regression

The derivations stay exactly the same, just the data matrix is now replaced by the basis function matrix, i.e.:

```
w^* = (\Phi^T \Phi)^{-1} \Phi^T y
```

with $\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \phi_n^T \end{bmatrix}$

## Overfitting for polynomial regression

The error on the training set **is not an indication for a good fit!!**

- We always need an **independent test-set!**

## Overfitting:

- Training error goes down
- Test error goes up

The model is too complex. It fits the noise and has unspecified behavior between the training points.

## Underfitting:

- Training + Test error are high

The model is too simple to fit the data
