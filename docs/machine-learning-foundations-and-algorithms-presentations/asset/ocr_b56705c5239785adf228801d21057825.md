# THE SONNETS

by William Shakespeare

From faire creatures we desire increase,
That lovely beauty's rose might never die,
As fire is either by time deceives;
His tender might bear no memory:
But thou, constrained to thine with self-substantial fuel,
Making a famine where remembrance lies,
Thyelf thy fire, to thy self so cruel;
That art now the world's fair cement,
And only herald to the gaudy spring:
Within thine bands what burial cometh,
And thy cruel mask's water indigging:
Pity the world, or else this glutton be,
To eat the world's face, by the grave and thee.

When winters shall besiege the bro,
And dig trenches in thy beauty's field,
Thy youth's pride livery gone on snow,
Will be a tater's weed of small worth;
Then being-sued, where all thy beauty's lies,
Where all the treasure of thy lusty days,
To say, within thine deep sunken eyes,
Were as a-ating shame, and thrift's praise.
How much more praise deserve'd beauty's use,
If it could answer! Thy fair child of mine
Shall sum my cost, and make thy old excess,
Proving his beauty by succession thine!
This was to new made when thou art old,
And see the blood warm when thou it's cold.
