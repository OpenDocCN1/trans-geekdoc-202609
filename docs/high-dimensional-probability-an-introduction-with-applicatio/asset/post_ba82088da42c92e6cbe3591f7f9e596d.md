# 7 Random Processes

In this chapter, we turn our attention to random processes—collections of random variables \((X_t)_{t\in T}\), which may be dependent. In classical settings like Brownian motion, \(t\) represents time, so \(T\subset\mathbb{R}\). But in high-dimensional probability, \(T\) can be any abstract set. A key example is the canonical Gaussian process

$$X_t=\langle g,t\rangle,\quad t\in T,$$

where \(T\subset\mathbb{R}^n\) and \(g\sim N(0,I_n)\). We introduce it in Section [7.1](7.1).

In Section [7.2](7.2), we explore powerful comparison inequalities for Gaussian processes—Slepian, Sudakov–Fernique, and Gordon—using a new trick: Gaussian interpolation. In Section [7.3](7.3), we use these tools to prove a sharp bound on the operator norm of an \(m\times n\) Gaussian random matrix.

How does a Gaussian process \((X_t)_{t\in T}\) capture the geometry of \(T\)? In Section [7.4](7.4), we prove Sudakov's inequality—a lower bound on the *Gaussian width*

$$w(T)=\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}\langle g,t\rangle$$

using covering numbers. Upper bounds come later in Chapter [8](8). Gaussian width is a key concept linking probability with metric geometry; we take a closer look at it in Section [7.5](7.5) and connect it to other ideas like effective dimension.

In Section [7.6](7.6), we compute the size of a random projection of any bounded set \(T\subset\mathbb{R}^n\). The crucial quantity that determines it is the Gaussian width.

You will have plenty of chances to practice these ideas: prove symmetrization and contraction inequalities for random processes (Exercises [7.2–7.4](7.2–7.4), [7.8](7.8)), derive the important min–max inequality for Gaussian processes—Gordon's inequality (Exercise [7.9](7.9)), get sharp bounds for Gaussian matrices (Exercises [7.11](7.11) and [7.13](7.13)), compute the Gaussian width for the \(\ell^p\) balls (Exercise [7.17](7.17)), explore the nuclear norm (Exercises [7.18](7.18) and [7.19](7.19)), effective dimension (Exercises [7.21–7.22](7.21–7.22)), random projections of general sets (Exercises [7.25](7.25) and [7.26](7.26)), and matrix sketching (Exercise [7.27](7.27)).

## 7.1 Basic concepts and examples

**Definition 7.1.1** (Random process). A *random process* is just a collection of random variables \((X_t)_{t\in T}\) on the same probability space, which are indexed by elements \(t\) of some set \(T\).

In some classical examples, \(t\) stands for *time*, in which case \(T\) is a subset of \(\mathbb{R}\). But we primarily study processes in high-dimensional settings, where \(T\) is a subset of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and where the analogy with time is lost.

**Example 7.1.2** (Discrete time). If \(T=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), then the random process

\[
(X_1,\ldots,X_n)
\]

can be identified with a *random vector* in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

**Example 7.1.3** (Random walks). If \(T=\mathbb{N}\), a discrete-time random process \((X_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\) is simply a *sequence* of random variables. An important example is a *random walk* defined as

\[
X_n:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_i,
\]

where the increments \(Z_i\) are independent, mean-zero random variables. See Figure 7.1 for an illustration.

![](img/b122213aed28c1888de83a894123323e_201_0.png)

**Example 7.1.4** (Brownian motion). The most classical continuous-time random process is the standard *Brownian motion* \((X_t)_{t\ge 0}\), or the *Wiener process*. It can be characterized as follows:

- (i) The process has continuous sample paths, i.e., the random function \(f(t):=X_t\) is continuous almost surely.
- (ii) The increments are independent and satisfy \(X_t-X_s\sim N(0,t-s)\) for all \(t\ge s\).

Figure 7.1 shows a few trials of the standard Brownian motion.

**Example 7.1.5** (Random fields). When the index set \(T\) is a subset of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), a random process \((X_t)_{t\in T}\) is sometimes called a spatial random process, or a *random field*. For example, the water temperature \(X_t\) at a location on Earth parametrized by \(t\) can be modeled as a spatial random process.
