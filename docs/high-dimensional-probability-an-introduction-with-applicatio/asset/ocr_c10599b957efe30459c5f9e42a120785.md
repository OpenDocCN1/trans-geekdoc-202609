The left tail \(\mathbb{P}\{S_N \le (1-\delta)\mu\}\) can be bounded similarly, using **Remark 2.3.2**. (Try it! You should get even a better bound: \(\exp(-\delta^2\mu/2)\).) Combine the two tails by a union bound to complete the proof.

To see a Gaussian tail in **Corollary 2.3.4**, try normalizing \(S_N\) roughly as in the central limit theorem. If we set \(Z_N=(S_N-\mu)/\sqrt{\mu}\), we can rewrite the conclusion of **Corollary 2.3.4** as follows:

\[\mathbb{P}\{|Z_N|\ge t\}\le 2\exp(-t^2/3)\quad\text{for every }0\le t\le\sqrt{\mu}.\]

**Remark 2.3.5** (Small and large deviations). Figure **2.1** shows the probability mass function of Binom\((N,\mu/N)\) with \(N=200\) and \(\mu=10\). Near the mean \(\mu\), the bell-shaped curve reflects Gaussian behavior. Far to the right, the slower decay reflects Poisson behavior. These two regimes align with the central limit theorem and the Poisson limit theorem, respectively.

# 2.4 Application: median-of-means estimator

A fundamental data science problem is learning an unknown distribution from a sample — for example inferring a country’s income distribution from a survey of \(N\) people.

The most basic task is estimating the mean. Let \(X\) be a random variable with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) (representing a population), and let \(X_1,\ldots,X_N\) be independent copies¹ of \(X\) (representing a sample). We would like to find an estimator \(\hat{\mu}=\hat{\mu}(X_1,\ldots,X_N)\) that satisfies \(\hat{\mu}\approx\mu\) with high probability.

The simplest and most popular mean estimator is the *sample mean*

\[\hat{\mu}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i.\tag{2.11}\]

The expected value and variance of this estimator are

\[\mathbb{E}\,\hat{\mu}=\mu;\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mu})=\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}.\tag{2.12}\]

¹ Independent copies of \(X\) are independent random variables that have the same distribution as \(X\).
