### Disadvantages of Blockchain

The hype surrounding the benefits of blockchain might lead many to believe that it does not have any disadvantages, but that is not true. Just like every other thing on earth, blockchain also has some disadvantages.

#### 51 Percent Attacks

One of them is the 51 percent attacks. Of course, over the past decade, the proof of work consensus has successfully protected Bitcoin blockchain from hacks. But blockchains are susceptible to a few potential attacks that can affect their operations and one of such attacks is 51 percent attacks. A 51 percent attack takes place when a person or group of persons manage to have more than 51 percent of the hashing power of any given blockchain network. This would end up giving them the power to influence the network in any way they desire by intentionally modifying or excluding the ordering transactions.

The Bitcoin blockchain has not suffered a successful 51 percent attack even though it is theoretically possible. This is because it will always become increasingly difficult for miners to spend their funds in attempting to attack the Bitcoin blockchain since they are still receiving rewards for their honest dealings in the network. But you should bear in mind that if a 51 percent attack is successful, those behind the attack can make changes to the most recent transactions – this will be for a limited time since all blocks are chained to each other via cryptographic proofs. So, there would be a need for extremely high levels of computing power.

#### Data cannot be Modified Easily

The second challenge is also one of the merits of blockchain technology and that is its immutability feature. Once data is added to the blockchain, it is extremely hard to modify it. Of course, this stability feature is not often a good one since there may be a need to make changes to records which would be a difficult task. To make changes to the blockchain code or data, the network may have to opt for a hard fork. A hard fork involves a process where the network abandons an old chain and adopts a new one. One good example of a hard fork is Ethereum and Ethereum Classic. Ethereum Classic is the old chain that some members of the Ethereum community chose to retain while the new chain that was adopted is what we now know as Ethereum.

#### Private Key

The use of public and private key in blockchain guarantees the ownership of cryptocurrency units or any data stored in blockchain by individuals. Every public key also has a corresponding private key. The public can be shared but that is not the case for the private key as it needs to be safely kept away from the public. While it is extremely hard to hack the blockchain, it is not often difficult for hackers to gain access to people's private keys.

Since the private key grants anyone access to funds or resources, they are just like individual banks. Once this private key falls into the hands of the wrong individuals, the funds will be lost. Also, there have been numerous cases of people who either lost their private key or forgot them and what that means is that all the funds in that account will be lost and there is absolutely nothing anyone can do to retrieve them.

#### Storage

Remember, we described blockchain as a digital ledger that stores entries. However, as more information is stored in the blockchain it will become very large. A good example of this issue is the Bitcoin blockchain which needs about 200 GB of storage space. The present growth in the size of the blockchain appears to be getting too large for most of our hard drives. What this means is that the network will stand the risk of losing a large chunk of its nodes once it becomes too large for participants to download and store.

Amid its drawbacks, blockchain technology's advantages are far more than its disadvantages and that is why it is not just here to stay but will transform the way we store files. ### History and Basics of Smart Contract

Smart contracts have been around for over two decades, but many came to know about it when the Ethereum blockchain was launched. During the mid-1990s, Nick Szabo came out with several papers where he successfully conceptualized smart contracts. The computer scientist described it in his papers as

> “A set of promises, specified in digital form, including protocols within which the parties perform on these promises.”

The goal of smart contracts is to capture key elements of a contract that are algorithmic and also prevent agreements from being tampered with through cryptography. In a straightforward manner, we can see a smart contract as an automated and enforceable agreement. While a smart contract is automatable courtesy of a computer, some aspects of it may need human input or control. Taking a look at Szabo's definition of a smart contract, we can find key elements of a smart contract.
