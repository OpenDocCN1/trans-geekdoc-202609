CHAPTER 1: HOW IT ALL BEGAN: THE HISTORY OF BLOCKCHAIN TECHNOLOGY

One of the most significant innovations that we have witnessed in the 21st century is the emergence of blockchain technology. Within a short time, it has already established a ripple effect on different sectors – the financial industry, businesses, education, supply chain management, etc. However, many are not aware that blockchain technology has been identified as early as the 1990s. Every blockchain aspirant and enthusiast needs to understand the history of blockchain. Back in 1991, two individuals, Stuart Haber and W. Scott Stornetta conceptualized what we now regard as blockchain.

Their first project was a cryptographically secured chain of blocks that would ensure that no individual or group of persons could make changes to the timestamps of documents. Interestingly, they took it a step further in 1992 by upgrading their system and incorporating Merkle trees. This helped to enhance efficiency and also allowed the addition of more documents to one block. This is the aspect of blockchain technology that most people, including blockchain enthusiasts hardly know about. All through this book, you will be coming across terms like digital ledger technology, nodes, and distributed ledger technology and as you read on, you will learn more about what they mean. I will be using blockchain and digital ledger technology interchangeably.

Hal Finney and Reusable Proof of Work

One of the names that you will often come across while learning about blockchain and cryptocurrency is Harold Thomas Finney II, popularly known as Hal Finey. In 2004, he came out with a system regarded as Reusable Proof of Work (RPoW) which functioned by receiving a token that is a non-exchangeable or non-fungible Hashcash-based proof of work and in return produced an RSA-signed token that people can transfer between themselves.

One of the problems that early digital currencies faced was the issue of double-spending and the RPoW was able to resolve the issue simply by maintaining the records of ownership of tokens on a server that is registered and trusted. The server was designed to permit users around the globe to easily verify the integrity and accuracy of records. This is seen by many as an early prototype of the existing cryptocurrencies and also forms part of the concepts that helped Satoshi Nakamoto to build the Bitcoin blockchain.

The popularity of blockchain however began in 2008 when an individual or a group of persons with the name Satoshi Nakamoto came out with Bitcoin. Presently, we credit Satoshi Nakamoto as the brain behind blockchain technology after they came out with Bitcoin which is also the first major application of the technology. After conceptualizing the first blockchain in 2008, things started happening so fast as other individuals started exploring “outstanding applications of the technology beyond cryptocurrencies.”

Satoshi Nakamoto did not remain in the spotlight for too long as he handed the development of Bitcoin over to other developers and exited the scene. Well, digital ledger technology has since evolved and there are now many applications that belong to the history of the technology. So, we can say that blockchain technically came into existence in 1991 and was popularized by Satoshi Nakamoto with the creation of Bitcoin in 2008.

Before we go into the various phases of the development of blockchain, I need to point out that you will be seeing two versions of the word “bitcoin” as you read further. One will be in lowercase while the other one is in uppercase. They mean two different things. I am referring to “Bitcoin” blockchain and “bitcoin” cryptocurrency which makes use of the Bitcoin blockchain. I guess you understand what I mean now. Chapter 2: Blockchain vs. Distributed Ledger Technology

For most people, blockchain technology is just the same as distributed ledger technology. Well, is this really true? One of the goals of this book is to simplify blockchain technology and help you understand what it means, its potential to revolutionize the way we handle data. Understanding Distributed Ledger

We can see a distributed ledger simply as “ a decentralized database” which means that it is a database that is stored in hundreds or thousands of computers or nodes. All the nodes in distributed ledger maintain the ledger and the moment any data is added, the ledger will also be updated. The update of the ledger happens at each participating node and this is done independently. In this setting, all participating nodes enjoy the same level of authority and no single server or centralized authority is in charge of managing the database.

This is what also ensures that the technology is very transparent. The process of updating and validating the existence of data on a distributed ledger is very straightforward and the nodes can verify every single transaction based on the existing consensus algorithm of the ledger. In some cases, just a few selected nodes can participate in verifying transactions while others allow all nodes to participate in the process. The transaction will secure a place in the ledger when all nodes give the approval and the status of the ledger will also be updated.

Apart from the increased level of transparency for a distributed ledger, it also offers an excellent level of security because there is no central authority like traditional databases. A point of corruption does not exist in this technology. All participating nodes get the chance to verify a transaction and this explains why the financial industry is at the forefront of adopting this technology.

Blockchain Technology

To address the question of whether blockchain is the same as DLT, you can see DLT as the parent technology of blockchain. Blockchain technology is just one type of distributed ledger that managed to become more popular than the parent technology. Interestingly, some core developers are beginning to come out of the blockchain shadow and this explains why there is also an increase in the number of people interested in understanding the difference between the two.

Just like its parent technology, all participating nodes on the blockchain have a copy of the ledger. Whenever a new transaction is added, every single copy of the ledger on any given blockchain network will get updated. Before appending the transactions to the ledger, they must be encrypted. There is also no need for a central authority who manages the system just like DLT. This explains why it is a decentralized system. Blockchain arranges the data stored in it as blocks and this is the reason for its name.

All the blocks are connected and for security purposes, they are also encrypted. One of the features of blockchain is the fact that it is impossible to delete or modify existing data. The blockchain only permits adding operation, unlike traditional databases. All transactions that are recorded in the blockchain exist in history and no one can modify or delete any of them. So, similar to DLT, blockchain is extremely transparent and this is why there are predictions that the blockchain market will increase to $16 billion in 2024.

So, what is the Difference Between DLT and Blockchain Technology?

Although the two technologies share so many similarities, there are some differences. As I mentioned before, distributed ledger technology is simply a parent technology of blockchain. We can also see blockchain as simply an advanced version of the DLT. Blockchain technology is just one version of distributed ledger which explains why the two are often used interchangeably. However, it is wrong to regard every distributed ledger as a blockchain.

It is similar to a situation where some products turn out to be extremely popular and become more popular than their parent company name. Blockchain has simply gained its identity and perhaps the reason is that it became popularized with the creation of bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies. There are several differences in the way DLT works too.

A consensus is vital for distributed ledger technology, but in blockchain, developers can actually adopt various strategies to achieve consensus like Proof-of-work, Proof-of-Stake, Delegated Proof of Stake and several others. Other features of blockchain that you may not really find in distributed ledgers include:

  • Sequence: All blocks in the blockchain are often arranged in a specific sequence. This is not the case for DLT as there is no need for a particular sequence of data. One of the things that distinguish blockchain from other DLT platforms is the sequence of blocks.
  • Block Structure: This is perhaps the most remarkable difference between blockchain and DLT. In Blockchain, you will find blocks of data and this is not the case with distributed ledgers. A distributed ledger is just a database that is distributed across various nodes. It is possible to represent the data in a distributed ledger in various ways.
  • Energy Consuming Consensus: The recent outburst by Elon Musk that resulted in a significant drop in the price of bitcoin is as a result of the energy usage of bitcoin miners even as the world is racing to deal with the issue of global warming. Blockchain technology makes use of the proof-of-work consensus and other mechanisms but this usually consumes a lot of energy. This is different for a distributed ledger since it does not require such kind of consensus and they are relatively more scalable.

Now you have a better view of the two. Blockchain technology comes with extra functionality and exceeds that of the traditional distributed ledgers. Blockchain technology can execute several functionalities that some distributed ledgers cannot perform like interoperability. We can confidently say that blockchain technology is moving distributed ledger technology to the next level.

The Categories of Blockchain

Few years after the launching of blockchain, it would be difficult to write over 20,000 words about blockchain and cryptocurrency. However, you can write over 100k words of a book on blockchain and cryptocurrency now. This is because the blockchain and cryptocurrency space is evolving fast and has become very broad. What began with Satoshi Nakamoto's launching of Bitcoin with a public blockchain which is also known as the first class of blockchain has transformed into multiple use cases.

Generally, Bitcoin's blockchain is seen as the first generation of blockchain technology but things have changed so fast and we now have over three different classes of blockchain technology. Every type of blockchain in existence was created to deal with a specific set of issues. We are now seeing more companies exploring ways to take advantage of the benefits of blockchain technology as much as possible.

Before we dive into exploring the different categories of blockchain, we need to understand why we need different types in the first place. The first popular use case of blockchain was when it was launched alongside bitcoin back in 2008. The intention of Satoshi Nakamoto is still difficult to ascertain, but the use of the public blockchain for bitcoin also opened the door for people to explore the concept of decentralized ledger technology.

The emergence of distributed ledger technology also transformed how we solve issues in our businesses as organizations started discovering that it is possible to execute several transactions without requiring a centralized entity. While distributed ledger technology helped deal with several challenges of centralization, it also brought with it new challenges that we need to solve when applying blockchain technology to other use cases.

One good example is Bitcoin's consensus algorithm (PoW) which is quite inefficient as it needs all participating nodes or miners to solve complex mathematical calculations. The process of solving the calculations demands an enormous amount of energy. In the early days of Bitcoin, the proof-of-work consensus was not really an issue. But with the significant increase in the level of difficulty of the calculation, the energy and time needed to resolve the calculations also jumped.

The inefficiency of this algorithm which would increase with time made the entire system unattractive in the long-term regardless of how attractive it may appear. A practical example is the use of this consensus algorithm by banks. With the high volume of transactions of these banks, such a system would be quite unsuitable for them. Other challenges facing first-generation blockchains such as Bitcoin and Ethereum include scalability, etc.

A Different Angle

Apart from the issue of high energy needs of the public blockchain and the proof of work consensus, there is also another viewpoint to consider. Remember, public blockchains are exactly as the name applies – the transactions are public. So, anyone can easily view the transactions that took place. Well, the truth is that we all may not need a public blockchain, especially organizations. Most companies would not want to disclose their business transactions to the public by using a public blockchain. They may be interested in concealing some critical data that ensures the success of their businesses because their competitors may take advantage of their trade secrets or strategies.

This eventually exposed the need for a different kind of blockchain. To help deal with this issue, two new kinds of blockchains emerged - private and federated blockchain. So, the multiple drawbacks of the first-generation blockchains such as scalability and inefficiency led to the discovery of other types of blockchain. Also, public blockchain is not suitable for all purposes as some people are not ready to disclose their data to the public.

Tyes of Blockchain

Due to the issues we just discussed, blockchain technology now comes in about four different types:

  • Public
  • Private
  • Hybrid
  • Consortium/Federated

Public Blockchain

This is the first known type of blockchain and it is regarded as a “permissionless” distributed ledger technology since we all are free to get involved and carry out our transactions. We can see it as a non-restrictive version of blockchain and as a peer-to-peer network, anyone can own a copy of the ledger. Since anyone is free to own a copy of the ledger, we can all also access the public blockchain as long as there is an internet connection.

The Bitcoin public blockchain is undoubtedly among the first ones to enter our world and it granted everyone permission to execute transactions in a decentralized way as long as there is an internet connection. A public blockchain will not be functional if it fails to get the required participating peers who solve the transactions.

Although many blockchain platforms still make use of a public blockchain, they all create additional features to distinguish themselves from other platforms. Excellent examples of public blockchains are Litecoin, Bitcoin, NEO and Ethereum.

Benefits of Public Blockchain

Despite some of their shortcomings, the truth is that public blockchains also have remarkable benefits:

  • Becoming a part of the public blockchain is free.
  • There is no need for intermediaries to make the system work.
  • Public blockchain engenders trust among the entire community of users.
  • Since the available data is open for verification purposes, it also fosters transparency for the entire network.
  • All participants in the public network are incentivized to ensure the system keeps improving.
  • Depending on the number of participating nodes, public blockchains are often very secure.

What are the Demerits?

Just like every other thing on earth, public blockchain also has its challenges. The first one has to do with transaction speed. It usually requires a few minutes and, in some cases, hours before the completion of a transaction. For instance, VISA which is a centralized payment platform can process 24,000 transactions per second and when we compare this to Bitcoin which manages about seven transactions per second, then you will agree with me that Bitcoin’s capacity is extremely low. The reason for the slow transaction speed has to do with the mathematical problems that must be solved before completing a transaction.

Scalability is a big issue with a public blockchain. As a result of how they work, public blockchains cannot scale because the network will keep getting clumsy as more nodes join and this also makes the network slower. This issue is being addressed in different ways by different blockchain networks. While Ethereum is looking at embracing the Proof of Stake (PoS) consensus mechanism, Bitcoin is networking on lighting the network and this eventually takes transactions off-chain. When this is finally completed, the main Bitcoin network will be more scalable and faster.

One more demerit of public blockchain has to do with the energy usage of their consensus method. Presently, Bitcoin still uses the PoW which requires more energy. As I mentioned earlier, Ethereum is attempting to solve this challenge partially by working toward the use of PoS.

Public Blockchain Use Cases

Despite the many challenges of the public blockchain, it has several amazing use cases and one of them is in voting. Since voting requires a significant level of transparency and trust, governments can actually use public blockchains for voting. It is also an excellent solution for fundraising for organizations and initiatives that desire to improve trust and transparency.

Private Blockchain

Just like the name, this type of blockchain is simply private and functions in a restrictive environment – a closed network. Also, a private blockchain is commonly known as “permissioned” blockchain which implies that its control is in the hands of a specific entity. Perhaps one of the best use cases of private blockchains is in private companies that desire to use it internally. When an organization decides to use a private blockchain, then they will permit a certain number of participants to access the network. The company can even take it a step further by specifying the levels of authorization, accessibility, etc., for every user on the network.

What exactly are the major features that separate the public and private blockchain? The major difference between the two is based on accessibility. Apart from the way they are accessed, both types of blockchain offer users similar features since the two can provide security, trust and transparency to participants.

A permissioned blockchain differs from the permissionless one based on the fact that it appears to be a bit centralized. Since there will be an authority in charge of the network, it is centralized and this also means theoretically that it is not decentralized. I need to point out that although you can call a private blockchain a permissioned one, the concept of permissioned blockchain is quite broad and can even be used in public blockchains too. Some excellent examples of private blockchain include Hyperledger Fabric, Corda, Multichain and Hyperledger Sawtooth.

Merits of Private Blockchain

  • One of the striking benefits of this type of blockchain is speed – they are fast. The increased speed of private blockchains is as a result of the few participants involved. The network can easily reach consensus in less time and this leads to faster transactions.
  • Another excellent advantage of private blockchains is that they are more scalable. Just a few nodes are granted the right to validate transactions in private blockchains and this makes it more scalable. So, regardless of how much the network grows, it will continue to function with its initial efficiency and speed. The major determining factor here is the centralized authority that ensures faster decision-making.

Are there disadvantages?

Of course, it also comes with some demerits. They are not really decentralized blockchains and this is seen by many as the greatest challenge of private blockchain. The fact that it is not decentralized is against the philosophy of blockchain or distributed ledger technology. It is often challenging to achieve trust within the private blockchain since those who make the last call are the centralized nodes.

One more challenge with private blockchain has to do with the impact of the number of nodes. There are just a few nodes and this lowers the level of security of this type of blockchain. If a specific number of nodes goes rogue or fails to function, this may compromise the consensus mechanism being used by the private blockchain network and will eventually lead to security issues.

Use Cases

There are numerous use cases for private blockchains and here are some of them:

  • Internal voting: It is useful for internal voting in an organization.
  • Supply chain management: It is possible for companies to adopt the private blockchain to help handle their supply chain and we shall be looking at various ways blockchain can transform supply chain in chapter 10.
  • Asset ownership: We can also track and verify assets with a private blockchain.

Federated/Consortium Blockchain

The third class of blockchain technology is consortium or federated blockchain. This class of blockchain came into existence as a creative approach to resolving the needs of an organization, especially where an organization requires the combined features of public and private blockchain. So, while some aspects of a company are made public, the company can make other aspects private. Also, the consensus procedures of this category of blockchain are handled by the preset nodes.

Although it is not completely accessible to the public, it still enjoys a decentralized structure. The reason why it still maintains a decentralized system is that many organizations are involved in managing a consortium blockchain. So, no single or centralized body can make decisions. Another feature of a consortium blockchain that helps to ensure proper functionality is the presence of a validator node that can perform two different tasks – initiate or receive transactions and also validate transactions.

On the other hand, a member node can initiate and receive transactions. Now you can see that a consortium blockchain offers users all the unique features of a private blockchain such as efficiency, privacy and transparency without also placing control in the hands of a centralized entity. Examples of this type of blockchain include IBM Food Trust, Marco Polo, and Energy Web Foundation.

Some Benefits of Consortium Blockchain

Of course, users of this type of blockchain can enjoy the advantages that a private blockchain has to offer. Some of them include:

  • They offer better scalability and are more secure.
  • Consortium blockchains also provide excellent customizability and control over resources.
  • They function properly with established governance structures.
  • When compared to public blockchain networks, consortium networks have proven to be more efficient.

On the flip side, consortium blockchain has several challenges and one of them has to do with its transparency. It is not as transparent as the public blockchain. Even though it is secure, it is possible to compromise the entire network as a result of the integrity issue of a member. The functionality of the entire network could also be influenced by regulations and censorship. Finally, when compared to other types of blockchains, it is less anonymous.

Use Cases

Examples of excellent use cases of consortium blockchain include:

  • Research - We can use consortium blockchain in collaborative work to share research data and results.
  • Another area of use is in banking and payments. For instance, it is possible for several financial institutions to establish a consortium and agree on the nodes that will validate all the transactions.
  • One more excellent use case of the consortium blockchain is in food tracking.

Chapter 3: Key Components of Blockchain

As I explained before, blockchain is simply a type of distributed ledger technology. There is no centralized administration of data; instead, data is accepted based on consensus by every participating node in the network. Several components make up a blockchain and understanding each of these components and their roles will give you a clearer picture of how the blockchain works regardless of the type of blockchain.

Nodes

They are an essential aspect of a blockchain network since they ensure the appropriate distribution of digital data on a peer-to-peer network. As I earlier mentioned, they are simply computers that have a copy of the blockchain database. However, there are two variants of nodes; we have validator or full nodes and member or partial nodes. The validator nodes are the ones that maintain a complete and updated copy of every single transaction on a given blockchain network. The validator node can validate, accept or even reject any transaction on the network.

On the other hand, partial or member nodes do not maintain the complete copy of the blockchain ledger. The lightweight node simply maintains just the hash value of transactions and can only access these transactions only with the hash value. The storage and computational strength of member nodes is low, unlike the validator nodes.

Ledger

The first component we shall be discussing is the ledger. It is just like the common word ledger most people know, but a blockchain ledger is an electronic record of several transactions. Although assets form a crucial part of transactions, the ledger is not still a collection of assets. Instead, it keeps a record of every transaction of the assets. So, we can record every transaction involving an asset like bitcoin with a distributed ledger and not just bitcoin. To create the ledger, blockchain technology builds a consecutive and chronological chain of blocks. Also, just like the case of Ethereum and Hyperledger, it can record a collection of codes known as smart contracts.

Transactions, Blocks and Chains

In this context, we can see transactions that are:

  • Records such as an event
  • Chronologically ordered
  • Verified and secured cryptographically
  • Bundled to create a block

As mentioned before, a block simply comprises a set of transactions all grouped before being added to a chain of other blocks. This is how we derived the name blockchain. As all the blocks are chained together, every new block will be assigned a reference to a special identifier from the previous block and this is known as a cryptographic hash. You can add one or more transactions to a block, which implies that we can actually batch several transactions to a single block.

The goal of batching transactions is to enhance the throughput rate of transactions for various blockchain platforms with a specific block throughput rate. Details you will find in each block are:

  • Timestamp: This refers to the timestamp of every single block that is added to the chain.
  • Previous cryptographic hash: We can see this as a unique identifier known as hash and it is referenced from the previous block.
  • Nonce: Number only used once (Nonce) is the cryptographic challenge number that all participating nodes are solving for to enable them to propose a new block. The nonce is actually a hashed block; once rehashed, it will meet the difficulty level restrictions.
  • Merkle root hash: A block also contains the Merkle tree root hash for every transaction that was added to the block.

Wallet

In the blockchain space, a wallet refers to a software that enables users to store the details of their cryptocurrency. So, each node on the network also requires a wallet and the security of the wallet is guaranteed with the public and private keys. There are two categories of wallets in the cryptocurrency space:

  • Hot wallet: This refers to wallets we use on a daily basis for various transactions. Hot wallets are linked to the internet and this also exposes them to hackers who can gain access to the wallets via the internet. There are different varieties of hot wallets and they include online/web wallets.
  • This class of wallets is cloud-based which implies that as long as you have access to the internet you can connect with your smartphone. MetaMask and MyEther Wallet are two good examples of online wallets. Also, desktop and mobile wallets belong to this class. You can download this type of wallet on your laptop or smartphone which also implies that you have full control of the wallet. Mycelium, Coinomi are two examples of software wallets.
  • Cold Wallet: The second class of wallets is often called cold storage or wallets and that is because they are not in any way connected to the internet. Cold wallets are generally the safest class of wallets since it is extremely difficult for hackers to access them. Cold wallets can come as hardware or paper wallets. Paper wallets are completely offline and the details of the wallet are stored on a piece of paper. The details of the paper wallet include the cryptocurrency address as well as a private key which is stored as a QR code. A hardware wallet, unlike a paper wallet, is an electronic device. It utilizes a random number generator linked to the wallet.

Nonce

Nonce is one of the aspects of blockchain that ensures its safety courtesy of cryptography. It is simply a shortened way to say “number only used once.” It refers to a number that is added to an encrypted or hashed block in a blockchain that satisfies the difficulty level restrictions once rehashed. In fact, what miners are solving for in a blockchain like the Bitcoin blockchain is the nonce and the moment they discover the nonce, then miners will get rewards in the form of cryptocurrency for their efforts. When a miner solves the mathematical computations, they are rewarded for their skill and time.

However, it is often very hard to find the nonce and in blockchain technology, this difficulty is one strategy for dropping cryptocurrency miners that are not very talented. Getting involved in the mining process demands exceptional computational power that can solve the nonce. Apart from blockchain and cryptocurrency, other fields that use nonce include several computer networking applications such as the popular two-factor authentication, authentication for purchases, electronic signatures and other forms of account recovery and identification.

Cryptography

Trust in blockchain technology is achieved without a central authority like traditional institutions. Instead, trust is established via cryptography and consensus. The function of cryptography is to move the burden of trust from third parties to cryptographic algorithms. Cryptography helps to encode or encrypt data and ensure that third parties or external actors are unable to decipher any given message. So, we can say that cryptography provides a confidential two-way exchange where every party can freely encrypt or decrypt the cipher before they can access the message payload.

Cryptography also helps in the validation of data integrity in blockchain. To create an encrypted version of a message also known as cipher, the cryptographic algorithm makes use of the message and the key. A sender and receiver can exchange the encrypted version of the message or the cipher.

Public Key Cryptography

The function of public-key cryptography in distributed ledger technology is for data validation in addition to user authentication via digital signatures. We can achieve user authentication via a combination of the public and private keys of a user through what is known as the “Public Key Infrastructure” (PKI) framework. In cryptography, a public key is a representation of a user's public identity and they can share it with other users on the network.

Every user on any blockchain platform holds a private key that is stored in a digital wallet. A private key keeps the details of the private credentials of a user. Mathematical algorithms are employed to help generate the pairing of these public and private keys and they ensure that we can encrypt and decrypt a message. It is also possible to use the public and private keys in a manner that can help preserve users' anonymity.

All parties are often aware of the public key – the sender and receiver. But only the sender knows the private key and when sending a message to the recipient, the sender will render the message unreadable (ciphertext) via encryption using mathematical formulas. This is to ensure adequate protection of the data and the recipient can only decrypt the “ciphertext” and transform it into a readable text with the private key.

Hash Functions

Blockchain technology utilizes a particular kind of cryptography known as cryptographic hashing. A mathematical function creates a hash code by taking a digital object and generating a fixed string of numbers and letters (32-character) to represent the hash code and a good example is a transaction represented as d7w0993waty9n33234qw949g02b9o34238878501032ff2si04d3d99sq93jzwa9.

One of the things that cryptography is used for in cryptographic hashing is for one-way conversion which leads to the creation of encoded data or hash. Parties involved in any transaction can share the hash that is created but they cannot decode it. Mathematically, there is no way anyone can successfully decrypt or reverse engineer the encoded hash. This also makes deriving the input message that was used in creating a hash code from the hash code itself impractical.

Distributed ledger technology leverages cryptographic hashing to ensure that it is possible to prove data without really sharing what the data contains. So, by matching and validating that the calculated hash matches each other, all stakeholders or parties involved in the transaction can prove the data. In fact, one of the core functions of hashes is to help in maintaining the immutable qualities of blockchains. It is quite easy to detect changes to any blockchain because any change made to the original object will eventually generate a new hash.

Every single block is a hash code that is computed as a combination of the block's data and the previous block's hash code. This is how hash codes are chained. It is not difficult to compute hash codes and all participants can easily verify that no one has made any kind of alteration to the data. The chain of hash codes will instantly become invalid when there is an attempt to delete or modify the data on any given block and participants can easily detect such changes.

Merkle Trees

The function of Merkle trees is to reduce the volume of data in any blockchain network and also ensure the efficient validation of data on the blockchain. Each block stores a Merkle root hash which is created by hashing transactional data. The transactional data will also become a part of the block. One of the things that the Merkle root hash does is to make transaction validation more efficient. Instead of validating every single data in a block, it is possible to send one transaction in addition to the relevant hash values. Any validating computer or node can now calculate the hash values for the data and also confirm the validity of a transaction without requiring all the data in the block.

Understanding Blockchain Networks

Every blockchain network comprises network participants and the participants are computers or nodes where the digital ledger is generally stored, updated from time to time and maintained. All through this book, you will certainly come across the word “node,” and it simply refers to computers in a blockchain network. Every participating node in the network has a shared copy of the ledger and constantly synchronizes with every other node across the network.

The ultimate goal of the network of nodes is to provide consensus and also make sure that each copy of the shared ledger across various nodes is consistent and valid. They also validate every new block that is added to the ledger. Every network is regulated by certain consensus algorithms or protocols that help to define the way every node in the network communicates and interacts with others. As consensus is achieved, every node will have to synchronize their copy of the shared ledger based on established consensus protocols of the blockchain network.

The specific consensus protocol of any network is usually determined by the platform that was used in implementing the network. It is the consensus of a network that guarantees the consistency and validity of every copy of the blockchain on the participating nodes. Blockchain networks are of different types and we shall be looking at some of them shortly.

So, why are blockchains like Bitcoin blockchain called a “peer-to-peer” network? The phrase “distributed” in distributed ledger technology shows that the technology utilizes a peer-to-peer network structure. Unlike a central server network, nodes on a P2P network are all linked to one another instead of connecting to a central server. There is no need for third parties like a clearinghouse or banks to process a transaction.

Distributed Storage

One of the important facts about blockchain technology is that it is essentially a kind of data storage that differs from the traditional database most of us have known for so long. Also, its characteristics are not the same as the popular databases we all know. When considering the various use cases of blockchain technology, it is crucial to understand the differences between traditional databases and a distributed storage. This is an excellent way to determine whether the distributed ledger technology is the appropriate one for your needs.

Digital ledgers are designed to help store our transactions which we can then later query regarding different characteristics of each of the blocks. So, how is data maintained and stored? It achieves proper storage and maintenance of data by having multiple copies of the shared digital ledger across different nodes. Every participating node on the network has a copy of the shared ledger.

Network nodes can only agree on the valid transaction blocks via a network consensus protocol. The consensus protocol used by any network is unique to the platform and it is only when the nodes unanimously agree on the validity of a block that they will add it to the shared ledger and also synchronize every single copy of the ledger accordingly. It is this unique architecture that adds to the resilience of the technology because even when several nodes fail, the impact on the integrity of the data of the shared ledger will not be affected as long as a critical mass of nodes still functions.

Blockchain Fork

The term may appear complex to most readers, but I will provide a simple explanation that you can understand. In blockchain technology, it is a term that illustrates divergence or cutting off from the current code or software. But this word now has a broader meaning and you can see it as what occurs the moment a blockchain splits into two potential paths or a divergence that results in a change of protocol. Forking also means the point where the network requires significant changes or technical updates in codes.

Remember, some blockchain networks such as Ethereum or Bitcoin are decentralized and this also implies that all participants on the network need to agree on the state of the network – including the shared blockchain protocol, blocks, etc. Of course, when all participants agree on the results of the blockchain network as well as all aspects of its operations, then the blockchain will stay unchanged.

This also means that the network will have a single blockchain that maintains transaction records that are verified and confirmed by participants to be correct. However, this is not always the case as there may be moments when the nodes fail to unanimously come to a consensus about the future condition of the blockchain network. This results in disagreements that eventually lead to forks – a point where a “single” will split into at least two or more chains that will all remain valid. Check out the illustration below to fully understand what forking means.

In blockchain, the process of forking leads to a shift in the existing rules to a set of new and predetermined rules. In some cases, not all members of the network can support the changes in the rules but this does not change the uniqueness of the rules. It is crucial for the rules to be identified by the nodes running on a blockchain network. In the event that a node refuses to adhere to the modifications to the rules, the node will be allowed but this decision also comes with several consequences. This brings us to the two major types of fork – hard and soft fork.

Hard Fork

This type of fork is regarded in blockchain space as a “non-backward compatible” upgrade to a blockchain that already exists. In the case of a hard fork, the nodes on the blockchain network either comply with the update to the software protocol or decide to stay with the outdated protocol and this leads to a separate blockchain entity. A good example of a hard fork is that of Ethereum and Ethereum Classic. Also, Bitcoin has experienced hard forks like the existence of Bitcoin and Bitcoin Cash.

Soft Fork

You can see this as the opposite of the hard fork. It refers to a “backward-compatible” upgrade of a blockchain network where the nodes that are upgraded can still communicate with the ones not upgraded. So, the newly created rules in a soft fork do not in any way clash with the ones already in existence. For instance, Ethereum and Bitcoin have used soft forks to execute new updates that are usually backward compatible. There are occasions where a soft fork takes place as a result of a temporary shift in the blockchain whenever miners make use of nodes that are not upgraded in violation of the new consensus rules. However, this scenario is very rare and such events are very unlikely to happen.

Once a hard fork occurs, all the changes on a node that is still making use of the old set of rules will not be valid. This implies that an old node that tries to create and verify a block will not be considered as valid. Every single change that occurs in the course of a hard fork cannot be deleted or modified upon the completion of the fork. On the other hand, an old node that chooses to make and verify a block in a soft fork will be seen as valid by every single node on the network regardless of its state – old or new.

Chapter 4: Decentralized vs. Centralized Networks

In the first chapter, I promised that you will understand the meaning of some of the terms you will come across repeatedly in this book. So, it is crucial to explain what they all mean to ensure that you do not get confused. Earlier, we discussed the difference between blockchain and distributed ledger technology. I believe you now understand what both concepts mean. Let's take a look at the difference between two commonly used words – decentralized and centralized networks and understand the pros and cons of each of these types of networks.

Centralized Networks

Primarily, the control of centralized networks rests purely on one centralized server or what you can call a master node. This centralized server manages all the data processing tasks of the entire network and also stores data as well as user information that other users on the network can access. Client nodes connect to the centralized server and instead of carrying out direct data requests, client nodes need to submit their data requests to the main server.

Excellent examples of companies that operate a centralized network include most web services such as your online banking account, YouTube, Google, mobile app store and several others. The operations of these companies are coordinated by a centralized network owner which implies that there is a need for verification by a third-party authority for every data transaction in such networks.

Currently, the most common type of network is the centralized network as most traditional companies and organizations maintain a centralized structure. Before they can connect other users and devices, they require a central network owner and this also means that a single point of failure exists in such networks and malicious actors can deliberately exploit such flaws.

Advantages of Centralized Networks

Centralized networks have several advantages and that is why they are still in use. Here are some of the merits of this type of network.

  • Low maintenance cost: Of all the different types of networks, centralized networks are the least expensive, especially for small systems and they do not need a lot of resources to set up. In the event that a network administrator wants to carry out an update of the network, they can simply update the central server and this reduces time wastage and overhead cost required to maintain an up-to-date network.
  • Deployment is straightforward and fast: In centralized networks, command chains are explicitly defined and it is relatively easy to delegate within the network. Adding or removing client nodes from the centralized server of the network is also an easy task because all that is required is to cut off the connections between the main server and the client node. But you should bear in mind that this does not increase the computing power of the network.
  • Consistency: Considering the hierarchical structure of the centralized networks – from the top- down, the interactions between the client nodes and the main server can easily be standardized. This eventually results in an end-user experience that is more streamlined and consistent. It is also not difficult to identify and eliminate extraneous activity within the network in line with the priorities of the network because the process of tracking and collecting data across the network is quite straightforward.

Demerits of Centralized Networks

Some of the demerits of a centralized network have also motivated individuals and businesses to explore other types of networks. Here are some major disadvantages of a centralized network:

  • Increased security risks: The fact that there exists a single point of failure increases the possibility of security disruptions or breaches due to cybersecurity threats like DDOS attacks. The reason for this is that all a malicious actor needs to do is to target one source which is the main server. In addition, the existence of a single server where users' data are stored implies that centralized networks will often experience privacy issues. There may be a permanent loss of data when the main server gets corrupted or is no longer online.
  • Risk of increased downtime: The main servers of centralized networks also lead to the issue of prolonged downtime. What happens when the server crashes? Of course, it will cause the whole network to shut down. This will affect the entire network since client nodes may not send or receive information. Furthermore, whenever there is a need for server maintenance, the company may power off their main server temporarily. This temporary shutdown may likely lead to interruptions in service which further lowers the reliability of the entire network from the view of users, especially with the associated inconvenience.
  • Scalability limitations: It is usually more challenging to scale centralized networks beyond specific points. The reason for this is that the company will need to increase the storage capacity of the server, its processing power and even add more storage to the central server before scaling beyond its existing point. But what happens when the network witnesses a sharp increase in traffic – exceeding the capacity of the network? When this occurs in centralized networks, there may be information bottlenecks and users may need to be taken out from the main server that is experiencing increased latency.

Decentralized Network

You can see this as the opposite of the first type of network which we just discussed. It is a network that spreads information-processing workloads to several devices rather than depending on just one central server. Every single device on the decentralized network serves as a mini central unit that also engages in independent interaction with other nodes.

The implication of this arrangement is that even when one of the master nodes gets compromised or crashes, others will keep providing users with access to data. So, the failure of one or more nodes does not impede the operation of a decentralized network as there will be no disruptions in their operation. One of the factors that motivated the use of decentralized networks is the recent advancements in technology that empowered most devices and computers with enormous processing power.

It is easy to sync most of these new devices and use them for distributed processing. Although decentralized networks differ significantly from centralized ones, data storage and processing is not uniformly distributed across the whole network. This type of network still depends on main servers, but there are usually more than one of such main servers for each network.

Merits of Decentralized Network

  • Privacy: One of the striking merits of decentralized networks is that it enhances user privacy because data storage on the network is distributed across various points rather than passing through one main point. Of course, data flow in such a network is not always easy to track across the network. malicious actors would also find it difficult to attack since there is no particular target they can attack.
  • Enhanced scalability/flexibility: Unlike centralized networks that could halt their operations when their main server fails, a single point of failure does not exist in decentralized networks. This also means that normal operations will continue even if a master node shuts down or gets compromised. Also, scaling decentralized networks is not hard because they can simply increase the number of devices on the network and boost their computing power. There is often no need for complete network shutdown during the network's maintenance.
  • Increased speed: Since it is possible for network administrators to set up master nodes in locations where there is high user activity instead of routing connections over great distances to one centralized main server, user requests are quickly completed in a decentralized network.

Demerits of Decentralized Network

  • High cost of Maintenance: When it comes to the network that is more fault-tolerant between decentralized networks and centralized networks, the decentralized network has an upper hand. But this also comes with a cost as it is often more labor-intensive and costly to maintain them. The foundation of decentralized networks are the multiple devices in the system and this eventually adds a corresponding burden on the IT resources of an organization. So, decentralized networks may not be ideal for companies that need a small system. The cost/benefit ratio of making use of a decentralized network will not favor such companies.
  • Issues of Coordination: Bigger companies may experience coordination challenges and even some difficulty in directing and accomplishing collective jobs with decentralized networks. This is as a result of the fact that the master nodes within a decentralized network operate independently and may actually fail to communicate with the others.

Distributed Network

This type of network shares some similarities with the decentralized network and one of such things is that it does not utilize a centralized main server and instead has several network owners. But one major difference between the two is that, unlike the decentralized network where there are master nodes, the distributed network comprises interconnected nodes that are all equal. So, data ownership as well as the network's computational resources are distributed across all nodes in the network equally.

Sometimes, the phrase “distributed network” is referred to as a network that is distributed geographically while adopting a top-down node hierarchy structure. But in most cases where it is used, it applies to a type of network that evenly distributes computational resources across different node locations.

The absence of a separate class of master nodes or a centralized server in a distributed network implies that the task of data processing is the responsibility of all users in the network – crowdsourced. Every node on the network enjoys equal access to data. In a distributed network, all participant nodes partake in all decision-making processes as they can vote to allow a new state. The aggregate results of the decisions of all nodes will determine the final behavior of the entire system.

The network's consensus mechanism is also responsible for determining the particular processes by which participants vote and make decisions in a distributed network. However, it is crucial to know that distributed networks are highly fault-tolerant and secure as a result of their truly decentralized nature. While they have similar merits and demerits with decentralized networks, it is at a higher level in both cases.

Merits of Distributed Networks

  • Speed and scalability: When compared to centralized and decentralized networks, the distributed network is more scalable. This type of network offers low latency as a result of the network processing power and data that is evenly distributed.
  • Extreme fault-tolerant : When a node fails in a distributed network, it cannot affect the entire system. When this happens, the entire network will instantly rebalance the computational workload among other active nodes. This makes distributed network remarkably more robust than many other network structures that usually depend on top-down node structure.
  • Increased transparency: Since data is evenly distributed across the whole network in a distributed network, it is extremely more difficult to censor, alter or erase any information on the network successfully. This also implies that a distributed network is more transparent than other systems, especially when they secure their data cryptographically.

Demerits

While it provides several benefits, it also has some drawbacks just like most things in life:

  • Maintenance cost is high: Just like decentralized networks, companies that desire to use the distributed network system will need more resources to reconfigure or maintain it. The reason for this is that any reasonable change requires that you update all the existing nodes. Considering the fact that there will be different latencies for distributed nodes that do not work with the same clock, it is not possible for network administrators to temporarily order commands or logs. This makes it extremely hard to design and debug algorithms for this type of network.
  • Coordination challenges: There is no node hierarchy in this type of network and this also means that no single node is responsible for monitoring the activities of subordinate nodes. What this implies is that individual nodes on the system may not be properly regulated. It also implies that achieving large-scale tasks or making timely decisions may not often be an easy thing to do in distributed networks. It is even more difficult for nodes in the system to make well-informed decisions while considering the state of the other nodes in the network because it is often hard for any single node to acquire a global view of the whole network.

51 Percent Attacks

One of them is the 51 percent attacks. Of course, over the past decade, the proof of work consensus has successfully protected Bitcoin blockchain from hacks. But blockchains are susceptible to a few potential attacks that can affect their operations and one of such attacks is 51 percent attacks. A 51 percent attack takes place when a person or group of persons manage to have more than 51 percent of the hashing power of any given blockchain network. This would end up giving them the power to influence the network in any way they desire by intentionally modifying or excluding the ordering transactions.

The Bitcoin blockchain has not suffered a successful 51 percent attack even though it is theoretically possible. This is because it will always become increasingly difficult for miners to spend their funds in attempting to attack the Bitcoin blockchain since they are still receiving rewards for their honest dealings in the network. But you should bear in mind that if a 51 percent attack is successful, those behind the attack can make changes to the most recent transactions – this will be for a limited time since all blocks are chained to each other via cryptographic proofs. So, there would be a need for extremely high levels of computing power.

To make changes to the blockchain code or data, the network may have to opt for a hard fork. A hard fork involves a process where the network abandons an old chain and adopts a new one. One good example of a hard fork is Ethereum and Ethereum Classic. Ethereum Classic is the old chain that some members of the Ethereum community chose to retain while the new chain that was adopted is what we now know as Ethereum.

The use of public and private key in blockchain guarantees the ownership of cryptocurrency units or any data stored in blockchain by individuals. Also, there have been numerous cases of people who either lost their private key or forgot them and what that means is that all the funds in that account will be lost and there is absolutely nothing anyone can do to retrieve them.

History and Basics of Smart Contract

Smart contracts have been around for over two decades, but many came to know about it when the Ethereum blockchain was launched. During the mid-1990s, Nick Szabo came out with several papers where he successfully conceptualized smart contracts. The computer scientist described it in his papers as

“A set of promises, specified in digital form, including protocols within which the parties perform on these promises.”

The goal of smart contracts is to capture key elements of a contract that are algorithmic and also prevent agreements from being tampered with through cryptography. In a straightforward manner, we can see a smart contract as an automated and enforceable agreement. While a smart contract is automatable courtesy of a computer, some aspects of it may need human input or control. Taking a look at Szabo's definition of a smart contract, we can find key elements of a smart contract.

A smart contract comprises “a set of promises” that may either be contractual or non-contractual – this depends on the model of the smart contract. Also, such promises may comprise of rule-based operations and/or contractual terms that are established to execute business logic.

The second element in the definition is “specified in digital form.” This also implies that a smart contract mainly operates in an electronic form consisting of several lines of code and the software that helps to prescribe the right conditions as well as outcomes. All the contractual clauses and/or functional outcomes are all added to the software as code.

Also, “protocols” as used in Szabo's definition comes in the form of an algorithm that establishes specific rules that govern the way all stakeholders process data in relation to a smart contract. It also refers to rule-based and technology-enabled operations that allow certain actions to be executed like the release of payment. One of the core components of smart contracts has to do with automated performance.

We can also examine the definition of smart contracts provided by the International Financial Corporation (IFC). They defined it as, “Self-executing software code that runs on a blockchain.” In line with this definition, we shall focus mainly on blockchain-based smart contracts. Smart contracts have the potential to provide an efficient, automated and transparent way to execute several contractual processes when paired with distributed ledger technology, especially in keeping track of the performance of agreements without depending on intermediaries.

So, based on the two definitions we have seen so far, we can conclude that smart contracts are simply blockchain-based computer protocols. These protocols digitally facilitate the control, verification and actual execution of an agreement between two or more parties – there is absolutely no need for intermediaries.

How Smart Contracts Work

The starting point is for the contracting parties to come to an agreement. At this point, all the parties involved will need to identify a corporate opportunity and expected results. The opportunity they identify is usually something that may benefit all parties involved in one way or another. Agreements, in this case, could include asset swaps, the transfer of rights, business processes and several others.

Once the parties to the contract have identified areas of opportunity and desired results, the next phase involves setting the conditions of the smart contract. It is possible for parties involved in a smart contract to initiate it by themselves or it could also be initiated when specific conditions are satisfied such as natural disasters, financial market indices or any given event. Also, temporal conditions could actually initiate smart contracts on religious events, birthdays or holidays.

The next phase involves coding the business logic and a developer can handle this aspect of the smart contracts if the parties are unable to do it. It involves a computer program that is written in a manner that ensures the automatic performance of the arrangement the moment the conditional parameters are satisfied. Then the message will pass through blockchain encryption to help verify and authenticate the message between all stakeholders in the smart contract. Once the developer has coded the smart contract, then a party that all stakeholders agree upon will validate the smart contract and proceed to record the agreement on a blockchain.

The type of blockchain that hosts the smart contract will also determine the nature of the smart contract. Transactions in a smart contract will be visible to the public if it is hosted on a public/permissionless blockchain like Ethereum.

The fourth step involves the execution and processing. In blockchain iteration, the smart contract will be written to a block after the nodes reach a consensus on the authentication and verification of the smart contract. At this point, the smart contract evaluates data and self-executes or expires – the terms of the agreement will determine the outcome. Then the network will update all nodes after the performance of the smart contract to reflect the new state. The record of the smart contract cannot be altered once it has been verified and posted to the blockchain network.

The third phase of smart contracts hosted on blockchain involves the smart contract connecting with external data sources and this is through database services such as accounts at financial institutions or a data feed. The most common statements included in smart contracts are simple ones like “if/when...then...” These statements are then written into code on the preferred blockchain network. Once the predetermined conditions are met and verified, then a network of nodes will execute the actions.

Examples of the actions include registering a car, the release of funds to the right individual, issuing a ticket or even sending a specific notification. Once the nodes execute the actions, then this information will be updated on the blockchain to show that it is completed. Several stipulations may exist within a smart contract as long as they are required to convince the participants of the successful completion of the task.

In the course of establishing the terms, all parties need to ascertain how their data and transactions are represented on their chosen blockchain platform. They need to also agree on the "if/when...then..." rules that will govern all their transactions. One more important factor that the parties to the contract need to agree on are all possible exceptions and in the event that disputes arise, they must agree on the appropriate guidelines for dispute resolution.

Apart from developers who can help in programming the smart contract, more options are emerging with the development of the sector. For instance, more firms using blockchain for business have now developed templates, nice web interfaces as well as other online tools that can help to simplify and structure smart contracts.

Scalability

Scalability is undoubtedly among the most difficult hurdles facing the blockchain space. You can see it as simply a lack of scale and what it means is that the state at which transactions are processed on distributed ledgers compared to the traditional platforms is limited. Presently, most legacy companies process thousands of transactions each second but most top blockchain networks are still far behind. For private blockchains, scalability is not often an issue since trusted parties are involved in the activities of the nodes in such networks and this is an excellent business strategy. The top blockchain networks such as Bitcoin and Ethereum have not been able to handle the level of transactions on their network effectively and this is because of the structure of their networks.

There is a limit to the number of transactions that we can process on-chain and this issue of lack of scale is a major challenge for organizations that require networks that can facilitate high transaction throughput while maintaining low latency. So, what are the available blockchain solutions to the issue of scalability?

1. Off-Chain Solutions

Many blockchain platforms feel that off-chain solutions will boost the transaction speed of blockchain networks while decentralization and security should also be maximized on the digital ledger. In case you do not understand what off-chain scaling solutions mean, it has to do with solutions that permit the execution of transactions without over-burdening the blockchain. So, any protocol that makes use of off-chain scaling solutions permits users on their platform to send and receive funds while the details of the transaction do not appear in the main chain. Currently, many blockchain projects are exploring various off-chain solutions in a bid to resolve the issue of scalability and we shall look at some of them.

2. Accelerated chips

Skynet Core is a forerunner in accelerated chips and this solution could increase the speed of confirmation as well as transaction times. The project is focused on dealing with the challenges of the adoption of blockchain technology as well as the functionality of the Internet of Things (IoT). One of the core goals of Skynet is to provide an end-to-end system and this includes an extremely scalable IoT blockchain network and a blockchain IoT chip that is license-free known as Skynet Core.

The project intends to provide billions of blockchain chips that are license-free and they will be deployed to devices around the world that will connect through the company's blockchain network. This license-free blockchain chip is intended to replace the CPU we all use presently and it has a core that is optimized for distributed ledger technology and IoT. With the hardware, Skynet Core devices can provide adequate protection from theft of digital currencies and easily run blockchain networks with high throughput.

3. Sidechains

One of the most promising solutions for the issue of scalability is the use of side chains. It is simply a separate blockchain but not a standalone platform because it is somehow pegged to the main chain. So, both the sidechain and main chain are interoperable – assets can freely flow from one chain to the other. The primary reason for creating side chains is to cut down on the load that a blockchain network is having. Achieve this involves sending transactions through all the linked sidechains and also adding the final state of the transaction on the main blockchain. This mechanism helps to relieve the main blockchain of the burden of processing numerous transactions.

To ensure the transfer of funds, there are a variety of side chain solutions. For instance, some solutions move data from the main chain to a special address while a matching amount will also be issued on the sidechain. Another option is sending the funds to a custodian who will in turn exchange the deposited funds for funds on the sidechain.

4. Sharding

Ethereum is one of the blockchain networks that is exploring the use of sharding as a scaling solution. Sharding simply has to do with spreading the computing and storage needs from just a blockchain network to single nodes. So, networks that adopt sharding split their blockchain into several separate areas known as shards. The network will assign to each of the shards a small group of nodes that they will maintain.

This implies that the nodes on the network will not process the transactional load of the entire network. All that each node is expected to do is to maintain the information that pertains to its shards or partition. The use of sharding eliminates the need for every single node in a blockchain network to be involved in a transaction.

5. Multi-Layered Structure

This is also one of the solutions that blockchain projects are working on to help upgrade scale. It refers to the isolation of two separate aspects of blockchain technology – transaction processing and data storage. Cardano is one of the blockchain networks working on this solution. Commonly regarded as a third-generation blockchain, the project is a decentralized and open-source blockchain project that has a layered structure. Its structure comprises two key elements; Cardano Computational Layer (CCL) and Cardano Settlement Layer (CLS).

The two-layered architecture of the project makes Cardano very unique since majority of the existing blockchain networks operate with a single layer which eventually leads to network congestion, reduces the speed of transactions and leads to spikes in fees. Cardano's settlement layer handles transaction confirmation as well as the flow of the native coin. On the other hand, the computational layer of the network is responsible for maintaining the network's security. Also, it handles the deployment of smart contracts and the core developers programmed it to recognize the identity of the data.

Blockchain is indeed a highly sophisticated weapon for fighting cases of data breach and other types of virtual crime. 1. Blockchain and Clinical Trials

Generally, clinical trials involve testing new drugs to ascertain how effective they are in a controlled environment. Unfortunately, most companies working on clinical trials can never disclose the cases of manipulation of information. A distributed ledger technology like blockchain can help in many ways. It can help by providing data integrity and acting as proof in the process of verifying the authenticity of a document. Blockchain's distributed network will help maintain the integrity of data and ensure that no one modifies the data without the authorized access. Among the solutions implementing the use cases are Sanofi, Pfizer and Amgen.

2. Pseudonymous Health Recommendations Focused on Patients

One of the exciting potential use cases of blockchain technology in healthcare is providing health recommendations that are pseudonymous, patient-focused or encrypted health warnings and advisories. What this means is that while a patient's identity is secure, their health details can actually be contributed to a collection of data in a distributed ledger. With this, it would be possible to provide encrypted health recommendations that are patient-specific which would be sent to the patient securely. This information (that would be sent to an anonymous patient) would strictly be based on the patient's data, risk factors, etc.

The recent development in the healthcare sector that is now increasingly focusing on a more community-focused and proactive preventive care model of treatment makes this scenario more relevant. In case you are unsure what the preventive care model is all about, it has to do with preventing the occurrence of an injury or a disease before it happens. With blockchain technology, it would be possible to securely share medical records in encrypted form and this would unlock a new opportunity of understanding for population health. Blockchain is not centralized, unlike traditional healthcare systems that have intermediaries serving as giant warehouses of patient information.

The healthcare sector faces the issue of claims, in fact, there is a high rate of fraud in billing and claims with billing being affected the most. 4. Identity Management in Healthcare

The health sector needs an improved identity management system in place. This will help to deal with differential versioning of identities and eventually facilitate secure identification. It is also essential for healthcare providers (organizational and individual) to provide updated and accurate information regarding the availability of their service and location to enable, for instance, accrediting agencies to identify them, payers to reimburse them properly and patients to locate them. With blockchain, we can actually improve the veracity and accuracy of the right information. By using a distributed ledger, stakeholders can create an immutable log of all the time a data record is modified or accessed.

Presently, Estonia already protects government data (this includes electronic health records) from internal misuse and external cyberattacks by using an application on the blockchain known as KSI. While providing security, the KSI technology does not also violate data privacy. They crosscheck any amendment to a record with separate electronic logs and this enables them to quickly verify any suspicious access and prevent potential malicious activities. Blockchain functions like a CCTV camera that is positioned in high crime-risk locations.

While someone is committing a crime, the blockchain does not intervene; instead, it offers transparency and makes it impossible for malicious actors to interrupt or modify the recording (immutability). Within a health information system, blockchain technology is most useful when it is utilized alongside other technologies such as the Internet of things and several others.

In 2020, a tourist destination in South Korea – Jeju Island revealed that they were deploying a solution that is blockchain-enabled to help in contact tracing of visitors to the Island who might test positive to COVID-19. Individuals who visited the Island were expected to download an app on their smartphone which they can use to scan QR codes of all the businesses as well as services they used all through their time on the island.

As soon as users download the app, they will need to confirm their identity through a public blockchain which will issue a certificate. Next, the app user will set up a digital fingerprint authentication and PIN code on their mobile device and a private blockchain will record a certificate for all the credentials. The app on the user’s smartphone will store both certificates while their identifying details will not be stored in the same location with the record of all the services and businesses they accessed. The healthcare sector as we discussed earlier is facing several challenges and one that is so crucial is protecting patients' data and their privacy. Whenever a person chooses to modify the terms of consent or permission, then all modifications will be added as a new block which will override the initial instruction that was recorded on the chain.

An excellent example of this use case is the Malta biobank project which manages the dynamic consent of people to utilize their biospecimens for research purposes by making use of a blockchain technology known as Dwarna. Another excellent example of how blockchain can help with managing patient consent and permission to access their data is MedRec. The system associates a medical record with viewing permissions as well as instructions for data retrieval for external databases. To record patient-provider interactions, the project makes use of smart contracts and as soon as a provider creates a record, it will be verified then the patient will authorize others to view it.

Those getting new information also get a notification (that is automated) as well as an encrypted pointer to the new medical record. The system is designed to grant patients access to manage their data and permissions are stored on the blockchain. This project has yielded positive results in areas such as vaccination histories, medications, therapeutic interventions and blood tests.

Models of Digital Identity Management

Let's take a look at the models of digital identity management. The first model is a siloed type and with this model, every organization provides a digital identity credential for their users. This allows their users to access various services on their platform. So, for every new organization a user engages with, they are expected to have a new digital identity credential and this offered a terrible user experience. Imagine registering and creating new login details and passwords for every website you want to access.

As a result of the awful user experience of the first model of digital identity management, a second model emerged known as a “Federated” one. Third parties started providing digital identity credentials that enable every user to log in to services and different websites. An excellent example of this model is “login with Google” or “login with Facebook” functionalities. Consequently, many organizations outsourced their company's identity management to firms with economic interests in collating the personal data of users.

Unfortunately, this has eventually raised privacy and security issues around the world with several lawsuits. Companies like Google and Facebook turned out to be intermediaries of trust. But we are now seeing a third model of identity management with the introduction of blockchain technology, verifiable credentials and decentralized identifiers. This permitted the creation of the Self-Sovereign Identity which we shall be looking at later in this chapter. So, what is the relationship between verifiable credentials, decentralized identifiers and blockchain identity management?

Well, verifiable credentials serve as statements that an issuer makes in a privacy-respecting and tamper-proof way. They give room for the digital watermarking of claims data via a mix of public-key cryptography and privacy-preserving strategies to avoid correlation. This ensures that physical credentials can be turned digital safely and such credential holders can reveal particular information from their credential without revealing the actual data. So, you can easily prove that you are above 18 without revealing your ID in cases where third parties can verify this specific data without requiring the attention of the issuer.

The second is decentralized identifiers and they are unique, persistent and global identifiers. DIDs are entirely under the control of the identity owner and are completely independent of authorities, centralized registries and identity providers. The distributed ledger serves as a verifiable data registry or you can see it as a “phonebook” which people can look up to verify the organization a particular public DID belongs to. When it comes to identity management, what blockchain technology does is to allow all participants on the network to have access to the same source of truth regarding the valid credentials as well as those who attested to its validity without disclosing the real information.

There are basically three actors when it comes to identity management with distributed ledger technology and they are owners, issuers and verifiers.

These are the three actors that play a crucial role when leveraging digital ledger technology for identity management. The identity issuer refers to trusted parties like government institutions and the government that issue identity owners’ personal credentials. The identity issuer instantly testifies to the validity of a users’ personal data by issuing a credential. Then the identity owner can choose to keep the credentials in their personal identity wallet which they can now use in subsequent transactions to prove the validity of their identity to third parties (verifiers). You can see credentials as several identity attributes while an identity attribute refers to a piece of data regarding any given identity such as date of birth, age, name, etc.

Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI)

It may sound a little bit tricky to explain the meaning of SSI. What it means, in reality, is that a person would be the sole owner of the analog and digital versions of their identity. This also implies that an organization can enjoy this kind of privilege too. Every user that has a Self-Sovereign Identity has the power to determine who would have access to their data. Considering the nature of this kind of identity, the most suitable technology for it would be distributed ledger technology.

This is because blockchain's decentralized nature ensures the absence of any third party. But for the system to function fully and successfully, it would require several principles. Blockchain-enabled Self-Sovereign Identity platforms can function alongside GDPR Law by using these guiding principles. Every platform will have to offer ten basic principles of SSI. So, what are the principles?

1. Existence: Users on the platform should have an independent existence and should also be in digital form too.

2. Access: Every Self-Sovereign Identity should offer users fill access. This implies that users on the platform can access their information and also access other claims without depending on any third parties. However, since the data has signed verification that cannot be modified, users may not be able to modify all the data.

3. Control: This principle means that users are in complete control of their identities. There is another angle to the principle of control since there are several instances where users may not have complete ownership of the Self-Sovereign Identity. However, the user still gets the chance to limit how their identity is handled by other parties in such instances.

4. Transparency: There is a need for transparency for all algorithms or systems handling the identities. It would be impossible for users to ensure that the Self-Sovereign Identity platform is properly managing their identities without an open system.

5. Portability: It should be easy for users on the platform to transport their identity anytime they need it. What this feature implies, in reality, is that users can easily export their identities and also make use of them in different scenarios.

6. Persistence: A good Self-Sovereign Identity platform will ensure the longevity of all identities. So, in the event that the system is going through an upgrade, users on the platform can still maintain their previous data just as they were before the upgrade.

7. Interoperability: The absence of excellent interoperability eliminates the flexibility of identities. So, for a Self-Sovereign Identity platform to be effective, then it should be acceptable in any location.

8. Minimization: This principle means that as much as possible, users' identities should not be completely exposed whenever third parties want to access them. For instance, when a third party requires access to a user's age, all the system should disclose to that particular user is the age and not the user's exact birth date. There is really no need to overexpose the identity of a user.

9. Consent: Third parties cannot access the information of users regardless of the reasons. In a situation where a third party wants to access the information of any user, the user must grant the third-party full consent. Without obtaining the permission of a user, no one can access the user's identity.

10. Protection: Users should have full rights to their Self-Sovereign Identity regardless of what happens. This means that all SSI platforms must maintain this standard no matter the pressure.

Decentralized Public Key Infrastructure (DPKI)

At the core of decentralized identity is DPKI and digital ledger technology enables DPKI simply by establishing a trusted and tamper-proof medium suitable for the distribution of the asymmetric verification as well as the encryption keys of the identity holders. With DPKI, it would be possible for anyone to create or anchor cryptographic keys on the distributed ledger in a manner that is tamper-proof and chronologically ordered. The purpose of these keys is to grant other individuals access to verify digital signatures or even encrypt data to any given identity holder.

Initially, traditional certificate authorities were responsible for providing users with digital certificates. However, with distributed ledger technology, a centralized Certificate Authority (CA) is no longer required. Apart from being indispensable to decentralized identity, DPKI is also an enabler for other use cases – verifiable credentials (VC). When people mention the phrase verifiable credentials (VCs) they often talk about digital credentials that are accompanied by cryptographic proofs.

Decentralized Storage

Generally, digital identities that are stored on distributed ledgers are quite safer than the ones anchored on various centralized servers. When blockchains that are cryptographically secure such as the Ethereum blockchain are combined with distributed data storage systems such as OrbitDB or InterPlanetary FileSystem, then we can disintermediate centralized data storage systems already in existence and still maintain trust and data integrity.

One of the advantages of decentralized storage solutions that are designed to be tamper-proof is that they significantly lower the ability of an entity to gain unauthorized data access just to exploit or even make money from users' confidential information. Undoubtedly, one of the core aspects of secure identity data management is decentralized storage. Credentials in a decentralized framework are often stored on the devices of users like their tablets, smartphones, desktops or private identity stores can also store them. These private identity stores are usually known as identity hubs and excellent examples include 3Box or uPort's TrustGraph.

Identities are regarded as self-sovereign when they are mainly under the user's control. The organizations providing the identity in centralized identity systems are mainly in charge of securing the identity data. When it comes to NFTs, every single token possesses a unique value and you cannot exchange it for another token of equal value. The value of cryptocurrency just like fiat currency is uniform and this implies that a $100-dollar bill in Jamaica, is the same value as the one in France or in the UK. You can exchange one bitcoin for the same price anywhere in the world. But two NFT tokens may have different values.

A fungible asset is regarded as an item that we can easily interchange its units and a perfect example of a fungible asset is money. You can even swap $100 for two $50 notes and it will retain its value. But this is not the case with non-fungible assets and you can easily guess what it means based on the definition of fungible assets. Non-fungible assets have unique features and this also implies that we cannot interchange them with anything else. Examples of NFTs include:

  • An in-game item
  • A domain name
  • A special sneaker owned by a limited-run fashion line
  • Unique artwork in digital form.
  • A coupon that grants the owner access to an event
  • A digital collectible
  • An essay
  • Sports highlights
  • Memes
  • Published columns
  • Viral photos

Why the latest increased popularity of NFTs? One of the factors that caused the increased popularity of NFTs is the pandemic. Also, the increased value of bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies played a major role in the popularity of NFTs.

The price of ETH has jumped since 2020 and anyone can purchase a part of an NFT or even a full one in some cases with ETH. It is the fact that anyone who buys an NFT will have access to an item that no other person could ever own without the deliberate effort of the owner to replicate it.

Anyone can buy baseball cards, paintings as well as other similar works that others can easily copy. But what differentiates NFTs is that they offer the buyer true sole ownership of the piece of art or any other item and this is very appealing to many people. The truth is that the popularity of NFTs will continue to increase as blockchain technology also becomes a preferred means of proof of ownership.

Investors seeking exposure to a different set of assets should lookout for more opportunities in this asset class either through individual NFTs or managed funds. But also, it is crucial to consider the risks involved in picking NFTs to avoid choosing the wrong one. Understanding BaaS

A straightforward definition of Baas is that it is a cloud-based service that allows users to create their digital products while leveraging blockchain technology. You can also see BaaS as a distributed ledger version of Software-as-a-service (SaaS) where businesses around the world subscribe to this service and gain access to cloud-based software. Examples of some of the digital products include decentralized applications (dApps) as well as other services that function without the need for setup demands of a complete blockchain-based platform.

Details of How BaaS Works

It involves a process where a third-party company installs, hosts and even maintains blockchain networks for other users. This third party that is also regarded as an external service provider decides to establish all the required blockchain technology as well as infrastructure and in return charges a fee. The external service provider will handle maintenance and infrastructure issues.

The truth is that the role of blockchain-as-a-service providers is just like a web hosting provider. They provide a platform where customers can take advantage of their cloud-based solutions. Also, BaaS assists various organizations in developing and hosting blockchain apps as well as smart contracts in a blockchain platform that cloud-based service providers manage and administer. They offer support services such as suitable resource allocation, bandwidth management, data security features and hosting requirements.

The advantage of this service is that enterprises do not need to bother about the daily complexities of managing and operating a blockchain. Instead, they can now focus mainly on their core business activities while BaaS providers handle the smooth functioning of the underlying technology.

The Importance of Blockchain as a Service

There is a growing interest by businesses to leverage blockchain technology, however, most businesses are faced with the operational overhead and technical complexities involved in creating, configuring and even operating a blockchain. In terms of setting up infrastructure and maintaining it, blockchain demands huge investment and when compared to other traditional databases, it is often more resource-intensive.

One more point I should add here is that blockchain requires huge bandwidth and a lot of energy. This will significantly lower the barriers to entry for enterprise blockchain applications. Government Regulation in a World that is Increasingly Getting Decentralized

A look at the architecture of blockchain technology will undoubtedly explain the reasons for its inherent challenges to legacy institutions and traditional regulation and governance approaches. However, we will likely experience more challenges with an increase in the use of permissionless or public distributed ledger technology. However, the underlying use cases of blockchain technology will certainly be regulated. Governments around the world will do this in collaboration with different regulators from several jurisdictions and others involved.

Although there is presently no precise combination of governance mechanisms for blockchain technology, when one is available, it is most likely to involve the combination of “regulatory levers” that may be pulled to identify the appropriate balance.

The regulation of blockchain faces some key dilemmas. For instance, we all know that regulation is essential to help protect users while ensuring security. However, when there is excessive regulation, it may eventually stifle innovation, especially in the financial sector. This also explains why the current dialogue on blockchain regulation should be sustained to ensure proper resolution to every aspect of its governance, especially when it has to do with privacy and security. States that desire to adopt decentralized business models may carry out several modifications to their existing legal frameworks.
