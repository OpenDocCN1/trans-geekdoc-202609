
# Blockchain History and Smart Contracts

We shall be looking at smart contracts in detail in Chapter 5, but it is crucial to talk briefly about smart contracts since they form a major part of blockchain history. Innovation is a daily occurrence in our world, and one of those who have made tremendous impacts in the history of digital ledger technology is Vitalik Buterin. He was part of a group of developers who believed that Bitcoin was yet to attain its full potential in terms of taking advantage of the capabilities of digital ledger technology.

The limitations of Bitcoin were part of the major factors that motivated Buterin to focus on creating a blockchain capable of carrying out functions other than cryptocurrency, in addition to being a peer-to-peer network. This led to the creation of the Ethereum blockchain in 2013, which went a step further to add other functionalities. This development became a turning point in the history of blockchain. With the Ethereum blockchain, people can now do other things like recording other assets and contracts, unlike Bitcoin, which focused mainly on serving as a database for bitcoin transactions.

This new and unique feature opened the door to the functionalities of the Ethereum network, and it was not just a cryptocurrency but also a platform where people can develop decentralized applications (dApps). The Ethereum blockchain was eventually launched officially in 2015 with its support for smart contracts, and the blockchain network has successfully attracted a very active developer community and created an amazing ecosystem.

### A Summary of Blockchain Technology and Its Three Generations

![Image](Image00002.jpg)

Presently, there are three generations of blockchain, and Bitcoin belongs to the first generation. The primary purpose and design of first-generation blockchains was to enhance financial systems; this explains why most of the cryptocurrencies created during this era were all focused on financial applications. Since Bitcoin's design was just to facilitate financial transactions, what about adding things like terms and conditions to the transactions?

Here is a simple explanation. James orders a wristwatch online from a lady called Jenifer and he provides his condition for making payment: *"I can only pay Jenifer her 5 bitcoins when she delivers the wristwatch I ordered?"*

In this case, it is not possible for Bitcoin to handle this type of transaction. This is where second-generation blockchains such as Ethereum come in. The two striking features that Ethereum introduced were smart contracts, which can now ensure that Jenifer can only get paid automatically when she delivers the wristwatch. The second feature that Ethereum introduced was a platform where developers can create their decentralized applications (dApps) and also launch their tokens.

This makes the Ethereum blockchain a digital ecosystem where different projects can operate. Interestingly, things have improved significantly with the introduction of a variety of functional uses such as decentralized finance (DeFi), gaming, supply chain management, non-fungible tokens (NFTs), etc.

Third-generation blockchains involve projects such as Cardano, Ethereum 2.0 (an upgraded version of Ethereum), Polkadot, and several others. When Satoshi Nakamoto and Vitalik Buterin came out with their various blockchain projects, they still had several teething problems. This also limited their capabilities, and one such issue is their capacity, commonly known as the scalability problem.

When there are too many individuals attempting to execute their transactions in a blockchain that has limited space, there will be serious congestion. So, the design of third-generation blockchains was primarily focused not just on providing most of the functionalities of first- and second-generation blockchains, but also on resolving the issues they had, especially that of scalability. One other issue that third-generation blockchains are solving is that of interoperability.

### The Foundation of Cryptocurrencies

We can define blockchain technology simply as *"cryptographically secured distributed ledgers."* It is basically a database where participants can view the information it contains at any time. Rather than storing the data on one centralized server (common among traditional organizations), the data is spread across various computers or "nodes" around the world. This ensures that all participating nodes can access the database. It is common to see people confuse blockchain and cryptocurrency. Also, some people feel that Bitcoin is the same as blockchain, but they are all different things.

Blockchain serves as the underlying technology that ensures the transparency of all transactions we record. On the other hand, cryptocurrency is simply a digital store of value that we can use for various purposes like the purchase of properties, goods, and services. Bitcoin is a popular example of a cryptocurrency.

Satoshi Nakamoto introduced cryptography as a way to secure the cryptocurrency against counterfeit and other security challenges associated with fiat money. So, the primary reason for creating blockchain technology was to facilitate Bitcoin's existence and use. It serves as the decentralized ledger containing every cryptocurrency transaction across any network regarded as peer-to-peer. You can call blockchain the *"internal banking system"* of cryptocurrencies that is decentralized. However, the use of blockchain has now grown beyond financial transactions, just as explained before, and we shall look at several use cases later.
