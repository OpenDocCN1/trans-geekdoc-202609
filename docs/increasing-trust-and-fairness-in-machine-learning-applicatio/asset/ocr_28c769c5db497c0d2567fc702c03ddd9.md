A second explainable AI system (MLX2) focused on global explanation of the market response, based on the What tool, BoxAuditing and Aequitas. It aims to provide insight into making investment decisions by allowing for quantitative (measuring) bias. In addition, instructions and examples were provided to the data scientists with the libraries. The techniques were executed with augmented data classification tasks to test for the possibility to model bias in this regard.

This collection of global explanation techniques was demonstrated to data scientists and representatives from risk, legal and compliance departments, and it also helped the organisation to address concerns concerned with the construction and administration of models.

Furthermore, we conclude that the explainable AI systems developed in this work have also helped the organisation in adhering to guidelines regarding the ethical use of machine learning in the insurance industry, improving accountability and quality as expressed in the Ethical Guidelines for the Insurance Industry. This enables the organisation to establish human control over AI systems, addressing potential transparency issues.

Overall, the findings of our research demonstrate the benefits of interpretable techniques in practice. The opportunity to implement and validate our systems in a business environment, with a wide range of stakeholders, from learners to others clearly demonstrates that these libraries render ML systems easier to interpret and critically assess.

Finally, number of practical observations we made while deploying explainable AI in a real-world setting. Developing an explainable AI in a business environment requires significant time, effort and expertise, as we need to curate the data set to select the top 15 most important features for our model.

Furthermore, experts do not want all the possible explained by ML technique; in our case, detailed explanations were provided to the reviewers of models in order to identify potential bias. As a result, we requested a prototype system that was based on part of the explanations, generated, comparisons to variants and the data features, in order to provide experts with additional insights.

Our implementation of MLXI showed that users experience a higher level of trust using AI systems more than MLXI without. However, when implementing business processes that guard against bias, users found to have their own concerns. Our readers encountered the oversight required by the Ethical Guidelines. In the mortgage application context, reviewers were required to verify and confirm every single risk indicator identified by the model and considered the model decisions and insights, but not blindly copy.

There are several limitations to our research that should be addressed. In the fact that we used only when suspected fraud was detected, as well as limited to reviewers, mean that the fact performance or potential of a more subjective implementation that adds more statistically based explanations, remains untreated.

Other limitations could arise when the methodology introduced in our work is used in practice, for example the added computation time of KernelSHAP in the case of a more complex architecture or data privacy laws making it impossible to aggregate data with results to mitigate bias.

Further areas for future work include comparing MLXI for explanations against a baseline providing a textual evaluation. Evaluating the Anchor component MLXI against manual group of results might provide insights as the reviewers presented with the mapping grouping having differing reactions. Furthermore, developing a method to quantify the trust in gains and debits, and allow for a comparison between the model variant that was built in this research, and a more subjective variant of MLXI context.

The research presented in this paper allowed the organisation to improve the acceptance and effectiveness of their ML models, by providing a new method for better understanding models. Furthermore, the paper presented an approach to test unbiased bias and distribution, in a bidirectional stream relating results model acceptance and decision. Both methods presented are designed to clarify the potential fraud risk domain, improving model bias and testing with AI models in high impact applications.
