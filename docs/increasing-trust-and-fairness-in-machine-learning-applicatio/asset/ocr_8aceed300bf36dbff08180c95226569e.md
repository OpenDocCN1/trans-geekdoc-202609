The integration of machine learning in applications provides opportunities for increased efficiency in many organizations. The deployment of such systems is often hampered by the lack of insight into how decisions are reached, resulting in about trust and fairness. In this article, we investigate what the extent of explainable AI components to ML applications can contribute to alleviating these issues. As part of this research, AI functionality was developed for an existing ML model used for mortgage fraud detection at a large institution. The global and local explanation techniques were deployed to support the day-to-day work of fraud detection experts with the model. In addition, a second system implementing explanation techniques was developed to support the management process. A large-scale controlled experiment was carried out to measure the effectiveness of the two systems, both qualitative and quantitative assessment showed that the addition of AI functionality resulted in a significant increase in trust in the model by its users. The AI system implementing global explanation was found to considerably increase confidence in the ability to perform the processes on compliance and fairness. In particular, bias detection towards certain demographic groups was identified and removal of such biases achieved with a mitigation strategy.

# 1.1. Problem statement

A trade-off in the deployment of high-performance ML systems is that they are often so that the exact decision is made by the system, making it very difficult for users to understand why a specific result was obtained. Many problems arise from this inability to examine the decision process. Firstly, it is important to validate that the outcome of the process is defensible, and that it follows a compelling path of thought. 

One frequently used for machine learning is in addressing the risk of fraud. A large case is the Dutch System Risk Indication (SRRI), which is linked to a large amount of demographic data and used to identify potentially fraudulent individuals. The requirements for the process to be fairly considered, combined with the fact that being marked as a potential fraudster had great impact on the industry, caused the system to be prohibited in 2020, after having been in use for many years (van Dijk et al., 2020).
