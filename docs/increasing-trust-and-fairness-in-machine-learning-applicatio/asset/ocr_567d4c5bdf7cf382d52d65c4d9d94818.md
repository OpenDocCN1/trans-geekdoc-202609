# 1.2. Hypotheses

In this article, we present an extensive evaluation and case study with a large Dutch insurer, hereafter referred to as "the organisation". The organisation offers life and non-life insurance for various products and services in different areas; it uses ML for fraud detection, risk pricing and underwriting. Our goal is to further improve its use of AI, in order to address the obligations set out by regulators. In order to achieve this, we would like to have a set of tools that help data scientists explain the models they use, to validate the decision process, and investigate and demonstrate the fairness of a given model. Furthermore, the organisation requires methods to explain the individual outcomes and for models with black box structures to explain the decision making process. Also, the organisation requires us to evaluate the interpretability of models using ML, as it is crucial for employees and managers to understand the predictions made by ML.

In this paper, we focus on the development of explainable AI systems and instructions proposed in this research helps organisations implement interpretable and unbiased ML systems, we set the following hypotheses:

+   1. Techniques for individual predictions to be interpretable and transparent increase trust, and usability of ML models with their users.

+   2. Techniques for ML to be globally interpretable and demonstrably free of discriminatory bias enables streamline internal processes concerning fair and balanced AI.

# 1.3. Methodology

Our study presented here researches design, by designing two prototype AI systems jointly with the organisation. By researching existing techniques in the domain of AI research, we selected a set of techniques and combine them to provide interpretability for the organisation to achieve the goals described. We evaluate the techniques identified through existing AI at the organisation, how effective these techniques are, and what should be considered for their implementation.

We propose two prototype systems. The first of these focuses on implementing operational explainability, granting users insight into the individual predictions generated by a model. In literature this is known as local interpretability. This first system will benefit from being locally interpretable. Our second system focuses on bias detection, granting insight factors into the overall behaviour of the ML model, and testing the fairness for a given model. This allows for a model to be fair and unbiased, which is known as global interpretability. This system will be called MLX2. These two systems allow for an organisation to gain insight into the behaviour of ML models on

a micro-level, as well as to ensure that an ML system is demonstrably free of bias.

MLX1 was validated in cooperation with a group of 11 mortgage application agents, who work with an ML model assessing applications on daily basis. A short survey revealed high satisfaction and satisfactory performance. We validated it with a second survey, in cooperation with data scientists, specifically focusing on the use of their system in development and process, and also with experts from Legal, Compliance and Risk departments, to evaluate the interpretability and insights gained from using it. The results showed that the system was accepted in every new and existing ML model in their use.

The result of our study presented here is an evaluation of techniques for the given problem, implementation and considerations in a practical scenario.

Another is focused on developing machine learning models that are interpretable, by creating methods to develop a separate layer that explains the interpretability of black-box models (Lipton et al., 2016).
