The integration of machine learning in applications provides opportunities for increased efficiency in many organizations. The deployment of such systems is often hampered by the lack of insight into how decisions are reached, resulting in concerns about trust and fairness. In this article, we investigate what the extent of explainable AI components to ML applications can contribute to alleviating these issues. As part of this research, AI functionality was developed for an existing ML model used for mortgage fraud detection at a large institution. The global and local explanation techniques were deployed to support the day-to-day work of fraud detection experts with the model. In addition, a second system implementing explanation techniques was developed to support the management process. A large-scale controlled experiment was carried out to measure the effectiveness of the two systems, both qualitative and quantitative assessment showed that the addition of AI functionality resulted in a significant increase in trust in the model by its users. The AI system implementing global explanation was found to considerably increase confidence in the ability to perform the processes on compliance and fairness. In particular, bias detection towards certain demographic groups was identified and removal of such biases achieved with a mitigation strategy.

# 1. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence is being deployed in a broad range of applications, increasing the scope of AI applications and the degree to which they affect our daily lives. These systems play important roles across various sectors, including healthcare, education, entertainment and finance. AI systems, or more specifically machine learning (ML), software can achieve high precision in prediction where humans are unable to detect the patterns to solve a given problem.

Some systems are unfairly biased towards certain demographics. Methods that can help gain insight into such opaque decision processes and ensure systems make fair decisions are extensive research. Learning from machine learning could produce knowledge of the target domains, and ensuring systems are unbiased and fair the decision process is a requirement for the ethical application of machine learning, and AI systems in finance.

# 1.1. Problem statement

A trade-off in the deployment of high-performance ML systems is that they are often so opaque that the exact decision is made by the system, making it very difficult for users to understand why a specific result was obtained. Many problems arise from this inability to examine the decision process. Firstly, it is important to validate that the outcome of the process is defensible, and that it follows a compelling path of thought.

One frequently used for machine learning is in addressing the risk of fraud. A large case is the Dutch System Risk Indication (SRRI), which is linked to a large amount of demographic data and used to identify potentially fraudulent individuals. The requirements for the process to be fairly considered, combined with the fact that being marked as a potential fraudster had great impact on the industry, caused the system to be prohibited in 2020, after having been in use for many years (van Dijk et al., 2020).

Insurers and banks use ML to estimate the risk of fraud with loan applicants, personal loans as well as mortgages. Organizations applying these systems want to ensure that the systems do not suffer from unintended bias, helping them adhere to anti-discrimination laws, these organisations must refrain from making automated decisions based on predictions from the ML system.

This guideline, as well as others, from the Ethical Framework for Insurers, a guaranteed review setup by the Department of Insurers based on the High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence addressing the European Commission (Verband van Verzekeraars, 2021).

# 1.2. Hypotheses

In this article, we present an extensive evaluation and case study with a large Dutch insurer, hereafter referred to as "the organisation". The organisation offers life and non-life insurance for various products and services in different areas; it uses ML for fraud detection, risk pricing and underwriting. Our goal is to further improve its use of AI, in order to address the obligations set out by regulators. In order to achieve this, we would like to have a set of tools that help data scientists explain the models they use, to validate the decision process, and investigate and demonstrate the fairness of a given model. Furthermore, the organisation requires methods to explain the individual outcomes and for models with black box structures to explain the decision making process. Also, the organisation requires us to evaluate the interpretability of models using ML, as it is crucial for employees and managers to understand the predictions made by ML.

In this paper, we focus on the development of explainable AI systems and instructions proposed in this research helps organisations implement interpretable and unbiased ML, we set the following hypotheses:

- 1. Explainable AI systems help organisations implement interpretable and unbiased ML.
- 2. Interpretable ML models help data scientists explain the models they use, validate the decision process, and investigate and demonstrate the fairness of a given model.
