## [Foreword](#contents.html#rfm05)

Bitcoin. Blockchain. Crypto. These days, you can’t go a minute without hearing about the blockchain revolution. Bitcoin has gone from being obscure and unknown to mocked, hated, revered and recognised globally. Blockchain has been lauded as a solution to all of the world’s problems and mocked as nothing but a glorified Excel spreadsheet, even vilified as an elaborate scam. Of course, none of that answers the underlying question: What is blockchain technology and why is everyone so excited about it?

From early adopters to those who are just now becoming curious, what unites many in turning to blockchain technology is growing concern over the power that global corporations wield, thereby increasing infringements on freedoms and privacy, political instability and inequality. Blockchain technology *could* impact every facet of our lives and offer solutions for many of these growing issues that are a result of the existing power structures in our world, from money to software to governments.

The operative word here is ‘could’. There is much work to be done.

While bitcoin may have started as a monetary revolution, ten years of the bitcoin experiment have highlighted that this is in fact an evolution, and likely a slow one with fits and starts.

Revolutions are inconvenient, messy and disruptive to the status quo, a default which we are unfortunately biased towards. Change is uncomfortable and, for most of us, terribly inconvenient. Revolutions have historically happened as an absolute necessity, when society has exhausted all other options and tensions have reached breaking point. Some may argue our society is reaching this point; others may feel there is still a long way to go. But what feels like a revolution to those of us on the front line likely looks like an evolution to those observing from afar.

Blockchain technology, when coupled with other technology innovations, is the perfect evolutionary response to the social, political and economic pressures we face in today’s world. It is no surprise that bitcoin was born out of the 2008 financial crisis – it was a direct response to the threat of central banking and quantitative easing. Our world will continue to evolve and adapt as we face new threats. Existing systems are not sophisticated enough to solve new problems. We are still in the early days of building these new systems, and much of the infrastructure needed to build new types of platforms, products and services has not yet arrived.

More importantly, the ideology and mental framing needed to deploy blockchain in meaningful ways has not yet been popularised and socialised in a way that most people can understand. The problems introduced by the architecture of our existing world are only now beginning to surface as we begin to understand how a series of choices made over the last twenty years as the internet was developing have locked us into a path that is fundamentally unsustainable and deeply unsettling to the future of humanity.

Many of the conversations you’ll hear about blockchain, and much of what’s detailed in this book, are actually not about technology. Rather, meaningful analysis of blockchain is about imagining what could be possible in the future with this new technology, and imagining new ways of thinking and organising and interacting as human beings – and also machines (perhaps our new robot overlords?). Blockchain has the potential to redefine the relationship between technology and the individual, and to create a new set of social, political and economic tools that provide privacy, sovereignty and user choice while increasing connectivity, efficiency and access. This is a worthwhile, but challenging, effort, and it cannot be implemented by technology or one company alone.

While the internet has ushered in a new wave of economic activity and entirely new forms of human interaction and connectivity, it has also highlighted the dangers of allowing a narrow set of players to capture vast swathes of a new digital frontier. Part of the impetus driving blockchain technology is a desire to change the power structure within this new digital world, and to minimise the ability of any one entity – whether a company or a government – to exercise control over a medium so critical to the growth and evolution of our world.

The gatekeepers of the world we live in today have every incentive to keep us out. We are in the midst of a renaissance for young people, for people who have historically been excluded, for people who believe in something totally different. Our gatekeepers recognise that they have to let this new thing happen because it’s part of the natural evolution of our world, but they will fight it because it threatens their status, and they will attempt to co-opt it.

So this is the challenge we face: how do we bring this change into being without watering down the ideals and principles we began with and our belief in what the future could look like? What will come in the future shouldn’t look like the past. I worry that much of what’s being done with ‘blockchain’ today is simply a facade built on top of the existing architecture we already have – minimal innovation and no actual change to the status quo. We have to break down barriers and silos – technical, social, political and economic barriers – to make the future we want possible. We must use science, mathematics, design and reason to build bridges, to build stories, to build transitions from our current world to the future we believe should exist.

As we move from the present into the future, each of us is responsible for making a series of choices about what we accept and reject, what we deem possible and impossible. Hopefully the ideas that have inspired the last decade of innovation, starting with bitcoin and evolving beyond into blockchain, will catalyse a new set of choices we have never had before, and bring about a world where we, as humans, can exercise choice, consent and agency.

I began working with David Shrier, the author of this book, when I was in graduate school at MIT. David was at the Media Lab, I was at the Sloan School, and as I was navigating my personal journey of asking ‘why’, I would regularly show up at Dave’s office and demand we teach students about cryptocurrencies, the history of money, the principles of bitcoin and what was happening around the world with entrepreneurs who were using this technology to attempt to solve a wide range of problems and to create new opportunities.

This book, and many of the efforts Dave, myself and others have collaborated on over the years have been an attempt to make this learning cross-disciplinary, but also to move out of the *how*, the practical technical conversation, into the *why,* which is what inspires hearts and minds.

So *why* blockchain technology?

Read on and find out . . .

Co-creator, Oxford Blockchain Strategy Programme

## [Introduction](#contents.html#rfm06)

‘Everything is possible until proven impossible, and then you just need to become more creative’

– *Scott Parazynski, physician, entrepreneur and veteran astronaut*

 

 

A revolution is under way in every continent on the planet save Antarctica. It isn’t led by gun-toting insurrectionists looking to profit from poppy fields. It isn’t governed by a handful of Silicon Valley elites who think that asset value creation is impossible outside a fifteen-minute drive of Sand Hill Road. It isn’t controlled by dusty bureaucrats in the back offices of governments. It isn’t a shiny new widget from a global consumer packaged goods company that will shed pounds from your hips in mere minutes. All of these parties would like to control, own, influence or use this revolutionary force, yet not one has exclusively taken the driver’s seat in this nitrous-powered rally car barrelling down the midnight highway of innovation.

I write, of course, of blockchain. Born from an obscure body of government-funded research on game theory called Byzantine consensus; championed by drug dealers and other criminals seeking to launder ill-gotten gains, and by libertarians disillusioned with the broken promises of the establishment; accelerated by entrepreneurs seeking to improve financial access for the poor; funded by giant corporate interests attracted to the potential for billions of dollars of cost savings; blockchain heralds a new era of financial inclusion, legal inclusion for the dispossessed, lower prices for consumers and higher anxiety for enforcement professionals all over the world.

The hype cycle is in full swing. New heights of absurdity were ascended when, at a reception at Davos, I spotted a known con artist passionately embracing a prominent blockchain CEO. One group of ambitious executives have even created a cryptocurrency backed by bananas. Parodists created a successful token system (well, one with a non-zero exchangeable value) fancifully affiliated with garlic. Major governments and NGOs are aggressively pursuing efforts to eliminate corruption, waste and fraud through blockchain-based financial and record-keeping systems.

In this volume, we will begin with the history of blockchain, with its possibly apocryphal and likely pseudonymous progenitor Satoshi Nakamoto. Blockchain was created in response to the economic and social crisis of the 2008 financial crash and its aftermath. The loss of trust in the conventional financial system created a receptive audience to the first popular blockchain, Bitcoin. We will see how other types of blockchain subsequently evolved, with fanciful names such as Ethereum and Ripple. We will gain an understanding of the core technologies driving blockchain, such as cryptographic hashes and network theory, all from the perspective of a general audience seeking to understand enough about how blockchain works to appreciate the possibilities it enables.

We will explore the philosophical divide between ideologues and technocrats within the blockchainerati, who argue about whether or not money should be controlled by governments, corporations, or a collective of anonymous individuals, and the moral dimensions inherent to this new technology. The efforts by non-profits and governments to apply blockchain at large scale to societal causes will also be discussed, from property rights in countries with a weak rule of law to ambitious initiatives to provide a digital identity for every person on the planet. A new version of the arms race is emerging with asymmetric power centres; Iceland is as powerful a player on this new arena of realpolitik as Russia.

A number of challenges are present in the blockchain world, with more on the horizon. In the immediate term, critical issues include scaling (it doesn’t scale very well), computational power and speed (it uses a great deal of energy and computer cycles, and is relatively slow), and its governance by a fractious group of individuals. Longer term, a number of questions arise from the fact that the integrity of bitcoin is controlled by half a dozen companies primarily located in China, which runs counter to the stated ethos of bitcoin as a currency that isn’t subject to a small number of control points. More ‘corporate’ blockchains like Ripple could potentially be subject to governmental pressure for the introduction of so-called ‘back doors’ that enable snooping on the contents of data flowing over these corporation-managed networks. Emergent technologies such as quantum computing hold the potential to break the core cryptography on which blockchain is built. Blockchain, in combination with AI, creates potential for tremendous employment disruption in financial services – perhaps as much as 30 to 50 per cent of current employees at banks could be surplus to requirements. In short, the advent of blockchain technologies brings with it profound ethical and societal implications. The question of ‘responsible innovation’ arises; when we throw large numbers of people out of work by adopting a new technology, should we adopt it?

A subtle issue also arises when you create an unchangeable record of data such as blockchain. What if the data isn’t good quality? What if it’s corrupted before it enters the ledger? What if someone deliberately injects bad data into a good record-keeping system? Now you have an indelible error. If, for example, this data system is managing the identity of the citizens of a country, or the record of who owns what property – the tangible impact of this intangible data corruption quickly becomes apparent.

Regulators are grappling with how to manage this radical movement – and movement it most certainly is, with its advocates espousing an ideological passion that goes beyond mere techno-fetishism. Some countries, such as South Korea, have moved to clamp down on many interesting applications. Others, like Switzerland and Singapore, have opened their doors and rolled out the welcome mat. The US federal government has offered a more measured response. Individual US states such as Wyoming and Vermont have offered friendlier regimens, contrasting with the innovation-destroying BitLicense applied in America’s financial capital, New York. Europe has gladly stepped into the breach thus opened by New York’s reluctance, with the EU parliament proposing new blockchain regulation to foster innovation and launching the EU Blockchain Observatory and Forum to accelerate experimentation.

A curious trend is emerging. The way we typically fund innovative new companies, once tightly concentrated in a handful of investment firms mostly found on a few miles of road in northern California, has now been democratised to an extent not seen since the coffee houses of Amsterdam during the Age of Exploration. Termed ‘initial coin offerings’ (ICOs), new financing vehicles have sprung forth, anchored in the principles of crowd capital and syndicated through Telegram and Instagram. In some countries these fundraising exercises have been labelled ‘securities offerings’, with the rules, reporting and investor protections associated with a more traditional, regulated financial function such as a stock or bond offering. Other countries, perhaps seeing a new economic value opportunity that could generate tax without adverse environmental or social impacts to the host country, choose a looser regulatory approach. Some countries explicity created a new asset class of ‘securities tokens’. We’ll discuss some of the risks this creates later.

All the while, entrepreneurs are building ambitious new platforms that seek to change the way we work, live and play – on this planet and beyond (yes, there are several ‘space coins’ under development). Unlikely places like San Juan, Puerto Rico; Tallinn, Estonia; and Port Moresby, Papua New Guinea host digitally connected, wired-up pioneers attempting to reshape the way people relate to the businesses and societies around them.

Join us now for this safari into the wild jungles of distributed ledgers . . .

## [The ascent of bitcoin](#contents.html#rchapter01)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ Blockchain is a special kind of database, initially created to support a new digital currency called bitcoin

◆ Blockchain grew out of a need to create digital trust where we had lost trust in institutions

◆ The system works because of clever mathematics. It achieves common agreement, or ‘consensus’, about what is true among parties who don’t have to trust each other

◆ The system can also embed economic incentives to run a large network on many computers owned by many people

We stand on a dusty battlefield, with an army surrounding a besieged city. Blood and dirt smudge the faces of the soldiers. Several generals, rivals in the complex court politics of the Byzantine Empire, now command the field in an uneasy coalition. If they all attack together, they will take the city. If they all retreat in good order, the troops will live to fight another day. If some generals attack and some flee, if one or two of the generals can be suborned through bribery, the entire army will be destroyed. To complicate matters, they can’t see each other with signal flags and are coordinating their attack through runners going from tent to tent.

What if someone intercepts the message? What if a general has taken money from the enemy to endanger his compatriots by sending misleading communications?

How can we coordinate action in such a way that a group of generals who don’t trust each other attack or retreat in unison?

This description of a fictional battle in the days of the ancient Byzantine Empire formed the basis of a real mathematical problem: that of establishing trust in a trustless environment. SRI International researchers Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease posed this thought experiment while working with the United States National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). Their 1982 paper [¹](#bm05.html#en1) on this arcane area of game theory later found notoriety by providing one of the building blocks of blockchain. In the classic formulation of the Byzantine Generals Problem, as long as two-thirds of the system participants are loyal, and agree to the veracity of information or a decision, it will function as intended. A computer network that is built using this method can be described as ‘Byzantine fault tolerant’, meaning it will work correctly while having redundancies built in for failure under the circumstances of one-third of the computers showing a problem, with two-thirds showing no issues (or vice versa). If you have a computer built into a spaceship, and it shows that the oxygen system is failing or the engines won’t work, you can’t simply have an array of repair technicians evaluate to see if the error is real or of the computer has a glitch. You need multiple computers, for redundancy, and a mechanism, such as Byzantine fault tolerance, to ascertain if the problem is real or if the computer is faulty. NASA designed their space shuttle computers to be Byzantine fault tolerant.

Move forward in time to 2008, when the overheated real estate bubble burst and rocked the world’s financial systems to their very foundations. In this heavy mix of excess and excessive speculation there were many ‘haves’ – people who profited from the crunch – but an even greater number of ‘have-nots’. People were becoming increasingly frustrated with the concentration of wealth and corruption they saw around them. The bursting bubble only served to confirm their worst fears about ‘The Man’ controlling their lives and taking their money.

Enter Satoshi Nakamoto. Who he is, no one has been able to say for sure. Several have attempted to claim his mantle. Some blockchain-savvy friends of mine think it is a pseudonym for a cabal of like-minded software developers. For our purposes, let us think of him as an unknown, iconoclastic genius.

In October 2008, he published a paper entitled ‘Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System’.[²](#bm05.html#en2) In it he described what have become familiar to many as the foundational elements of blockchain. Taking the Byzantine consensus of nearly three decades prior, and combining elements from other projects, he proposed a new ‘trustless’ networked currency. The key elements of this were:

◆ a **distributed ledger** to keep track of a new digital currency, meaning a database with many copies that all update themselves automatically

◆ **Byzantine consensus** to govern changes to the ledger

◆ a **cryptographically secure** currency

◆ ‘**mining**’ incentives to encourage people to burn the computer cycles necessary to manage the currency

◆ ‘**blocks**’ of transactions describing when people trade the currency, strung together in what’s called a ‘Merkle Tree’ to form an immutable record, a ‘blockchain’.

The next section takes a very simplified approach to describing the mechanics of how this bitcoin blockchain works. It’s not terribly technical, but it still might be a challenge if you are more interested in the business or societal applications of the technology. In simple terms, I’m going to describe for you how we create digital trust even among people who don’t necessarily trust each other, in place of having a third party serve as a trusted intermediary.

I’d recommend that you try to engage with the material, even if you find it challenging (the glossary on [page 155](#bm03.html#page_155) may be helpful). However, if you find it too tough going, feel free to skip ahead to [Chapter 2](#chapter02.html) where we get into the hoopla of hype that has emerged as more and more people have got excited about blockchain.

### **DISTRIBUTED LEDGER**

One of the basic problems with a network or data system that puts all of its eggs in a single basket, so to speak, is that it is susceptible to hacking or breach. You need to access just one system to change the amount of money in a bank account or the record of someone’s criminal history – or even their identity.

Another issue is one of central control. If all the records of who owns land are kept in one place by the government, you’ve got an issue if you don’t trust the government. Maybe you have good reason – maybe the government in question is corrupt, stealing the assets for itself.

Or maybe it’s corruptible; its civil servants can be bribed. During the era that gave rise to bitcoin, a number of people felt that way about the United States government and the governments of several other G7 countries, but the concept of the need for data security transcends the politicised birth of blockchain technology.

What if we had a record book, a ledger, that had many identical copies?

What if these copies talked to each other and automatically updated themselves, creating resilience in the network so that if one copy was corrupted deliberately, by someone trying to commit fraud or embezzle money, the other copies would fix the problem?

Instead of a centralised ledger with a single point of failure, you could have ‘distributed ledgers’ that would replace trust in a single entity (like a government) with trust in technology, trust in a peer-to-peer network that would intrinsically keep the system honest.

It is important to note that all blockchains are distributed ledgers, but not all distributed ledger technologies (DLTs) are blockchains.

The terminology can get confusing fast, not least because different people use different words to describe the same thing. For example, Vitalik Buterin, progenitor of the popular smart-contract protocol Ethereum, uses the word decentralised when everyone else and sixty years of network theory say distributed. We’ll talk about Ethereum a little later, but for the sake of sanity and consistency, we will call the general class that blockchain falls into ‘distributed ledgers’ and move on.

### **BYZANTINE CONSENSUS / BYZANTINE FAULT TOLERANCE**

Byzantine fault tolerance proposes a way to better manage a network of decision makers (or in this case, a network of information storage devices). Each storage device is a ‘node’. To change the information stored in the network, you need to convince 51 per cent of the nodes that the information is valid (in this instance, varying the threshold versus the two-thirds of the original paper). Now let’s distribute this across 10,000 nodes. We’ve created a system whereby you would need to corrupt 5,001 nodes in a short amount of time in order to put faulty information into the network. The method to do this is computationally expensive, compared to, for example, the computational cost of running a credit card network or an electronic securities exchange, but what you sacrifice in terms of processing power you gain in terms of confidence in the network’s results. You create digital *trust*, in other words. Only through such rigorous mathematics and computation could a group as suspicious of authority as the earliest adopters of bitcoin freely accept the results of transactions with total strangers they connected with on the internet. Remember, this was a loose confederation of anti-authoritarian idealists, overly curious academics and hobbyists, and (many say) money-laundering criminals. While there are a number of efforts to optimise consensus, either making it more resistant to subversion or more efficient and scalable, the basic concepts were established in that 1982 SRI paper.

### **BLOCKS AND MERKLE TREES**

Blockchain needs to solve a problem of *immutability*. What does that mean?

Well, part of how you build trust into a trustless system is by letting everyone see a record of every transaction, a record that can’t be altered. This gives you confidence that your data has integrity, which matters for all kinds of reasons like making sure someone has actually transferred you some money and that if I give you a digital dollar, I don’t still have the ability to spend that dollar somewhere else (the ‘double spend’ problem). The idea is that with a physical currency, when I hand you a bill or a coin, a physical token, then you and I both know that I don’t have that piece of currency any more and you do. In a digital world, where I could simply copy your 1s and 0s, how do we ensure that only you have that unit of currency and I do not? With conventional banking, we trust that the bank is going to do a good job of deducting a unit from my account and adding a unit to your account.

What if we don’t trust banks, like many people during and after the 2008 financial crisis? We solve this problem mathematically. We create an accounting mechanism, a strongly secured database, that tallies who has what and makes sure that we take one unit from my account and we put it into your account.

Merkle trees are an interesting mathematical method that can help establish an immutable history of transactions. With a Merkle tree, you perform a set of calculations on a number. The output of that equation is then used as the input for a second set of calculations. The output to that second set is used for a third set, and so on. What this means is that you can’t change an element of the second set without also having an effect on the third set; you can’t change the first set without changing the second and third, and so on. These different equations are linked in a tree-like structure. If you take a look at the following diagram and start from the bottom, working your way upward, you can see how individual data elements are combined to form this tree.

![Illustration](images/f0022-01.png)

You can’t chop down the middle of the tree without having the top of the tree fall down as well. Thus, the entire sequence of equations has integrity, because of how they are linked to each other.

What if we grouped a set of unrelated transactions together? Bill gives money to Susanna, Mari gives money to Fred, Davis gives money to Mohammad. None of these people know each other. But the record of these different financial transactions is bundled up mathematically into a single unit, or a ‘block’. When you perform a cryptographic calculation on the block, you’ve now linked the transactions so that you can’t change one without changing all of them, because they are all part of the output of that equation.

![Illustration](images/f0023-01.png)

Let’s say Susanna then sends some of her money to Archie, Fred sends his money to Quentin, and so on, forming a new block.

![Illustration](images/f0024-01.png)

You use the output of the equation from block 1 as part of the mathematics that you use to calculate block 2.

![Illustration](images/f0024-02.png)

By using the output of one block as one of the inputs to the next block, you end up linking several of these blocks together in a Merkle tree or chain, and you’ve got a ‘blockchain’. This blockchain makes it very hard to insert a fake record, because you’d have to change all of the different calculations in order to modify one single transaction.

This concept is sometimes referred to as ‘proof of work’. Originating in a paper written in 1999 by Markus Jakobsson and Ari Juels,[³](#bm05.html#en3) proof of work (POW) is intended to defeat attacks such as denial of service (DDOS). With a DDOS attack, a cybercriminal will enlist an army of machines to spam a web server rapidly and repeatedly with requests, until the system gets overloaded and fails. You can think of it like a horde of zombies attacking a castle under the direction of a mad scientist. They aren’t very sophisticated, but if enough of them punch the castle wall, it will fracture. A defence against DDOS is to force each attacking machine to do a medium-complicated calculation, making it effectively take too long and too many computers to overwhelm the network. Extending our analogy, it would be like forcing each zombie to complete a few complicated dance steps before it can strike its fist. It ends up making the attack unworkable, and the castle (your network and the data inside) is safe.

You need proof of work along with Merkle trees, or a cybercriminal could just recompute the entire tree and inject fraudulent entries. Proof of work isn’t the only solution, of course. Estonia, for example, which uses a private chain versus the public, permissionless chain of bitcoin, has figured out how to show integrity in the system without proof of work: they publish the top hash in their Merkle tree in the *New York Times* once a month.

But generally, proof of work has proven popular, particularly without the agency of trusted bodies like a government or a media outlet of repute. It operates in synchronisation with Byzantine fault tolerance to create a resilient, redundant network that has a very high standard of data assurance. Digital trust, in other words. It should be mentioned that there are a number of other techniques used to establish digital trust, like proof of stake and proof of authority and such, but for this book I’ll use proof of work as an illustrative mechanism because it underpins one of the largest blockchains, the bitcoin blockchain.

I’m using money in this example to make the concepts understandable, but really money is just data, a series of 1s and 0s. These kinds of blockchains can be formed using any kind of data, not just financial data: for example, keeping a record of clinical trial results, tracking the origins of a particular kind of food that ends up in your kitchen, or mapping the records of who owns the land that your house sits on.

### **MINING**

Mining is a fascinating concept within the world of blockchain. Let’s take our old friends Byzantine consensus, Merkle trees and blocks, and add an element of competition to the mix. Because there are several mathematical routes that can be followed in order to generate a particular block, we want to find the most efficient one.

What if we offered a prize? Let’s say that the financial example I gave above involved a total of £10. What if we offered someone, say, 10 pence to calculate the block for our series of three unrelated transactions, but only to the first person to complete the task correctly. Someone who has a computer and cheap electricity might be willing to do this to earn a little extra money. We call that 10 pence the ‘mining fee’. And there is a competition among many miners in order to win the mining fee, with the calculation that is mathematically proven to be the most efficient crowned the winner of the competition.

This mining fee is what incentivises thousands of people to load software onto their computers to conduct the complex transactions necessary to make blockchain run. While blockchain is very secure and great at producing an immutable record, it’s ferociously expensive in terms of the number of computer cycles it consumes. What if you didn’t want to have to buy a bunch of computers or server time on Amazon Web Services (AWS)? By giving many people a small piece of the action in the form of a reward, you now have them using their computer capacities in a highly distributed manner to solve your mathematical puzzle.

Like every other aspect of blockchain, the ideas behind how you enlist a network of miners to do the calculations on a distributed network of computers are not new. There were a number of previous examples of this concept of distributed mathematical computation. Two widely known crowd calculation efforts are [SETI@home](mailto:SETI@home) and [Folding@Home](mailto:Folding@Home).

In the case of [SETI@home](mailto:SETI@home), astronomers were trying to decipher radio signals gathered by sensors to see if there were patterns indicative of alien life (SETI stands for the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence). Like many in academia, they were chronically underfunded relative to the scope of their ambition. As personal computers became more popular in the 1990s, researchers David Gedye, Woody Sullivan, Dan Werthimer and David Anderson got the idea that if you gave people a little bit of code on their computers, they would run it in the background when the computers were running but ‘idle’, and do a little piece of this complex analysis, strictly because they thought it was cool. Similarly, [Folding@Home](mailto:Folding@Home) wanted to perform a large number of molecular biology calculations about how proteins fold, in order to investigate new life-saving drugs. They, too, didn’t have the resources to purchase all the computing time they needed, so they crowd-sourced the calculations using distributed network computing, just like [SETI@home](mailto:SETI@home).

So now we take that distributed computation idea from [SETI@home](mailto:SETI@home), and we apply it to creating a new currency. We incentivise people to participate in the calculations not with the noble ambition of finding intelligent aliens, but in order to make some money, literally and figuratively. Well, that, and to build an alternative to the conventional financial system, since the first users of blockchain tended to be idealists who wanted bitcoin to become an alternative to ‘fiat currency’ like the US dollar or the euro. Just because you make some money from an idea doesn’t mean you can’t be an idealist.

With the built-in economics of mining attached to blockchain, we now have the incentives aligned for thousands of miners to participate in the network. But let’s say you are specifically interested in the financial currency application of blockchain (as opposed to another data management example). Let’s say that you don’t want an endlessly inflationary currency, where a monetary authority can simply print off more units. With bitcoin, Satoshi Nakamoto sought to make the currency valuable by making it scarce. He imposed a hard limit of 21 million bitcoin that could ever be created, embedding this limit in the very code itself. It also gets increasingly difficult to create (or ‘mine’) bitcoin the more that exists, so the supply automatically slows down its own increase as people create more of it. There is a finite supply, not unlike gold. This creates a certain logic such that in some countries, cryptocurrencies like bitcoin are regulated like commodities, such as gold, rather than like currencies.

### **CRYPTOCURRENCY**

OK, so now we’re on to a big idea. You could say that digital currency is just bits and bytes, so you can just create more. You could also say that when, for example, the US government wants more money, they just print more dollars. The dollar isn’t linked to any other commodity, it’s just worth whatever people are willing to accept it as, backed by the ‘full faith and credit of the American government’, whatever that means in the minds of the people who accept dollars. Instead of dollars, now let’s imagine a group of computer programmers created a currency that is ‘minted’ on servers. Because it’s secured using cryptography, let’s call this kind of digital money a ‘cryptocurrency’.

Recall the trust-starved times in which bitcoin was born, the widespread disillusionment with the conventional financial system and with conventional institutions more broadly, such as government and corporations. Satoshi Nakamoto wanted something that had a finite amount, like gold, rather than something that could be infinitely created, like the dollar. He built into the code of bitcoin itself a few clever elements that turn it into a digital currency with scarcity value.

For one thing, there’s a built-in limit on the total number of bitcoins that can be mined, ever. It’s about 21 million. These bitcoins are being created through the calculation of new blocks in the bitcoin blockchain. The speed with which new bitcoin is created via mining is slowing down on a curve. Theoretically, unless a group of a few hundred developers known as the ‘bitcoin core’ decide to create new computer code, and the group of people who control bitcoin servers effectively vote to accept a change in the software by uploading this new code, we will hit that limit and then bitcoin can appreciate in value due to its scarcity.

Just to make things more interesting, it’s believed that more than 10 million bitcoin have been lost forever. Perhaps you forgot your cryptographic keys to unlock your money, or you threw out the computer that had the data on it. Satoshi Nakamoto doesn’t seem to be spending his initial batch of bitcoin. So now the actual tradeable universe of bitcoin is much smaller, perhaps a total of 11 million, ever.

And unlike Beanie Babies, a collectible fad from the 1980s that bitcoin has been disparagingly compared to, bitcoin does have value beyond simply the fact that it has a certain scarcity. It can be used to reduce frictions in the financial system, like cross-border money transfers – instead of paying a 15 per cent fee to transfer money into Kenya, using a bitcoin system you might pay 1.5 per cent or even 0.15 per cent, by removing multiple layers of middlemen and antiquated money transfer technology. I will argue that there is, at least today, a non-zero value attached to bitcoin. I can’t tell you if it’s worth £20,000 per coin, or £1,000, or £100, but today it’s worth more than £0.

Other cryptocurrencies have been created following this model of scarcity. At the time of writing, the nominal market cap of all of these digital tokens is in the hundreds of billions of pounds in equivalent value. I always have to be careful about writing on the value of crypto; bitcoin was worth maybe £7 billion when I wrote my first fintech book in 2016, more than £400 billion when I was giving a talk in the autumn of 2017, and now is hovering around £150 billion when you combine bitcoin (BTC) and bitcoin cash (BCH). By the time you read this book it’s going to be different (no, I don’t know if it will be higher or lower).

I recognise that I’m also describing a structure that could be used for Ponzi schemes: people who are already in the network make money from new people coming into the network. And in fact, Italian researchers Massimo Bartoletti, Salvatore Carta, Tiziana Cimoli and Roberto Saia investigated over 1,500 blockchain token offerings in 2017, and determined that more than 10 per cent of them were true Ponzi schemes. Some in the community have expressed surprise to me that the percentage is so low, and stories abound of abuse.

As with many other new technologies, the creation of economic value has also attracted con artists who seek to profit from the excitement and confusion about what the technology is actually capable of and how to monetise it. This doesn’t invalidate the opportunity of the technology more broadly, but it does introduce a reason for caution if you plan to speculate financially on blockchain-related assets. *Caveat emptor*. Do your due diligence.

### **SECURITY**

Let’s get back to the question of trust and how we make a secure data system. At the time of its creation, the bitcoin blockchain used elliptic curve encryption. One of the fundamentals of encryption is how many characters go into making the ‘key’ that is used to ‘lock’ and ‘unlock’ – encrypt and decrypt – the data. A key that consists of a123 would be less secure than a key that consists of abc12345678 because a hacker would have to try more combinations in the longer key to crack the code. Elliptic curve encryption uses clever mathematics so that you can have smaller (shorter) encryption keys than a number of other types of public key cryptography. Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) means that anyone can encrypt a message, with the public key, but only the recipient or holder of the private key can decrypt the message. Other forms of cryptography require that you have the private key to encrypt as well as decrypt a message. Smaller keys mean fewer computer cycles to work with the cryptography, making the process faster and more efficient.

In theory it would take many decades to crack 256-bit elliptic curve cryptography using conventional computers. However, it is believed that quantum computers, once they are commercially viable, will be able to break the encryption we currently use across our financial systems (as well as in new financial instruments like bitcoin and ether) in a matter of minutes. We need to think proactively about a post-quantum world in which we have stronger encryption – and many people are doing exactly that. But for now, the bitcoin network is reasonably secure with its elliptic curve cryptography.

### **WE’RE FORKED?**

Something wonderful and terrible emerged out of the distributed governance structure of a network of thousands of nodes where each node gets a vote on adopting changes to the core software. Governance is the way that a system, in this case the bitcoin blockchain, makes decisions. If you can’t get the participants in the system to agree, some will start working with one version of the software, and some begin working with a different version of the software. And thus is born the ‘fork’, the separation of two flavours of bitcoin code, which is why we have bitcoin (BTC) and bitcoin cash (BCH). Hard forking is an unexpected consequence of the democratic decision making behind blockchain governance. It’s a bit like an accelerated version of how Christianity branched off from Judaism, and then Islam developed some centuries later using Judeo-Christian scripture as a base.

When the first blockchains were put together, there was an idealistic notion that people would vote on adopting new refinements. It’s a great concept; it puts the network into the hands of many instead of just having it in the hands of one or two.

We can only assume that the expectation was that either an idea would be adopted, or not. Instead, Darwinian forces led to an unforeseen outcome. An idea emerged of creating a new variant of a particular protocol. To describe this effect, a phrase was borrowed from multi-threaded processing: there is a ‘fork’, or a split, in the universe of the protocol. Instead of one bitcoin, now there are two bitcoins.

It’s kind of like the multiverse theory from the Marvel cinematic universe. These forks diverge from a key decision, and then begin to take on separate lives.

While on the one hand it diminishes the critical mass the particular protocol might have, on the other hand it creates the opportunity for Darwinian evolution to play out. The best idea will prove itself over time.

### **ETHEREUM AND DAPPS**

Ethereum was a new type of blockchain that was initially launched by Vitalik Buterin in 2013. Ethereum is much easier to learn how to program and arguably more flexible than bitcoin blockchain, but shares many of the same characteristics such as mining and blocks. The biggest point of difference of Ethereum was the introduction of ‘smart contracts’. The idea behind smart contracts was to make blockchain more of a distributed operating system on which you can run different programs, or applications. Instead of being optimized primarily to run as a currency, in the case of bitcoin blockchain, the Ethereum blockchain was constructed to be multipurpose.

Smart contracts are misleadingly named; they are neither smart nor contracts. A smart contract is a specific set of instructions that execute under specific circumstances. Ethereum is an exciting idea because it was designed as an operating system for running various kinds of computer programs on top of a blockchain network. These programs are known as distributed applications or DApps.

Putting these elements together gives us a distributed programming platform with good security. Now, we have migrated blockchain from a currency-specific system into something that can be used in any number of use cases to solve problems where redundancy, resilience and/or trust are critical factors, such as who owns a piece of property, or correctly recording someone’s vote in a political election.

### **HASHGRAPH, LIGHTNING AND OTHER HACKS**

With systems like the bitcoin blockchain and the Ethereum blockchain, we’ve now solved problems like creating trust in a trustless environment, immutability, and accessing large amounts of compute power. We’ve sacrificed, however, speed and scalability.

The more people use bitcoin, sending it back and forth, the larger the block sizes get and therefore the longer it takes to perform the calculation on each block. If several people want to send money at the same time, as happened in December 2017 and January 2018 during a run-up and crash of the price of bitcoin, the processing speed for each block calculation slows down dramatically. MasterCard can process around 44,000 transactions per second (TPS), PayPal about 600 TPS,[⁴](#bm05.html#en4) while on average Ethereum processes about 15 TPS[⁵](#bm05.html#en5) and bitcoin (after it was modified to be faster) averages between 7 and 9 TPS. If you get a bunch of people wanting to transact, given that there are only so many miners and mining computers, you have to wait in a queue for your block to clear. During the spike I mentioned of winter 2017–18, it took more than ten minutes, sometimes substantially longer, to clear blocks.

There are different approaches to solving the speed issue. For your core bitcoin/blockchain enthusiasts, new code was put together that lives on top of bitcoin called Lightning. Lightning overlays on bitcoin to create subsets of nodes that talk to each other to accelerate the number of TPS you can push through the system. Because it runs on a subset of the nodes instead of needing to talk to all of the nodes, it’s much faster.

Similarly, Ripple cleverly takes the basic, fully distributed network of blockchain nodes and breaks them up into smaller sub-trees for its XRP protocol. Each sub-tree then works on a subset of the calculations, dramatically improving speed.

Hedera Hashgraph takes advantage of highly distributed networks of devices to accelerate compute speed by propagating what is called ‘gossip’ (pieces of data) in clusters, instead of requiring coordinated synchronous consensus, theoretically yielding 250,000 TPS (although we have yet to see this operating at scale . . . bearing in mind that VISA or MasterCard are global businesses with hundreds of millions of transactions processed each day).

You now know enough about the basics to begin looking at blockchain’s potential applications. We’ve explained the fundamental building blocks behind blockchain, we’ve illustrated some of the issues with it and potential solutions, and we’ve laid the foundations for exploring use cases in different industries.

Before we can delve into those, however, we must speak about the hidden shoals that could scuttle the ship: the blockchain hype train.

## [The blockchain hype train](#contents.html#rchapter02)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ The adoption of new technology is often accompanied by an emphasis on marketing and storytelling, and blockchain is no exception

◆ Some of this is necessary to enlist open-source developers

◆ When the hype goes too far, it results in over-promotion that negatively impacts the credibility of the field, and possibly even violation of securities laws

There’s a patina of rock and roll surrounding the blockchain hype cycle. Industry events resemble rock concerts (Snoop Dogg headlined an industry party), and blockchain players can be found behind velvet ropes in Ibiza and Cannes, or pursuing transcendence in the sands of Burning Man.

The blockchain hype train rides on the twin rails of open-source developer initiatives and investor relations. Open-source developer initiatives are where a company recruits numerous contributors, who mostly don’t work for the company, to provide code improvements for a piece of software. The developers do this to get access to the code, so the trade-off is that they get software for free in exchange for contributing any improvements they make to the software. Investor relations are how a company maintains stability and interest in the price of its securities, through sharing information about performance and future initiatives. These two ideas come together when blockchain companies start putting the word out about what they are doing and, sometimes, raising money through selling their tokens. It has spiralled into an inescapable freight train of promotion chugging down the track.

All aboard . . .

### **OPEN-SOURCE COMMUNITIES**

Perhaps the most legitimate exemplar of where marketing meets blockchain is in the domain of open-source developer initiatives. There isn’t one monolithic standard for blockchain. While bitcoin was the first protocol, many others have since emerged. A protocol, in this context, is a set of rules around how a blockchain network exchanges information, manages transactions and handles governance (decision making). It’s the basic operating system of a given blockchain. Sometimes it has an applications programming interface (API), to make it easier for developers to work with the particular blockchain.

Open-source initiatives publish their code for all to see, and encourage a community of developers to make improvements or extensions to it. Linux, a free operating system, is an example of one widespread open-source project (UK-based Ubuntu, from Canonical, is one of the most popular flavours of Linux). The Android operating system for mobile phones, the most popular in the world, is open-source, although many refinements since are not. The open-source nature of many blockchain instances enables users to have greater trust in the system because they are able to see how blocks are formed, how security is handled, how tokens are transacted and so on.

Open-source initiatives are a clever way to get thousands or millions of developers to build on top of a particular code base, demonstrating the power of ecosystem innovation. Instead of investing significant resources in getting your own people to build a system, enlist the labour of others.

If you’re going to have a vibrant open-source community, you need to dedicate time, energy and expense to community relations. You need to inform developers about what you’re doing, have presence at trade shows, perhaps even provide developer grants to fund promising projects that show off the power of your protocol. For this reason, the Consensys Ethereal Lounge at Davos features funky club lighting, custom cocktails from a house mixologist and a living wall to accompany talking-head panels with the likes of Adam Lindemann discoursing on the tokenisation of the art market. We get Snoop Dogg headlining a Ripple party in New York City, and Irfan Aulia, an Indonesian musician, joining with his government to promote a blockchain project to help manage digital music royalties. Blockchain is truly rock and roll in all senses of the phrase.

Over-amplified promotion is hardly original thinking in the tech sector. Former Microsoft games executive Alex St. John, who helped create the Xbox, ordered a ‘$2 million alien spaceship to be built as part of a gigantic game promotion’[⁶](#bm05.html#en6) according to Dean Takahashi, and would host lavish, extraordinarily creative parties. If you want to get an idea of what I’m talking about, feel free to look up the launch promotion that accompanied id Software’s breakthrough title *DOOM*, but please not from a work computer!

There is a storied history behind over-the-top marketing for tech, and the open-source efforts of the blockchain builders are no exception. When this activity carries over into regulated financial services, however, we run into issues . . .

### **INVESTOR RELATIONS**

A number of blockchain token issuers have gone to great lengths to try to sound like they’re not issuing securities like conventional corporate. ‘We’re a foundation,’ DFINITY insists,[⁷](#bm05.html#en7) ignoring the substantial number of tokens thought to be held by founder Dominic Williams. Former CFTC regulator Gary Gensler cautions against ‘hasty regulatory pronouncements’ and tries to normalise bitcoin by claiming it’s just as arbitrary in value as gold.[⁸](#bm05.html#en8) Instead of ‘investor relations’, token issuers talk about ‘community relations’ and ‘community initiatives’, but what they are doing is engaging in public dialog with people who have put money into a particular cryptocurrency or token, and those people expect the value of the token to go up. They are quite vocal when the value of the token goes down.

‘HODL,’ they shout, a typo that turned into a meme to represent a buy-and-hold ethos. Statistically, given the volatility of the top ten tokens, they can’t all be HODLing. Speculation is rampant. Indeed, one of my former students paid for grad school and a down payment on a house by speculating on bitcoin.

Regulators who still work for a government, versus regulators who used to work for government but now work on behalf of private interests,[⁹](#bm05.html#en9) have by and large taken the position that token issuances are securities. The United States, most notably, has taken this position, but other jurisdictions have also gone along a similar path.

There are notable exceptions. Domiciles like the United Kingdom have put forth specific regulation that identifies ‘securities tokens’ versus ‘utility tokens’ versus ‘exchange tokens’, with different scrutiny and reporting requirements for each.

The activity of ‘community affairs’ is functionally equivalent to investor relations. The company issuing the token wants to regulate demand so that they can continue to raise money to fund operations and the founders’ personal spending. They engage with a large number of institutions and individuals who purchase, sell and, in some cases, buy derivatives on, digital tokens. Their engagement with their communities includes the broadcasting of company endorsements, and breathlessly touting this software release or that government initiative using the token. Rejoice! HODL!

In most circles these types of activities would be deemed investor relations, and thus the nature of information that is communicated would be subject to regulation and oversight by securities agencies. Regulators are less concerned with billionaires throwing play money at cryptocurrencies, and more concerned about the working poor leveraging their credit cards to buy a volatile security that is manipulated and then crashes, creating a cycle of debt for an already strained household.

Techniques for promoting crypto tokens include events; information sessions; webinars; email lists; press releases; actively managed chat groups on Telegram, WhatsApp and other platforms; news interviews; parties; digitally broadcast promotional stunts; and practically anything else an array of clever minds are able to conjure up. PT Barnum would be proud.

A number of token issuers have taken to the barricades to combat efforts to constrain these activities, insisting that the right to sell a digital asset is a fundamental liberty.

### **PASSIONS RUN HIGH**

The emotions that people attach to blockchain technologies exceed those of even the most devout Apple addicts, who are known to fetishise electronics in an unseemly manner. Blockchain protocols have achieved ideological proportions, with angry factions at times slinging words at a rate only slightly less rapid than bullets in an active conflict zone.

Internet ‘flame wars’ abound. A recent dustup about the definition of tokens saw a pedant insisting they were only units for the storage and transfer of value (a narrower definition than how regulators are using the term ‘token’), while one jokester dubbed them ‘a replacement coin in an arcade hall used to play Dig-Dug, Pacman and other games’.

Conspiracy theories are unsurprisingly rampant in a countercultural community, one that includes a number of people suspicious of big government and big institutions, seeing cryptocurrency as a means of breaking free from government control of money. MIT was accused by a ‘crypto threat analyst’ a few years ago of secretly trying to undermine bitcoin. Then, conveniently, according to this ‘threat analyst’, no one could talk on the record about this plot to destroy bitcoin because of nondisclosure agreements (NDAs that didn’t exist, but how does one prove a negative?).

The accusation was particularly unfair because when the Bitcoin Foundation imploded, the MIT Media Lab stepped in to fund core developers, helping keep the community vibrant and alive through the Digital Currency Initiative. MIT has more recently been underwriting industry advocacy efforts to keep bitcoin out of tight government regulatory action. This is not lobbying, which is geared towards specific legislative action and/or regulatory relief and which requires registration, but rather providing background information to politicians and regulators.

But why let fact stand in the way of a good story? Like the flat-earthers and the moon-landing hoaxers, certain elements of the crypto community only cling more fiercely to their paranoia when confronted with evidence.

### **SATOSH-WHO?**

Nothing has attracted more hype than the mystery of bitcoin’s inventor, known as Satoshi Nakamoto. Speculation about his true identity ranges from a group of developers who all work together to any number of ‘bitcoin core’ developers, each being put forward as the progenitor of bitcoin.

Every few months, it seems, there is a new claim to the title. Australian Craig Wright offered to prove his,[^(10)](#bm05.html#en10) garnering support from bitcoin core developer Gavin Anderson – purportedly the last person known to have communicated with Nakamoto – which Anderson has now rescinded while regretting that he became involved.[^(11)](#bm05.html#en11)

More recently a young Pakistani man claimed the title, suggesting he created bitcoin in response to the collapse of the Bank of Credit and Commerce International where his father was an executive. Conveniently, the laptop housing 980,000 bitcoin along with their private keys, worth at one time more than £15 billion, was ‘bricked’, rendered unusable thanks to a crashed hard drive, and his emails were purportedly hacked, meaning he couldn’t step forward and verify his identity that way.

It almost doesn’t matter. Bitcoin today is controlled by a core of a few hundred developers on the one hand, who manage the code base, and half a dozen mining groups or ‘pools’, who have consolidated production. These two groups can’t agree on how to upgrade bitcoin beyond its capacity and speed limitations, leading to a ‘fork’ of the code into bitcoin (BTC) and bitcoin cash (BCH). Block computation has gotten so complex that no longer can an undergraduate in a dorm room connect a low-end laptop computer and compete effectively in the mining marketplace: professional organisations with advanced computational capabilities are needed.

And the original bitcoin code base was a bit of a hack. Arguably more elegant protocols have emerged since, whether it’s the Interplanetary File System (IPFS) approach to largescale data storage or the distributed app (DApp) capabilities of Ethereum.

In the new, more complex world of blockchain that has emerged since bitcoin’s inception, Nakamoto’s identity is more of a historical curiosity than anything else.

### **RIDING THE HYPE TRAIN**

Consulting firm Gartner has posited that all new technology goes through a predictable hype cycle of rising expectations, overinflated promises, despair on discovering the disappointing truth, and then some productivity plateau when the real applications of the technology are deployed. Blockchain technology may be past its absolute peak, but still has a bit of froth surrounding its appeal given the nature of projects that continue to receive funding from well-intentioned investors.

Blockchain is both more, and less, than it is purported to be.

The discerning executive must be able to tell the difference between a meaningful business opportunity and another piece of vapourware backed by a Ponzi scheme. Make no mistake: the blockchain revolution offers ample helpings of both.

To help weed through the dross for the valuable nuggets, my colleague Meltem Demirors and I have developed a set of frameworks to facilitate rapid understanding of blockchain protocols and technologies – what we term the Oxford Blockchain Frameworks. We’ll discuss these in more detail in the next chapter.

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

In understanding blockchain’s impact on incumbent systems, and how to evaluate new blockchain technologies, my colleagues and I have generated a series of frameworks to understand strategy and regulation with respect to blockchain.

Meltem Demirors, a frequent collaborator, and co-creator of the Oxford Blockchain Strategy Programme, has crafted the Oxford Blockchain Strategy Framework.

Blockchains operate within a complex network of interconnected participants, and blockchain companies (not all of whom curate their own protocols) fit somewhere within this ecosystem.

### **DO YOU NEED A BLOCKCHAIN?**

The first set of steps we take our students through in assessing a business use case is to figure out whether the business needs a blockchain – or, indeed, any variant of distributed ledger.

There are many instances in which you don’t need a distributed ledger, where other kinds of databases will do, whether a ‘big database’ like Hadoop or a relational database like Oracle, or simply a ‘flat’ (linear stream of data) file. Blockchain systems are generally ill-suited to applications like high frequency trading (HFT), for example, which is a ‘big money’ activity for certain financial services companies. To make HFT work, you need specialised systems that operate within a tightly controlled environment and use state-of-the-art hardware and software. HFT firms engage in heated discussion about the length of fibre-optic cable connecting their servers to the trading system, because the speed of light over a distance is a material factor in relative performance between firms. In that kind of environment, where every picosecond matters, it is not practical to absorb the amount of time it takes to calculate a block and have a network of distributed nodes reach consensus. Thus, despite the value of HFT, and despite its heavy use of advanced technology, blockchain cannot realistically be applied to it.

The risk you face when engaging with an exciting new technology is the idea that to someone with a hammer everything looks like a nail. Ungainly, Rube Goldberg-esque technology architectures are being created by over-optimistic entrepreneurs, who in turn are taking advantage of easily accessible investor funds combined with insufficient judgement. Blockchains are being thrown at business use cases to which they are ill-suited.

So when is a blockchain needed? Meltem devised a set of questions to answer in order to assess, or ‘screen’, whether or not a blockchain is potentially suitable to a given business problem. The screening characteristics used in the OBSF are:

1. Automation

2. Repeatable process

3. Multiple stakeholders

4. Reconciliation

5. Value transfer

6. Immutability

#### **1) Automation**

Do you have some form of predictability or repeatability in a process that can be automated? If regular human interventions are required during the process, it’s going to be difficult to put that process onto a blockchain. Theoretically you could, but the latency would be so great it would be impractical. Imagine you had a mostly automated system that required a person to make manual adjustments before each critical decision is made. The functioning of the system would slow down to the speed of the fastest person, rather than the much faster computational speed of machines.

#### **2) Repeatable process**

Is the process something that happens all the time and/or frequently, or is an ongoing activity? Or is it a process that only occurs once? A one-time process does not justify the expense and operational complexity involved in implementing a blockchain. For example, you could make a blockchain to transfer powers from an incumbent head of state to their successor, but that’s something that typically only happens every four to eight years in most countries. The blockchain would require a great deal of investment but would mostly sit idle.

#### **3) Multiple stakeholders**

Are there multiple parties involved in the process or value chain? Blockchain is designed specifically to help multiple people reach agreement in a trustless environment, as we discussed in [Chapter 1](#chapter01.html). If you are engaged in a bilateral negotiation or activity, there are much less complicated ways to achieve your desired outcome, even if you may not necessarily trust your counterparty in the negotiation. For example, a group of buyers and sellers might be trading with each other. They are competitive, so they may have concerns about sales agreements being broken. In financial markets, this role is served by intermediaries such as exchanges and firms called ‘market makers’ who match buyers and sellers. Those intermediary roles could be replaced by a blockchain, which helps explain the significant blockchain research investments being made by the top trading firms and the major securities exchanges.

#### **4) Reconciliation**

When you have disparate data, is there one party or only a few parties involved in reconciliation? For example, if you’re doing inventory control, or taxes, or auditing music royalties, you might have the internal audit function and internal tax function of a corporation interacting with an external auditor or tax consultant. There could be more than one person involved from each group – you might even have dozens or hundreds of people in large, complex organisations – but you aren’t going to have tens of thousands of people. In some of these situations, it may not be necessary to use blockchain. Other approaches might deliver superior price and performance. On the other hand, what if you need to have greater trust in the results of the audit? You might then consider blockchain. A number of governments, for example, are considering the use of blockchain around tax and the related financial audits that accompany verifying that corporate tax has been paid correctly. In theory this can not only improve the quality of the information but substantially reduce the cost of tax collection and, for the corporation, of tax compliance.

#### **5) Value transfer**

Does the process or activity involve some kind of transfer of value? Note that there are many kinds of value, not just monetary. Information itself, such as a patient’s medical records or a digital identity profile, could comprise value.

#### **6) Immutability**

Would there be some form of benefit, or possibly even a requirement, that the record be unchangeable, immutable? Immutability, as we have seen, is a key feature of blockchain. For example, if you were buying a used car, you would want a clear and comprehensive record of its repairs to make you aware of any mechanical issues. Similarly, if you wanted to buy an expensive piece of art, you would want a clear record of its previous owners from the moment it was created to make sure you were not facilitating money laundering or buying a stolen work or a forgery.

These six criteria are intended to be MECE – mutually exclusive and comprehensively exhaustive. In combination, they provide you with a rigorous assessment of whether or not blockchain is beneficial for a particular situation or circumstance, enabling you to perform a rapid diagnostic.

### **HOW SHOULD YOU USE YOUR BLOCKCHAIN?**

Once you have determined that you need a blockchain, the questions for the OBSF then centre on where and how.

There are three basic components, or layers, of the ecosystem to consider:

◆ The protocol layer

◆ The network layer

◆ The application layer

#### **Protocol layer**

Protocols, as we discussed earlier, are the basic language of how different parts of the blockchain network talk to each other. When devising your strategy for blockchain, you will want to determine whether you can use a public blockchain, such as bitcoin or plain-vanilla Ethereum, or if you have a specific need for a private blockchain.

Within your assessment of protocol, you should examine design considerations such as speed, programmability and payment functionality.

◆ **Speed** is a function of the performance requirements of your business process. Is it acceptable if a block is calculated every ten minutes? Do you need 40,000 blocks calculated per second, operating at speeds similar to the major credit card networks like Visa and MasterCard?

◆ **Programmability** relates to the question of how you adapt your blockchain on an ongoing basis. If you are going to be repeating the same thing over and over, you can probably hardwire the system. If you need greater flexibility and adaptability, you are going to want to be able to alter your blockchain with simple instructions.

◆ **Payments** may or may not be a requirement of your blockchain. If you are creating a private blockchain to manage the transfer of patient medical record data, or you are creating a government blockchain to handle digital identity files, you might not need a payment capability (note I said ‘might’, since I can envision multiple circumstances in which you could need payment capabilities in either of these examples provided). If you are running a blockchain to enable cross-border remittances, by definition you are going to need a payment functionality. But, going back to our overall protocol design considerations, perhaps Ripple can provide that payment rail, or bitcoin. These are all decisions that need to be factored into your evaluation of the protocol layer.

#### **Network layer**

The infrastructure of your blockchain lives in the network layer, which is built around the nodes onto which your blockchain is loaded. Do you have restrictions or requirements on who runs a node? Can anyone write to your blockchain, or is it limited only to certain trusted parties? How will this blockchain be integrated with other technology, such as a corporate IT system?

Your network layer will also address issues around storage. How will old copies be archived? Are you subject to privacy regulation such as GDPR? If so, how do you address privacy with an immutable record? One approach on the GDPR front is to store sensitive private data off-chain, and only house a pointer (a piece of code that tells you where to look for the rest of the data, pointing at it) housed on the blockchain or ‘on-chain’. If someone elects to be forgotten, you wipe the off-chain data, so the pointer is now pointing to a string of nonsense characters.

#### **Application layer**

At the application layer, our software directly comes into contact with the end users. Who is actually interacting directly with your blockchain?

You also need to look at how the new blockchain will become part of your daily workflow. How is the organisation structured today? How are users behaving before it is launched? How will users need to change their behaviour to work with your blockchain, if at all?

We get into the realm of technology adoption and change management at this layer. If you do need to effect either organisational or individual user behavioural changes, what is your plan of transition to take them from the old way of behaving to the new?

### **REGULATION**

Regulation is inescapable in the realm of blockchain.

If you deal in cryptocurrencies, digital currencies, digital assets, securities tokens, utility tokens, coin offerings and digital asset exchanges, you need to be able to identify and understand the ‘rules of the road’ for a particular domicile in which you are seeking to do business, or you will face serious risk of government intervention. Regulators have now become sensitised to the nuances of blockchain.

There is the opportunity to proactively shape an ecosystem through the judicious application of regulation, perhaps with greater impact at scale. In a number of domiciles around the world, regulators are examining the regulatory shortcomings of their respective countries, and standing up pilots and policy interventions that can advance their strategic objectives.

At this point it is worth discussing the merits of principlesbased regulatory systems versus rules-based regulatory systems.

#### **Principles-based regulatory systems**

With principles-based regulation, rules are articulated in broad strokes so that their interpretation is somewhat flexible. The overall goals of the regulation are patent. How it gets applied, both by the companies and by the regulators, becomes more of a dialogue between the parties and an application of art rather than science. Principles-based regulation allows for a more flexible framework that can be adapted to different circumstances and different levels of regulatory competition.

When innovation strikes, as with blockchain, innovative companies engage in a practice known as ‘domicile shopping’ as they seek out the optimal regulatory regimen to propel growth. In blockchain, small and nimble countries like Mauritius, Bermuda, Barbados and Malta have enjoyed outsized rewards by showing regulatory flexibility, both in adapting existing regulations to new technology and in promoting new pro-innovation regulation. Principles-based regulation also requires sophisticated regulators and compliance professionals at companies to navigate this interpretive path.

#### **Rules-based regulatory systems**

Rules-based regulation sets out remarkably clear activities that must take place in order to achieve compliance. The individual rules are apparent, but creating a generalisable framework that can apply broadly and that ties to regulatory goals is a more arduous task. How can regulators stitch together a patchwork quilt of highly specific rules that ensure outcomes like greater financial inclusion or financial stability? Rules-based regulatory systems are easier to understand, insofar as a market participant follows a checklist to make sure he or she is in compliance, but very slow and prone to inertia when faced with innovation. Blockchain is still in an evolutionary phase, and its various derivations and the business ecosystems surrounding it are changing rapidly. Rules-based systems are ill-suited to engage with blockchain.[^(12)](#bm05.html#en12)

#### **Regulatory primitives**

Our first consideration is to look at regulatory primitives, the ideas that serve as the underpinnings of effective regulation in a variety of domiciles.

If you are an innovator faced with regulation, you serve yourself well to decode the primary objectives of the regulator and work out how to showcase your alignment with the desired regulatory outcomes. If you are a regulator, you should more readily identify which key objectives you are achieving with your regulatory intervention. A proactive and strategic approach by a regulator incorporates clear planning about outcomes. An effective regulator might say, for example, ‘We want to have 80 per cent of our population provisioned with a digital identity by 2025, up from 20 per cent today, and will use blockchain as an enabling technology to reduce cost and improve data sharing across institutions.’ This might then lead to a specific intervention such as the government establishing a set of standards around blockchain-based digital identity that private identity providers could seek to comply with, to serve the market need.

The elements of a framework approach are:

1. Focus on outcomes

2. Protect all stakeholders

3. Foster trust

4. Balance competition

5. Promote innovation

##### *1) Focus on outcomes*

Can you identify the desired outcomes of the given piece of regulation? Are they readily measurable? How can you align the specific behaviour of a market participant with the target outcome of the regulation?

##### *2) Protect all stakeholders*

All parties, not just the primary market participants, need to be protected. This encompasses consumer protection as well as providing support for enterprise.

##### *3) Foster trust*

Trust and transparency must be encouraged through regulation in order to enable stability. When consumers have greater trust it leads to a robust and healthy financial system. Trust in institutions leads to job creation, capital investment and foreign direct investment.

##### *4) Balance competition*

Fairness should be a regulatory objective; regulations should not favour one group over another. Balancing competition is a delicate and nuanced act, because there is significant risk of unintended consequences, but without balance a typical market or system will devolve into oligopoly, duopoly or monopoly. Without specific regulatory intervention to support new entrants, markets tend to aggregate the same way water will pool in a depression. Many competitive parties should be supported, as opposed to favouring a single group or a single class of competitors.

##### *5) Promote innovation*

New ideas must be given space to evolve and grow, before being subjected to significant regulatory oversight. In some domiciles regulators deliberately turn a blind eye to experimentation as long as it does not egregiously violate key regulations, taking the stance that they want to allow innovation to occur around the edges, and only intervene when innovation grows to a certain scale. In other domiciles regulators have created specific programmes to encourage innovation, providing clear boundaries in which experimentation may occur with ‘safe harbour letters’. A safe harbour letter is written by a regulator and defines a set of permissible activities a company may engage in. The letter says the regulator won’t pursue enforcement action against the company if it stays within these defined rules. They are often used in areas where companies want to innovate.

Regulator support of innovation can start with sandboxes or sandpits, which are defined environments in which companies can experiment and get feedback from the regulator, then move to a light-licence arrangement, and finally take on a full licence. In the United Kingdom banking sector, this progression takes the form of the FCA Sandbox, the e-money licence, and the full banking licence. These programmes represent a well-thought-out, highly structured set of stage gates for entrepreneurs.

In my policy work with more than seventy governments around the world, I have found that while some governments prioritise one dimension over others – small countries have different needs to large countries, and different regions face different imperatives – the highly diverse priorities and regulations of numerous regulators can be distilled down to one or more of these elements.

#### **Evolving regulatory models and approaches**

As we see more sophisticated regulatory approaches emerging from a number of domiciles, it’s worth being aware of several common policy interventions that ease the burden of regulatory compliance for innovators. These include, but are not limited to:

◆ Standards and coordination

◆ Tiered licensing

◆ Safe harbour

We’ll now explore these ideas in more detail. Why are they necessary? It is because of the difficulties of ‘harmonisation’. Harmonisation is the concept of bringing different countries’ laws into tight alignment. It is important when you have issues that span national boundaries, such as how cross-border fund flows are handled for investment securities in new instruments like blockchain tokens. However, it is exceedingly difficult for a variety of reasons ranging from the legal heritage of the particular domicile to the social and political context within each country; the problems grow exponentially as one moves from bilateral agreements to multi-country harmonisation. Instead, a selection of less tightly aligned, but fast and effective techniques, have evolved to move countries forward in a coherent fashion.[^(13)](#bm05.html#en13)

##### *Standards and coordination*

Organisations such as the OECD have been working to establish standards that can help regulators and policy makers in multiple countries navigate the use of blockchain. These broad principles, aligned across jurisdictions, allow each country to interpret the standard within its own legal and regulatory context, ensuring a level of consistency across different domiciles without the degree of line-by-line rigour full harmonisation requires.

##### *Tiered licensing*

The integration of new technologies can often present challenges for regulators and innovators alike, since the existing regulatory structures are often ill-designed to deal with new and disruptive technologies, even if a loose and principles-based regimen is in place. Countries like the United Kingdom have created tiered licensing for fintechs, for example in banking with an e-money licence, a ‘halfway’ banking licence, and a full banking licence. Each tier has a different level of regulatory scrutiny and compliance requirements, and each tier allows for a wide array of banking-related activities. A similar tiered model is being considered or implemented for blockchain securities in some countries.

##### *Safe harbour*

Safe harbour, as mentioned earlier, is a compelling tool in the policy maker’s toolkit, and is used in a variety of settings. At its heart, safe harbour consists of a regulator providing a company with the explicit statement that a certain set of business activities will not violate a given regulation or set of regulations. When regulators coordinate with one another effectively and create standards that apply across domiciles, it becomes even easier for private sector interests (companies, investors) to deploy capital into a new area. A good set of safe harbour letters can provide companies and their investors with the confidence to deploy a product or service, without the government or governments in question having to exert the same level of political and organisational effort typically required when new regulation is created. It’s also much faster. Use of safe harbour letters allows the regulator to experiment with new technologies and gain real-world experience in an emerging technology area. This then helps the regulator decide if new regulation is needed, and if so, what form it must take.

The degree to which countries are receptive to the potential of blockchain varies. Some domiciles have developed explicit regulation or economic support for blockchain initiatives, some have established specific rules, or interpreted existing rules, and some are still following a wait-and-see approach. For example, the European Union has set up a large-scale investment fund to support blockchain projects. The EU believes that blockchain can help address small business finance deficiencies. The Bank of England has made significant research efforts in the area, including assessing the viability of central bank digital currencies. Island nations such as Bermuda and Mauritius have adopted new laws that seek to make their domiciles friendlier to blockchain-related businesses, and/or make it easier to raise funds using token offerings of one type or another.

With the frameworks and tools provided in this chapter, you now have the means of assessing and understanding whatever environment you are operating in, and also the tools to design your own blockchain ecosystem, whether you are an innovator or a regulator.

Now that you are acquainted with some of the core ideas, you can begin thinking about how you can apply blockchain. In almost every domain of human endeavour, there are ways in which blockchain technologies can shape markets, enable prosperity, reduce corruption, enhance security and generate efficiencies. Blockchain also creates new and dangerous mechanisms for unintended consequences when we bring immutability, an inerasable record, together with poor data quality and total transparency. Imagine if a jilted lover put negative, false information about you into a permanent record that is then accessed by a potential employer. Or one government’s espionage service injected false voting data into another country’s election. As we advance to [Part II](#part02.html) of *Basic Blockchain*, we will examine how blockchain is creating new opportunities for industry, and where some of the risks lie.

◆ How regulators may think about disruptive technology like blockchain, and how tools like safe harbour can facilitate innovation

## [Financial services](#contents.html#rchapter04)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ Services businesses operate across data streams, and blockchain can make them more efficient

◆ Financial services businesses can unlock new capabilities and improve efficiency by using blockchain, to the benefit of commerce and of society

◆ These applications span both consumer-facing and institutional-facing domains

Companies in the financial services sector are data-intensive businesses that benefit directly from the application of distributed ledgers. There are a number of natural use cases for blockchain, and some of the earliest lessons about how to deploy blockchain and what its limitations are have come from pilot projects and deployments in financial services. The economic stakes run into the trillions of dollars annually, and the potential benefits of applying blockchain could include new heights of efficiency, cost reductions and quality improvements to the services offered by financial institutions.

### **THE SERVICES LAYER**

Services businesses employ systems that deal with information flow, tracking and management of human activity, and attribution of economic benefit. Developed countries have seen their economies shift from a reliance on manufacturing during the Industrial Revolution to a primarily service-based economy in the modern era. Financial services, health services and hospitality services can make up more than half of GDP in these countries. Emerging economies are increasingly seeing more and more economic output from the service industries.

The financial services industry can trace its antecedents back centuries. The invention of writing is traceable to 3200 BCE, when the cuneiform script emerged from the need to record financial transactions in a clay ledger.[^(14)](#bm05.html#en14) The King James Bible also refers to moneylenders and temples – more than two thousand years ago, society was already struggling with regulation and trust when it came to bankers. Modern finance was born out of cross-border trade and the Age of Enlightenment, with the London Stock Exchange playing a key role in the ‘shadow banking’ system of the Victorian Age (nonbank financial institutions operating outside of the regulated banking system).[^(15)](#bm05.html#en15) Throughout its history, financial services has been predicated on intermediaries and record keeping, with varying degrees of trust amongst parties and a desire for greater transparency that traditional financial record keeping has always struggled to satisfy. It is no wonder that blockchain technology was developed as a response to the failures of the contemporary financial system, and why the largest investments to date in blockchain technology have related to improving or usurping the current financial structures.

### **EXPERIMENTATION WITH BLOCKCHAIN**

Financial services has been at the epicentre of blockchain experimentation and, increasingly, blockchain deployments. These experiments are motivated by the complexity of financial services itself, driven by the fact that the industry is riddled with layers upon layers of intermediaries. Financial institutions are also bedevilled by antiquated processes that have been transferred from manual to digital form without re-engineering the fundamental operational function behind the process, and blockchain can be used to reshape these processes. Financial services firms are highly incentivised to implement new technology that can improve margins and competitiveness.

Broadly speaking, we can divide blockchain activity in financial services into internal or infrastructural applications of blockchain, and external- or market-facing applications. But there is another lens too, one that looks at industrial or institutional applications, and consumer applications as well.

##### **Consumer-facing applications**

Blockchain began with a public- and consumer-facing application, bitcoin. If we step back and think about the broader category of distributed digital currencies, a number of interesting implications emerge.

##### *Currency*

The provocative concept of removing currency from government control will increase trust among consumers who don’t trust the government. Indeed, when some government currencies have experienced monthly inflation rates of 100 per cent or more, one understands the appeal of finding something other than existing fiat currencies to serve as a store of value (a way to save an asset, keep it somewhere, and then access it later). The idea behind cryptocurrencies such as bitcoin is that in theory they are not subject to external pressures divorced from economic reality, such as political pressures. However, if you look at the volatility of blockchain-based currencies – cryptocurrencies – it might be difficult to pay for food or rent when your store and transfer of value fluctuates several hundred per cent over the course of a few months. Bitcoin prices change up and down with great rapidity and without a direct link to the economic productivity of a particular country or region. This lack of correlation makes it a somewhat interesting alternative asset for investors, but not a great way to pay workers who rely on stable prices to pay rent and buy food.

We will touch further on central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), a different variation on e-money, when we consider government applications of blockchain in [Chapter 10](#chapter10.html).

##### *Payments*

Other consumer applications of blockchain in financial services include payments and cross-border remittances. Particularly in emerging markets, moving money across borders could cost 10 to 15 per cent or more of the transaction value. This effectively acts as a ‘poor tax’ levied on people living in emerging economies (if you are poor, you pay more for something on a unit-for-unit basis than someone who is wealthier). With the advent of payments and remittance systems that ride on blockchain rails, we now see costs plummeting below 1 per cent.

Consumers can also now directly transfer payments to each other using a blockchain network, so-called ‘peer-to-peer’ payments, without the intermediation of a bank, transfer agent or another financial services institution. These transactions take, at most, a few minutes instead of the days that it takes the conventional financial system. Given that volume on more conventional rails such as Paypal’s subsidiary Venmo is accelerating well past 100 billion total payment transactions per year,[^(16)](#bm05.html#en16) blockchain’s lower cost and higher speed offer a compelling value proposition with direct and immediate benefit to consumers.

##### *Lending*

Peer-to-peer or distributed lending is another consumer domain that could be improved by blockchain. If we think about the function of a bank today, it accepts deposits and pays a certain interest rate for those deposits. It then puts out that money in the form of loans and charges a higher interest rate. The spread between those two points, or net interest income, is a major source of revenue for a bank. The bank gets paid because it identifies the new credit risk (a consumer to lend to), underwrites them (by calculating the risk of default by that consumer, and pricing the loan accordingly), and services the loan (making sure the consumer is paying on time, and handling situations where they get behind on payments or default). There are some other dimensions – for example, most lenders don’t carry the loans on their books; they bundle them up into securitised portfolios and sell the risk off to others such as hedge funds. But these are the basic mechanics of lending.

All of these functions could be embedded in a blockchain. Deposits and loans could be recorded on a distributed ledger. The underwriting of a credit risk could be conducted by a DApp. The loan servicing could be handled by a smart contract and a DApp. We extend this idea further in the next section.

##### *The native digital bank*

If we combine artificial intelligence, new data sources, digital currency and blockchain, we can automate all of those functions. The bank becomes unnecessary. The blockchain can have code embedded to handle pooling money, making loans and servicing loans. It can underwrite credit using an algorithm instead of a human underwriter. It can make interest payments or collect debt service from a digital wallet. The cost of blockchain-aided financial services can thus be dramatically lower to the loan recipient, and the profits for the person making the loan dramatically higher compared to traditional lending methods, by removing intermediation and inefficiency. Given that consumer financial products are the highest-profit area of a bank (versus corporate or institutional business lines), major institutions should be justifiably concerned about disruption from blockchain collectives.[^(17)](#bm05.html#en17)

##### *Insurance*

In fact, the same logic can apply to insurance. An insurance policy, let’s say a car insurance policy, is in essence a pool of risk. You and a number of other people pay your premiums every month to the insurance company on the expectation that you are not going to get into a traffic collision. The insurance company has gone to the effort of finding you, pricing your policy and collecting payments. When someone does have a problem, the insurance company deals with it, making sure the claim is valid, then paying out.

The insurance market is a space in which blockchain can offer efficiency improvements. Hypothetically, a smart contract tied to good artificial intelligence could handle 90 per cent of underwriting, claims adjudication and settlement, exponentially reducing costs for claims processing. A blockchain-based risk pool would require dramatically fewer people and lower cost compared to conventional insurance.

##### **Institutional applications**

Blockchain can enable significant improvements to speed, cost and compliance in the trading of securities, and other institutional applications.

##### *Settlement and clearing*

Settlement and clearing consist respectively of the acts of transferring funds and updating records to reflect the ownership of securities. This is a notoriously records-intensive and heavily intermediated process, with multiple stakeholders, that can be dramatically streamlined through the application of distributed ledgers and digital tokens. Juniper research believes blockchain could drive a £22 billion reduction in the annual cost of settlement.[^(18)](#bm05.html#en18) The amount of financial trade fraud potentially mitigated by blockchain, according to IBM, could exceed £11 billion per year.[^(19)](#bm05.html#en19)

##### *Derivatives*

Derivatives contracts are now being stood up and settled automatically using blockchain, creating new efficiencies to improve the speed of money within financial trading. Derivatives contracts operate under a certain set of conditions to settle or resolve themselves, which could be tied to code instead of requiring people to manually verify them. Even with certain kinds of commodities, such as futures contracts on delivery of crude oil, where physical delivery of the good is required to settle the contract, adding smart data systems tied in to derivatives could address most of the traded contracts. Specifically, you would use Radio Frequency (RFID), GPS and artificial intelligence together. It would require a change in behaviour, and a change in technical infrastructure, but given that the dollar value of open interest is equivalent to more than £163 billion, it is being seriously evaluated.

##### *Value transfer*

Blockchain has tremendous potential to improve inter-institutional value transfer. Global financial institutions trade large volumes of currency with each other. When a consumer sends money overseas, their host institution and the recipient’s destination institution need to work with each other to settle the transaction, typically involving other intermediaries as well such as a correspondent bank. They don’t do this one transaction at a time, like when John in Ireland sends £100 to Mary in South Africa. For efficiency, the transactions are gathered up at the end of the day, and the banks engage in a process known as ‘netting’ where they calculate the residual value transfer (taking the ins and the outs, and then seeing what’s left over – does HSBC owe an extra £100 million to JP Morgan at the end of the day, or is it the other way around?). This predictable, repeatable process, involving fixed steps and reconciliation of documentation, could just as easily be handled by a blockchain as it could by an army of accountants. Blockchain infrastructure provider Ripple estimates there is more than £8 trillion of global liquidity ‘trapped’ in the funds flow among institutions and governments as money crosses borders,[^(20)](#bm05.html#en20) and they have designed a high-capacity protocol (one that can process transactions faster than bitcoin) to specifically address this issue.

##### **Infrastructure applications**

Blockchain systems offer the possibility of eliminating a significant amount of labour and inefficiency inside a financial institution or financial market provider.

##### *Exchanging places*

Some securities exchanges are now beginning to experiment with putting the entire exchange onto a blockchain. While it will be some time before we can anticipate replacing the entire infrastructure of a major market like the NASDAQ or the Deutsche Bourse, smaller markets are beginning to dip their toes into the water.

##### *Compliance*

Compliance is another area where blockchain can dramatically improve what has become a crushing burden to global financial institutions. According to McKinsey, compliance costs have spiralled past US$220 billion per year and continue to grow. Blockchain-based identity systems tied back to compliance functions could eliminate a significant portion of this cost. Just on Know Your Client (KYC), BIS Research estimates £4 billion or more of annual savings through blockchain.[^(21)](#bm05.html#en21)

##### *Personal data assets*

Some of the largest financial services institutions in the world have begun revising their accounting systems to allow for the value and management of personal data assets, a new asset class that is beginning to take shape at scale. The basic concept is that, particularly owing to new regulations like GDPR and PSD2 in Europe and analogous constructs elsewhere, consumers will control their own personal data (not Mark Zuckerberg). This new architecture of who controls data leads to the question of how one manages personal data. In turn, this requires new systems within financial institutions to manage the data. Blockchain systems with appropriate cryptography can not only assist with the secure storage of sensitive personal data assets, but also the governance of that data itself to automate compliance with privacy regulation. Thomas Hardjono, Alex Pentland and I discuss the new data architecture for personal data and related systems in our book *Trusted Data* (MIT Press, 2019).

Blockchain can completely reshape a financial services industry that is built on record keeping and intermediaries. The world’s largest financial services organisation for the post-trade world, the Depository Trust and Clearing Corporation (DTCC), would become obsolete almost overnight if the major exchanges and institutional shareholders adopted a blockchain system – which no doubt explains the DTCC’s extensive investments in blockchain research (not to mention the NASDAQ Exchange’s similar efforts).

Millions of middle- and back-office jobs may be lost in the next five years owing to the adoption of blockchain, and the savvier financial services executives are preparing for the deluge. My anecdotal experience over the past few years bears this out: every time I meet an innovative, forward-thinking executive at a major financial services organisation, there is a 50 per cent chance they will leave within twelve months to start or work for a blockchain-related company.

In this chapter we covered:

◆ Financial services experiments with blockchain

◆ The institutional and infrastructural impact blockchain could have on the financial services, as well as ramifications for consumers

◆ Some of the potential effects of blockchain on the role of intermediaries

## [CHAPTER 5](#contents.html#rchapter05)

## [Health](#contents.html#rchapter05)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ The health industry is beginning a range of experiments around blockchain that can lower costs and improve care

◆ The most immediate applications relate to management of patient data and medical records for care delivery

◆ Other opportunities exist in clinical research that can reduce cost and speed up time to market for life-saving drugs

◆ Over time, there is potential for blockchain to fundamentally change how healthcare is delivered

Blockchain technologies could be applied in the health industry in a scalable manner with high-impact results, such as improved welfare for patients and reduced running costs for healthcare systems.

There are some interesting dynamics when it comes to personal data in the healthcare services. On the one hand, people want security and privacy where their medical information is concerned. Most domiciles have put in place regulation in law that governs how personal information can be shared, used and stored. The average consumer would want to know who has accessed their medical information and under what circumstances. Blockchain could provide an immutable record of who looked at a patient’s medical records, how often, and with what authorisation. Patient trust in the system can be enhanced because they have a greater insight into the governance of their data.

We have seen a number of instances of the violation of personal data privacy through improper access to personal health records, notwithstanding the global emergence in recent years of new health data privacy regulations that are accompanied by stiff penalties. First, the patient needs to know there was a violation, and second, he or she typically needs to go through a laborious grievance and investigation process. A blockchain audit trail could offer consumers greater insight as to who accessed their information and when it was made available, and with the help of artificial intelligence blockchain could provide better monitoring and alerts than current data systems.

On the other hand, there are instances where a consumer wants quick access to health information. For example, if you are having a medical emergency, you don’t want to have to wait to access your information. You want a ‘break-glass-in-case-of-emergency’ capability. Or if you want to move healthcare providers, you want to easily consolidate and migrate your personal health records. Many medical providers still use fax machines, even in the third decade of the twenty-first century, an antiquated remnant of the era of paper records that can and should be replaced by secure blockchain protocols. Even in single-payer systems, patient access to data has proven highly problematic.

### **OPENING THE MEDICAL RECORD**

In today’s health systems in many countries, accessing your own medical record is challenging because, for competitive reasons, the healthcare companies that provide electronic medical records have deliberately designed their systems to be incompatible with each other. Opening up this closed architecture through blockchain enhances the portability of the personal health record, making it easier for consumers to get better and more cost-effective healthcare because now they can more easily move their care from one provider to another, or more easily see specialists and get the treatment they need more quickly.

### **IMPROVING THE CARE EXPERIENCE**

Within the hospital or clinic, transitions in care – the moments when a patient is handed from one medical provider to another, or moves from one department to another, for example going from the emergency department to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), or from the hospital to a rehabilitative or outpatient clinic – are a major source of medical error, hospital-based injury and sometimes even death. Data slippage is often the cause: did the doctor or nurse know about an issue or allergy? Was the correct procedure performed? When you have your leg amputated, your healthy leg is marked with a black magic marker so the surgeon knows where to cut . . . Foolproof, perhaps, but is this the best we can do in the digital age? Better data systems, such as a distributed database, could help consolidate and highlight key medical information more seamlessly across the cycle of care.

### **REDUCING WASTE AND FRAUD**

Medical fraud costs more than £324 billion per year, according to the World Health Organization.[^(22)](#bm05.html#en22) As with other areas, we can remediate this through blockchain’s ability to provide an immutable record and tie together disparate pieces of data that, had they remained separate, would obscure the truth. One area of fraud is miscoding of diagnosis – a person has one condition, but in utilisation-based models, a provider might code a different condition that it costs more to treat. Blockchain would dramatically improve reconciliation of treatment and billing data. Statistical analysis of payment and treatment patterns would enable insurance companies and governments to do a better job of detecting anomalies.

### **LONG-TERM SAFETY**

The quality and safety of a medical device can be tracked by companies like Spiritus Partners, which uses blockchain to monitor the ownership, service history and any safety issues. Defective devices can be discovered and remedied in a fast, reliable and transparent fashion.

### **BETTER REFERENCE AND CARE DATA**

Even something as simple as knowing who a physician is, what their credentials are and what private insurance they accept (important particularly in the US market) is today fraught with data quality, timeliness and availability issues. I once ran a business whose product kept up-to-date records of a physician’s address and other contact information, what insurance they accepted and what credentials they had. All of that could be largely or entirely automated with the right distributed ledger.

### **PUBLIC HEALTH**

Improvements to population health can be made with blockchain. While patient privacy needs to be protected, there are also issues of public good to consider. Being able to access elements of medical data across large populations of patients is highly desirable for epidemiologists, so they can assess morbidity and mortality data, and for population health interventions. With the right kind of blockchain protocol applied, disease registries can become more dynamic and easier to manage, with better data governance.

### **MEDICAL RESEARCH**

Going beyond health services into the broader category of health, in biotech, pharmaceutical research and medical research more broadly it is not unusual for only positive results to be published by organisations. But it is the negative data that is instructive as it helps to avoid waste and accelerate drug or device development. Today, there is no effective, large-scale mechanism for compelling pharmaceutical or biotech companies to share failure data or any clinical data. With certain variations of blockchain technology, specifically multiparty computation with secret sharing, it becomes possible to facilitate the exchange of anonymised, critical data elements across patient data sets, without revealing information that would lead to competition or privacy issues. The details of this are beyond the scope of this book, but are covered in part in our book *Trusted Data* mentioned earlier, as well as the 2016 white paper ‘Blockchain and Health IT: Algorithms, Privacy, and Data’.[^(23)](#bm05.html#en23)

So, too, blockchain and tokens can deliver a new model of clinical research, in which we can obtain automatic capture of a patient’s consent to participate in the study (an expensive and time-consuming process to manage), and the incentives given to a patient to do so can be provided through digital tokens, facilitating contribution of data to the trial.

This feeds into the broader category of real-world evidence, a critical input for the long-term viability of the pharmaceuticals and biotech industry, enabling patients to consent for their data to be used as part of a study even after a drug has gone into the market. As new issues with drugs or products surface, relevant or affected patients can be allocated or moved into the research study rather than having to initiate a new trial, wait for data collection, and then analyse the results years later. The clinical data acquires a ‘time machine’ that effectively lets it press a rewind button, letting clinicians go back and ask different questions about patient data without having to re-enrol said patients or rerun a trial, through a different model of patient engagement.

We see thus that the patient journey, both in emergent (urgent/emergency) and chronic (recurring clinic/hospital visit) settings, can be tracked accurately and the patient experience enhanced by the application of blockchain. At the individual level, medical care of both the preventative and interventive varieties is predicated on the acquisition of data (e.g. patient vitals, history), the interpretation of data (diagnosis), and the recording of progress over time (vitals, labs, etc.). But on a larger scale, for a variety of reasons that span competitive and regulatory domains, critical information about population health and clinical research remains trapped in inaccessible forms.

The data-intensive applications that are starting to emerge in digital health share a common hub, the patient, but unfortunately have fragmented the storage of this data into silos. Blockchain in health can bring these scattered bits of information together cohesively for the betterment of the individual and of society. Data silos are starting to open up a bit under legislative pressure in certain countries, but meanwhile the model of care is evolving globally from a transaction-based model in many countries to more of a continuous-care engagement model.

Some bold visionaries imagine a ‘tipping point of transitioning to a person-centric model’ as Frank Ricotta of Burst IQ puts it, where the various elements of consumer health (services like those offered by DNA-testing company 23andMe and other quantified-self products) combine with conventional clinical care, propelled by precision medicine, artificial intelligence, connected devices and other technologies.

In this paradigm blockchain could be the transport and storage network that makes the disparate pieces of healthcare work together smoothly to deliver better patient outcomes. Blockchain can also provide ratings for data quality. For example, a pulse rate measured in a hospital by high-end clinical equipment will be more accurate than your Apple Watch, but that consumer device will almost always be with you, and so offers a larger set of observations for a more complete assessment of the patient. Health data volume is increasing exponentially, and blockchain married to intelligent code can deliver order to the chaos of patient information.

## [CHAPTER 6](#contents.html#rchapter06)

## [Energy and food](#contents.html#rchapter06)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ Blockchain can help you understand in detail where something came from and how it is used, which is known as provenance

◆ In the energy sector this can enhance both the production and delivery of energy, and make the financial markets around it more efficient

◆ In the food sector this can both augment food safety and improve the economics for farmers

Being able to establish provenance is a key benefit of an immutable ledger with a clear history of additions and modifications. Provenance is quite simply the ability to trace where something comes from, and who took possession of it at various points in time.

For example, conflict diamonds are produced in war-torn regions, most commonly in certain countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Their sale is used to fund further bloodshed, giving them the sobriquet ‘blood diamonds’. The jewellery industry has agreed to a set of standards around proving where a particular gem is mined and ensuring that it was not a product of exploitation in support of warfare. A physical good is reduced to a numbered asset, to data, in a domain where reliability of information and transparency is important – we want to make sure that engagement ring we just purchased wasn’t created using slave labour and isn’t supporting genocide. Blockchain systems can provide an immutable record of the provenance of the gem, tracing its ownership from production to sale. Art, similarly, is a market that relies on provenance as part of the valuation and management of assets, and is another industry that has begun experimenting with blockchain.

Luxury gems are a niche market, albeit a large one, with more than £16 billion in annual sales.[^(24)](#bm05.html#en24) Provenance and transparency are equally as, or perhaps more, important in two other sectors that collectively represent more than £12 trillion globally: energy[^(25)](#bm05.html#en25), [^(26)](#bm05.html#en26) and food.[^(27)](#bm05.html#en27)

Both the energy and food markets have supply chains that are arranged with production in one set of locations, transportation through a complex network, and distribution at multiple locations. There is also a time consideration (notwithstanding advances in energy storage), whereby the resource being generated needs to be consumed within a certain duration or it will ‘expire’ and be rendered unusable. Both energy and food are regulated markets that have historically been burdened with antiquated tracking, reporting and compliance requirements, enmeshing an interdependent set of actors ranging from small, private-sector individuals up to and including country-level government agencies. The energy and food industries appear tailor-made for blockchain and, indeed, DLT entrepreneurs have launched hundreds of pilot projects around the world in recent years.

### **ENERGY**

The energy sector has a complex supply chain involving multiple actors and domiciles, that are in regular and interconnected interaction with each other, governing more than £1.5 trillion of spending annually.[^(28)](#bm05.html#en28) We briefly touched on certain financial aspects of blockchain and energy in [Chapter 4](#chapter04.html). We’ll now take a look at how greater transparency in energy production and distribution offers the potential to better manage supplies, navigate shortages or surpluses, and moderate changes in pricing.

##### **Logistics**

Logistics is the lifeblood of the energy sector. Heavy equipment must be deployed and managed at extraction and production sites. A network of suppliers, contractors, sub-contractors, sub-sub-contractors and service providers creates a large volume of contracts, invoices and tracking documentation, and requires a material effort devoted to reconciliation. Cost allocation is a major function within energy production and distribution. Frequently, energy flows across borders, adding trade documentation and reconciliation mapped to varying rules and regulations.

The Oxford Blockchain Strategy Framework tells us that this repeating process is well suited to blockchain applications, given that it involves multiple players and layers of intermediaries, with paperwork and calculation subject to automation.

##### **Grid management**

Management of the energy grid can be empowered by blockchain. Historically, energy distribution was a one-way street. Utility companies would produce energy and send it to consumers’ homes. As consumers install solar generation tech at home, and ‘smart grid’ technologies are implemented to manage load, we are witnessing the emergence of a bilateral energy economy. Consumers aren’t just paying for energy to be transmitted to their homes; they are also sending back surplus energy generated during peak solar production. By keeping energy transmission to a localised level, efficiencies can be introduced by reduction of transmission loss.

Blockchain offers the possibility of seamless, dynamic, multilateral energy markets.[^(29)](#bm05.html#en29) In geographies ranging from Australia to Estonia to Brooklyn, NY, we are seeing efforts to implement microgrid-blockchain from companies such as WePower and Brooklyn Microgrid (part of the Consensys family).[^(30)](#bm05.html#en30) Peer-to-peer (p2p) energy markets represent a potential democratisation of end-point energy management, facilitating more efficient markets and lower costs to consumers.

Provenance emerges as a factor also. Consumers are selecting green energy, for example, in lieu of gas or oil. This requires knowledge and certification of how the electricity is produced and a mechanism to ensure that it isn’t commingled with ‘dirty’ energy sources such as coal.

The application of blockchain to energy distribution management isn’t limited to the retail market. Wholesale energy management is a complex endeavour capturing multiple actors (including government as well as the private sector) and requiring the transmission not only of energy itself but also volumes of data across an interconnected set of disparate entities. Hydropower might be produced in Quebec, and then transmitted to a high-energy-consumption locale like Massachusetts.[^(31)](#bm05.html#en31) The wholesale energy market, likewise, would benefit from the transparency, ease of data sharing and automated balancing of multiple actors that blockchain provides.

There are other areas in which blockchain can potentially add value. Utility companies can do a better job of inventory management, at a lower cost, through the implementation of automated blockchain asset management systems. Billing and smart metering could see greater automation with blockchain. Transitioning from one energy supplier to another could be facilitated with the data portability that blockchain could enable.

If we think about the strategic goals of government, we see opportunities to apply convergent technologies (instances in which we combine two or more recent innovations, including blockchain). Municipal and regional economic analysis could be conducted for the purposes of (1) understanding economic development; (2) assessing progress towards UN Sustainable Development Goals; and (3) infrastructure planning. Econometric insights, for example, could be made more transparent, leading to more efficient ways of addressing energy consumption and demand variability. These functions are greatly enhanced by combining artificial intelligence, big data systems and blockchain.

### **FOOD**

The world’s food supply is ripe for blockchain, especially with regard to provenance and transparency. Food traceability and ingredient supply chain management have numerous implications for agriculture, food production and distribution.

##### **Food safety**

High levels of transparency and clear traceability of individual ingredients in a product allow for a cost-effective approach to the management of safety issues ranging from contamination to recall. A salmonella outbreak, for example, could be more effectively identified and contained, and retailers, consumers and key authorities notified rapidly – and it would be possible to quickly trace the source of the infection. Research firm Gartner suggests that as many as 20 per cent of the world’s grocers will employ some form of blockchain by 2025, to introduce greater transparency into the food supply chain.[^(32)](#bm05.html#en32)

As consumer demand rises for organic, pesticide-free, farm-to-table produce, large retailers such as Walmart are partnering with tech providers such as IBM to introduce total transparency into their food supply chain. They report that tracing food sources can be accelerated from seven days to fewer than three seconds using blockchain.[^(33)](#bm05.html#en33)

##### **Food provenance**

Food provenance also has implications for environmental, social, and governance (ESG) factors. Not only can a food product be certified as free of certain pesticides or hormones, but also worker conditions and appropriate labour treatment can be monitored and certified. Guidelines such as the OECD’s Responsible Business Conduct (RBC)[^(34)](#bm05.html#en34), which incorporates standards like the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs), can be attached to the food supply chain and enable high-trust certification at the point of sale that ethical work conditions were provided at the point of production.

More broadly, we can think about the provenance of commodities. According to Serge Schoen, the former CEO of industry giant Louis Dreyfus Commodities, Egyptian cotton sells for 200 per cent more than other kinds of cotton . . . and there is 200 per cent more ‘Egyptian’ cotton sold every year than is actually produced.[^(35)](#bm05.html#en35) The profit motive to counterfeit this premium product is as clear as the opportunity for blockchain to provide assurances of a product’s origin and quality.

##### **Improved collaboration**

Cambodian rice farmers are banding together, empowered by distributed ledger technology, to ensure they get better prices for their grain.[^(36)](#bm05.html#en36) More broadly, there is opportunity to drive effective supply and demand aggregation through blockchain systems to improve fairness and transparency within the highly inefficient world of food production and distribution.

##### **Higher velocity**

Blockchain is a viable tool for increasing the speed with which the food supply chain is managed. Commodities giant Louis Dreyfus was able to decrease transaction time by 80 per cent for wholesale trade (60,000 tons of soybeans, in the case of their pilot) using blockchain technology.[^(37)](#bm05.html#en37) By digitising a currently analogue-heavy world of papers and faxes, and tying it together in a manner that can only be achieved through networked technologies, blockchain delivers enormous change to the basic logistics of the world’s food supply.

Applying blockchain to the food supply chain may result in higher prices for consumers, as near-slave-labour conditions at the point of production are remediated. This may compress profits for food companies until they rationalise their work-forces for the newly digital era, which in turn would create a labour challenge that would need to be addressed. On balance, however, greater equity, greater efficiency and greater food safety are enabled by the application of blockchain.

### **ENHANCING SYSTEMS**

What we see, with the application of blockchain to sectors such as energy and food, is the development of a systems approach to interconnected players, one that can reconfigure itself to changing circumstances and new entrants. An ephemeral, consumable resource, whether it be a mango or a kilowatt of electricity, is now better adapted to the needs of society because blockchain creates greater transparency, higher velocity of information exchange and a democratised locus of control.

We also see the strange intermarriage of capitalist and communist ideals as the means of production are more firmly placed in the hands of the worker, and the collective bargaining power of numerous small actors is automatically aggregated and tied to economic outcomes through distributed ledgers and even tokenised exchanges. Tokenised exchanges of, for example, farmer outputs could create a new currency linked directly to the good or service produced, making value exchange more transparent and attributable within a large network of numerous small actors.

## [Real estate, the environment and natural resources](#contents.html#rchapter07)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ The physical world can be made easier to understand and interact with through the application of blockchain to newly available data sets

◆ Real estate can be bought, sold, developed and maintained more efficiently and effectively

◆ The intersection of environment, policy and economics can be vastly improved through DLT

◆ Natural resources such as gold and other commodities can more readily be transacted

Activities in the realm of place and space offer a novel set of use cases for blockchain, as we seek to translate the tangible world through which we move and live into a readily accessible, communicable set of information. The legacy practice of real estate, for example, is dominated on the data front by a monopoly provider of pricing information with closed systems and archaic data sets. The blockchain-enabled future will make granular data sets widely available and shareable, possibly with small bits of artificial intelligence attached so that the data automatically enriches itself to make itself even more useful. This could improve the velocity of information flow and eventually the flow of goods and services, which would increase economic productivity and reduce costs.

Our approach is predicated on the idea that we can find some way of attaching a digital data set to a physical asset that is fixed in place or in some other way tied to a fixed geography. By taking the tangible, the intractable, and giving it fluidity and a more easily manipulable form, we open up new vistas of possibility.

### **REAL ESTATE**

Real estate is a massive asset class, representing more than £228 trillion in value,[^(38)](#bm05.html#en38) but until recently it was subject to transacting in large, ungainly portions (when have you ever heard of someone legitimately buying £10 of real estate?). With blockchain and tokenisation, we can reduce real estate into micro-fractional elements, and open it up for investment, asset transfer and economic analysis. The individual consumer could own a portion of his or her favourite local coffee shop. The small real estate investor could have a more diversified portfolio, or one constructed on novel insights about real estate assets. New kinds of derivatives could be created to introduce greater stability or control around risk.

The transactions involved in the purchase and sale of real estate hold the potential for massive simplification and cost reduction in the blockchain era. For example, many real estate transactions today require title insurance and clarity around chain-of-title. Both considerable expense and considerable delay are an accepted reality of real estate transactions for even the simplest of home purchases, much less for multi-lot, mixed-use development projects. Other data need to be tied to the real estate transaction as well, such as environmental assessments and financial records searches. A properly constructed blockchain-based system could greatly mitigate the complexity and sluggish speed of the average real estate transaction.

In the commercial real estate (CRE) world, blockchain can help automate the entire transaction process. Searching for properties is a laborious and data-inefficient process, with multiple and sometimes conflicting data sets that could be harmonised through a distributed ledger. Time and effort is expended with pre-transaction due diligence. A variety of disparate data needs to be compiled, synthesised and distributed in a controlled fashion. Take, for example, an environmental report detailing whether a building has toxic chemicals stored underneath it or asbestos in its walls. If such things existed in the past but were remediated, one needs to know when this action was taken, what kind of action was taken, by whom, and who has certified that the building is now safe. There are reams upon reams of documents to consider, and a blockchain could help navigate this complexity. Blockchain systems could potentially assist with the organisation and data management of access control, distribution and validation of information quality. Another potential blockchain application within CRE is the creation of an intelligent and flexible real estate lease that could adapt on the fly to the needs of tenant and landlord, a smart contract attached to a distributed ledger.[^(39)](#bm05.html#en39)

Insurance crosses the real estate market in novel ways. For example, the insurance policy for the World Trade Centre was purportedly unsigned at the time of the 9/11 attacks. This caused some consternation as to whether or not billions would be paid out to policyholder Larry Silverstein. While he eventually cleared more than £3 billion, other policy ambiguities surrounding his coverage led to more than a decade of sprawling multiparty lawsuits.[^(40)](#bm05.html#en40) An AI-managed smart contract for the execution and adjudication of insurance might have saved considerable time, expense and struggle.

### **THE ENVIRONMENT**

Some data is dauntingly big. When we think about the environment writ large, we think about air, water and soil on a global scale. Fine-tuned data on these axes would exceed the capacity of even the most powerful computers that exist today. There are acceptable workarounds, however, that reduce the impossible to the merely difficult. Weather forecasting, for example, is conducted on the basis of a series of compromises that approximate reality – you take a series of data points (rather than an infinite stream of continuous data), collected at various sensors, and then put them into a computer model that makes intelligent guesses about what is going to happen. Similarly, we can approach the introduction of distributed ledgers into environmental systems management in a step-by-step fashion.

Weather, in fact, represents an interesting use case for blockchain. Currently, governments around the world fund the collection of critical weather data that is used in weather forecasting. Private-sector actors also generate weather data, sometimes from satellites. Private weather companies accumulate various data sources and generate forecasts that they sell. The UN administers an agency, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), which coordinates the sharing of weather data by various countries. However, there are challenges when you mix publicly funded, ‘free’ weather data with fee-bearing private weather companies – incentives get misaligned. The issue of attribution becomes challenging, as does the issue of how to properly compensate micro-contributors of data whose various pieces go into making a more complex weather model. A tokenised system that incorporates smart contracts with encapsulated weather data on a blockchain could help provide appropriate attribution, and remuneration, to various contributors to a weather forecasting system.

Another way in which blockchain can be involved in stewarding and managing the environment is with carbon credits. The carbon credit is a concept that seeks to use market forces to mitigate environmental damage. In its simplest form, a carbon credit is built around some form of document that allows the owner to release one metric tonne of carbon dioxide or equivalent within a fixed length of time.[^(41)](#bm05.html#en41) The idea is that by limiting how much carbon can be generated, and attaching value to those limits, overall pollution will be reduced. While not without its detractors,[^(42)](#bm05.html#en42) the concept of carbon offsetting has gained meaningful currency in a number of sectors. And it relies on data, documentation, multiparty transactions that repeat, and intermediaries. The portable data elements that constitute a carbon credit or offset require traceability and exchangeability, which suits them well to blockchain. Indeed, a vibrant array of early experiments has sought to migrate the carbon credit market to DLT.

Blockchain has other applications within the world of sustainability and the environment. For example, a number of companies have been accused of ‘greenwashing’, claiming to engage in environmentally friendly activities, or to be ‘greener’ than their competitors, but fail to provide robust, transparent evidence. The digital trust systems afforded by blockchain could improve the transparency and verification of environmental impact claims by corporations.[^(43)](#bm05.html#en43)

Recursively, blockchain has raised concerns in certain circles regarding its environmental impact.[^(44)](#bm05.html#en44) Blockchain is designed to be highly secure, highly transparent and highly distributed. By its very nature, with hundreds or thousands of nodes constantly talking to each other and churning computer cycles in order to calculate blocks (or block equivalents, if a DLT like Corda), blockchain is highly energy intensive. It is one of the least efficient mechanisms of computing. There is a degree of irony in the application of blockchain to sustainability, given the massive energy consumption of blockchain networks. Various solutions are being explored to address this, from more efficient algorithms to greater use of renewable sources in powering blockchain.

### **NATURAL RESOURCES**

Natural resources are worth approximately £102 trillion per year globally,[^(45)](#bm05.html#en45) and the tracking, commodification, and conservation of nature’s bounty relies on multiple inputs spanning numerous geographies. The intrinsic characteristics of natural resources have historically limited scalable access to this sector to very large and wealthy parties. For example, the typical timber transaction entails vast tracts of land and long-term (100-year) leases or decades-long return on capital expectations. It has typically been the province of family offices, insurance funds, sovereign funds and similar dynastic investors. Blockchain could change all of that, introducing new levels of liquidity to a previously immobile and opaque asset class.

The democratisation of natural resources is accelerating with the advent of greater financial and political instability in major power blocs around the world. In an effort to make bitcoin more palatable to regulators and policymakers, rising above its sometimes-seedy origins, its most ardent proponents have been comparing bitcoin to gold, describing it as a store of value with a limited supply, treating it as a virtual precious metal or commodity. But there’s no need to take a circuitous route in order to get precious metals onto a digital token. People are already tying rare metals to digital tokens.

With interest rates dipping into the negative in a number of countries, gold is assuming a new lustre. It still suffers from the same problems it has always suffered from, namely those of security, portability, volatility and transferability – as well as the risk of confiscation or regulation by a given government.[^(46)](#bm05.html#en46) In response, a network of intermediaries and agents have arisen who assist with the acquisition, transport, storage, trading and management of gold assets.

Enter blockchain. From a traceability and transaction standpoint, blockchain offers a number of benefits when it serves as the foundation for a token backed by gold. Gold is never going to assume a negative value. It has an absolute floor of zero. And a properly designed digital token that ties to gold, likewise, would enjoy similar stability.

Some of the efforts to create digital currencies that embed commodity prices for some form of hedge stability, such as Ven or Tradecoin, use gold as one of their components. Other gold tokens are strictly tied to the value of gold by itself – so-called ‘pure plays’ – and sometimes linked to individual gold bars or coins. With the degree of traceability that blockchain allows for, you can create a fine level of specific information about the underlying asset.

## [CHAPTER 8](#contents.html#rchapter08)

## [Organisations and governance](#contents.html#rchapter08)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ The changing velocity of business, driven by the technology revolution, has left governance (how we manage and make decisions) behind

◆ Blockchain can address this by enabling better kinds of collaboration across organisations

◆ It also can make possible new kinds of distributed organisations that previously couldn’t exist

Perhaps one of the more novel applications proposed for blockchain is the reimagining of organisations through the lens of highly distributed decision making.

The heritage of open-source computing and highly distributed community projects provides robust foundations for an expansion of blockchain’s potential from the organisation of facts and figures to the shaping of human systems into new, flexible, creative and rapidly changing forms. The burgeoning needs of organisations to quickly respond to changing circumstances and markets provides the impetus for adopting new and different approaches to getting groups of individuals to move in a coordinated fashion.

### **IT TAKES A VILLAGE**

Open-source computing was born out of frustration over the growing corporatisation of computer software development. A number of the original hackers who helped shape computer technology came from a laid-back, almost hippie counter-culture of the United States of the 1960s and 1970s. One such figure, computer scientist Richard Stallman,[^(47)](#bm05.html#en47) wanted to implement a simple workaround for a common problem: when a shared printer jams, send a message to everyone in the group, so someone can fix it. He discovered that the software for their new laser printer was ‘closed’ and was proprietary – prohibiting modification. This and other efforts to restrict information led him to create an open operating system, and help birth a movement.[^(48)](#bm05.html#en48)

Stallman was followed in the 1990s by Eric Raymond, whose essay ‘The Cathedral and the Bazaar’ served as a call to arms for what he began to term the ‘open-source’ software movement, accompanying the release of his open-source operating system Linux.[^(49)](#bm05.html#en49) With open source, the source code (written instructions) of the software is openly published for anyone to use for free. The social contract, however, is that if you make improvements, you need to publish those improvements back to the code so that others can take advantage of them. Instead of the proprietary software licensing model of companies like Microsoft, open-source companies like Red Hat made their money from selling services and solutions to enhance the open-source software implementation.

This has led to some large-scale projects, hosted on shared repositories like Github, with thousands of software developers around the world adding to the code and enhancing the code base for the benefit of the many. The MIT License is one of the most popular open-source software licences extant today – a commonly understood legal framework for how to manage intellectual property rights that accompany software projects.

Decisions about what gets accepted into an open-source code library are determined by the project creators. The three most common models, according to the Open Source Guide,[^(50)](#bm05.html#en50) are:

◆ **Benevolent Dictator for Life**: one individual, typically the one who came up with the project, makes key decisions

◆ **Meritocracy**: people who make a meaningful contribution to the project make the key decisions and hold votes to implement changes

◆ **Liberal Contribution**: the most active current contributors drive the process, and it is a process of consensus building (rather than a pure vote) that implements change

These different models of managing open-source projects are often considered and applied to blockchain projects, themselves often open-source.

### **BLOCKCHAIN DEADLOCKS**

Variations on meritocracy and liberal contribution have been used in numerous blockchain projects, including bitcoin. Both resulted in the unexpected consequence of the hard fork as an output of the democratic decision making behind blockchain governance, as we discussed in [Chapter 1](#chapter01.html).

Indeed, if we look at bitcoin versus bitcoin cash, the former has a considerably larger market share.

### **ORGANISATIONAL DECISION MAKING**

As we apply the lessons and formulae from open source and blockchain to the management of organisations, we see an outline emerging of what a responsive organisation of the future might look like.

Why is this organisation needed?

Technology adoption has been accelerating on a power law curve (basically, it’s getting faster and faster with each new technology). It took a century for the telephone to reach 80 per cent of the US market. It took about two decades for the World Wide Web to get to similar levels. It took only a couple of years for mobile internet to achieve the same penetration. By some measures, the ascent of bitcoin was even faster.

As the pace of change accelerates, we run into the fundamental challenge that the way we organise ourselves, in groups and companies, isn’t keeping up with the kind of decision making, creativity and flexibility that is needed from such structures in the face of a rapidly evolving technological environment. If a large organisation takes a year to study how to reorganise itself around a new opportunity, a year or two to act on those changes, and a year to absorb how to operate in the new structure – three or four years in total – how can it possibly address the next technology that might achieve widespread adoption in as little as twelve months? In three months? How did we wind up in such a state of rigidity and sluggishness?

Starting in the 1700s, the steam engine, a technology first conceived of by the ancient Romans, was perfected for mass adoption. This fuelled the Industrial Revolution that created empires and brought new models of affordability and prosperity to millions around the world. The organisational systems that were developed to cope with this era of highly scalable production gave rise to a command-and-control architecture, with one big boss at the top and armies of workers in highly specialised roles underneath.

Taylorism reigned supreme.[^(51)](#bm05.html#en51) Henry Ford’s assembly line turned out millions of cars in the early part of the twentieth century, happily replicating vehicles through highly specialised manufacturing, one of the most prominent examples of ‘mass production’ with a highly stratified organisational model.

### **OUT OF CHAOS, THE NEW WORLD ORDER**

The Second World War brought about innovations in organisational design. The German High Command evolved into a ‘general staff’ model where a council of senior officers had defined areas of responsibility and a reasonable scope of authority and autonomy within that. Yet even with this we still see a command-and-control mindset. The generals had a level of autonomy in their decision making, but individual foot soldiers were still cannon fodder. ‘I was just following orders,’ became a common defence at the Nuremberg Trials.

On the US side, a bit of frontier spirit and invention prevailed. As German Admiral Karl Dönitz said, ‘The reason that the American Navy does so well in wartime is that war is chaos, and the Americans practise chaos on a daily basis.’[^(52)](#bm05.html#en52) He may have simply been faced with unfamiliar patterns of action which he failed to recognise. Some in the German military dismissed America’s role in the Allied victory as merely providing numerical superiority, hearkening to an indictment of General Erich Ludendorff’s earlier First World War strategic failures, that the Americans were simply able to produce soldiers faster than Germany could produce bullets.[^(53)](#bm05.html#en53) However, some military historians point to the conscious encouragement by American forces of inventiveness, local-group initiative and autonomy as a contributing factor to Allied success. Psychologists were recruited into the US military, and began screening special operations troops for creativity and improvisation skills.[^(54)](#bm05.html#en54) Indeed, the US Marine Corps has adopted the informal motto of ‘Improvise. Adapt. Overcome.’

For about forty years, the improvisational lessons learned on the battlefields of the Second World War were lost in the boardroom, despite the fact that this kind of improvisational problem-solving happens to be highly similar to the mode of thinking and behaviour of an innovative, high-performing team.

Mega-corporations like IBM, Xerox and Exxon replicated a command-and-control structure with a rigid and fixed org chart. The term ‘organisation man’ grew to describe a particular kind of groupthink, where ‘yes men’ reinforced whatever decisions were made at the very top.

With the introduction, between the 1970s and the 1990s, of microcomputers, better communications networks, outsourcing, business process engineering and de-conglomeratisation, and the pace of business accelerating from years to months to weeks to days to hours, new organisational models arose.

### **ENTER THE MATRIX**

Matrix management added flexibility . . . and confusion. In matrix management, the conventional organisational chart would be set aside when a particular project need arose. People would be recruited from across the organisation and put under the direction of an individual who did not necessarily write their annual performance review, but was tasked with guiding their work for that particular project. This enabled a business to more readily adapt to a new need and draw from across the organisation the necessary skills and talents required to address that need. Some matrixed teams endure for months or years; others are more ephemeral, existing merely for a matter of weeks.

Information systems struggle to keep up with human systems and the perversity of human behaviour. By the time the central information technology group had re-assigned your data privileges and permissions so that you could work on that matrix-driven project, you had already returned to your home organisation. This informational friction has led to a number of cybersecurity failures, because it was more convenient for people to share passwords and logins than it was to go through the process to assign someone formal access for a short-term project – leading to unwitting holes in cyber protocol. Edward Snowden’s breach of the National Security Agency data systems was enabled in large part by his ability to convince more than two dozen colleagues to provide him with their security credentials under the guise of collaboration, stating he needed their credentials in his role as computer administrator.[^(55)](#bm05.html#en55) *Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?*

Startup companies have to deal with an exceptionally rapid rate of change and respond accordingly. Many tend towards a loosely coupled management model with a high degree of individual autonomy and decision making. Trying to apply conventional data models to a rapidly-evolving startup creates a host of challenges.

### **HOLA BACK**

‘Holacracy’ is a form of management popularised by an American ecommerce company, Zappos. Led by Tony Hsieh and still operated at the time of writing with a high degree of independence even though owned by Amazon, Zappos trialled going a step further than matrix management. Tony wanted to do away with layers of management and coordination entirely. He proposed using something called ‘holacracy’, where self-organising teams and groups would attack challenges.[^(56)](#bm05.html#en56)

The advantage of a truly empowered individual is a greater sense of ownership and personal responsibility for work and outcomes. Particularly for the millennial generation, who seek recognition and meaning in their work, the new concept of ‘holacracy’ holds a beguiling promise of relieving the drudgery of acting as a small cog in a very big machine.

A completely democratic organisation that seeks to move with common purpose in a competitive environment will face severe challenges, particularly in times of crisis or stress. At the same time, large organisations are seeking to reinvent themselves for the high-velocity future by decentralising authority, granting greater decisional autonomy to individual business units and individuals, and using intelligent computer systems to tie together activity and action into a coherent whole.

In Zappo’s holacracy, an internal jobs board lets teams post tasks and individuals post skills, creating an ability to dynamically allocate labour and empower people to highlight the training they require to broaden or deepen their capabilities. When I visited Zappos a few years ago and met with Tony, I saw both an organisation made up of people who exuded purpose and enthusiasm, and a leader who could see beyond the edge of the map.

Holacracy is well suited to the open-source-inspired world of blockchain. And blockchain, in turn, can help make holacracy more viable. Information systems and data governance structures can adapt themselves – thanks to smart contracts and DApps and AI integration with distributed ledgers – to the dynamically changing needs and organisational structures of the institution.

Properly constructed AI agents could help mediate this reconfigurable organisation across a blockchain, automatically adjudicating what today requires human intervention. Ordinarily, when you move from one group to another, or get assigned temporarily to a project team, IT needs to change the permissions information that determines what data you have access to and how you access it. This can sometimes take longer than the duration of the project you are working on. The right kind of AI engine can make this automatic. Data permissions can accompany this reconfiguration on the fly, allowing for better data security and better data utility at the same time. And in an era of distributed teams, widely dispersed across many geographies, tightly linked networked communications can facilitate nimbleness and smooth functioning.

The organisation of the future may or may not live on a blockchain, but without question blockchain holds the potential to make that future organisation more effective. One of the challenges of decentralised decision making is the loss of coordination of activities. In complex systems this can lead to gridlock and chaos, as nobody knows what the rest of the organisation is doing or how their work fits in with the greater whole, sometimes leading people to work at crosspurposes. Relatively rapid dissemination of information, and coordination of information, can help address some of this chaotic behaviour and better conform it to a desired goal or outcome, such as completing a particular project on time and in synchronisation with a larger strategy.

Transcending the organisational, the ability of blockchain to address multi-institutional concerns, spanning non-profit, individual, and for-profit actors, assumes greater import and impact. Whereas trading systems might have multiple parties all governed by a basic motive of profit, societal systems introduce multiple dimensions of sometimes-conflicting imperatives. [Part III](#part03.html) of this book looks at these multi-stakeholder systems and selects societal impacts of blockchain.

## [Education](#contents.html#rchapter09)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ Educational institutions have helped enable the blockchain revolution and continue to power it

◆ The central product of the university, the credential, can be improved with blockchain

◆ New approaches to education are exceptionally well suited to blockchain’s philosophy and technology

The education sector has served as a nest to nurture and extend blockchain innovation, and also as a beneficiary of fledging explorations of blockchain.

### **ORIGIN STORY**

In the wake of the collapse of the Bitcoin Foundation, MIT supported core developers and funded a number of projects to keep bitcoin afloat, until the industry could mature further. This predated the extraordinary run-up in bitcoin price that created a new generation of bitcoin billionaires. Not long after, MIT loaded the software and turned on the first Ripple validator node in a North American university (a Ripple validator node is a piece of the Ripple network that helps to ensure the integrity of the data flowing through it). Other Ethereum-based projects began to emerge. As I launched the first graduate fintech class in North America, *MIT Future Commerce* (at Meltem Demirors’ insistence, when she was an MIT student), and brought it online to over 130 countries, we saw an explosion of startups exploring and exploiting this new technology – not only out of MIT, but also Stanford, Princeton, Cambridge, National University of Singapore, and a host of other institutions.

Less well known, perhaps, is the role MIT played in preventing bitcoin from being shut down before it scaled. In 2014, Bitcoin was just beginning to emerge from a fringe project of hobbyists and the ultra-paranoid into a more mainstream set of business and other applications. Other protocols were being developed. As the scale of bitcoin transactions grew, so too grew government concern about the use of this novel digital currency for illicit transactions. Professor Alex Pentland of MIT, John Clippinger (then attached to Harvard’s Berkman Center) and I organised an off-the-record conference engaging leaders from incumbent corporate interests, innovative bitcoin-related startups, government and academia called the Ecology of Digital Assets: Identity, Trust and Data. John then hosted a retreat at Windhover with some of the attendees, and out of it came the Windhover Principles,[^(57)](#bm05.html#en57) a set of guidelines used by exchanges and wallets to show compliance with anti-money-laundering (AML) and Know Your Client (KYC) regulations.

Without the Windhover Principles, bitcoin stood a serious risk of being shut down by government enforcement agencies. Without the ability to translate fiat currency into bitcoin and bitcoin into fiat, the protocol’s usefulness would have been severely limited, perhaps fatally, perhaps relegating it to an obscure curiosity rather than an industry-defining advancement. With the Windhover Principles, variants of which were adopted into the terms of service of all of the major exchanges, government saw good-faith efforts on the part of the nascent bitcoin ecosystem to comply with the rule of law. And bitcoin innovators were informally but substantively provided the room to innovate, without heavy-handed intervention by regulators. Stan Stalnaker then provided the first deployment of Windhover with HubID (a verified digital identity system) attached to Ven, an early entrant into the digital currency market.[^(58)](#bm05.html#en58)

Yet decades earlier, work on Byzantine consensus and other distributed database applications was pioneered in the halls of academe. Without work on network systems, on game theory and distributed decision making, and on cryptography, we would not have the critical components that make blockchain innovation possible. There are even those who theorise that Satoshi Nakamoto is an academic and not someone in the private sector.

Today, academic institutions around the world continue to advance the state of the art of blockchain technologies. The major protocols have formal or informal links with an array of centres of academic excellence. Corporate actors support applications of blockchain technology in opensource code consortia, helping provide both funds and use cases to build out blockchain infrastructure. New ventures continue to emerge from academic programmes, not only commercial startups and corporate innovation projects, but also nonprofits applying blockchain for social good, and new open-source code projects to experiment with a variety of improvements, extensions and inventions around distributed ledger technologies.

### **RECURSION AND CREDENTIALS**

Academia is not only the source of many of the ideas and technologies that went into making the blockchain revolution possible. It also has been and continues to serve as a beneficiary of certain applications of blockchain. Recursively, we can apply blockchain to the core product that academia manufactures.

I refer not to the emergence of programmes that award a ‘PhD in Blockchain’. In my opinion, those programmes do a disservice to their graduates. You don’t hear people today bragging about their ‘PhD in HTML’ or their ‘PhD in UNIX’. A PhD founded in a broader set of theories and capabilities provides someone with utility for decades, versus years, such as a PhD in Computer Science or even a PhD in Database Systems.

Instead, I am intrigued by the application of blockchain to the recognisable output of an academic education: the credential – whether it be a degree, a diploma or a certificate – as well as the accompanying academic transcript. Many jobs require the completion of a certain level of academic training. Some mandate the applicant to have taken specific courses, or maintain a certain grade point average. These requirements are reliant on attestation by an institution that the student matriculated, and actually completed the class or degree or programme.

Corporate scandals have arisen over people claiming false credentials from institutions they never graduated from, or never even attended. The time and expense, and functional burden, of verifying every degree or every grade is too much for many employers to undertake. As a consequence of this informational friction, the honour system is instead used *de facto* if not *de jure*. This may work for the majority of employees, but also creates an opening for the unscrupulous to create unearned opportunity for themselves.

Blockchain offers an elegant solution to the challenge of the academic transcript. A host of blockchain credential companies have arisen to deliver these digitally verified attestations of academic achievement – we use one such system for our Oxford online programmes. Blockchain could provide an immutable record of classes completed and even grades received, that can be automatically furnished without having to go through the process of contacting registrars, paying fees, and waiting days or weeks for documentation.

Multiple stakeholders must be engaged in verifying and validating credentials, another reason why blockchain has applicability to this problem space. The university or other institution, of course, must issue the credential. We also have involved the student, the instructor, perhaps an industry accreditation or standards body, the employer, perhaps a lender, possibly one or more government bodies. All of these participants have to rely on the veracity of the attestation, requiring a digital truth that is provided by a cryptographically ensured permanent record.

### **THE UNIVERSITY OF THE FUTURE**

Perhaps one of the more exciting areas to explore is how blockchain and other technologies in combination could transform the educational experience of the future.

University education is more than just sitting in the classroom and listening to a lecture. Co-curricular activities and community form some of the most durable elements of residential education. The most lasting experiences of education are when we are working in groups and teams, solving problems as part of closely knit team.

One example of how university education goes beyond sitting in a classroom is something known as a ‘hackathon’, where multiple teams of people compete over a short period of time to solve one or more defined problems. Hackathons are a staple of many technology and business educations. We even see them cropping up in the creative arts. From time to time, but with increasing frequency, we see the distributed hackathon, an event in which people based in various locations around the world work together to solve the same problem at the same time. There have even been hackathons featuring fully distributed teams – where no two people are in the same physical location – bringing diverse perspectives from diverse geographies and cultures.

This notion of the distributed team in the educational hackathon increasingly resembles the future of work, where people might be brought together for specific projects and seek novel solutions. Whereas the conventional company might first address one market and country, and grow meaningful scale there before seeking to enter additional markets or geographies, a new generation of startups are attacking multiple countries and geographies simultaneously, at earlier and earlier stages of the company’s life cycle.

How do we manage the information flow in these highly distributed entities? How do we navigate the nuances of intellectual property and ownership and decision making in the formative stages of a new venture? What about when these issues are compounded by distributed teams and disparate locales?

Perhaps blockchain can offer answers – not to every challenge I’ve presented, but possibly to help achieve a more rapid means of reaching a communal decision on key issues, particularly in distributed environments. It could help establish the authorship of new ideas and some of the other aspects of new business formation. And as the university spin-out[^(59)](#bm05.html#en59) that is created from these ideas, perhaps ideas emerging from a hackathon, begins to raise money and achieve independence, blockchain can help manage the shareholders in new and fascinating ways. Echoing the distributed organisation we read about in the last chapter, higher-order shareholder governance could be navigated through the consensus algorithms of blockchain. Management might present key decisions not to a board of directors but to a distributed array of investors, who are able to rapidly reach a conclusion about a course of action through a computer-mediated network.

### **IDEAS AND PEOPLE**

At their very hearts, universities have two products: ideas and people. Universities also frequently work with each other around these dimensions of ideas and people, through mechanisms like collaborative research projects or student and researcher exchange programmes. These kinds of activities serve to crossfertilise new fields, new thinking and new competencies. At the individual researcher or research group level, these are relatively seamless exchanges and commonplace. When the interactions assume greater scale, however, the inter-institutional dynamics become extraordinary sources of friction. Ownership of intellectual property (IP) and management of funding streams become rivers of delay, distraction and often destruction, making agreements impossible.

These numerous contentious data streams of IP are tamed in a blockchain-enabled environment. Perhaps the multi-university collaboration of the future could be coalesced under a blockchain umbrella, with the nuances of idea flow and funding flow automated through algorithms and distributed ledgers.

### **FROM ACADEMY TO SOCIETY**

In much of the developed world, some form of post-secondary education is common. Increasingly in the developing world, with the advent of better digital platforms and new forms of information distribution mediated by mobile communications networks, the potential for secondary education, and beyond, becomes ever more attainable for ever greater numbers of people.

These networked communications and connectivity systems, that when coupled to blockchain permit audit, tracking, management and governance, form the millions of droplets that run together into the ocean of society. They arrive at a time when we find ourselves at a juncture in history where the institutions of democracy are under cyberattack, digital technologies such as Facebook have led to the fragmentation of the common discourse through which we form a cohesive state, and our trust in government is plummeting to lows typically seen in the past when societies are on the road to revolution. Ever widening gaps between rich and poor exacerbate the plutocratic effects of accelerated capitalism, with half a dozen individuals holding as much wealth as the poorest 50 per cent of the world’s population.

Blockchain is entering its maturity at a pivotal moment in history.

## [CHAPTER 10](#contents.html#rchapter10)

## [Government](#contents.html#rchapter10)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

◆ Some of the sharpness of recent attacks on government institutions can be blunted by distributed ledgers

◆ One of the main instruments of government policy, the fiat currency, is being digitised through blockchain systems as Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs)

◆ Government functions like law and tax are being reinvented in multiple countries, and blockchain technologies are central to the discussion

Caught between the deafening peals of new technology and the demands of the populace, government stands at a transition point. Paradoxically, this disruption and discontent could give rise to a new age of enlightenment, much as the Industrial Revolution led to greater literacy and greater prosperity for more people in more societies. This only happened after a period of bloodshed and revolution and the displacement of millions of workers from lifetimes of drudgery. The illiterate starved and the literate grew into a new middle class. Are we at a similarly dramatic moment in history at this writing? What role can technology in general, and blockchain in particular, have in cushioning the shock of this societal evolution, or revolution?

Human civilisation as we understand it is barely more than 10,000 years old. We formed hunting groups and tribes, and then city-states, nations and empires. In Ancient Greece, we advanced a notion that everyone had a vote in the decisions of the city state (as long as he was male, owned sufficient property, was over a certain age, etc.). And in forming the grand American experiment in representative democracy, in turn influencing the new French Republic in 1789 and others around the world, the progenitors of this government took their inspiration from Athens, even down to the architecture of the American capital in its Greco-Roman glory.

Government *qua* government is, in one way or another, a representation of the will of the people. Governments that fail to adequately express this will sooner or later be replaced by governments that better express this notion. Arguably, by ignoring labour dislocation caused by automation, rightsizing, outsourcing and reengineering, the US and the UK created sufficient voter discontent to lead to Donald Trump and Boris Johnson assuming the leadership of their respective countries. Conventional candidates and politicians, they were not.

### **ASIDE FROM DEMOCRACY . . .**

I am wary of cultural relativism in describing government. Although most of this chapter will describe the promise that blockchain offers to representational and parliamentary democracy, I will note in passing how it might be applied within other forms of government.

Russia experimented with conventional democracy, and appears unhappy with the results, having reverted to a nominal dictatorship in which a small circle of oligarchs control the levers of the economy and empower the government. The management of this particular form of government could benefit from reduced corruption and improved oversight through blockchain.

China seems to have navigated from a descriptively communist society to one that has many of the economic benefits of Western capitalism without the same mechanisms of enfranchisement of election. The stability of this form of government could be safeguarded by blockchain. China has created something known as the ‘social score’, which uses data about how well people cooperate with their neighbours and obey the dictates of the ruling party. Your social score determines your ability to get certain employment, permission to travel, and so on. The social score of China could be audited and safeguarded through blockchain, providing for greater stability. Although Western democracies may find the social engineering of the Chinese government of its population to be abhorrent, the Chinese in turn may point to the spectacle that has become the governments of the United Kingdom and the United States and espouse the virtues of a stable society. China’s new digital currency may present one of the first credible threats to the hegemony of the world’s current reserve currencies, dominated by the US dollar.

### **THE DOWNFALL OF DEMOCRACY?**

Let us consider the potential applications of blockchain in democracies and republics. We see in the United Kingdom and in America the vestiges of true Athenian democracy at the local level. The New England ‘town meeting’ or the UK ‘town hall’ bring individual voters forward to debate and discuss with their friends and neighbours, and directly express the franchise.

At the time of writing, Western democracy is under siege. The free flow of information to create an informed population has been contaminated by ‘fake news’, troll farms and information that leads large numbers of people to believe in non-existent conspiracies and give credence to the unreal. The methods by which citizens record their votes are being subverted by the compromise of electronic voting machines.

It is the culmination of a gradual process that has been creeping along for decades. The fourth estate, the free press that provides for an informed electorate, has been neutered by our preference for Instagram and blogs and sound bites on television news. Polling used to be a means of assessing sentiment. With the advent of political push-polling used by Republican operatives in a 1980 local election, polling became a vehicle for propaganda, happily embraced and exploited in subsequent elections around the world.[^(60)](#bm05.html#en60)

More sophisticated and more subtle means of manipulating voters also have emerged. As highlighted in Rachel Boynton’s seminal documentary *Our Brand is Crisis* (later made into an inferior Sandra Bullock fictional feature), the best and most effective techniques of focus groups – the art of defining messages, disseminating them and finding more negative messages to create through something called ‘opposition research’ – are codified and exported globally. A superclass of election engineers craft messages and shepherd voters along desired paths, seeking both to inspire the base of the home candidate and to dispirit and suppress the vote of the opposition.

Our trust in our institutions is being hollowed out at its very core. We made electronic media available to everyone, and found it subverted by the enemies of the state. We applied algorithms, analytics and social influence to the innermost workings of our institutions of state and now we are seeing what happens when we use AI and social networks to create echo chambers where conspiracy theories and fake news can flourish. Yet the same artificial intelligence and social analytics that helped sow chaos, if coupled with blockchain-based trust systems, could bring about a benevolent, utopian future that brings us closer to the ideals around which we formed our governments.

### **BLOCKCHAIN POLLING**

Antiquated electronic voting machines are in use in many parts of the world, obsolete almost from the very moment they were installed. Calls for improvement to election security have gone unheeded or, worse, been deliberately ignored. Loss of confidence in the security of the vote could lead to lower voter participation and by extension a sense of diminished legitimacy of their governments.

In a number of countries, means as crude as indelible ink are used to mark the fingers of voters to ensure election integrity and counteract voting fraud.

Blockchain, in concert with AI and a new generation of biometric technologies, could provide for a vastly more secure and trustworthy election process. Voters would have confidence that their votes would be correctly recorded and influence the outcomes of elections, the will of the people made manifest in the ones and zeroes of algorithmically secured data systems.

Functional governments, particularly democracies and republics, rely on trust and transparency. For example, a voter wants to believe that when they vote, their vote is recorded accurately and honestly. Recent elections in the United States have seen some concern expressed over how ballots are designed, how votes are recorded and how vote counts are verified.

Experiments have begun to use blockchain to record votes. The theory behind these pilots is that people will trust the system more because they will have greater assurance that their vote is being accurately counted. However, the introduction of technology also introduces new questions. For example, there appears to be some consensus that Voatz, a blockchain voting technology company from Boston, Massachusetts, had a certain degree of greater cyber-resilience when someone tried to hack it. However, security experts have raised concerns about the information that is presented before the vote gets recorded. If a hacker were able to take over someone’s mobile phone, and engage in what is known as a ‘man in the middle’ attack, basically where the hacker stands between the mobile phone and the ledger upon which the vote is being recorded, voter fraud could be done by computer.

Another kind of hack could place a different image on someone’s mobile phone, so that they thought they were voting for one person and in fact ended up voting for someone else. So the blockchain ledger would still accurately record the data it was fed, but the data corruption would be occurring further upstream.

Thus, the issue of data quality comes up again. It is one of the least talked-about problems with blockchain, and the one that most urgently needs to be addressed as we begin to adopt blockchain solutions, particularly on critical applications such as financial services, health and government. Effective blockchain-based voting systems will need to place a sharp focus on the issues of security and data quality, or they will degrade the very trust that they are trying to promote.

### **CENTRAL BANK DIGITAL CURRENCY**

The ability to issue and manage currency is one of the most jealously guarded prerogatives of government. Indeed, the management of government financial obligations and government bonds is a critical tool to fund policies, programmes and pay for the society a government represents. It is unsurprising that governments are now experimenting with the creation of digital versions of their fiat: central bank digital currencies (CBDCs). Unlike the public and permissionless blockchains of bitcoin and Ethereum, these CBDCs are permissioned blockchains. Proponents of CBDCs argue that they could facilitate financial inclusion, that they could introduce greater transparency into society, and could reduce corruption and improve public trust.

And yet there are fundamental issues with the idea of launching CBDCs if we fail to fully consider the role of banks in the overall financial system.

Professor Alex Lipton argues a ‘theory of monetary circuit’.[^(61)](#bm05.html#en61) In it, he describes how money is created. Not, as one might assume, by government. Governments can create currency by printing more notes. This is an inflationary act; these new notes are ultimately spread across the same amount of economic value. Monetary circuit theory posits that money is created by banks. In its simplest form, we look at the difference between interest paid and interest income. Banks borrow money from the central bank at a very low rate and use it to fund banking activities. When consumers make deposits, the bank is paid some amount of interest (setting aside the recent phenomenon of negative interest rates) that is higher than the interest rate the bank pays to the central bank. When another consumer takes out a loan from the bank, the bank uses its deposits to fund this loan, and charges interest at a higher rate than the consumer making the original source deposit. The difference between these two figures – the interest paid to the depositor and the interest due from the loan recipient – is the creation of new money. Economic value, arguably, has been created by the bank.

In a world where there are no retail banks – a world in which you store your money in the CBDC, you borrow from the same blockchain network and even make loan payments automatically through smart contracts – where is the creation of new money? What is the role of the retail bank in the world of CBDCs? Functional blockchain-based systems using CBDCs will need to either support or replace the systems that today create new money. Either the critical functions of the retail bank will need to get replicated or replaced in some fashion, or the CBDC will need to be constructed so that it does not displace those functions. For example, the RMB coin that the Government of the People’s Republic of China has proposed as a CBDC is strictly a wholesale instrument. Retail banks purchase these digital RMB at 100% of face value and then provide them directly to consumers. Other designs are possible, but in all cases there needs to be a means to create new money (new value) as Lipton’s monetary circuit describes, not simply duplicate units of currency.

### **AUTOMATING LAW**

Governments express their policies and principles in the form of laws and regulations. Today, these laws and regulations are interpreted by individuals and adjudicated by courts. Whether in American- or British-style jurisprudence, the foundational construct for law remains an argument. There is the written word, such as a contract, a criminal or civil law or a regulation, the meaning of which then gets argued about by parties represented by various advocates. The format for the argument is intrinsically disputational (think of a lawsuit or a criminal charge, which ultimately plays out in a courtroom, whether civil or criminal). At the end of the process, some humans interpret events and law, and make a decision about an outcome.

The current mechanism of law could be transformed through blockchain, potentially by evolving something called a ‘smart contract’. The smart contract emerged out of the efforts to make blockchain more flexible and useful. It’s a misnomer, as we have mentioned before; smart contracts are neither smart, nor contracts, but merely a set of instructions that execute under certain circumstances. Smart contracts are computer programs with a minimal degree of autonomy.

A colleague of mine likes to say that law is the oldest form of computer software, and it runs on the very inefficient hardware of lawyers and judges. What if we took this whimsical notion literally, and hacked the legal system to improve it?

We already have in the legal profession something known as ‘algorithmic discovery’. Algorithmic discovery occurs in complex legal cases, where large law firms and corporations are using sophisticated computer software to dredge through millions of pages of documents to extract critical information that would aid their work instead of having to have armies of lawyers combing through these same documents. AI bots actively search out information in databases and social media websites to assist with the development of cases. Computational social science is already being applied to jury analysis and jury selection. Algorithmic analysis of lending and spending patterns is being used to determine the fairness and compliance of financial institutions with inclusion regulation. Even in the adjudication of human rights abuses, satellite imagery and AI-enabled image analysis are being used to look for signs of genocide. Technology is infiltrating the law at ever-increasing rates.

What if we integrate technology even more into the legal system, by turning smart contracts and DApps into actual algorithmic or computational law? What if entering into a legal agreement meant establishing paired algorithms, one representing each party, that could automatically verify and adjudicate disputes, and digital tokens were used to resolve payment due from one party to another? In this model, the essence of legal principles would be turned into computer code, and that code put into a network with other code so that all of the details of a legal agreement among parties would be automated. What if blockchain were the foundation layer across which this computational law expressed itself, becoming the network that enables algorithmic (computer-driven) law?

These ideas aren’t so radical as they may sound at first.

Indeed, a band of computer programmers and lawyers at various institutions are working to create computational law, to create means for law-as-code. The future is already here, even if it’s unevenly distributed.

### **TAXING EXERTIONS**

A hot area at the intersection of government and blockchain is that of revenue generation, specifically tax. Trust and transparency are paramount in the payment and collection of tax. Tax typically forms the core revenue of a government, and the ability to accumulate tax is central to a government’s ability to deliver its services, whether that be healthcare or a justice system or farming subsidies. Taxation is both the prerogative and the lifeblood of government, providing the tangible means by which policies are funded.

Tax structure and tax avoidance are global challenges, especially in emerging economies. A number of emerging economies, for example in Africa, have average tax collection (as a percentage of GDP) that is about half of the average of thirty-six OECD nations,[^(62)](#bm05.html#en62) hindering the ability of governments to fund critical infrastructure and social programmes. In other words, these African countries are only about 50 per cent as effective in collecting taxes, the core revenue necessary to provide essential services, as countries that are core OECD member states. It is suggested by some that this low tax collection rate is due to a severe lack of trust, a fear that monies collected would not go towards their stated goals but instead would be skimmed off by corrupt officials.[^(63)](#bm05.html#en63) In the developed world, armies of accountants and lawyers navigate their way every year through the structuring and reporting of taxable income, tax payments and tax rebates.

The example of tax presents us with a repeating, predictable, transaction-driven multi-stakeholder process in which trust and transparency are important and where significant quantities of money are on the line. It should not be surprising that all of the ‘Big Four’ accounting firms, which control 80 per cent of the accounting market, are running initiatives that investigate the application of blockchain to tax.

Governments are engaged in the conversation as well. In a blockchain-enabled tax regimen, an audit could take seconds or minutes, and cost pennies (even taking into account the computing power required to run this blockchain), versus the sometimes millions of pounds and months of effort it currently requires. A government’s AI algorithm could contact the corporation’s AI, and use blockchain to securely and authoritatively generate response data to an inquiry without having to enlist legions of human actors on each side.

### **REVERSION TO DIRECT DEMOCRACY**

In addition to bolstering our voting and polling systems, the execution of a government’s laws, and the collection of revenue to support societal goals, what if the mechanisms of blockchain facilitated government by enabling the plebiscite? We elect representatives because the electorate does not have enough time or knowledge to consider the specifics of governance. Artificial intelligence and data analytics coupled to blockchain could lead to an augmented electorate, one in which the will of informed people, reliant on cryptographically trusted sources of information to guide their decisions, is directly expressed in a blockchain-provisioned government.

In our lifetimes, we could see truly direct democracy on a scale never before dreamed of in the days of Socrates and Demosthenes. The convergence of blockchain with other new-generation technologies could help our existing mechanisms of organising human society keep pace with the changes introduced by the technologies themselves.

Yet we still speak in terms of technology that exists today. We talk of blockchain capabilities that, although not ready to be deployed into everyday use right now, could be usable in a matter of months or at most a few years.

What are the next great leaps forward in technological and social evolution that may see a distributed ledger element or even a distributed ledger core, as we look further out in time? Our final chapter will postulate some views beyond the near tomorrow. *Here be dragons.*

## [Future horizons](#contents.html#rchapter11)

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

We are now going to reach a bit deeper, a bit further, and indulge in a few flights of fancy regarding the potential of what could be enabled with distributed ledgers. Prediction is notoriously difficult, even for professional futurists. I am reminded of the joke: economists have predicted eleven of the last three recessions.

What we discuss is, of necessity, not strictly limited to blockchain. It would be like trying to build a mobile phone using only silicon. Sure, it’s a critical element that goes into microprocessors, but you also need gold or tantalum for circuitry, plastic and treated glass for the touchscreen, lithium for the battery, and so on. Similarly, future tech will see blockchain systems inextricably linked with artificial intelligence, networked communications, advanced analytics and other technologies, perhaps even quantum computing, smart polymers and nanotech or some other capability that can only be dreamed of by science fabulists today.

As a child, one of my most treasured memories is of my father reading me bedtime stories of adventure, intrigue and fantasy. Indeed, with my own children, when bedtime stories – not streams of Twitch gamers and YouTube clips – capped the end of the day, our favourite bedtime story was of our own creation, with each person creating a line and then handing the narrative to the next, every night imagining a totally different cast of characters and fantastical realms.

Why do I share this personal story with you? Innovation science teaches us that the same wellspring of creativity that enables us to imagine new worlds as a child helps us create new ventures and envisage innovations that can change business and society. Conventional schooling frequently leads us away from our innate creativity and imagination; standardised tests leave little room for inventive answers. The society of tomorrow and the global problems that humanity must solve will require inventive and imaginative thinkers who harness this childlike creativity to invent a better future.

I find it fascinating that the mathematics and algorithm development that made blockchain possible was conducted by researchers trying to make rocket ships safer. Just as they helped us on our continuing journey into outer space, they have also enabled some journeys of the imagination that may create a better way for human society to work together for our collective good. Just as I explored new fantastical worlds as a child, let us imagine a few worlds of tomorrow in the blockchain galaxy . . .

### **THE CONNECTED WORLD**

In this world everything we see or touch will have a tiny bit of hardware that links it to ubiquitous communications networks. We see the antecedents of this future Connected World in our world of today, with the RFID tag and the proliferation of the ‘internet of things’ linking physical devices with digital networks. In the Connected World, every object and every device is tied together to provide insights and capabilities we can only dream of today. Imagine a world in which every object in a city streams data that can be used to forecast the weather better, or communicate about traffic congestion and help automatically reroute cars so that traffic flows smoothly; a world in which crimes are predicted using AI models drawing from numerous data feeds, allowing people to live with a greater sense of safety. Privacy, Big Brother, fear of hacking, fear of dictatorship, these all naturally flow from the vision of this same world, but blockchain can both make the Connected World possible, and provide better protections for our intimate data.

The Connected World also harnesses new levels of computational power. Today’s modern cars, for example, have more than seventy onboard computers. Imagine if, when these computers were not engaged in operating those vehicles, such as when the car is parked, they were participating in creating, maintaining and analysing ubiquitous blockchains woven through our everyday lives. The potential scale of this network is bigger than you may think. Massively distributed computing tied to the internet of things can be extended to all of the microprocessors that are now starting to pop up in electronics in all aspects of our lives, whether in our baby monitors and refrigerators or in our traffic lights and airconditioning systems.

I heard at a dinner with the former CEO of Intel that they have been effectively subsidising the distribution of connected microprocessors by making them available very inexpensively in many markets. They perceived their future as a business in the value of the data in the network riding across these chips, not in the hardware itself. In the 1980s, Intel famously pivoted their business model from selling memory chips to selling the microprocessors that actually form the heart of computers. As the world of microprocessors gets more competitive, with new manufacturing techniques that remove some of Intel’s prior advantages, they are looking to move to higher value-added, advanced data applications. Intel is pivoting again into a data-driven future that appears a great deal like the Connected World I am describing here, where their greatest value will be derived from enabling and empowering advanced networked data applications (like what happens with DApps on blockchains) versus simply providing the inner workings of the hardware connected to that network.

The Connected World not only brings us new sources of data about how we live and work, but also new abilities to compute this data in a highly distributed, dynamic fashion. This in turn can give us an environment that is more responsive to our needs and a society that more rapidly adapts to change.

### **THE AUGMENTED WORLD**

With the rise of digitally connected devices and better AI, a new kind of reality may emerge where little computers stay with us all the time and tell us things about the world around us. Dreamed of for decades, this augmented reality is increasingly coming into being, and should over the next several years begin to permeate our everyday existence.

Built in harmony with the Connected World, the Augmented World is one in which our lives are enriched by an interpreted and extrapolated stream of data that is played back across our field of vision – whether through implanted devices or digitally enabled spectacles. We gain a greater understanding of what is happening in the world around us in the process. The hidden data flows that surround us are now made explicit, which lets us have a greater insight into our world and make better decisions about it as a result. If we’re driving down the street and a heads-up display on our vision warns us that a car is swerving into our lane, we (and/or the AI driving our car) can take action to make us safely avoid an accident. If we are walking by a store that has something we need available on sale, our Augmented World can let us know about this product’s availability. If we are sick, or someone near us is sick, the Augmented World could detect symptoms and help recommend intervention. Physical safety, commerce, health, all could be enabled and improved in the Augmented World.

The cybersafety and cybersecurity of our personal data in this Augmented World can be assured by blockchain. There are many benefits that can be derived from the Augmented World, but in making data readily accessible and analysable, we also create cybersecurity risks. The benefits of safely pooling data derived from these augmented interactions, without creating undue risk, can be realised through specially engineered distributed ledger and artificial intelligence hybrid systems.

There is even the possibility that, in the Augmented World, our daily interactions with each other can be mediated and improved through the combination of distributed ledger data systems and personalised artificial intelligence engines. Our personal AI engine could learn our preferences and our needs, through the simple course of interacting with us on a daily basis and understanding how we behave and what we want. This AI engine could then automatically interact with other AI engines for other people, companies and systems, thus extending our ability to express ourselves at greater and greater scales. The security of these interactions can be provided for using advanced forms of blockchain and cryptography.

### **THE ADAPTED WORLD**

The Adapted World sees us enjoying the ultimate in customised goods and services. Templates for anything you can imagine could be stored in blockchains. Some would be open source or free to the world. Others might have a hefty royalty or fee for use. And these templates would be used to power nanofabrication machines. Instead of logging on to Amazon and ordering that new rice cooker or microwave, waiting days or a week for it to arrive, you’d access the template and have your supply of nanomachines manufacture it for you in minutes, with blockchain automatically tracing the use of the intellectual property and allocating and auditing payment for license rights.

### **THE DISTRIBUTED SOCIETY**

We already see multinational corporations providing their own private security forces – take for example cruise liners in international waters. Some are even launching their own digital currencies, giving billions of people who currently find themselves outside the conventional systems of government the ability to access the financial system, a critical function of society. Governments themselves have been thinking about how blockchain can be integrated into core government functions such as currency issuance, voting, distribution of government benefits and tax collection.

What if we shaped a Distributed Society where we see multiple governments and their citizens coming together in new and unexpected ways, around shared issues or concerns, with blockchain and blockchain-like governance mechanisms providing a means of accessing the collective intelligence of millions, or billions, and directing positive outcomes on a global scale?

What would this kind of a society look like? A hundred years ago we could only vaguely imagine how our world would be with electric vehicles, mobile communications, the ability to engineer genes and rockets that fly to other planets. Such fanciful ideas were the province of science fabulists like H.G. Wells and Jules Verne, but today they populate our physical world.

The Distributed Society seeks to enable something a bit more amorphous, intangible, but powerful nonetheless. It could unleash the capacity for true collective intelligence, which is the mechanism of drawing on the wisdom of hundreds or thousands of people to solve a problem or predict future events (and which preliminary research even today shows is far more powerful than individual intelligence). We may possibly approach the utopian ideal envisioned by Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, of an evolution of human culture to a higher order of consciousness by connecting people’s thoughts together in a harmonious fashion so that all may benefit. We could see the emergence of something we don’t yet have a name for, a new form of commerce and of society which improves the lives of humanity in ways we only vaguely begin to grasp today.

The future, as James Cameron (*Terminator*, *Avatar*) would have it, is what we make of it.

## [Glossary](#contents.html#rbm03)

**Algorithm**: A set of instructions for a computer program.

**AML**: AML, or anti-money laundering, refers to a set of regulations governing how institutions must protect against moving criminal funds. Often referred to in conjunction with Know Your Client (KYC, see [page 158](#bm03.html#page_158)).

**Artificial intelligence hybrid systems**: A new kind of AI system that combines people and machines, in theory delivering greater capabilities than can be found with either alone.

**Augmented reality**: Enhancing a human’s experience of reality with digital data, typically overlaid on top of an image of the ‘real’ world.

**Big data systems**: Systems to deal with data that exists in very large volumes, velocity (it changes rapidly) and variety (it’s very disparate). Such ‘big data’ is more complex to manage and calculate on than conventional data.

**Bitcoin**: A kind of decentralised digital currency, not delivered by a government but instead by a network of computers that runs on a specialised database called a ‘blockchain’ (see [page 156](#bm03.html#page_156)).

**Blockchain**: A distributed ledger (see [page 157](#bm03.html#page_157)) that uses a particular mechanism to ensure the integrity of the database, specifically block transactions with some form of mathematical proof of integrity of the transaction (such as proof of work; see [page 160](#bm03.html#page_160)). The first blockchain was created to run bitcoin.

**Byzantine consensus**: A mathematical means of ensuring parties can trust a result without having to trust each other.

**CBDC**: Central Bank Digital Currency – in this context, a kind of cryptographically secured currency issued and controlled by a government, in contrast to the digital currencies such as bitcoin that are issued by a network of individuals and companies.

**Command-and-control**: A highly centralised form of management or governance, whereby one individual or a small number of individuals makes all key decisions, and the rest of the organisation exists to execute those decisions.

**Consensus**: In this context, an algorithmic mechanism to reach a decision among many parties, such as whether or not to accept a new piece of data into a database such as a blockchain.

**Cryptography**: The mathematical discipline that allows plain text or easily read information to be obscured to unauthorised eyes, providing for greater security.

**DApps**: Distributed applications, for example those that run on the Ethereum protocol, meaning pieces of software that run on many linked computers in a distributed network, rather than a centralised piece of software.

**Derivatives**: Financial instruments that have their value based on other, underlying assets. They ‘derive’ value from these assets. For example, a simple derivative might be something called a ‘call option’, which means the right to buy a certain asset at a certain price within a certain period of time. Such ‘call options’ are used to make a bet about the price of the asset moving in a certain direction: up or down.

**Distributed ledger**: A database that has many copies that update each other automatically.

**Elliptic curve encryption**: A form of cryptography that is very efficient but secure, and is used in the bitcoin blockchain.

**Ethereum**: A blockchain protocol that provides an operating system on which distributed apps, or DApps, can run.

**Flat file**: A linear data stream (a string of text), where one character is in sequence after another (‘flat’), in contrast to a relational (two-dimensional) database such as an Oracle or SAP database, or a ‘big data’ database like Hadoop.

**Granularity**: Resolving information into very fine, or granular, detail, like focusing on a single grain of sand.

**Hash**: A mathematical operator that transforms one number into another number, often used in cryptography as a way of concealing the original number.

**High frequency trading**: High frequency trading, or HFT, is a type of financial trading conducted by superfast computer systems, typically in fractions of a second. HFT is believed to represent 50 per cent or more of all trading volume.[^(64)](#bm05.html#en64)

**Holacracy**: A form of distributed corporate management featuring a liquid labour market and no formal managers.

**ICO**: Initial coin offering, or ICO, was an effort to describe selling tokens to the public typically outside conventional regulatory scrutiny. As of writing, most regulatory bodies have either defined such offerings as securities offerings, and/or created a special category of securities offering for token sales.

**Immutable**: Unchangeable; permanent. Immutability is a core feature of blockchain.

**KYC**: Know Your Client, or KYC, is a set of regulations requiring institutions to verify who someone is, including collecting and verifying certain information like name and address. It is often discussed in conjunction with AML.

**Ledger**: A record-keeping system: in the context of blockchain, a database.

**Lightning**: A software refinement to blockchain than enables it to run much faster by breaking a complicated calculation down into smaller pieces running on a subset of network nodes, thus reducing the complexity needed to calculate a particular result.

**Matrix management**: A form of corporate management that organises people around a particular project or task, and sets aside the traditional organisational chart in terms of who directs their work for that project. They still formally receive their performance review and management from the conventional organisation chart, hence are in a ‘matrix’ that includes both their task supervisor and their formal boss.

**Merkle tree**: A mathematical construct for a stream of data that builds on itself so that you can ensure the integrity of the data; if someone tries to change one historical part of the ‘tree’, the entire edifice becomes corrupted, telling you that there was an effort to make a change.

**Mining**: The process of adding new entries into a cryptocurrency ledger, in the process creating new units of that currency. Mining consists of engaging in complex mathematical calculations.

**Nanomachine**: A microscopic machine, often highly configurable and potentially self-replicating.

**Network theory**: In the context of computer systems, the science of how an arrangement of devices are connected to each other.

**Node**: A server attached to a blockchain network that has the software for that network loaded onto it.

**OBSF**: Oxford Blockchain Strategy Framework.

**Open-source**: A way of developing software whereby the source code (or underlying instructions) is made available freely, but to use it, people need to commit to providing back any improvements that they make to the code so that all may share in the benefits of the commons.

**Pilot project**: A small-scale test of a technology.

**Pooling money**: Assembling disparate bits of money into a single unit, or pool, that in turn might be used for purposes that benefit from the scale that is thus created.

**Proof of work**: A specific mechanism to achieve Byzantine consensus that relies on certain mathematics that is difficult to do, and that forms a foundation of how the bitcoin blockchain conducts transactions. The difficulty of the maths serves to deter cyberattackers, who can’t simply overwhelm a network by throwing a massive attack on it (known as a ‘Sybil Attack’). Other protocols might use other consensus algorithms, like proof of stake.

**Protocol**: The set of instructions that allows nodes (participating computers) in a network to talk to each other and exchange information.

**Provenance**: The chain of ownership or title of an asset, ‘proving’ from where it comes.

**Quantum cryptography**: An advanced form of encryption that uses quantum computers.

**Relational database**: A database where the data elements are described in terms of their relationship with each other. For example, a birthday database would have the date of someone’s birth, one data element, cross-referenced to the name of an individual, another data element.

**Ripple**: A blockchain-based system to settle, exchange money, and handle remittances in an international network. Its underlying protocol is called XRP.

**Safe harbour**: A means by which a regulator provides guidance to a company about a permitted set of activities, typically so that the company might explore a new or experimental area of activity.

**Securities**: Financial instruments that are used to represent a particular asset and which can be traded.

**Settlement and clearing**: The act of transferring ownership of a security from one party to another. Settlement refers to the payment of value (money or other) for a security, and clearing is the updating of the records that reflect ownership of that security.

**Shadow banking**: A non-bank financial system that engages in banking-like activities but is outside conventional banking regulations.

**Smart contract**: A set of instructions that are followed by a software program on a blockchain when certain conditions are met.

**Taylorism**: A form of management espoused by Frederik Winslow Taylor that focuses on efficiency and strict time utilisation. Popularised by American industrialist Henry Ford among others.

**Token**: Tokens have a few meanings in the context of blockchain: they are (1) a store and mechanism for transfer of value; and (2) a digital security that is regulated by financial authorities.

**Tokenised exchange**: Variously (1) a marketplace on which blockchain tokens can be bought and sold; and (2) a securities exchange that uses blockchain tokens to better automate settlement and clearing of traded securities (not necessarily or exclusively blockchain token securities).

**Value chain**: A set of interrelated activities that create (or ‘add’) value to a product.

**Value transfer**: A means by which one party provides funds to a second party, so that the second party may transfer it to a third party. Western Union would be such a second party, accepting money from one person in one location and providing it to another person in another location.

**Windhover principles**: A self-regulatory set of guidelines adopted by some cryptocurrency companies to explain how they would comply with AML and KYC regulations in handling currencies such as bitcoin.
