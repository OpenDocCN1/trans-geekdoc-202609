# # 4 European options

# ## 4.1 Introduction

A European option taken out at current time $t$ gives the owner the right (but not the obligation) to do something when the option *matures* at time $T$. This could, for example, be the right to buy or sell stocks at a particular *strike* price. The option would course be exercised if it was in the owner's interest to do so. a single asset *vanilla put option*, strike price $E$ and *expiry* time $T$, gives the owner the right at time $T$ to sell a particular asset for $E$. If the asset is worth $S_T$ at maturity the value of the put option at maturity, known as the *payoff*, is thus $max(E - S_T, 0)$. By contrast a European *call option*, with strike price and expiry time $T$, gives the owner the right at time $T$ to buy an asset for $E$; the payoff of a call option is $max(S_T - E, 0)$.

The owner of an American option has the right (but not the obligation) to exercise the option *at any time* from current time $t$ to maturity. These options are extra flexibility. The simple single asset American put has no analytic solution and requires finite-difference and lattice methods to estimate its value. Many European options, on the other hand, take the form of a *relatively easy* definite integral from time which it is possible to close a complete form solution. The valuation of multiset European options, depend on a large number of underlying assets, is more complicated but can be achieved by using Monte Carlo simulation to compute the required multidimensional integral.

The expected *current value* of a single asset European option will depend on the current asset price at time $t$, $S$, the duration of the option, $	au = T - t$, the strike price, $E$, the riskless interest rate, $r$, and the probability density function of the underlying asset at maturity, $p(S_T)$.
