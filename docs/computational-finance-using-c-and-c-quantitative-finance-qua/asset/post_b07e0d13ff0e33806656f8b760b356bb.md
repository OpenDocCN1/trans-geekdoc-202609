# 7 Other financial derivatives

## 7.1 Introduction

In the preceding sections of the book we have only dealt with the valuation of equity derivatives. We are now going to consider some of the other types of trades such as:

- Interest rate derivatives
- Foreign exchange derivatives
- Credit derivatives

A selection of these trades will be used by the C# portfolio pricing example in Chapter 8.

## 7.2 Interest rate derivatives

It is not possible to make real profit without risk. For example, if we (without risk) invest £1 in a bank account, then for interest, the total number of pounds at time T will be $1+\Delta I(t,T)$, where $\Delta I(t,T)$ is the amount of interest accrued from t to time T. Since our investment grows by the riskless rate, the real value must still be £1, so: $DF(t,T)[1+\Delta I(t,T)]=1$ where $DF(t,T)$ is the discount factor from t to T.

### Continuously compounded rate

When continuous compounding is used $1+\Delta I(t,T)=\exp\{R(t)(T-t)\}$, where $R(t,T)$ is the continuously compounded rate between t and T years. We have thus:

$$DF(t,T)\exp\{R(t)(T-t)\}=1 \quad (7.2.1)$$

so the discount factor is

$$DF(t,T)=\exp\{-R(t)(T-t)\} \quad (7.2.2)$$

and the continuously compounded rate is:

$$R(t,T)=-\frac{\log(DF(t,T))}{T-t} \quad (7.2.3)$$

### Simply compounded rate

When simple compounding is used $\Delta I(t, T) = L(t, T)(T - t)$, where $L(t, T)$ is the simply compounded rate between time $t$ and $T$. Thus,

$$DF(t, T) = \{1 + L(t, T)(T - t)\}$$

and so the simply compounded rate:

$$L(t, T) = \frac{1/DF(t, T)-1}{T} \quad (7.2.4)$$

### Forward rate agreement

A Forward Rate Agreement (FRA) is a contract between two counterparties (referred here as $A$ and $B$), in which one counterparty (say $A$) agrees to pay interest at the (variable) spot rate, while the other agrees to pay a fixed rate. The agreement starts at time $T_s$ and ends at the maturity $T_m$, at which the counterparties settle the amount owed. If $P$ is the principal, then at time $T_m$, the contract has the following value:

$$V(T_m) = P\{(T_m - T_s)K - L(T_s, T_m)(T_m - T_s)\} \quad (7.2.5)$$

where $K$ is the agreed fixed rate, and $L(T_s, T_m)$ is the simply compounded rate between times $T_s$ and $T_m$. From Eq.(7.2.4) we have:

$$L(T_s, T_m) = \frac{1/DF(T_s, T_m)-1}{T_m - T_s} \quad (7.2.6)$$

The value of the FRA to $A$ at time $t \le T_m$ is therefore:

$$FRA(t) = DF(t, T_m)V(T_m)$$

which means that

$$FRA(t) = DF(t, T_m)P\{(T_m - T_s)K - L(T_s, T_m)(T_m - T_s)\} \quad (7.2.7)$$

Using $DF(t, T_m) = DF(t, T_s)DF(T_s, T_m)$ we can rewrite it as:

$$FRA(t) = DF(t, T_s)P\{(T_m - T_s)K - L(T_s, T_m)(T_m - T_s)\} \quad (7.2.8)$$

The value of $K$ that sets $FRA(t)$ to zero is the forward rate between times $T_s$ and $T_m$, and it is denoted by $F(t, T_s, T_m)$. From Eq.(7.2.9):

$$F(t, T_s, T_m) = \frac{1}{T_m - T_s} \frac{DF(t, T_s)}{DF(T_s, T_m)} \quad (7.2.9)$$

Combining Eqs.(7.2.8) and (7.2.10) we can express the value of the FRA as:

$$FRA(t) = DF(t, T_m)P\tau\{K - F(t, T_s, T_s + \tau)\} \quad (7.2.10)$$

where $\tau = T_m - T_s$ is known as the tenor of the FRA, and $T_s$ is the reset time for forward rate $F(t, T_s, T_s + \tau)$.

## 7.2.2 Interest rate swap

Interest Rate Swaps (IRS) are very common financial instruments—it is not uncommon for 80 percent of the trades in a portfolio to be IRS deals. Here we will provide a description of some of the characteristics of interest rate swaps; more detail can be found in Hull (2003).

A vanilla IRS consists of a strip of FRA trades, each FRA starting when the previous FRA finishes. The maturity date of the IRS corresponds to the maturity date of the FRA.

Let the start of the FRAs be $t_i, i = 0, \dots, n-1$, and the maturity times of the FRAs be $t_i, i = 1, \dots, n$; note that the FRA start corresponds to the forward rate reset times, and the maturity times correspond to the FRA payment times. We will now adopt the (common) convention of calling the trades *swaps*, so an IRS is made up of a number of swaps.

When the counterparty A pays the fixed rate and receives the floating rate (from A perspective) it is a payer IRS. Alternatively, if A receives the fixed rate and pays the floating rate, the term is a receiver IRS.

The value of an IRS at time $t_k$, where $t_{k-1} < t < t_k$, will be considered. We will assume that discount factors used to compute the forward rates and those used to discount the coupon payments are associated with the same yield curve. Using Eq. (7.2.10) we have:

$$IRS(t) = CDF(t, t_k) + \sum_{i=k+1}^n DF(t, t_i)\tau_i[K - F(t, t_{i-1}, t_{i-1} + \tau_i)] \quad (7.2.11)$$

where $C$ is the *next coupon payment* after current time $t$ (this occurs at time $t_k$), and $\tau_i$ is the tenor of the $i$-th swap which starts at time $t_{i-1}$ and ends at time $t_i$.

Note that the next coupon payment $C$ for the swapping starting at $t_{k-1}$ and maturing at $t_k$ is already known with certainty at time $t$ because the forward rate $F(t, t_{k-1}, t_k)$ was reset in the past; $t_{k-1} < t$.

We will now rewrite Eq. (7.2.11) as:

$$IRS(t) = CDF(t, t_k) + FXD(t) - FLT(t) \quad (7.2.12)$$

where $FXD(t)$, the time value of the fixed leg is:

$$FXD(t) = PK \sum_{i=k+1}^n DF(t, t_i)\tau_i \quad (7.2.13)$$

and $FLT(t)$, the time value of the floating leg is:

$$FLT(t) = P \sum_{i=k+1}^n DF(t, t_i)\tau_i F(t, t_{i-1}, t_{i-1} + \tau_i) \quad (7.2.14)$$
