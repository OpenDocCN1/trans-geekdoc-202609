However, our location in the world is processed by place cells, grid cells, and head direction cells in the brain. To cover large part of object size (33), 38 of 48 different objects from different categories were shown (Fig. S1). For images which destroyed object identity but maintained low-level statistics, the 48 objects were split into two blocks of images and four blocks of scrambling, each labeled with index (1–4) to determine the direction of evoked changes in each visual volume (Fig. 1b).

Animals use visual objects to guide navigation-related behaviors. However, visual-object preferences have yet to be described in the mouse brain, limiting our understanding of how visual-objects affect spatial navigation system. Using functional ultrasound, we identified brain areas that were preferentially activated by visual objects. While visual cortex did not show a preference, areas associated with spatial preference were activated by objects. Electrophysiological recordings in the dorsal cortex using HD system confirmed for fast-spiking neurons, visual objects increased rates of HD cells aligned with but decreased activity in HD cells for other directions.

First, we examined the response in anesthetized, mice to all eight blocks (i.e., combined and scrambled stimuli). We used a mouse that works for both (Fig. 1C). After antagonistically stimulating the brain into +100, we observed evoked visual activity in two brain areas (Fig. 1D). We computed visual object sensitivity (VOS), to quantify preferences of visual objects over scrambled stimuli. We found a small number of brain areas with statistics that showed a significant preference for visual objects: the postculub (Pscb), Parasihubular, and area rostralis (Fig. 1D). By contrast, many areas showed no preference for visual objects over scrambled stimuli. For example, superior colulus (SC), we responded significantly to scrambled objects (Fig. 1D, F). We found similar results in areas that responded preferentially to visual objects, we ranked brain-wide areas responding to scrambled objects (Fig. S2A).

The spatial navigation system comprises neurons whose firing rate is modulated by the direction an animal is facing and location in environment (3). During locomotion, the spatial navigation system is modulated by visual inputs (6), while visual objects can serve as environmental markers (8). Furthermore, an anchor in the turn can lead to instability (12). Thus, visual-objects can anchor the internal representation of space (14).

In the rodent brain, studies on visual-object preference have been performed primarily in primates (19). This has revealed a series of brain areas, arranged in a hierarchical manner and referred to as the 'visual system', which areas are sensitive to high-level representations of visual objects (19, 20). In rodents, lateral visual cortex appears to excite vertical-stream-like properties (21–23), and perform neurons can respond to turn visual objects (24, 25, 26).
