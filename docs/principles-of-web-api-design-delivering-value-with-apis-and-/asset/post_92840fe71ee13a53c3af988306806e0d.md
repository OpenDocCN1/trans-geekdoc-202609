Following is a summary of the common sticky note types—and the colors typically designated for each type—that are used throughout an EventStorming session:

- **Business event (orange):** The result of an action or policy that indicates progression through a workflow or process
- **Hotspot (bright pink):** Unknown or missing data that requires research and follow-up after the session.
- **Command (dark blue):** An action taken by a user or system.
- **Aggregate (large, pale yellow):** Behavior or logic that executes as a result of a command and behavior often results in one or more events. In domain-driven design (DDD), Aggregates are defined as units of transactional consistency. In EventStorming, this is a higher-level representation of workflows, state machines, and other behavior.
- **Policy (lilac):** The triggering event or motivation for a new command is executed and is always required. It acts as the bridge or glue between an event and a command. Policies may start with the word when or whenever.

- **External system (pale pink):** Systems outside of the solution. These may be external to the team internal to the organization or a third-party system. They are best thought of as aggregates outside of the group's control.
- **User interface (white):** A user interface that will offer one or more roles the capability to execute a command against an aggregate.
- **User (yellow, small):** A specific role that is interacting with the system, typically via a UI, but perhaps as a result of an automated call, or other mechanism.

Start by adding commands (blue stickies) to capture what actions are taken by a system or user that will result in one or more of the identified business domain events. Commands are sent to aggregates, to capture those as well. Business rules, often phrased as “when xyz happens, . . .” may be captured as policies (lilac). Hot pink stickies are used as hotspot indicators where more information is required.

## The Benefits of EventStorming

Alignment between stakeholders and development teams is achieved through EventStorming. There are five additional benefits of conducting a session:

1. Shared understanding of requirements
2. Shared understanding of business rules, and constraints
3. Establishment of a ubiquitous language
4. Identification of unknowns that require follow-up
5. Identification of boundaries within the solution

There are circumstances when EventStorming is most effective:

- Prior to API and microservice design and implementation
- Prior to software design and development
- After clearly documenting desired outcomes, typically through the use of
- When all roles are represented in the session
- When embarking on a significant scope of effort or one that spans multiple

There are also circumstances when the value of EventStorming session may be reduced. To avoid spending unnecessary time in an EventStorming session, consider these factors that contribute to an ineffective session:

- The process is well known and documented, as results will likely produce the same
- The scope of the problem is small enough that the identified business requirements are sufficient and complete
- The business requirements have not yet been identified. In this case, start with constructing job, covered in Chapter 3, to clearly define the desired outcomes and parties involved
- Business stakeholders cannot attend or not see in the exercise. While development teams may still conduct a session, doing so may lead to a process based on too many technical assumptions that do not meet business needs
- Software delivery has begun, and delivery dates are fixed. If the teams are early in the process, the output of the session may be used to make software architectural and design adjustments prior to a release. Otherwise, teams will be forced to proceed with existing decisions rather than the insights

## Who Should Be Involved?

Including the right mix of attendees is critical for a successful EventStorming session. A session must involve a few representatives from various roles and responsibilities. It should be kept to no more than twelve people to ensure full participation by all attendees. Larger groups can prevent assertive attendees from engaging in the session.

Optional participants that may raise the group size should be considered for inclusion on a case-by-case basis to avoid overloading the session with too many voices. With dozens of people often slow down or result in participants looking down at their phone or checking email. Smaller groups benefit from the need for smaller space when conducting an in-person session. Avoid observers whenever possible, as they dont add value and may distract from the session.
