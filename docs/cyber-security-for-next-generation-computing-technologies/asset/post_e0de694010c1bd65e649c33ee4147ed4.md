## 12.4.5 INTERNET OF THINGS SPOOFING ATTACK

The Internet of Things was created in 1998 by Kevin Ashton. He claimed that an IoT computer is good for communication without human interference. He collected data from things that are connected to wireless devices in terms of smart devices like smart watches, smart appliances, and smart cities. Moreover, IoT cities utilize the cloud networking, which may start a new epoch of changes in Internet networking, as big data interconnected with clouds to organize and manage the city hub. A big network fact carries many attacks in different scenarios, as network attacks, distributed attacks, spoofing, intrusion detection systems, flood attacks, etc., as discussed in the literature [43].

Providing good network service calls for secure communication, without which attackers can attack systems and, for example, steal confidential information during remote surgery, perhaps leading the patient’s death. A spoofed attack works with the electromagnetic wave spectrum and is directly connected with hackers and military action. It is illegal activity and may be responsible for starting information warfare (IW). A spoof provides a manipulated GNSS data attack on the receiver end; as the result, the signal is altered in velocity and real-time interactions on the receiver side [44].

The spoofing objective may be to eliminate navigation reliability in a confidential area, for example, to falsify the supposed location of the receiver. In this case, the receiver would detect this spoofing attack and transmit a counterattack to maintain navigational ability and reliability. A great amount of the literature is focused on anti-spoofing methods [45], routing-based methods and non-routing based methods.

In the routing method, spoofing packets are entered into the signal network. In this way, the prefix is entered in a filter on the signal network. Routers use ternary content addressable memory (TCAM) to find IP routes to route sockets quickly, but TCAM has limited memory space, which increases the robustness of routers and ultimately signal flow, thus speeding delivery. However, another key that gets the attention of practitioners is the security and privacy of smart grid citizens. The important thing is how to strongly secure all. Security and privacy are not only linked to basic human rights but are the responsibility of smart grid governance as part of the right of citizenship in a smart city [50]. To resolve this cyber security issue, the first priority is to have a proper strategy against data attacks (DTA). If the data attacker attacks power grid, the attack on meters is remedied with fundamental limits of meter measurement strategies, which are intended to work aggressively. In the meantime, the governors of the grid station may detect the malicious data attacks and react to the interruption [51].

In brief, the route of attacks on smart cities is very different from communication networks compared to the Internet. An attacker's goal is not only to obtain illegal information but to hold on smart area hubs. Most attacks are on energy management systems (EMS). In this case, the attacker receives information from external meters, which can be used within a few minutes to immobilize the power grid and revise estimations of the system state. An attacker that can hold the power grid stations can create false meter data to the grid EMS meter. This action confuses the EMS of the control center, which makes faulty decisions regarding the analysis, transmission, or calculation of incidents.

Poison data injection is another way to attack a system. In this method, attackers must have information about the system configuration topology of the smart grid. These configuration settings change systematically, and, in these cases, the attacker won't have information about the system configuration settings. All information normally is kept secret and under the observation of control-center-regulated [52].

## 12.5 PRIVACY AND MANAGEMENT IN THE INTELLIGENT TRANSPORTATION SYSTEMS

The intelligent transportation system works dynamically in nonlinear sophisticated systems. In these types of networks, they depend on a source of information, which is provided on a real timeline. Multi-agent cooperators work in ad-hoc-based networks, which may mislead the information related to the task and put ITS management and privacy at risk [53].

Attackers could be tempted to the authorized supervision and fairly confidential information. These malicious actors can enable attackers to easily access top-secret records internally and externally. An external attacker may constantly control the connection and examine traffic information, but the information cannot be interpreted. On the other hand, internal attackers, such as malicious agents or employees, have full authority to control public and private information of transportation networks.
