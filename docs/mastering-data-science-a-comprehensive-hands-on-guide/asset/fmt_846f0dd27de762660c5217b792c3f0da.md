
ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) and Seasonal Decomposition are two fundamental techniques in time series analysis. They serve different purposes: `ARIMA` is primarily a forecasting model, while seasonal decomposition helps uncover underlying patterns in the data.

## 24.4.1 ARIMA: AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average

`ARIMA` (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) is a widely-used statistical model for time series forecasting that helps to predict future values based on historical data. It combines three main components: Autoregression (`AR`), Integration (`I`), and Moving Average (`MA`).

### Autoregression (AR)

Autoregression models the relationship between the current value of a time series and its past values, or lagged observations. This component assumes that the past values of the series can provide useful information for predicting future values. For example, predicting today's temperature based on the temperatures of the last few days can be done using the `AR` component of the model.

### Integration (I)

The integration component of `ARIMA` deals with making the time series stationary by removing trends or seasonality. Stationarity means that the statistical properties of the series, such as mean and variance, do not change over time. To achieve stationarity, the series is differenced by subtracting the previous observation from the current one. For instance, if the series shows a seasonal or trend-based pattern, integration would involve calculating the difference between consecutive data points, which can help eliminate the trend or seasonality.

### Moving Average (MA)

The moving average component models the relationship between an observation and the residual errors (forecast errors) from a moving average model applied to lagged observations. This helps adjust predictions based on previous forecasting errors. For example, in time series prediction, if a model has consistently overestimated or underestimated the data in the past, the `MA` component helps correct this error based on previous prediction inaccuracies.

### ARIMA Model Order (p, d, q)

`ARIMA` is specified by three parameters:

- `p` (Autoregressive component): The number of lag terms (previous time steps) used in the model. A higher value of `p` means using more past data points to predict the current observation.
- `d` (Differencing): The number of differencing steps required to make the time series stationary. If the series is already stationary, `d` is typically set to `0`.
- `q` (Moving Average component): The number of lagged forecast errors to include in the model. A higher `q` indicates that past forecast errors have a greater influence on the current prediction.

### ARIMA Workflow

1. Check Stationarity: Before fitting the model, it is essential to check whether the time series is stationary. This can be done visually through plots or using statistical tests such as the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (`ADF`) test. If the series is not stationary, apply differencing to make it stationary.
2. Determine p, d, q Values: Once the series is stationary, the next step is to determine the optimal values of `p`, `d`, and `q`. This can be achieved through techniques like examining Autocorrelation Function (`ACF`) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (`PACF`) plots. `ACF` helps identify the moving average order (`q`), while `PACF` helps determine the autoregressive order (`p`).
3. Fit the Model: Using the determined `p`, `d`, and `q` values, the `ARIMA` model is fitted to the time series data. This involves estimating the parameters of the model that minimize the forecast error.
4. Forecast: Once the `ARIMA` model is fitted, it can be used to make forecasts. The model provides predictions for future data points, and these can be used for decision-making, planning, and analysis.

`ARIMA` is particularly effective for forecasting in stationary time series with linear trends and seasonal patterns. However, it is important to note that `ARIMA` may not perform well with time series that exhibit non-linear trends or complex seasonality, in which case other models like `SARIMA` or machine learning techniques may be more suitable.

Example with Python:

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

# Fit ARIMA model
model = ARIMA(data['sales'], order=(1, 1, 1))  # p=1, d=1, q=1
results = model.fit()

# Summary of the model
print(results.summary())

# Forecasting
forecast = results.forecast(steps=12)  # Forecast next 12 periods
print(forecast)
```

Advantages of `ARIMA`: Effective for short-term forecasting. Captures trends and noise in time series data.

Limitations of `ARIMA`: Assumes stationarity. Struggles with highly seasonal data without extensions (e.g., `SARIMA` for seasonal data).

## 24.4.2 Seasonal Decomposition

Seasonal decomposition is an essential technique in time series analysis, used to break down a time series into its underlying components. It helps identify the separate influences of trends, seasonal patterns, and residual (noise) within the data, making it easier to analyze and forecast.

Trend refers to the long-term movement or direction in the time series data, such as an overall increase or decrease over time. For example, a company's revenue might show an upward trend, reflecting growth over several years.

Seasonality represents recurring patterns or cycles that occur at regular intervals, such as spikes in sales during holiday seasons or annual fluctuations in energy demand during winter months. These patterns repeat at consistent time periods, such as daily, monthly, or yearly.

Residual (Noise) is the random variation or irregular fluctuations in the time series that cannot be explained by the trend or seasonality. These are the random factors or anomalies that occur in the data but do not follow any predictable pattern.

### Types of Decomposition:

There are two primary types of decomposition:

Additive Decomposition: This model assumes that the time series is the sum of its components:

`y(t)=Trend + Seasonality + Residual`

Additive decomposition is most suitable for time series data where the magnitude of the fluctuations remains relatively constant over time. For example, sales might fluctuate consistently with some seasonal effects, but the fluctuations do not grow or shrink as time progresses.

Multiplicative Decomposition: In this model, the components are multiplied together: `y(t)=Trend × Seasonality × Residual`

This approach is used when the seasonal fluctuations increase or decrease over time, such as during periods of rapid growth where the magnitude of seasonal effects becomes more pronounced. A typical example might be the exponential growth of a business where seasonal peaks become larger over the years.

By understanding the decomposition of time series data, analysts can better understand the underlying patterns and more accurately forecast future values.

Example with Python:

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

# Decompose the time series
result = seasonal_decompose(data['sales'], model='additive', period=12)

# Plot the components
result.plot()
plt.show()
```

### Interpreting Seasonal Decomposition

Interpreting seasonal decomposition involves understanding how data is split into different components: trend, seasonality, and residuals. Each of these components gives unique insights that help to understand the underlying behavior of the dataset.

Trend: The trend component reveals the long-term direction or growth pattern of the data, indicating whether the values are generally increasing or decreasing over time. This could represent a range of factors, such as an upward economic trend or seasonal demand increases. For instance, if a dataset shows monthly sales figures, the trend component would help identify whether sales are growing or shrinking over a year.
