# ABSTRACT

**Background:** Increasing an individuals' ability to focus on concrete, specific detail, thus reducing the tendency toward broad, decontextualized abstractions about the world is a goal. However, empirical investigation of the impact of treatment-specific CBT on outcomes remains. We evaluated whether the specificity of patient dialogue predicted (a) end-treatment symptoms and (b) session completion in patients with common mental health disorders.

**Methods:** We used a deep learning model to score the specificity of patient dialogue in transcripts from 353,614 recorded CBT-based sessions for common mental health disorders, derived from UK NHS services. We included 65,030 patients (n = 478, female = 241) aged 18-94 years (n = 34,569, SD = 12.35). Descriptive scores were the most common (39.1%) primary finding. Primary outcome was end-treatment response on the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Secondary outcome was number of sessions.

**Results:** Linear-effects models demonstrated that patient specificity predicted lower post-treatment symptoms on the PHQ-9, although size and direction of the effect were associated with therapeutic activity. Higher specificity was associated with a greater number of sessions.

**Limitations:** We were unable to infer causation from our design. **Conclusions:** Effect sizes were small, although specificity was observed across common mental health disorders. Further studies are needed to explore whether encouraging patient specificity during CBT may provide enhancement of treatment attendance and treatment effects.

# 1. Introduction

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is our leading treatment for common mental health disorders. However, CBT fails to remission for 30-50% of treated individuals (Clark et al., 2018), leaving many with significant symptoms and distress. Increasing the usage of therapy techniques in favor of good outcomes is a matter of vital importance to return function.

In both traditional and third-wave CBTs, one can potentially improve mechanistic aspect of change movement from an abstract, generalized style type, to concrete, specific processing (Beck and Haigh, 2014; van der Velden et al., 2015). A variety of treatment components facilitate patient specificity. Mindfulness experiences aim to improve ability to focus on (concrete, sensory) detail and decrease from abstract patterns (worry). Oldandy and Igesan (2020), cognitive restructuring aims to generalize beliefs about the world and specific contexts, environments, and techniques require help to identify specific instances and to focus on concrete-specific details (Craske et al., 2014). It is important to illustrate (e.g., with argument and personal history) that the response function of) claims made on the basis of such events are only applicable within context (when deciding who is going to do the deeds). A focus on the specific is important for the context to be contextualized beliefs of worthiness, jeopardy, failure, and vulnerability which drive common mental health disorders (Beck and Haigh, 2014). To this science-driven interventions have been offered as adjusts to CBT to enhance specific cognition, cognition, regulation (Watkins et al., 2012), overgenerised autobiographical memory (Hallford et al., 2021a), and mental imagery (Powell et al., 2021) as a training modality. Similarly, processed-focused adaptations of CBT have integrated a greater focus on being concrete (Clark et al., 2016). Machine learning techniques have been examined lately as a linear process, partly due to the quantity and structural format of such data.

Machine learning techniques can provide insights into various factors during therapeutic exchanges between therapist and patient, at scale (Chekroud et al., 2021). Recent research has used deep learning models to identify features of treatment (e.g., topic of discussion, duration of session) and features on outcomes (Ewbank et al., 2020), allowing to identify features of CBT success across large numbers of patients.

The quality of information within therapeutic interactions may determine the degree of patient specificity in post-treatment outcomes. We expected that increased specificity during therapy period would predict better outcomes at least for individuals with severe symptoms, and more concrete processing specificity with CBT, or more specific detail during therapeutic activities (e.g., when designing experiments) may lead to improved solving (Sloan et al., 2021). We also wanted to examine whether specificity is more important in some tasks than others, and specificity within different feature categories (Ewbank et al., 2020), including goal setting. Our aim was to determine whether patient specificity predicted treatment outcome of CBT sessions or not; while CBT tasks require the individual to provide specific information, those who are inclined to specificity may find CBT easier, and complete a greater number of sessions.

We trained a deep learning model to quantify the specificity of patient dialogue within internet-enabled CBT. Our primary hypothesis was that specificity of patient dialogue would predict recovery, as well as specificity of patients would predict better outcomes (PHQ-9). We found that specificity is linked to (Ewbank et al., 2021) were deployed by both therapist and patient. We also wanted to evaluate specificity in internet-enabled sessions (Hallford et al., 2021). Our second hypothesis was that specificity of patient would predict greater number of sessions.

# 2. Method

### 2.1. Approvals
Cambridge University Ethics committee granted ethical approval (2017).

### 2.2. Participants
Participants (n = 65,030) were receiving treatment from NHS Access to Therapies. Inclusion criteria were age < 18 and registration with a general practitioner; Exclusion criteria were experiencing cognitive impairment, brain injury or dementia; history of psychosis; low literacy levels; visual impairment preventing using device.

### 2.3. Internet-CBT
CBT was administered via instant messaging in online room by trained therapist (www.iesapt.org). Sessions were approximately 45-60 minutes in length, and on a weekly basis.

### 2.4. Specifying
We analyzed 353,614 internet-enabled CBT sessions completed June 2012 to May 2021. Each therapy transcript were submitted by the user, and text entries were submitted by the therapist. The therapy transcript was elicited for linguistic features that could indicate (e.g., “can you tell me of an example/the task’s time you tell me this happened?”, “how do you know that is true?”). We identified specificity in patient responses during different types of task (Ewbank et al., 2020). Our results were clinically significant (see Fig. 1). It showed that, for example, being specific when completing therapy tasks was more important for outcome than specifying in the sessions. We separated specificity by the tasks elicited during agenda setting, homework setting, goal setting, planning for the future, discussing concerns and worries, completing CBT tasks (included any cognitive or behavioural activities, homework, education), assessing risk of suicide, summarizing the session, or other tasks. Full details on the different tasks are in Ewbank et al. (2020).

Patient responses were classified using a supervised deep learning model, which predicted whether the response was specific. The model trained on a dataset of describing patient experiences (10,000 entries), written by individuals of interest. These entries were then scored as specific or non-specific by a human rater using criteria for the Autobiographical Memory Test (Williams and Benedict, 2008). We used 800 examples to train our model and 1000 to validate model performance using overlapping sets. The remaining 1000 entries were used as the held-out test set. We further validated the model on another separate dataset of out-of-test describing patient experiences (1632 entries) written by individuals.
