## What Is Blockchain?

https://www.shutterstock.com/it/image-vector/bitcoin-cryptocurrency-blockchain-technology-money-transfer-773006551

Blockchain technology makes it possible to carry out transactions (such as payments) and transmit information (contracts, sales) in a secure manner and without an intermediary (bank, credit institution, etc.) The resulting information is stored in chronological order and time-stamped. When a set of operations is saved, the previous set of operations becomes unalterable, and so on. Thus, all the transactions recorded on the blockchain, as they are passed, are kept, viewable by all network participants, and tamper-proof.

There are many types of blockchains. Two of them are particularly popular: the one with the so-called “proof of work “consensus and the “proof of stake” consensus. To function, blockchains need validators, that is to say, entities that will check the register of operations that have taken place on the network.

Blockchain can be applied in other industries apart from cryptocurrencies.

## Introduction to Blockchain Technology

The first term you’ll hear when delving into the world of cryptocurrencies is “blockchain.” To understand Bitcoin, cryptocurrencies in general, and the future of decentralized computing, it is critical to understand the principles of blockchain technology.

The blockchain is the framework, or “protocol,” on which Bitcoin functions, as well as many other cryptocurrencies and emerging technologies. Blockchain can be termed as a public ledger that is distributed across many computers throughout the world. Each Bitcoin transaction that happens is recorded and added to the blockchain as a new “block” and also linked to the previous transaction with a special timestamp, like a link in a long “chain.”

How the Blockchain Works

 

![Introduction to Blockchain technology | Set 1 - GeeksforGeeks](Images/image00123.jpeg)

T o make it easier to understand everything, we need to give a concrete example of the most typical use case concerning this technology: the transfer of value (or money) from one user to another. To transfer money from one person to another, the method most of us are used to is wire transfers; what happens, very simply, is that each bank keeps registers in which it reports the total balance of each account holder and the movements made to and from that particular account.

When I make a transfer (let's say € 100) from my account to that of another person, my bank tracks the movement and marks on its register a transaction for an amount equal to - € 100 from my account, then scales this sum from my total balance and sends the money to the bank of the recipient. This, in turn, will do the same by marking (but this time on its register) a movement of + € 100 and adding it to the total balance of the current account holder beneficiary of the transfer. 

Exactly the same thing happens with a blockchain, except that the register does not hold it by a bank (as we have illustrated in the previous chapter.) Instead, all the computers participating in the network have a copy of this document; for example, when I send a Bitcoin (BTC) to a person, all the computers participating in the network mark the movement on the register and deduct 1 BTC from my account while, at the same time, add up the same amount for the benefit of the recipient. 

The first question that arises at this point concerns the fact that, since the register is not only shared by all the computers on the network but is also public (that is, it is accessible to everyone in consultation through special sites called “explorers”), anyone could have access to the handling of my account, thus damaging my privacy. In reality, the accounts (or “addresses”) are not attributable to a name and a surname (i.e. to a natural person) but are “strings” consisting of a minimum of 26 to a maximum of 35 alphanumeric characters.

For this reason, bitcoin transactions are said to be anonymous. Bitcoin is not anonymous, but “pseudo-anonymous.” This means that although the addresses (those we call “current accounts” in the bank) are not registered in the name of real natural persons, it is still possible (though not easy) to follow the computer traces that these transactions leave on the web up to the user (i.e. the point where the user connected to the internet or the device with which they are connected) and thus define the identity of the natural person who controls that particular address.

Let's go back to our money transfer and introduce another relevant difference compared to what happens in the banking system. While when I move my money through a bank it immediately traces each transaction and does the same with any other movement, in a blockchain the operations made by the various users are “merged” and inserted into blocks. To understand what a block is, we can imagine it as a box that contains the information (sender address, amount handled, recipient address) relating to all transactions “ordered” by users in the unit of time. With Bitcoin, for example, a block is generated every 10 minutes.

Blockchains are very similar to taking inventory of physical goods. When we do an inventory, all we do is take all the goods we have in stock, put them inside some boxes (numbered in ascending order), recording the contents of each individual box on a register. By carefully ordering the different boxes, once the inventory is finished, we will also have a paper “map” that illustrates where every single item is located in the warehouse. If we imagine the inventory of a restaurant that is closing its business, for example, we find all the kitchen utensils (knives, cutlery, plates, pots, glasses, etc.), stored in a warehouse and placed inside boxes. Since the boxes are numbered and I have recorded the contents of each box in the register, whenever I need, for example, the colander, consulting the register I could know its exact location. A blockchain, therefore, can be thought of as the inventory of all transactions made. In practice, it is nothing more than a huge register that records the trace of all the blocks executed by the network since its birth.

As you read this text, for example, the network is processing a new block and adds it to the register of all blocks processed over time. This aspect is fundamental to understand why this technology is so reliable. If an attacker tried to manipulate the information contained in one of the blocks already processed by the network, this modification would cause a series of chain anomalies on all the blocks. The other computers on the network, finding themselves dealing with a document different from the one they had at their disposal, would be able to define the malevolent nature of the operation, thus blocking it instantly. When the block is validated by the network it can no longer be modified. The blockchain, therefore, is not only an armored and distributed register (or database,) but it is also immutable.

When a user wants to transfer money to another user, what they do is send the sum from their address to that of the recipient. The information of this single transaction is entered inside a block together with the information relating to all the transactions ordered in the last ten minutes. The network then takes charge of the block, processes it, validates it, and transcribes it on the blockchain.

From that moment, the information contained in the block becomes immutable and can no longer be modified. Easy, right? Well, next we will illustrate how the network validates and processes the blocks, and we will have the opportunity to understand why the nodes that are part of this network cannot in any way manipulate the information contained in the block that they are processing. In other words, we will define who the miners are, what kind of role they have, and why they are so important in the functioning of a blockchain.

## How to Buy Cryptos in the Safest Way

Before we discuss ways of buying cryptos, have you ever wondered how cryptocurrencies are created? We will refrain from diving into the technical details of how cryptos are created. Instead, we will break down the ways in which they come into existence, in the simplest terms possible.

Many of the cryptocurrencies that come to market are backed by a start-up company. This company has developed the blockchain that tracks the coins for a specific purpose, whether it's to help send money across borders more easily, to improve tracking of supply chains, or some other attribute that might appeal to businesses and consumers. To raise money for the projects and to improve the appeal of the cryptocurrency, the company will run an initial coin offering (ICO). These are in some ways like an initial public offering (IPO) through which a company will raise money by selling stock. But there are major differences between an IPO and an ICO.

Now that we know how cryptos come into existence, let’s discuss how to buy them in the safest way possible: 

### You Don't Own the Company

When buying into an ICO, you're purchasing the cryptocurrency that the blockchain firm has offered. This in no way entitles you to an ownership piece of the business. By owning XRP, you don't suddenly have a vote in how Ripple operates. By purchasing ether, you do not have a say in Ethereum's next upgrade. Instead, you're simply buying the currency and can decide when to sell it.

This differs significantly from an IPO. By owning stock in a public company, you do get a vote. If you have thirty shares, you have thirty votes. While this doesn't necessarily allow you decision-making powers, it does give you some say in how your investment operates. It also provides you with some investor protections, offering recourse if the company acts fraudulently.

### You Don't Get Insight into the Company

ICOs have become a notorious microcosm of the Crypto investing experience since they've become a prominent way to scam investors. This has brought much criticism toward the regulations regarding ICOs because companies releasing the coins don't have to provide much information about how they operate, what strategy they have for growth, and what plans they have moving forward. They don't have to share their balance sheets, provide documentation that they've even made a sale, or show profit figures. In essence, these companies are ghosts beyond what information they independently choose to share with potential buyers of the coin.

That's not the case when you invest in an IPO. Prior to the launch of the IPO, the companies are required to provide detailed financial information to the SEC. This becomes public prior to the date of the IPO, so investors interested in the company can weigh the health of the firm.

### It's a Start-up That's Launching the ICO

The companies that launch ICOs are fairly young firms. They don't have a long history of success, years of profits, or even a plethora of investors (although some do have an angel or series-A investors). Typically, a company launching an IPO has shown the ability to grow revenues or profits for some time. Since the financial information of the company will become available during the IPO process, institutional and retail investors will judge the company's performance, providing a harsh look at where it has been and where it's going. This weeds out companies that have no real reason to go public other than as a quick money grab. These bad players are more difficult to discover during the ICO process.

The appeal of investing in ICOs comes from the ability to get into a new coin for incredibly cheap, which gives you the opportunity to own a larger share of the name. The other reason? Bragging rights for getting into a coin early that then takes off. Both of these are fine reasons for owning an ICO name. Just know that if you choose to go this route, there's a far better chance that the money you're spending will never be seen again.

### You Can't Know Which Coins Will Rise

It took Ethereum's ether nearly two years to rise consistently above $20 a coin. Early investors in ether wouldn't have found it to be a particularly exciting investment until 2017. There's a likelihood that many early investors left, tired of waiting. The point is that investing in ICOs is extremely risky since you can't know which coins will take off, because there's very little information about the blockchain that supports the coin, and it could take years before your initial investment thesis plays out.

It, therefore, won't hurt to wait a while before selling off your cryptos after an ICO. The flip side of the ether story, taking two years to reach $20, is that it would have given any investor far more time to buy into the coin, as more information about the technology became known. Over the two years in which ether did very little, the world grew to know the founder, Vitalik Buterin, and Ethereum had started to court large companies that showed interest in the blockchain technology. It gives you time to know which ones seem like legit businesses. Sure, you'll own fewer shares, most likely, but there's an improved chance you're not throwing your money at a bad business.

#### 1.    They're Mined

This is the process that Bitcoin made popular. Deep within the blockchain's code, there are a certain number of coins, and miners look for those coins to uncover new ones. Once they're uncovered, miners can then sell the coins to the market, just as you would sell them. The difference is the miners didn't have to pay market rates to obtain the treasure. If there's a market, as there is for Bitcoin, then the coins will be snatched up quickly.

#### 2.    They're Controlled

The company Ripple used a controversial strategy when it launched its Crypto, the XRP. Instead of an ICO, it mined all the coins, gifting a large portion of them to their executives. They then trickled out coins periodically, based on a number that the company determined would drive demand and ensure supply. The community turned against XRP due to this setup, so the company decided to take about half of the total XRPs and place them in an escrow account. The account funnels out 1 billion XRPs each month. If any of the coins go unclaimed that month, then the XRPs return to the escrow account until all of the XRPs are in circulation. It's a unique setup, one that might have an appeal to other companies, since it does offer some control in the coin.

#### 3.    They Fork

Blockchains are (typically) open-source software, and a community can sometimes tweak, test, change, and improve the system, seeking upgrades to the way it operates. Since there are weaknesses in the original blockchain (particularly when speaking of Bitcoin) the community sometimes wants to tweak the blockchain that the Crypto runs upon. If the community, in mass, approves the tweak, then a new coin isn't produced. But sometimes, the community disagrees with a solution. In this case, the minority group produces what's referred to as a hard fork, where an entirely new coin develops. A “hard” and “soft” fork.

While the mechanics of creating a hard and soft fork can be complicated, generally speaking, if the community of users and developers of a blockchain decides to make an improvement that the majority of the community or developers agree with, then it creates a soft fork or a switch in the code that the original cryptocurrency will adhere to. This means the currency continues on under the new rules, without changing the makeup of the coin.

Sometimes, though, this change isn't approved by a vocal minority within the crypto community, which leads to a disagreement on the future of the Crypto. These disagreements can arise when the minority group believes the blockchain should change to improve the coin in some way, or it can arise because the majority believes a change must occur and the minority group disagrees. Instead of holding hostage the wants of the vast majority of the community, which agrees with the change, a minority forces a hard fork, in which a large percentage of developers will move with the original coin. The ones who disagree with the larger community will create a new cryptocurrency, which takes into account the less popular decision.

But what happens in a hard fork? When a hard fork occurs, creating a new cryptocurrency, it's similar to a stock split. Current holders of the currency will receive a certain amount of the new coin. For instance, when Bitcoin cash was created during a hard fork of Bitcoin's blockchain, a Bitcoin holder received one Bitcoin cash if they owned Bitcoin at the time of the fork and the exchange, they used recognized Bitcoin cash. It's not always this one-to-one dynamic; there are instances where a split will lead to receiving multiple coins of the new token. It's dependent on the reason for the fork and those driving the change.

If Airbnb was to be taken over by Google or Amazon in the future, these companies will know everything about your life. Even better than your family would.

## So How Might a Decentralized Airbnb App Work?

[https://www.shutterstock.com/it/image-vector/dapp-vs-app-decentralized-applications-explained-1500606653](https://www.shutterstock.com/it/image-vector/dapp-vs-app-decentralized-applications-explained-1500606653)

### DApps

Let’s call our DApp, the dAirbnb. This app rests on the blockchain and in a series of connected Smart Contracts. For starters, the app would house a review system that allows all users to verify one another’s behavior and track records. Is the owner’s property a good deal? Is the renter a good person to rent to? Everyone can upload pictures of their properties but don’t have to upload pictures of themselves if they don’t want to. I’ll explain why shortly.

The filters that you find on Airbnb regarding wifi, laptop-friendly workspaces, and washing machines will exist. In fact, the interface will look exactly like Airbnb’s. The difference is entirely under the hood. When you communicate on the app, messages and interactions aren’t stored on a database. They’re stored in a blockchain ledger. The messages themselves are cryptographically signed, which makes them impossible to hack.

You and the property owner are the only ones who can ever read those messages. What’s more, you can exchange contact information with one another easily. Airbnb doesn’t allow its users to do this since it leads to both parties signing a deal offline. That hampers the platform’s revenue. However, the DApp isn’t concerned with this since the incentive to interact on it is far greater than offline interaction. 

### Reputation

A decentralized app doesn’t have any single authority in charge. This means the review system is more important than ever. The more reviews a user has, the better their experiences will be. Now let’s take this a step further. What if dAirbnb was just one app that rested on a blockchain that contained several other apps?

There could be dGrocery that allows you to order groceries online, dUber for transportation, dStylist for any personal grooming, and so on. An entire ecosystem of apps that collect reviews from various other users who can attest to your character after having interacted with you. If you’ve never rented on dAirbnb before, landlords can view your reviews from other interactions in the ecosystem.

That is a dystopian nightmare if a centralized system was in charge. However, everything is on the blockchain, and thus, users are in full control of their data and interactions. Reveal what you would like to and withhold everything else. If another user needs sensitive information (as defined by you), you can send it to them securely, knowing that they cannot exploit it. That information isn’t going to be stored on some database where it’s vulnerable to a hack. Thus, managing and building a reputation on dAirbnb is far easier than choosing to do so offline.

### Identity Verification

Talk about the blockchain, and the first objection people have is that no one’s identity can be verified. That isn’t correct as you’ve already learned. Opponents of the blockchain like framing it as a system that allows anyone to assume any identity. However, this isn’t how the blockchain works. Users can verify another person’s ID depending on their needs.

It’s just that identity is removed from the center of attention, and this is a tough pill to swallow. Furthermore, the way blockchain apps verify ID is different from a centralized app. Airbnb, for instance, allows you to view the other person’s name and other information. Generally, this is harmless since people don’t share sensitive information. However, to verify your ID, Airbnb collects information from sensitive documents like passports and IDs. An ID verification protocol on the blockchain works differently. In essence, it’s a Smart Contract drawn between two parties. The contract sits in between both parties and verifies that both are who they say they are. Neither party is provided sensitive information or any information beyond what the other person is willing to disclose.

Let’s say you’re booking a room on dAirbnb, and the landlord wants to know whether you are who you say you are. dAirbnb invokes a SmartID protocol, a separate app on the blockchain that verifies your ID securely. Again, there’s no danger of a hack or cyberattack. You share your picture and name with the landlord, and that’s it.

In return, you get to verify whether the landlord has a physical space ready for you and that they aren’t copying someone else’s listing or doing something shady. The result is a system that is far more secure than conventional Airbnb. The decentralized app is completely peer-driven and enhances trust between people despite very little personal information being revealed.

Compare this to an offline transaction where you’ll need to hand over your physical ID document and have someone verify it. The blockchain offers a far safer experience, and this is a powerful incentive.

### Risk Management

Risk on the blockchain isn’t managed as much as it’s eliminated. Centralized databases can be leaked, hacked, and sold to the highest bidder. Decentralized apps don’t store information in a single place. Thus, there’s no comparison to safety on the blockchain versus what any other solution offers.

### Insurance

Airbnb offers property owners $1 million in insurance protection against damages and theft. It’s one of the few value drivers that the platform provides. A cynic might say it’s just another way of washing their hands of responsibility; however, the presence of insurance cover gives landlords peace of mind.

A decentralized app would provide the same degree of protection but with greater efficiency. dAirbnb would simply invoke another protocol, call it dInsurance, where multiple insurers get to view renter and landlord data and tailor quotes based on the individual.

Thus, good renters will have to pay lower insurance premiums while renters with bad reputations will pay more. Landlords can rest assured that their unique needs are being covered. Note that risk evaluation doesn’t depend on someone’s identity. It solely depends on their transaction history on dAirbnb.

Insurers won’t need ID proofs or any other sensitive data to verify who you are. Relevant information is already present on the network, and all they need to do is access publicly available transaction data. The insurance contract can be sent as another Smart Contract that involves all parties and is secured by a multi-sig.

Thus, the whole experience is peer-driven, and dAirbnb facilitates this connection. It doesn’t sit in the middle collecting rent for the privilege of offering you a connection to some other party.

### Payments

That is one of the biggest advantages of using a dApp. Owners don’t have to wait for lengthy periods to receive money since they get it instantly. Renters often complain of security deposits being withheld for no reason. Well, a Smart Contract that places deposit money in escrow solves that issue. In fact, there won’t be any need for a deposit in a long-term rental arrangement.

The lease itself will be a Smart Contract. If the renter breaks terms, the contract automatically compensates the owner for damages. Any data and evidence to back claims up and to record original conditions can be uploaded to the contract, and all parties can verify it.

In case of a dispute, an impartial third party can be given access and the issue can be settled. If the dispute goes to a court of law, there’s no way for either party to modify or manipulate the situation since everything is recorded. The result is a safer and seamless experience for everyone involved.

### Access

One of the hurdles of monetizing a property on Airbnb is that guests can rarely check themselves in. IT technology has improved to the point where everyday devices can now run themselves or can be controlled through an app. Smart locks are available but automating their functions is tough. For instance, owners can activate smart locks to let people in, but they have to physically verify whether the guest’s information matches what is on the platform.

The blockchain can automate this entire process thanks to Smart Contracts. A Smart Contract’s terms can be linked to a smart lock. Once the guest approaches the lock, they can present an electronic key that is encoded into the Smart Contract. If the key matches, the door unlocks, and the guest checks themselves in.

A single Smart Contract can govern the entire rental workflow this way. Owners can manage their properties remotely knowing that their properties are secure and that no manipulation is possible. It reduces the impact a property management firm can have on fees and profits that the owner can collect.

## Open Network Companies

As the case study of the dAirbnb platform shows, peer-driven networks can change the nature of an organization completely. Instead of assuming a centralized shape, organizations of the future can simply transform into a network of connected apps. The dAirbnb platform you just looked at uses an identity verification app and an insurance app. These entities are separate from each other and don’t have to be owned by the same corporation.

Decentralizing the structure of an organization has other effects too. For starters, the amount of capital needed to compete in the marketplace is reduced considerably. If someone wants to create a platform that has better features than dAirbnb, all they need to do is establish connections to apps in the ecosystem and develop their app to the best of their abilities.

Given the open-source nature of the blockchain, it opens everyone up for competition, and big companies cannot buy their way forward. For example, Google currently buys any company that can even remotely develop a better search algorithm than them. That is to stave off competition. Most owners sell to Google because the prospect of going up against a trillion-dollar behemoth that effectively controls the internet is a losing prospect.

Similar patterns play themselves out in social media networking and technology. For instance, Apple has walled its app store off completely from other networks in the name of security and holds developers captive to its demands. The internet was meant to be an open marketplace, free for everyone to compete, but big corporations have captured their corners of it and will never let go.

The blockchain brings greater efficiency to the market thanks to decentralization. Companies can reduce their internal search costs as previously described, and this leads to the creation of novel business models. Here are some possible ventures that we might see in the future.

### Just in Time Sourcing

Just in time or JIT deliveries offer the best combination of efficiency and cost control for manufacturing firms. Setting up JIT sourcing currently costs a lot of resources, and only large corporations can afford them. The idea of JIT is that raw materials arrive just when they’re needed for production. That reduces storage costs and makes freight transportation more efficient.

To make sure this happens, companies have to create large databases of suppliers and delivery tracking systems of their own. That allows them to monitor every action and predict the efficiency of their systems. The irony is that increased control doesn’t give them an advantage as JIT is hard as ever to set up.

The blockchain simplifies JIT immensely by making the entire network of suppliers transparent. What’s more, the track record of every supplier can be instantly verified, and there’s complete trust in the system. Thus, smaller organizations can create better processes and reduce their working capital needs even more. The result is more efficiency, lower costs, and better business practices for everyone.

### Autonomous Agents

When talking of companies, we think of corporations that have employees, offices, servers, and so on. What if the company can be fully automated via a smart agent that roams a decentralized network, making deals that benefit the organization, updating itself to account for competition, and incorporating new products to serve customers better?

Smart Contracts are more than just lines of code. They can be taught to do literally anything. Currently, technology limits us from automating them beyond a few simple tasks. A lot of automation comes in the form of validation against existing conditions. However, nothing is stopping Smart Contracts from learning and interacting with a larger ecosystem.

For instance, Amazon’s pivotal business for a while has been its AWS or cloud computing business. The company allows other organizations to rent server space and remove the need for physical server infrastructure. What if AWS was replaced by a Smart Contract on an open blockchain?

This contract would seek out peer computers in the network and evaluate the amount of free storage space available. The contract would sign another contract with the peer computer and lease storage space. It would then turn around and list that space to users who can rent that server space.

All a human being would need to do is monitor the rules governing the contract. They would need to ensure it doesn’t violate any peer rules and that server needs are correctly met. Thus, companies can become far leaner than they currently are and become a network of dApps and Smart Contracts interacting with each other.

If you’re catching on, this sounds eerily similar to AI development. The problem with AI development currently is that it’s entirely in the hands of a select few corporations. What if its development were democratized? Surely, the pace of development would be much faster, and the result would be a corporation run by Smart Contracts, with human handlers making sure the boundaries of that system are preserved adequately.

The nature of a corporation as we know it is to earn profits. However, such open-source corporations aren’t in it to make money. A robot doesn’t need to be paid after all. Service fees can be kept low, and value becomes the primary driver. As a result, the cost of resources decreases.

How will this interact with a capitalist society? Well, it’s too early to tell. However, there’s no doubt that the paradigms that we take for granted will be challenged. Will the increasing development of Smart Contracts lead to humans not being needed at all? What if one Smart Contract could monitor another, thus removing the need for a human handler?

What does the function of humanity become in such a society? Again, it’s too early to tell. There are best and worst-case scenarios at play. Whatever happens, there’s no doubt that removing something as powerful as AI from centralized control can only be a good thing for society as a whole.

### Art Creation

While the internet did a great deal to bring information to the masses, it never managed to compensate the people who created that information. The downfall of newspapers and publishing houses and the takeover of nonsensical Buzzfeed-like websites is one of the many ways the internet affected media consumption.

What’s more, these media houses still exercised massive control over what was reported. These days, we have social media platforms like Facebook to contend with over and above these clickbait websites. While the blockchain cannot resuscitate long-gone newspapers, it can definitely ensure that the people who create original content are rewarded for their efforts.

Currently, content creators have to use centralized platforms to spread their message. For example, an aspiring journalist has to maintain a blog, a Twitter account, and a Facebook profile to source and report stories. In addition, their stories appear on a website that exercises further control over output. In most cases, content creators lose rights to their work and don’t get compensated for how often it gets shared or consumed.

The blockchain enables creators to digitally sign their art and lease their artwork to other consumers. Non-fungible tokens or NFTs have become hot recently, and speculators have rushed in increasing the prices of everything. While the speculative boom shows how little people understand blockchain technology, the NFT itself is a Smart Contract that can change the way creators collect royalties.

On a decentralized platform, an artist could create a work of art (or content of any kind) and sign it, thereby verifying their rights to the work. The NFT can be coded to lease the artwork to other users in exchange for a token, and thus, royalty collection is automated. If anyone wishes to share the artwork or display it to the public, the NFT would ensure that the artist is compensated accordingly for it.

Currently, anyone can take a picture of a work of art or download a digital work of art and repost it on a website. Google picks this image and displays it in its image results, without paying the creator a fee or acknowledging that someone else created it. Facebook and Google display millions of pieces of content on their platforms, increase their share of the internet’s traffic, earn ad revenues from advertisers thanks to this traffic, and don’t compensate people who drive traffic in the first place.

If no one posted anything on the internet for a day, what would happen to Google and Facebook’s business model? The blockchain would allow people to share and use the content as they please but would also ensure fair compensation to creators. Google could upload pictures of artwork to its image search results but would have to pay the artist royalties for doing so.

A Smart Contract tied to an NFT can resolve all scenarios where artwork would be displayed. A display in image search results would require a smaller royalty than one displayed on a website. If the image is being displayed for educational purposes or for purposes that the artist doesn’t wish to collect a royalty from, the Smart Contract can specify these conditions.

There is no limit to how much this will impact the likes of music creators, artists, and digital content creators. Record companies, walled app stores such as Apple and Google, and centralized platforms will face a reckoning and deservedly so. Their days of rent collection will be numbered.

Aside from helping artists, the blockchain will also eliminate fraud. Currently, the art world is inundated with fakes that cost a ton of resources to verify and discredit. Insurance companies build entire business lines insuring art and verifying authenticity. The blockchain will simplify all of this since the record of a piece of art will be immutably stored on the network.

Verifying its origin and creation will be simple. Even if a single record is lost, its existence is stored on the entire network and can thus be restored easily.

### Cooperative Companies

The term sharing has been co-opted by the likes of Uber and Airbnb-like platforms. According to these platforms, they’re participants in a “sharing” economy and, they position themselves as peer-to-peer companies. However, as you’ve already read, these companies don’t offer anything to share. They’re merely aggregators that host a bunch of traffic on their platforms without assuming any responsibility.

The word sharing implies a free exchange of goods and services. Think of kids sharing toys. Uber and Airbnb don’t offer people the chance to share anything. An Uber driver is a taxi operator at the end of the day and has to pay Uber a cut of their earnings for the privilege.

The blockchain can reinvent the so-called sharing economy of today by turning it into an actual peer-driven network. You’ve already read about how such decentralized platforms would work, so I won’t dive into the details again. There’s no doubt that such dApps give users greater control over their data and business models. As such, if the price of this control is the obsolescence of centralized platforms, then it’s worth it.

A side-effect of the blockchain is that it can turn a sharing economy into a metered economy. For instance, when you hire a car these days, it’s tough to hire one for a few hours. You’ll probably be charged for an entire day no matter how little you need the car. The reason is it’s tough to locate renters for the remaining hours of the day and enforce rental terms over short periods. It also adds significant costs. For instance, the car company will need to inspect the vehicle multiple times, run paperwork just as often, and coordinate collection and pickup. All of this means more employees and processes. The chances of things going wrong are high.

Zipcar made waves when it allowed people to rent cars hourly. However, this platform didn’t really add any value. It merely aggregated people who were willing to rent their cars when the vehicles were lying unused. The blockchain doesn’t have a billion-dollar valuation but delivers far more value than Zipcar claims to do.

By tying rental terms to Smart Contracts and linking with a decentralized review app, a company could create a system where people can rent their cars when unused easily. Identities will be verified by the network and contract terms will be enforced automatically. Owners will need to physically check their vehicles for damage, of course; that cannot be automated away.

However, this business model reduces costs significantly and improves everyone’s experience. It isn’t just vehicles that can be rented. The seed of Airbnb emerged from the thought that people have a variety of resources that they don’t use all the time. For instance, many people have tools in their garage but rarely use all of them. Most tools are bought with the intention of having them when the time comes.

What if a construction worker needed a certain tool and could rent it from a decentralized tool renting app? People could specify the exact number of hours they would like to rent a tool, and tool owners could enforce rental periods by Smart Contracts. Powered by a network of trust verification and insurance apps, a peer-to-peer economy where resource usage is metered is now a reality.

The possibilities are endless. We could sublease our Wifi hotspots, storage capacity, even the heat generated by our computers. The only question is, how creative can we get when it comes to monetizing our assets?

### Reinventing Platforms

The blockchain can remove these fees entirely since trust is built into the system. However, this won’t necessarily kill their businesses. They can use the redressal of data usage to their advantage. Let's take a company like Airbnb, for instance. Currently, this platform monitors your usage and collects data about everything you do for free.

What if Airbnb instead allowed you the freedom of sharing specific data and compensated you for it? The company could then use that data and make its platform better. Health tech is another example. Wearable technology allows people to track everything they do, but they also share these data with companies.

The blockchain can redress this imbalance. What if you bought a shoe with a chip in it that transmitted data to the manufacturer? What if these data were on a blockchain, and you could choose which data to share and what to withhold? Furthermore, what if the manufacturer paid you for sharing data?

Platforms, for their part, can scale down and provide more value to their users. Airbnb won’t need massive security functions and won’t need to worry about storage since an entire network does it for them. Instead, they can use the data they collect (from their data suppliers as raw material) and make their platforms a better place (finished products).

### Transparent Supply Chains

The food industry has a massive problem. Every year, billions of food go to waste due to improper storage, faulty sourcing, and just bad luck. The frustrating thing is these companies spend a lot of money strengthening their supply chain.

Food that arrives in improper conditions is unsafe and cannot be salvaged. Add to this consumer concerns about sustainability, and you have a system that everyone invests a lot of money into but doesn’t quite deliver. Manufacturers these days stick QR codes onto their products to help consumers view where their food came from.

This system is problematic on many levels. What prevents a manufacturer from cheating the system and creating their own sustainability data? They could have sourced tuna from a farm but might tell the consumer the tuna was caught wild under sustainable circumstances. Consumers have no way of knowing.

What if every piece of raw material in an industry could be tagged with a token (like an NFT) and tracked on a blockchain? The moment a fish is caught from the sea, a token is created, and this token can be broken into sub-tokens. This way, every part of the fish that is processed into other goods can be tracked, and consumers can quickly refer to the source of their food. No fancy QR codes or anything of the sort.

With regards to product tracking, the blockchain will not eliminate costly product tracking infrastructure. However, it will help companies create stronger audit trails that help identify the exact moment of failure. The result is a transparent community that knows where its products come from.

This use case applies not just to food but to every other industry—industrial manufacturing, healthcare, precision electronics, you name it. All of these industries spend billions tracking their products by using massive centralized systems. An open blockchain removes much of these costs and delivers value to the consumer. The blockchain also opens collaboration to a much greater degree.

If a manufacturer wishes to source logistics partners, they can trust that the suppliers of these services on the blockchain are verified. What if their shipments are too small for an entire truckload? Currently, these manufacturers have to pay extra for shipping. What if there were a dApp that listed spare cargo capacities in all vehicles in the area?

What if they could send a package through a regular vehicle instead of a truck, assuming safety protocols were followed? That would revolutionize the supply chain and increase margins in a notoriously low-margin industry.
