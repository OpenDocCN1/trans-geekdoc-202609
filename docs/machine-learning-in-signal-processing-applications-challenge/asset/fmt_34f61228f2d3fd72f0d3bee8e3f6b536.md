
Block diagram of the proposed `WNN` `DFE` is shown in [Fig. 8.6](#ch08.xhtml#f8_6). The equalizer consists of three subsections: `N[ψ]` wavelet basis functions which map the input to vector [`ψ[1](x)`,…, `ψ[1](x)`], a feedback finite impulse response (`FIR`) filter with coefficients `b[1]`,…, `b[N[B]]` and a feedforward `FIR` filter with coefficients `a[1]`,…, `a[N[I]]` connecting inputs to the output `ŷ(n)`. The input to the equalizer `x(n)` is composed of vector of length `N[I]`, which is applied to `N[ψ]` wavelons or wavelet basis functions and feed-forward `FIR` filter. Input to the feedback filter part is delayed hard decision outputs [`û(n−1)`,…, `û(n−N[B])`] of the equalizer. Output `ŷ(n)` is obtained by adding signals from these three subsections.

$$\\hat{y}\\left( n \\right) = \\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{N\_{\\psi}}c\_{j}\\psi\_{j}\\left( {\\mathbf{x}\\left( n \\right)} \\right) + \\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{N\_{I}}a\_{i}x\_{i}\\left( n \\right) + \\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{N\_{B}}b\_{k}\\hat{u}\\left( {n - k} \\right) + a\_{0}$$

(8.25)

In Equation (8.25), `c[j]`, `j` = 1,…, `N[ψ]` are weights of the connections from the output of multidimensional wavelets `ψ[j](x(n))` to the output adder. For binary transmission, the estimate of transmitted symbol is obtained at the receiver as ([Figure 8.6](#ch08.xhtml#f8_6))

`û(n)=sign{ŷ(n)}`

(8.26)

where
1. `sign{ŷ}` returns the sign of `ŷ`.

Parameters of the equalizer are:

`w` = {`a[0]`, `a[i]`, `c[j]`, `τ[i j]`, `δ[i j]`, `b[k]`}

(8.27)

where
1. `i` = 1,…, `N[I]`, `j` = 1,…, `N[ψ]` and `k` = 1,…, `N[B]`.

The basic idea of training the equalizer is to adjust parameter vector `w` so as to minimize MSE given by

$$\\mathcal{M}\\left( \\mathbf{w} \\right) = \\frac{1}{2N}\\sum\\limits\_{n = 1}^{N}\\left| {y\\left( n \\right) - \\hat{y}\\left( n \\right)} \\right|^{2}$$

(8.28)

Argument in MSE cost function ℳ(`w`) indicates that its value is dependent on network weight vector `w`. Conventional techniques of training the `WNN` involve algorithms like `BP`, `EKF`, `LMS`, which follow the hill-climbing approach to achieve optimality. However, these techniques are limited by slow rate of convergence and get easily trapped in local optima. In this work, ℳ is minimized iteratively with the `CSO` algorithm.

### 8.5.2 Cuckoo Search Optimization

`CSO` is an optimization algorithm which mimics the breeding behavior of cuckoo birds ([Yang and Deb 2014](#ch08.xhtml#r8_55)). Cuckoo birds are known for their aggressive breeding strategy. To increase the probability of survival of their offspring, they practice various types of brood parasitism. Birds of many cuckoo species, like European common cuckoo, lay their eggs in the nests of host species. Generally, eggs of cuckoo birds hatch earlier than the eggs of host bird, and cuckoo chicks can escape before detection. Moreover, shells of cuckoo eggs are stronger than the host bird so that they are not easily damaged. But the cost incurred by the brood parasitism is high on the host species, and it makes all attempt to identify and remove the cuckoo eggs. Thus, there is an evolutionary “arms race” where cuckoos try to fool the host species into making them raise their chicks and host birds trying to prevent it. This competitive behavior is utilized in `CSO` for finding the optimal solution.

Levy flights are random or quasi-random walks which model the search of food by insects, birds, and animals. It was shown that some fruit flies explore landscape by flying along a straight flight followed by sudden 90-degree turn. Similarly, in some hunter gatherer tribes of human, hunting is done by exploring the landscape in a series of straight paths and then taking a sudden turn. Time series representation of Levy flight is characterized by short burst of activity throughout the time series, resulting in a “heavy tailed” distribution. That is, it has high concentration of short steps and low concentration of large steps, though the concentration of large steps is higher than usual random walk. A distinguishing feature of Levy flight which gives it an edge over normal random walks for exploring wider search space is that its variance increases faster than normal random walks. This reduces the required number of iterations by approximately four orders compared to normal random walk ([Mareli and Twala 2018](#ch08.xhtml#r8_32)).

In `CSO`, potential solutions are associated with cuckoo eggs. Cuckoo birds lay their eggs in the nest of other birds with the expectation that their offspring will be raised by the proxy parents. If some of them are identified by the host bird, they are removed from the nest. Based on these ideas, `CSO` is developed based on the following rules ([Abdelaziz and Ali 2015](#ch08.xhtml#r8_1)):
* Each cuckoo lays an egg and randomly places it in any one of the nests.
* The nest having good quality of eggs (or solutions) will be permitted to carry over to the next generation.
* In a fixed fraction `p[a]` ∈ [0,1] of nests, the host bird will be able to identify parasitic eggs. In such cases, these eggs are either dropped, or the host bird completely abandons the nest.

Based on these three rules, the `CSO` algorithm is summarized in Algorithm 1. In the implementation, initially `P` random solutions `w[p]^{(0)}`, `p` = 1,…, `P` are generated. New solution `w[p]^{(l+1)}` at (`l+1`)-th generation for `p`-th cuckoo is generated as

`w[p]^{(l+1)} = w[p]^{(l)} + α L(β)`

(8.29)

where
1. 0 < `β` ≤ 2 is Levy flight parameter and
2. `α` > 0 is step-size scaling factor.
3. `L(β)` are random numbers drawn from Levy distribution.

In practice, Levy flights are generated using Mantegna’s algorithm as ([Yang 2010](#ch08.xhtml#r8_54)):

`L(β) = s (w[p]^{(l)} - w[min]) ⊗ r`

(8.30)

where
1. `w[min]` is the best nest (with least cost),
2. `r = [r[1],…,r[D]]^T` with `r[i] ∼ N(0,1)` and ⊗ indicates element-wise multiplication of vectors.

In Equation (8.30), step length `s` is computed as

$$s = \\frac{u}{\\left| v \\right|^{1/\\beta}}$$

(8.31)

where
1. `u ∼ N(0,σ[u]²)` and
2. `v ∼ N(0,σ[v]²)` are Gaussian random variables with standard deviation.

$$\\sigma\_{u} = \\left( \\frac{\\Gamma\\left( {1 + \\beta} \\right)\\sin\\left( \\frac{\\pi\\beta}{2} \\right)}{\\Gamma\\left\\lbrack \\frac{\\left( {1 + \\beta} \\right)}{2} \\right\\rbrack\\beta~^{2^{{({\\beta - 1})}/2}}} \\right)^{1/\\beta}$$

(8.32)

`σ[v] = 1`

(8.33)

However, to accommodate exploration capability of the algorithm, some fraction `p[a]` of new solutions are generated as given below ([Abdelaziz and Ali 2015](#ch08.xhtml#r8_1)):

`w[p]^{(l+1)} = w[p]^{(l)} + α δ ⊗ H(p[a] - ó) ⊗ (w[i]^{(l)} - w[j]^{(l)})`

(8.34)

where
1. `δ` is step size,
2. `H` is Heaviside function,
3. `ó` is a uniformly distributed random number.

* Algorithm 1 `Cuckoo Search Optimization (CSO)`
1. **Input:** Objective function `ℳ(w)` with `w = [w[1],…, w[D]]^T`
2. **Initialize:** Generate a population of `P` nests `w[p]^{(0)}`, `p = 1,…, P`.
3. Evaluate cost of each nest as `ℳ[p]^{(0)}`, with `w[min]` having minimum cost `ℳ[min]`.
4. **while** (`l < MaxGeneration`) or (stop criteria) do
   1. Generate new solutions `w[p]^{(l)}` using `w[min]` and Equation (8.29) for `p = 1,…, P`.
   2. Evaluate its cost as `ℳ[p]^{(l)}`, `p = 1,…, P`.
   3. **if** `ℳ[p]^{(l)} > ℳ[p]^{(l-1)}` **then**
      1. Replace `w[p]^{(l)}` by older solution `w[p]^{(l-1)}`.
   4. **end**
