
Obviously, `NL 0` is a linear function (linear functions are subclass of nonlinear functions), while other two relations represent different degrees of nonlinearity.

### 8.5.4.2 Effect of EVR

In the next set of simulations, we study the effect of eigenvalue ratio (`EVR`) of the channel on the `BER` performance of the proposed equalizer and compare it with other schemes in the literature. `EVR` is defined as `λ[max]`/`λ[min]`, where `λ[max]` and `λ[min]` are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of received signal correlation matrix `R` = `E`[`x` `x`^(T)], respectively ([Haykin 2008](#ch08.xhtml#r8_17)). If the `EVR` of the channel is high, the receiver input signal is more concentrated in the direction of eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. The transmitted signal is usually uncorrelated, but on passing through a channel with large `EVR`, it develops strong correlation resulting in severe `ISI`. On the other hand, if the `EVR` of the channel is low, the transmitted signal is less impacted by the channel, and `ISI` is small. In general, larger `EVR` results in more distortion and requires more complex equalizer.

To study the effect of `EVR`, we consider the following raised cosine channel of length `N[h]` = 3 ([Haykin 2008](#ch08.xhtml#r8_17)):

$$h_{i} = \\left\\{ \\begin{matrix} {\\frac{1}{2}\\left\\lbrack {1 + \\cos\\left( {2\\pi\\left( {i - 2} \\right)\\text{/}\\Lambda} \\right)} \\right\\rbrack,~~i = 1,2,3} \\\\ {\\quad\\quad\\quad\\quad\\quad\\quad\\quad\\quad\\quad\\quad 0,~\\,\\text{otherwise}} \\\\ \\end{matrix} \\right.$$  

(8.43)

where

1. parameter `Λ` controls the `EVR` of channel.

For instance, `EVR` of [Ch 1](#ch01.xhtml#c1) in Equation (8.38) and [Ch 2](#ch02.xhtml#c2) in Equation (8.39) is 200 and 635, respectively ([Iqbal et al. 2015](#ch08.xhtml#r8_20)).

The effects of variation of `EVR` on different channels are shown in [Figures 8.8](#ch08.xhtml#f8_8)–[8.10](#ch08.xhtml#f8_10). In [Figure 8.8](#ch08.xhtml#f8_8), the effect of `EVR` is compared for different equalizers at `SNR` of 15 dB when the transmitted signal is passed through linear channel specified by Equation (8.43). [Figures 8.9](#ch08.xhtml#f8_9) and [8.10](#ch08.xhtml#f8_10) show the simulation results when the signal is transmitted through a cascade of linear channel in Equation (8.43) with nonlinear functions `NL 1` and `NL 2`, respectively. In all the figures, `BER` increases with increase in `EVR` and introduction of nonlinearity. In all these results, the proposed equalizer shows significantly better performance compared to all the schemes under consideration.

### 8.5.4.3 Effect of Time-Varying Channel

The transfer function of the channel in Equation (8.43) can be represented as `H(z)` = `h[1]` + `h[2]` `z`^(−1) + `h[3]` `z`^(−2) for a given value of `Λ`. If we assume the channel to be time-varying with channel coefficients `h[i](n)`, the linear part of the channel is given as follows ([Zhao et al. 2011](#ch08.xhtml#r8_58)):

`H[n](z)` = `h[1](n)` + `h[2](n)` `z`^(−1) + `h[3](n)` `z`^(−2)  

(8.44)

Time variation in channel filter coefficients is generated as `h[i](n)` = `h[i]` + `f[LPF]`(`ρ` `w(n)`), where `f[LPF]` is low-pass filter (`LPF`) with a normalized cutoff frequency of 0.1. `w(n)` is white Gaussian noise, and its standard deviation `ρ` is taken to be 0.1. Simulation results in [Figure 8.11](#ch08.xhtml#f8_11) show that the performance of the proposed equalizer is worse compared to the `FIR DFE` equalizer for lower `EVR` values, but for `EVR` higher than 400, the proposed technique exhibits significantly improved `BER` performance. `BER` performances of all other equalizers are far inferior compared to the proposed equalizer.
