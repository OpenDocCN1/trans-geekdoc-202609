### 13.3.3 Feature Selection

The process of limiting the number of input parameters or attributes by removing unnecessary features is known as feature selection. Reducing the number of factors is vital to improve model performance and lowering the computational cost of the modelling process. One crucial function that aids in reducing the number of input attributes or variables in forecasting is the “feature importance score,” which offers knowledge and insight into the working dataset.

The methods used to rate each input feature for a particular model are referred to as “feature importance,” and the scores only represent the “importance” of each feature. A higher score denotes a greater influence of that specific property on the model predicting the target attribute. In this research, the random forest algorithm is used to determine the feature importance score.

### 13.3.4 Data Normalization

It is necessary to scale variables that comprise multiple qualities with disparate units to a particular scale to guarantee that each attribute is given equal weight. Data normalization is the term for this procedure. This keeps a less significant characteristic with a larger range of values from overshadowing a possible more significant trait. In this instance, we use the mean-standard deviation scaling or Z-score normalization to rescale all of the attributes. The set has been standardized through the incorporation of the computational formula, which is expressed in the following equation:

\( X_{norm} = \frac{X - X_{mean}}{X_{std}} \) (13.4)

where X_norm is the feature value after normalization, X is the feature value, X_mean is the mean of the population, and X_std is the standard deviation of the population.

### 13.3.5 AQI Calculation

After testing with a normalized dataset using a minmax scale with a range of 0–1, the AQI values are determined. The formula to convert each concentration of a pollutant to the Air Quality Index is indicated as follows:

AQI = \frac{CB_{high}-CB_{low}}{C_{high}-C_{low}}(C - C_{low}) + CB_{low} (13.5)

where AQI is the Air Quality Index, CBhigh is the breakpoint concentration to Chigh, CBlow is the breakpoint concentration to Clow, Chigh is the concentration breakpoint ≥ C, Clow is the concentration breakpoint ≤ C, and C is the recorded pollutant concentration. The final air quality level can be calculated by taking into account the pollutant's maximum AQI value. There are six different categories of air quality: Severe (401–500), Very Poor (301–400), Poor (201–300), Moderate (101–200), Satisfactory (51–100), and Good (0–50). ## 13.5 Conclusion and Future Scope

The study concludes by showcasing the potential benefits of the hybrid models using DL for ambient AQI predictions in sustainable and smart cities. Through the use of a unified framework that combines RNNs, LSTM networks, CNNs, and GRU, we have reduced computational time and significantly improved prediction accuracy. Our findings highlight the significance of utilizing cutting-edge technologies in a world that is becoming increasingly interconnected to address urgent urban concerns like air quality control.

Even while our study has significantly advanced the use of hybrid DL models for outdoor AQI prediction, there are still some areas that merit further investigation. First off, even further enhancements in prediction precision and computing efficiency may be possible with additional refining and optimization of the combined model architecture. Furthermore, the AQI prediction framework’s ability to incorporate real-time sensor data with Internet of Things devices offers a promising chance to improve predictive skills and enable more proactive treatments. With the help of the abundance of data produced by smart city infrastructure, we can create dynamic and adaptable AQI prediction models that react instantly to changes in the surrounding environment and human activity.

In conclusion, the continuous development of outdoor AQI estimation in smart and sustainable cities is an intricate, multidisciplinary project that necessitates cooperation between data analysis, environmental research, public health, and urban planning, among other disciplines. We can build a future where cities are cleaner, healthier, and more adaptable for all of their residents in addition to being smarter and more sustainable by keeping up with innovation and improving current techniques.
