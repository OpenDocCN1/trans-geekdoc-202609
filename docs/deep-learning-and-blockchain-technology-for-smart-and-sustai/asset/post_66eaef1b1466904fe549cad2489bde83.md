
## 4.2.1 Computing Management

As IoT devices are resource-constrained, a lot of data has usually been sent to distant cloud servers for additional processing. But the IoT’s scalability is limited by the pure cloud-based computing paradigm, which also results in network traffic bottlenecks, prolonged latency, privacy exposure, and context unawareness [13]. By transferring computational workloads between remote cloud servers and MEC servers, which are usually placed at Small BS, Macro BS, Wi-Fi APs, and IoT gateways that are nearby users, MEC [14] is becoming a more and more essential part of cloud computing. Then, the response, privacy preservation, and context awareness can all be enhanced by moving the less computationally demanding, context-aware, and latency-critical workloads between distant cloud servers and nearby MEC servers.

### 4.2.2 Network Management

Nowadays, distributed IoT can benefit from the flexibility and scalability provided by software-defined networking (SDN) technology [15]. But Kalkan and Zeadally [16] demonstrate that the single point of failure stems from the centralization of SDN. Additionally, current SDN devices (such as gateways) are unable to analyze data streams computationally intensive. SDN’s drawbacks can be mitigated by integrating blockchain technology with SDN. A safe blockchain-based SDN framework for IoT, for instance, is suggested by the work of Sharma et al. [17]. To update the flow rule table securely and eliminate the need for an intermediary, a blockchain-based system has been built. Moreover, blockchain technology has the potential to enhance network security for network function virtualization (NFV) management. Specifically, Alvarenga et al. [18] demonstrate that NFV configurations may be made anonymous, consistent, auditable, non-repudiable, and immutable by integrating blockchain technology.

### 4.2.3 Communications Management

The 5G communication systems’ increased resource management is being driven by the rising demands of mobile data traffic. For example, the most important resource is radio spectrum and its components are spectrum sharing and auction [19]. Secure and dynamic spectrum management in 5G and 6G communication systems could be accomplished through the use of BC technology in the Federal Communications Commission [20,21,22].

Using blockchains to manage transactions securely and traceably without the requirement for a central middleman saves administrative costs, which is the major benefit of using blockchain technology for 5G-beyond networks. Yrjölä [23] provided numerous examples of how radio spectrum sharing might be improved by employing blockchain technology in terms of cost reductions, consensus, and reliability. A blockchain-based protocol was also proposed by Kotobi and Bilen [24] to ensure spectrum sharing in wireless communication systems between primary and cognitive users. Moreover, through traffic optimization, blockchain may be able to share link conditions with several IoT nodes while maintaining privacy, hence increasing spectral efficiency [25]. BC technology has the potential to enhance radio spectrum management as well as mobile services for clients. For instance, numerous scattered heterogeneous networks make up most 5G networks. By enabling smooth access between various networks, blockchains that are constructed on the uppermost layer of the network can aid in the combination of various networks. Working nodes can enhance their technical and service capacities with the support of a blockchain-based system, as the research of He et al. [26] shows. In the future, service providers will be able to deliver better services to users by extracting important insights from subscriber transactions based on the synthesis of BC technology and big data analytics.

### 4.2.4 Blockchain-Based Management of Edge and Cloud Computing

Resource sharing incentives and the offloading of computational tasks are two problems that are addressed when coordinating edge and cloud computing with blockchain technology. It is possible to greatly reduce the latency by shifting the computational workload to edge servers. Thus, edge-cloud interoperability is essential [27]. If every node transfers its workload to the mobile edge computing server, this could result in a single node of failure and a performance bottleneck. Dai et al. [28] presented an offloading method that considers load balancing over many MEC servers. In the interim, it is valuable to look into ways to reward cloud servers and edge servers.

## 4.3 Applications of Blockchain of Things

The use of BC technology in IoT is becoming more popular as a means of addressing the problems associated with IoT and offers a summary of the applications for BCIoT. It is essential to emphasize that blockchains have a wide range of applications, from smart grid to unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and Internet of vehicles (IoVs). This section primarily focuses on BCIoT's industrial applications, as seen in Figure 4.2. We classify the BCIoT applications into six broad categories.

- i. Smart Grid: The advent of dispersed sources of sustainable energy is changing the nature of energy prosumers from being solely consumers of energy to prosumers capable of producing energy [29]. The emergence of BC technology presents chances to guarantee safe peer-to-peer energy transactions. BC technology has been recommended by some recent article as a solution to these problems. Using consortium blockchains, Li et al. [30] created a safe energy trading system. With the distributed consensus of blockchains, this method can drastically reduce trading costs without utilizing a central broker. Additionally, using blockchain technology, Aitzhan and Svetinovic [31] created a decentralized energy trading system. It was shown that this technology is helpful in maintaining business energy trading agreements in decentralized smart grid systems.
- ii. Healthcare: Blockchain integration may be able to help healthcare networks get over issues with data security and privacy. Healthcare data kept on cloud servers can be safeguarded by employing blockchain technology, as demonstrated by Esposito et al. [32]. Meanwhile, in order to assure the private management of healthcare data, Griggs et al. [33] created a blockchain-based system. Specifically, medical sensor-generated health data could be automatically gathered and sent to the system through the execution of smart contracts, thereby enabling real-time patient monitoring. The underlying blockchains can be used to protect privacy throughout the entire process. Furthermore, the research of Bhuiyan et al. [34] suggested a blockchain-based method to control personal health information and facilitate data exchanges between various medical facilities, insurance providers, and patients. Healthcare data security and privacy can be guaranteed throughout the entire procedure.
- iii. Smart Manufacturing: Automated production is giving way to “smart manufacturing” in the manufacturing sector [35]. During this upgrade procedure, BDA (big data analytics) on manufacturing data is crucial. Every stage of the life cycle of a product, from designing to supplying raw materials to manufacturing, distribution, retail, and after-sales service, produces enormous amounts of data. Data analytics and data aggregation are challenging, nevertheless, because manufacturing data is extremely fragmented. By enabling data exchange between industrial sectors and linking IoT systems via peer-to-peer networks, BCIoT may solve the interoperability problem. For example, it is possible to build multiple distributed blockchains, each of which serves one or more sectors. Smart manufacturing security can also be enhanced by BCIoT. The IoT systems’ centralized maintenance is the key of obstacles preventing factories from being upgraded. For example, to address security vulnerabilities, IoT firmware must be updated on a regular basis. However, the majority of firmware updates on IoT devices are manually deployed after being downloaded from a central server. Installing and updating firmware updates for distributed IoT devices is costly and inefficient. An automatic firmware upgrading method based on smart contracts and blockchains is presented in the work of Christidis and Devetsikiotis [10].

The entire industrial network is specifically equipped with smart contracts that specify the firmware upgrading procedures (such as the times and locations for firmware upgrades). The firmware hashes can then be downloaded and installed on devices through automatically running smart contracts. Consequently, there can be significant savings on security maintenance expenses.
