## 13.3 Proposed Work

DL technologies are being used extensively in big data analysis to solve a wide range of problems related to regression and classification, including computer vision (Bengio, 2009), speech recognition, natural language processing, image processing, time series prediction, and particularly in the area of air pollution prediction. DL technologies have been used widely in big data analysis to solve a variety of classification and regression problems. In addition to categorizing pollutant levels and making long-term predictions for several days in the future, they are also capable of predicting particular amounts of pollutant concentrations.

While DL shows the potential to enhance air quality forecasting precision, a single network’s accuracy may be restricted in the face of more complicated issues. Numerous academics have looked into combining several DL networks. Furthermore, multiple investigations have demonstrated that DL algorithms can identify the fundamental causes of seasonal changes, regional geographical links, chemical processes, and other factors that can alter the amounts of air pollutants.

The accuracy of predicting pollution concentrations has significantly increased as a result of the gradual discovery of DL technology’s benefits in handling nonlinear spatiotemporal correlations. A hierarchical model is often used in DL techniques to map input to output. The modelling concept involves assembling certain blocks or layers to create a deep network structure that spans from end to end. When interpreting historical atmospheric pollution data with spatiotemporal correlations, DL models work pretty well (Kristiani et al., 2022).

The literature review makes clear that not much research has been done on AQI prediction across different datasets. The AQI is now exceedingly difficult to anticipate and monitor, especially for metropolitan regions where there is increased transportation and industrial activity. This work first uses samples collected from the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) to forecast the AQI in metropolitan areas. Following that, a hybrid model is being developed. The performance of the developed model is evaluated using the R2 score, MAE, and RMSE approaches. Finally, the performance of the proposed integrated model is compared with some recent existing models.

The main objective of the proposed work is to develop a hybrid DL model to predict AQI levels ranging from 0 to 500. Various familiar models like LSTM, RNN, GRU, Stacked LSTM, LSTM+GRU, LSTM+RNN, and RNN+GRU have been engaged for prediction. Among the different models, LSTM+GRU beats in terms of accuracy and outperforms in terms of computation time.

The proposed work consists of several modules from data collection, imputation, outlier detection and correction, data transformation, feature engineering, AQI calculation, data splitting, application of models, validation of models, and finalizing the best model based on performance metrics. ### 13.3.2 Data Imputation and Outlier Treatment

Data imputation is the process of replacing missing data with alternate values. The instances of values that are missing in the input attributes are eliminated when a data point is substituted.

When dealing with the target item, which is the pollutant, a ML-based multiple imputation technique (Narasimhan and Vanitha, 2023) is used to approximate the missing data using an imputation function that employs the mean value technique.

There are many missing values in the gathered data, and there are also extreme values that differ from other data observations. This could be a sign of experiment flaws, measurement variability, or uniqueness. The missing data causes noise to be added to the dataset, which negatively affects the model's predictive power. The missing data leaves gaps that are filled in using linear interpolation. It is a method for producing new data points that fall inside a predetermined range of an already-existing discrete bunch of data points.

The outliers are treated using the Inter-Quartile Range (IQR) method. It is a quantitative metric used to characterize data distribution. The process involves determining the data's 25th and 75th percentiles as depicted in the following equation:

IQR = 75th percentile – 25th percentile (13.1)

Upper and lower bounds are computed to handle outliers. The supplied information is replaced by the higher limit if it exceeds the upper limit, and the given information is replaced by the lower limit if it falls below the lower limit, as indicated in Equations (13.2) and (13.3).
