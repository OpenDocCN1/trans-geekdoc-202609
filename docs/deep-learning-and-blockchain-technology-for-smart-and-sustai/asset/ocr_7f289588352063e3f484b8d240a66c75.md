A novel authentication methodology was presented in the paper by Jain et al. [3] to secure communication between IIoT devices and servers. Because of its straightforward, lightweight, and secure architecture, this protocol is appropriate for IIoT devices with little resources. It does this by utilizing hash functions and XOR, two fundamental cryptographic procedures. According to the protocol, the server receives an authentication request from the IIoT device. Session keys are then returned by the server and are used to encrypt any further communications. Periodically, the session keys are changed to ensure security. The authors assert that their protocol is immune to man-in-the-middle, replay, and guessing attacks, among other types of assaults. To bolster their arguments, they also conducted a formal verification tool analysis of the procedure. A flexible and secure edge computing strategy for AI-driven Industrial IoT (AIoT) was presented by Zhao et al. [4]. It uses distributed computing and a software-defined industrial control architecture to overcome the security and flexibility issues in edge systems. Through industrial modeling and virtualization, the architecture decouples hardware from software, increasing programmability and flexibility. The use of resources and real-time performance are enhanced via distributed computing. Data availability, integrity, confidentiality, and privacy are further guaranteed by a security domain-based task offloading method and a decentralized service discovery and distribution paradigm. Experiments show that the suggested plan is effective. All in all, this plan presents a viable strategy for adaptable and safe edge computing for the IoT. Gateways serve as vital links between devices and the larger network in the context of the IIoT. But these gateways are also easy targets for cyberattacks, which can take down the entire system if they are compromised. The authors of the paper, Fröhlich et al. [5], suggested a unique Secure IIoT Gateway Architecture that makes use of Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) in order to address this. To improve security, TEEs—secure sections inside processors—isolate sensitive functions from the rest of the system. In order to protect the TEE from outside threats, this architecture assigns crucial responsibilities like device onboarding, data encryption, and authentication to them. The approach is also more adaptable because it doesn’t require prior trust between devices.
