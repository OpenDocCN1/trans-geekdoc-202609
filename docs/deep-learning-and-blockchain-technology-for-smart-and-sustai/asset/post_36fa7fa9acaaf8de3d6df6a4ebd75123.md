## 7.2 Related Works

In their exploration of the growing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in urban settings and their skillful delivery of AI applications in smart cities, Luusua et al. [1] investigate how technology might improve efficiency, sustainability, and urban living in general. The topics covered include AI-powered approaches to energy optimization, traffic control, public services, and other areas fostering the growth of intelligent urban environments. The essay also discusses possible obstacles, moral dilemmas, and how the use of AI will affect how cities develop in the future.

The study conducted by Lv and Gao [2] investigates how smart city efforts affect employment in China. It makes use of difference-in-differences analysis and data at the municipal level to determine the total impact of Demand and supply balance mechanisms in smart city building support urban employment, especially in secondary and tertiary industries. The study stresses the total employment creation fueled by smart city development, while acknowledging the possibility of job displacement owing to automation in some areas. It emphasizes how crucial it is to upskill and adapt the workforce in order to take advantage of smart city opportunities and traverse the shifting terrain.

Ullah and Al-Turjman’s research [3] offers a framework for using blockchain smart contracts to handle real estate transactions in smart cities. It draws attention to how disruptive this technology can be in the smart real estate market. The framework highlights that for blockchain smart contracts to be successfully adopted in smart city real estate, all six layers must be taken into account. It can streamline procedures, improve transparency, cut costs, and help a number of stakeholders, including government agencies, buyers, sellers, agents, and property owners.

In their study, Kumar et al. [4] presented a novel framework for traffic sign detection and forecasting in smart cities automatically. This is essential for effective traffic management as well as autonomous driving. Techniques like the Kalman Filter and Goat-based Convolutional Neural Network are included in the GbCN-KF framework that is being suggested. Combining these methods allows GbCN-KF to achieve high classification and detection accuracy for traffic signs, surpassing 99.8% on benchmark datasets. It is therefore appropriate for practical uses in smart city applications.

A multi-joint optimization technique is proposed by Liu [5] for the allocation of edge computing resources in Internet of Things (IoT)-filled smart cities. The approach met latency requirements for jobs produced by the aforementioned devices and tackled the problem of energy efficiency. For IoT systems in smart cities, traditional cloud computing designs may result in significant energy consumption and network latency. By putting networking, memory, and processor closer to the data source, edge computing provides an answer. For IoT-based smart cities, the authors suggested a four-layer network architecture. Next, in order to guarantee effective computation, they present a multi-joint optimization strategy that makes use of an auction-based technique to distribute edge resources for activities that are sensitive to delays.

A digital model that extracts properties from waste materials, including color, texture, and shape, is presented by Mohammed et al. [6]. Following that, these attributes are passed into various ANNs, each of which is taught to recognize a particular kind of garbage (paper, plastic, glass, etc.). To arrive at a final classification judgment, the outputs of each of these distinct ANNs are subsequently integrated using feature fusion techniques. Using a dataset of 2,400 photos of waste products, the model was tested and found to be 91.7% accurate. This shows that the waste can be efficiently sorted and classified by the model, which could lead to a more sustainable circular economy and higher recycling rates.

The paper by Homer [7] explores how people understand the idea of “smart sustainable cities.” Given that many cities throughout the world are attempting to become both smart and sustainable, it emphasizes how crucial it is to comprehend these viewpoints. The paper highlights the significance of having a nuanced understanding of people’s perceptions of smart, sustainable cities, taking into consideration both the advantages and disadvantages of this kind of urban planning.

The study by Khurana and Dutta [8] explores the ways in which smart cities support the development of resilient entrepreneurial ecosystems. It makes the case that these ecosystems undergo a “place-to-space” transition, beginning with locally focused resources and solutions and developing into more scaled and spatially adaptable models later on. The article offers numerous instances of sustainable entrepreneurial ecosystems found in smart cities across the globe, including the smart grid projects in Barcelona and the circular economy initiatives in Amsterdam. These examples show how various towns take advantage of their own advantages and difficulties to foster creative and long-lasting enterprises. It emphasizes how crucial it is to support place-based networks and promote spatial flexibility in order to ensure these ecosystems’ long-term viability.

Using a novel approach known as dynamic factor analysis, Vanli [9] evaluates and ranks 33 global cities over the period of 2005–2019. This method goes beyond static rankings, accounting for how cities progress and change over time in various dimensions of smart cities. The study deals with dimensions like digital inclusion, innovation, economy, human capital, sustainability, and quality of life. The study emphasizes the importance of taking dynamic changes into consideration when ranking smart cities. It also reveals interesting insights into which cities excel in specific dimensions and where others can better.

In the research, Alam [10] presented a novel method for limiting access to IoT device communication in smart city settings. This method takes advantage of the difficulties associated with access control in IoT connectivity, as well as the advantages of big data and blockchain technologies. Blockchain technology is used in the suggested approach to provide transparent and safe access control. To efficiently process and evaluate access control data, it also makes use of big data analytics. A potential solution to the problems with access control in IoT connectivity for smart cities is presented in this article. The suggested method aims to provide safe, transparent, and effective data exchange between IoT devices by merging the advantages of big data with blockchain technology.

A methodology and optimization strategy for assessing the life cycle cost (LCC) of photovoltaic (PV) systems in Indian smart cities were proposed in the paper by Beniwal et al. [11]. The authors optimize the system architecture and compute the LCC using the Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) method, taking into account variables including energy generation, savings, efficiency, and carbon credits obtained. Four smart cities—Srinagar, Jaipur, Pune, and Chennai—that reflect various climatic conditions and energy demands are examined in the study. According to the data, Srinagar has the lowest LCC (INR 4,125 million) and the highest energy production (435 MWh), with an 8.9% return on investment. The suggested methodology exhibits a 50.24% decrease in LCC in contrast to conventional techniques [19].

Müller-Eie and Kosmidis's article [12] describes how sustainable mobility is developing in mid-sized Nordic smart towns. In ten mid-sized Nordic cities—Aarhus, Finland; Espoo, Finland; Gothenburg, Sweden; Helsinki, Finland; Kista, Sweden; Linköping, Sweden; Lund, Sweden; Oulu, Finland; Tampere, Finland; and Umeå, Sweden—the writers carried out a document analysis of mobility projects. According to the report, the cities are pursuing sustainable mobility in a number of ways, such as by boosting electric cars and public transportation, building infrastructure for cyclists, increasing walking, and enhancing traffic control. According to the study’s findings, mid-sized Nordic cities are significantly further along in their development of sustainable mobility.

A blockchain-based system is proposed by Miloud Dahmane et al. [13] to provide data integrity and access management in smart cities. This is important because smart cities are susceptible to data breaches and unauthorized access because they are built on a massive network of interconnected sensors and devices. To provide integrity and access control in smart cities, the framework addresses tasks including determining the important parameters, enacting laws, and storing sensitive data on a blockchain. The paper also notes certain difficulties, such as the necessity for effective systems to handle access control regulations and possible scalability concerns with blockchain technology.

The significance of robots and AI in combating the COVID-19 pandemic is examined in the paper by Lauri et al. [14], with a focus on smart cities. It draws attention to the different uses, such as helping medical professionals, improving public health initiatives, and bolstering supply chains and logistics. In order to effectively use robotics and AI in the event of a pandemic, the essay highlights how critical it is to build resilient organizations inside smart cities.
