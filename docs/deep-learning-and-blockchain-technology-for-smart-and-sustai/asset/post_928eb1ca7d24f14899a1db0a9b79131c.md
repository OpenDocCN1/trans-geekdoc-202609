## Chapter 9

### IIoT for Smart Cities

Bala Krishnan Raghuopathy, V. Subramaniyaswamy, Manikandan Ganesan, and Rajesh Kumar Namachivayam

DOI: 10.1201/9781003476047-9

### 9.1 Introduction

The concept of a “smart city” has started to gain popularity because it has fundamentally altered how people use technology in urban settings. A wide range of electronic and digital applications, embedded ICTs (information and communication technologies) for administrative settings, ICTs for communication and information in operational systems, ICTs that assist people, and ICTs to enhance information and innovation are by definition the main components of a smart city. The terminology “Internet of Things (IoT)” is defined as a network of interconnected electronic devices that communicate with one another at the same time using wireless technologies. These devices have the ability to exchange, process, and store data. IoT devices are taking over a variety of application domains, including industrial, transportation, military, and smart homes. The term “Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)” refers to the imaginative attempt to create a smart manufacturing ecosystem through the integration of IoT benefits into industrial process management. Additionally, this technology is part of the infrastructure for smart cities that is both maintainable and upgradable, namely in the industrial sector known as the IIoT. Industrial devices are carrying out their functions in the context of IIoT, which aims to improve the production, performance, and maintenance efficiency of industrial activities. IoT is highly diversified, has a limited amount of processing power, and uses a variety of ever-evolving methods that expose them to a range of security risks. City governments use smart city capabilities to strive toward enhancing the quality of life for their citizens. Connectivity is essential for smart city applications, and a variety of new technologies are on the market to enable the increasing number of apps that aim to enhance communal life. City administrators must prioritize a number of issues in addition to dealing with demographic shifts and population increase. They also have to deal with a variety of difficulties, such as expensive operational expenses and infrastructure requirements. Although technology is becoming more and more important in helping local governments achieve their goals, improved coordination with utilities and regulators could hasten the public’s access to smart city services.

### 9.2 Challenges for Elevating and Developing Smart City

Linking smart city services to the priorities of the city is the first step toward their implementation. Public safety, enhancing people’s convenience in transit, public health, information sharing, and energy resilience are among the most commonly prioritized issues by city governments; however, they differ from city to city. The use of technology will be essential to raising public health and safety standards. A city’s demands will determine which technology investments are most important. Consider traffic concerns, for instance. Large-scale population inflows are occurring in places like Atlanta and Austin. Between 2021 and 2022, Austin actually saw the sixth-largest population increase in the US. Better traffic control priorities are required to enhance traffic flows and reduce traffic jams, pollution, and lost time as comparable regions grow and adapt to a growing population. Ensuring dependable energy should be a top responsibility for city managers and governments, particularly in light of the growing variances in national weather. To ensure efficient use of energy resources and reduce the risk of brownouts or blackouts, more insight into electric grid operations will be necessary for rooftop and community solar, district heating and cooling, and the electrification of cars. Realizing the full potential of smart city services is fraught with difficulties, even as municipal managers strive to better serve their inhabitants by addressing these goals.

### 9.3 Issues That Smart Cities Are Currently Addressing

The high cost of new infrastructure and regulatory issues are two of the major issues that cities now face. Smart city innovation and growth are hampered by these issues.

Government managers should understand that significant expenditures in infrastructure and technology will be required to make the transition to smart cities in order to stay up with the rapidly expanding trend of smart cities.

It could be challenging to swiftly adopt changes for smart cities and keep up with urban growth due to financial limitations and conflicting goals. Technological advancement frequently surpasses municipal resources, and when it does arrive, it might not be fully utilized.

Given their current communications infrastructure, the electric, gas, and water utilities are the industries best positioned to provide smart city services. Utility infrastructure could be used to lower deployment costs dramatically and hasten the rollout of smart city services. Existing laws governing public utilities, however, could occasionally function as a significant barrier. Restrictions imposed by regulations and worries about utility returns on investment (and the corresponding costs of electricity distribution) may force cities to invest large sums of money in developing their own infrastructure instead of utilizing the public utilities’ existing infrastructure, increasing costs and slowing down innovation.

Public utility regulation frameworks may also operate as roadblocks to urban modernization. For instance, Public Utility Commissions and other regulatory authorities must approve a specific tariff before a public utility can begin building out its infrastructure [11]. The influence on the electric consumer is the main concern of these regulatory agencies, which typically concentrate on the operations of public utilities. It is difficult to determine how a public utility’s smart city services should affect the rate base, which makes infrastructure investments even more difficult. The optimal way to deliver services for smart cities needs coordination between utilities, regulators, and governments. If local governments cannot afford to invest in the infrastructure and technology required to raise the standard of living for their residents, smart cities will not be able to expand and thrive.
