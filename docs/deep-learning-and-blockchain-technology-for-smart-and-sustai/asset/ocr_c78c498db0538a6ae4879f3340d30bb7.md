This is essential for effective traffic management as well as autonomous driving. Techniques like the Kalman Filter and Goat-based Convolutional Neural Network are included in the GbCN-KF framework that is being suggested. A multi-joint optimization technique is proposed by Liu [5] for the allocation of edge computing resources in Internet of Things (IoT)-filled smart cities. The approach met latency requirements for jobs produced by the aforementioned devices and tackled the problem of energy efficiency. For IoT systems in smart cities, traditional cloud computing designs may result in significant energy consumption and network latency. By putting networking, memory, and processor closer to the data source, edge computing provides an answer. For IoT-based smart cities, the authors suggested a four-layer network architecture. Next, in order to guarantee effective computation, they present a multi-joint optimization strategy that makes use of an auction-based technique to distribute edge resources for activities that are sensitive to delays. A digital model that extracts properties from waste materials, including color, texture, and shape, is presented by Mohammed et al. [6]. Following that, these attributes are passed into various ANNs, each of which is taught to recognize a particular kind of garbage (paper, plastic, glass, etc.). To arrive at a final classification judgment, the outputs of each of these distinct ANNs are subsequently integrated using feature fusion techniques. Using a dataset of 2,400 photos of waste products, the model was tested and found to be 91.7% accurate. This shows that the waste can be efficiently sorted and classified by the model, which could lead to a more sustainable circular economy and higher recycling rates.

The paper by Homer [7] explores how people understand the idea of “smart sustainable cities.” Given that many cities throughout the world are attempting to become both smart and sustainable, it emphasizes how crucial it is to comprehend these viewpoints. The paper highlights the significance of having a nuanced understanding of people’s perceptions of smart, sustainable cities, taking into consideration both the advantages and disadvantages of this kind of urban planning. The study by Khurana and Dutta [8] explores the ways in which smart cities support the development of resilient entrepreneurial ecosystems. It makes the case that these ecosystems undergo a “place-to-space” transition, beginning with locally focused resources and solutions and developing into more scaled and spatially adaptable models later on. The article offers numerous instances of sustainable entrepreneurial ecosystems found in smart cities across the globe, including the smart grid projects in Barcelona and the circular economy initiatives in Amsterdam. These examples show how various towns take advantage of their own advantages and difficulties to foster creative and long-lasting enterprises. It emphasizes how crucial it is to support place-based networks and promote spatial flexibility in order to ensure these ecosystems’ long-term viability. This method goes beyond static rankings, accounting for how cities progress and change over time in various dimensions of smart cities.
