- v. Supply Chain Management: A product frequently comprises several pieces that are supplied by various producers located in different nations. Nonetheless, the supply chain might come into interact with some forged or subpar items. The cost of integrating anti-fraud systems throughout a product’s entirety is high. This issue can be resolved by combining BC technology with IoT. Specifically, each component will be linked to a distinct ID throughout the creation process. Along with this ID, an unchanging timestamp is also attached. A blockchain, which is traceable and impervious to tampering, can then contain the identification of each component. For instance, research by Konstantinidis et al. [41] demonstrates how a blockchain-based system might be used to authenticate a product’s partial ownership. Moreover, Kim and Laskowski [42] suggest a traceability ontology with the integration of blockchain and Internet of Things technologies, based on the Ethereum BC platform. Provenance of data in the supply chain can be guaranteed by the suggested architecture.

- vi. Internet of Vehicles (IoVs) and Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs): Vehicle to vehicle, vehicle to roadside, vehicle to infrastructure, and vehicle to pedestrian networks are all essentially integrated by IoV. There are difficulties in securing message transmission and transaction execution because of the decentralized, heterogeneous, and untrustworthy nature of IoV. These issues can be resolved by combining BC technology with IoV. One such network for managing trust in the IoV was created by Yang et al. [43] utilizing blockchain technology. Specifically, roadside units (RSUs) can use PoW/PoS consensus to confirm the reliability of messages. Also, in smart grids, blockchain technologies can be utilized to safeguard information and energy exchanges between hybrid and electric cars [44,45,46]. In the future, resource allocation in IoVs can be further optimized by combining blockchain, MEC, and artificial intelligence [47]. UAV communication networks have the ability to make up for inadequate wireless network coverage in recent times [48]. Aside from product delivery, UAVs can also be applied to gather real-time traffic flow data and distribute product items [49,50]. Furthermore, a recent study by Cheng et al. [51] demonstrates that both MEC and content-centric networking can be supported by UAVs. But ensuring reliability in decentralized, untrusted UAV networks and controlling wayward UAVs are difficult tasks [52]. ## 4.4 BC Technology in Smart City

BC technology is realized as a serious facilitator of smart cities, notably in governance, transportation, asset management, utilities, healthcare, and logistics [53,54]. The solution is provided to the security risks faced by smart cities’ massive communication networks [54]. It is proposed to include blockchain-based technology in the promotion of smart cities and create a framework to assist policymakers and city owners. People have also investigated whether this method can be used to create smart cities with a low carbon and green environment in the future, focusing on the sharing of government information [55].

At present, individual organizations and companies conduct transactions with the assistance of third-party intermediaries like banks, financial institutions, standardizing bodies, or credit card providers. These transactions often require approvals, incurring costs and sometimes leading to inefficiencies that waste time and resources. BC computing has evolved as an approach for financial operations where communications are supported out online openly and transparently to discourse these issues [56].

The swift growth of cities poses a range of issues, including harm to the environment, increased resource use, heavy traffic jams, and amounts of greenhouse gases and carbon dioxide causing air pollution [57]. With limited resources available, it's crucial to plan for their use without jeopardizing the needs of future generations. The concentrated urban population means resources must be managed carefully to prevent crises in food, water, or other essential supplies. Technology underpins the idea of a city. This implies that IoT procedures are used by cities that are smart. ### 4.4.1 Architecture Layers of Smart Cities

The architecture follows a bottom-up technique. The sensor layer is the bottom tier, the transmission layer is the third tier, the data management layer is the second tier, and the application layer makes up this architecture as the topmost part. Security modules in every tier of smart cities are equivalent in protecting sensitive data. At the lowest level, collecting involves sensing and recording. This agrees it to accumulate knowledge from numerous diverse sensors and devices. It has several ways that enhance the efficient collection of different scenarios. It also gathers information from a variation of attributes such as temperature, light intensity, pressure, and humidity through sensors networks of its sensing layers. Some practitioners use webcams, Zigbee, Global Positioning System (GPS) terminals, Bluetooth, or RFID sensors and actuators for sensing activities.

The merging of numerous transmission or communication networks is at the transmission level. Within this tier, there are also networked and access transmissions with respect to their territories. Access network technologies with short-range coverage typically include technologies such as Bluetooth, Zigbee, M2M, RFID, and Zwave (Note – this is not a comprehensive list), and near-field communication (NFC). Similarly, transmission network technologies include those that provide more coverage, such as low-power wide area networks (LP-WAN), 5G, 3G, and 4G long-term evolution (LTE).
