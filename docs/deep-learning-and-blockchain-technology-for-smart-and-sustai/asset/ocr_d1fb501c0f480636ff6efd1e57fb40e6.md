The CDC's guidelines state that there is currently no effective treatment for monkeypox virus [36]. However, in response to the pressing need, the CDC authorized the use of two oral medications, Brincidofovir and Tecovirimat, which were previously primarily used to treat the smallpox virus, to treat the monkeypox virus. The most effective way to combat the monkeypox virus is by vaccination. Although there are vaccines against the monkeypox virus that have been licensed by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), they have not yet been given to humans in the US. Smallpox vaccinations are also utilized to treat monkeypox in other nations [37,38].

Early detection of the peculiar features of the skin lesions and the background of exposure are important elements of the monkeypox diagnosis process. However, employing electron microscopy to assess skin lesions is the only surefire method for identifying the virus. Furthermore, PCR is a useful diagnostic tool for COVID-19 patients. PCR can confirm the visibility of the monkeypox virus [39,40,41].

A new area of device discovery (ML) called transfer learning (TL) has shown promise in the identification and imaging of medical conditions, for example, it works with deep Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based methods to automatically identify malaria parasites in blood cell pictures in Reference [42]. To diagnose malaria from microscopic pictures, Reference [43] recommended combining models based on Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Visual Geometry Group (VGG)-19. In 93.1% of cases, their suggested algorithm correctly identified photos with malaria. To enhance the CNN model's performance in diagnosing breast cancer, Reference [44] developed a shallow-deep CNN-based model; their suggested techniques produced an accuracy of 85%. References [45,46] achieved an accuracy of 73.1% when they built a modified inception-based model utilizing 453 Computed Tomography (CT) scan pictures. In order to identify skin conditions including psoriasis, melanoma, lupus, and chickenpox, Reference [47] suggested using a low complex CNN. They demonstrate that skin illness can be detected accurately by 71% with picture analysis when VGGNet is exited. By obtaining an accuracy of about 78%, their suggested approach, in contrast, shows the best outcomes. According to Reference [48], who presented a smartphone-based method for identifying skin diseases using MobileNet, they were able to identify individuals with chickenpox symptoms with an accuracy of 94.4%. To identify skin conditions such as acne, candidiasis, cellulitis, and chickenpox, Reference [49] used a range of segmentation techniques.
