### 20.3.2 Blockchain

The blockchain technology implemented by this code allows for the safe transfer of data, including maybe the prognostic outcomes of a medical diagnosis. Envision an impenetrable chain where every link is dependent upon every other link. This is how it operates:

- 1. **Blocks and Security**: A timestamp, a unique fingerprint produced by a hashing function, and data (results) are all contained in each block that makes up the system. This fingerprint—or hash—is very important. The hash might be absolutely altered through any change to the information, making manipulation clean to spot.
- 2. **Building the Chain**: Predefined information is used to build the genesis block, which is the initial block within the chain. The additional data chunk is delivered to subsequent blocks with the aid of referencing the hash of the preceding block. Because of this, a sequence is shaped wherein every block depends on the integrity of the one before it.
- 3. **Transfer and Verification**: The code divides the data (anticipated outcomes) into digestible portions for transfer. Every segment gets a separate block, creating a blockchain unique to this record. By ensuring that the hash of each block accurately refers to the hash of the previous block, the recipient may also confirm the integrity of the contents. A break in the chain would suggest manipulation.

### 20.3.4 Existing Methodology

Polymerase Chain Reaction, or PCR, has been a total game-changer in molecular biology and all sorts of other scientific fields. It lets researchers copy and amplify specific DNA sequences, which is super useful for studying genes and all kinds of other genetic stuff. But even though it’s super helpful, PCR isn’t perfect. A couple of obstacles occur when matched with it.

This can be a real barrier for scientists who work in places with limited resources. And that suggests they could not have access to PCR technology, which can limit the kinds of research they can conduct.

Another challenge with PCR is that it can be a bit finicky. Sometimes, PCR can make mistakes and accidentally copy the wrong piece of DNA. To avoid this, researchers have to be really careful and follow strict procedures to make sure everything is as clean as possible. Even then, mistakes can still happen sometimes.
