# 3.4.1 ANONYMITY

Anonymity can be qualified as a condition of avoiding identification. It’s essential to the realization of human rights and fundamental [50].

Imagine walking in a mall covered with signs all over your body. Those signs indicate every store, the store you visited, the things you bought, what you like, what things you looked at, and so on. This varies close to what would happen when connected to the Internet.

Surfing the World Wide Web, we think that we have a confident expectation of anonymity, in the real world, we don't expect that individuals are tracking us or trying to observe us. But unfortunately, this is not the case. Using the Internet or anything connected to it generates endless trails of data specifying every step we make. Especially now with the growth of social media and digital advertising, “haven’t we all said we are scrolling on our media.” “How did I get an address for this device I just thought about?” Every move we make is registered and used by advertising companies to track us. With data being everywhere, every transactional data, mouse dropping, streaming data, or media data, we can create an online profile of each individual. A technology that made all of this possible is “cookies” [17]. Started directly on our hard drive, they provide the ability to connect collect information related to our online activities, and save for it future usage. Intended for the harmless motivation empowering our web sites to perceive a recent visitor and act appropriately, cookies were immediately taken on by Web scales and giant companies to target explicit actions for advertising. Companies such as Meta, Amazon, Netflix, and almost most webbased companies collect personal information to create fully identifiable profiles on a user’s online and offline behavior. Moreover, the rise of identification technologies, such as fingerprints, voice, face recognition, and many other types of use of biometric data, is wide-spreading, leading to zero anonymity.
