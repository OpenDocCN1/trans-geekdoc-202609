## 5.1 INTRODUCTION

### 5.1.1 AI DISCRIMINATION

Generally, the conversations surrounding risks or harms deriving from the use of AI have been often dominated by the overreach of power as public surveillance or police surveillance using AI facial recognition software, with a potential for discrimination, as such race, gender, and biases. Several examples of Uighur minorities surveillance in China by Huawei¹ or in the West by police forces using Clearview AI tools² have been widely publicized. They show their risk for societal harm and the need for ethical principles.

### 5.1.2 SOCIO-ECONOMIC DISCRINATION

Though AI proctoring software is not immune to the main types of discrimination mentioned above, there are additional layers one must consider when it comes to the potential for discrimination in this type of software. By design, proctoring software flags potential instances of cheating and, in attempting to do so, it can pick up other regular occurrences and flag them as suspicious activity that it deems as an attempt to cheat. Such activities could include the audible voice of a person a domestic pet, the presence of a second person on a camera, even if they are just passing by, the lighting quality, and the internet connection quality.
