## 6.3 FINDINGS

Having reviewed the technical primitives, system architecture, and data processing associated with each of the case study solutions, we now turn to evaluate how well each solution does in terms of data processing and solving the data double-spending problem. As previously mentioned, fairness in the processing of data generated and provided by individuals is supported when the individual controls and has custody of their own data; the individual consents to the sharing of their data; the individual is able to access and use their data; the individual has been appropriately compensated for the sharing of their data; and others cannot subsequently use the data for secondary purposes. We now review how each solution addresses these principles, both in respect to primary sharing and use and in respect to secondary sharing and use.

### 6.3.1 PRINCIPLE 1: THE INDIVIDUAL CONTROLS AND HAS CUSTODY OF THEIR OWN DATA

In each of our case studies, the provider of the data, who may be an individual, is recognized as the controller and owner of their own data. Moreover, both solutions use DID and DDO. In the case of OP, identifiers are assigned for datasets, whereas in the SSI solution, DIDs are used for data providers interacting in the marketplace. The use of dynamic DIDs in the case of SSI helps ensure that the data be traced back to a natural person once shared, and the source of the data remains pseudonymous. In the SSI solution, individual data providers are 'issued' data from whom the data originates; for example, biomarkers might be generated from blood samples and subsequently issued to the individual in the form of VCs. This model then makes it possible to verify the authenticity of the data (i.e., its origin and its integrity), a capability that is facilitated by using Hyperledger/Indy as a decentralized public infrastructure; however, it introduces reliance upon the data issuer's public key and data issuers may be able to revoke invalid VCs that they have issued. In the OP solution, unlike the SSI solution which relies upon VCs to transmit and share data, OP is an Ethereum-based solution that uses smart contracts for provisioning access to or performing computation on the data. Smart contracts and the ability to tokenize data are not provided for Hyperledger Aries/indy. OP operates as a permissionless ecosystem but can be configured to a permissioned ecosystem as well; whereas the SSI solution, it relies on Hyperledger Indy, only operates as a permitted ecosystem. In the OP solution, the 'keeper' maintains a registry of data ownership pseudonymous with the data provider, but there is no mechanism by which the original source of the data or its integrity, can be cryptographically verified, unlike in the SSI solution which supports such verification. This leaves the OP solution vulnerable in platforms offering artworks as non-fungible tokens (NFTs), as it is not possible to ascertain whether the NFT represents ownership of an original or is merely a copied image of an work.

In the case of the SSI solution using Hyperledger Indy/Aries, the individual does not permanently transfer the right to control the data or ownership; rather, the solution provides the individual with a mechanism – a verifiable credential for provisioning temporary access to a data consumer based on a proof. In the OP solution, individuals can provide sharing of the data or the right to compute the data to a consumer; however, in this solution, the mechanism used is a data token. When the consumer wishes to compute over the data, they send the data token to a wallet managed by the Brizio (Provider). Since access tokens could be sold and transferred to third parties, this increases the possibility of data double spend in the OP solution.

Defining terms of the SSI solution is achieved by the distributor app, which allows individuals to provide service terms and issue licences to consumers. In the case of the OP solution, the data token and Brizio (Provider) collectively fulfill the same role. Service terms are captured by the metadata of the data token while the Brizio (Provider) serves as a data access proxy of the data provider. It is responsible for accepting data requests, enforcement of service level agreements, verification of payments, and, finally, providing access to the data via a token. In both solutions, managing data terms remain quite centralized, relying upon a single distributor app in the case of the SSI, and a single Brizio (Provider) in the case of the OP solution. The Brizio (Provider) component may be managed by the data provider, but most likely will be managed by the OP marketplace. Centralization of these service components does not provide the same defense against a single point of failure or interference (e.g., Sybil attacks) as a decentralized architecture. Given this, it is possible that an individual could lose control over their data through malicious tampering or failure of the component. Assuming normal operating conditions, however, individuals would have custody in their data for any of use.

In terms of data custody in solutions, while data remains under the control of individual data providers transferred under a license, each provider must rely upon and trust a third-party data storage provider, whether that be a centralized store such as AWS or Google, or a decentralized store such as Filecoin. Nevertheless, the individual provider can choose which storage provider to trust, and the OP solution offers individuals the option to use a decentralized solution without having to place data in a centralized data storage provider. In a previous version of the architecture (V2), the URL for the stored data was kept in multiple secret stores (i.e., EVMs), with each EVM storing a fragment of a data location URL. In a later version of the architecture, encryption/decryption of the URL is handled by the Brizio (Provider). When this component is operated by the OP marketplace, it requires the data provider to place their trust in the Provider to keep the location of the data secret and secure.
