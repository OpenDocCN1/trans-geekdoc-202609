
## Blockchain, AI, and Data Protection in Healthcare

Neither solution really guarantees protection against the unauthorized secondary use. This suggests that much more work could be done to extend the mechanisms to prevent data double spending – whether algorithmic or afforded by human governance – in each solution. For example, one novel research direction could be to explore 'tokenomics,' or cryptocurrency-based incentive mechanisms, could be used in both solutions to incentivize data double spending by sharing data rewards (or rewarding consumers who use data appropriately).

Overall, we found that the SSI solution provided slightly greater protection against data double spending in that it does not use a persistent identifier for data providers, nor require them to provide identifying information or allow for data access rights (i.e., the access tokens) to be passed to a third party by the consumer, as in the case of the OP. The SSI solution also had an advantage in a license agreement, which as that the consumer can be of a certain type (e.g., for-profit or not-for-profit organization, from a certain jurisdiction, having a certain type of certification, etc.). And above this, the SSI solution also requires that computations compute-to-FHE data, while the OP solution allows computation over raw data. The requirement to compute over FHE data incentivizes the copying and retention of data for unspecified future use. Using homomorphic encryption to safeguard data privacy, however requires a significant amount of computational resources and is less efficient when compared to computation on unencrypted data. That is, in a commercial scenario, consumers may be forced to pay high computational resources in exchange for more stringent data protection. Thus, future research could be done to widen the scope of computations that can be performed over fully homomorphically encrypted data and increase the efficiency of processing in order to make the provision of FHE data more useful. Although processing of FHE data is costly at present, the SSI solution does not rely on smart contracts, which also require the use of gas to operate. In our view, would likely make the SSI solution less expensive to operate generally. Nevertheless, the OP solution offered merchants for price discovery in its marketplace.

We also found that both solutions still rely upon centralized trusts, working toward a greater level of decentralization of the architectures and trust models for each solution would be beneficial to guard against both single points of failure and malicious manipulation that could lead to data double spending.

Each solution has its strengths and weaknesses in relation to the principles of fairness in data processing, specifically with respecting to preventing unauthorized secondary use, or data double spending, the focus of our research. We acknowledge that the principles we explored did not address issues of data privacy and security, nor do they offer an exhaustive set of design principles for fair data processing (indeed, [17] points to how complex is the issue of interpreting what fairness means in the context of data protection legislation); nevertheless, our research helps refine understanding of the issue of data double spending and its relationship to the basics of fairness in data processing, which is an issue that has become of increasing concern to consumers and data protections alike. Further, this chapter provides a comparative analysis of the capabilities of two very different based marketplaces in relation to the principles of fairness and prevention of data double spending.

## Blockchain and Artificial Intelligence-Based Solution

From this comparative analysis, it is possible to see how the principles of fairness in data process design might be tackled by blockchain protocols and identify by novel capabilities that prevent or reduce the likelihood of double spending being introduced into data marketplaces. We believe these types of improvements would be socially valuable because, if individuals had greater ability to retain control usage of their data, even after initially consenting to its use, it could generate more trust and a greater willingness to share real-world data for preventing disease, identifying new therapies, and promoting health and wellness.
