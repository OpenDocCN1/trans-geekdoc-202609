## 7.1 INTRODUCTION

Cyber influence is fake propaganda on social media. Threat actors are doing this for a specific reason, which can be political, economic, or military purposes. Has anyone read fake news or watched fake videos and photos about Russo-Ukrainian? Yes. The cyber threat actors want to gain the public hearts; they send news to anyone, mainly on social media. That’s the new part and the black of social media.

Social media plays a big part in spreading fake news because it provides practical ways that make disinformation easy to do, fast, and cheap. Instead of humans, robots, called bots, send automated messages to thousands of users. Indeed, threat actors use different techniques to make fake news viral. Those well-known platforms, such as Facebook and Twitter, are the most used to communicate with now. Studies have been realized to understand influence competence. But others like YouTube and TikTok are social media that no studies have been realized as of today. There are real blocks.

Countermeasures can be applied by users and by social media themselves. In this chapter, we will show that it is impossible to eradicate fake news because measures are not enough automated and are not proactive or, in other words, The consequences are that cyber influence has negative impacts on society, from anti-vax conspiracy which has altered world unity to political influence. General Paul Nakasone, director of the United States National Security Agency, declared that “foreign influence operations would be the next disruptor” (de Rochechede & Tenenbaum, 2021).

Who influencers? We do not know exactly who they are. Social media platforms don’t have the responsibility to track them. Indeed, this is one of the most fundamental problems with social media. Some influencers are megadons, who have the power, in other words, who have the control; as example: the Mercer family, who had played a big role in the 2020 US presidential election and financed last year over $20 million in secret contributions. As another example, we can cite Steve Bannon, who was Trump’s advisor, and at the same time, was sitting on the board of Cambridge Analytica. In addition, those companies have created social profiles to influence American voters to vote for Trump.

## 7.2 BACKGROUND

### 7.2.1 CONTEXT

In this section, we will define what is cyber influence and then we will portray the type of influencers.

We notice an absence of standardized definitions of cyber influence. Here are the frequency terms to describe cyber influence, by order of importance: fake news, misinformation, propaganda, disinformation, influence campaigns/operations/war (Smith & Thompson, 2020).

Those words are all FALSE. Information can be intentional (disinformation) or not (misinformation). Disinformation is “information that is false and deliberately created to harm a person, social group, organization or country social platforms are conduits of the information. Misinformation is generally used to refer to misleading information created without malicious intent (Berge, 2018). Propaganda is the promotion of ideas.

Cyber influence is to obtain an advantage over adversary (Grimes, 1978). We will see further that it could be a political, economic or ideological advantage. So cyber influence is generally part of a whole strategy but to avoid a military response, the cyber actors adapt their digital techniques to stay anonymous (Hhenschke et al., 2020). In general, fake news, propaganda and disinformation are all synonyms. Cyber influence is spreading fake news or disinformation on social media to have a huge influence without spending a lot of money. It's worth it.

It's important to distinguish the word “influence” and “interference.” Interference is activities that violate the law (Government of Canada, 2021). But the line between legal and illegal is sometimes fuzzy.

### 7.2.2 TYPES OF INFLUENCERS

The Communications Security Establishment of Canada has identified cyber threat actors’ profiles. Those who can influence the democratic process are mainly those: nation-states, hacktivists, and political actors (Government of Canada, 2018). We will detail each of these.

#### 7.2.2.1 Nation-states

Influenced countries, such as the US, China, and Russia, have the most sophisticated means and use cyber influence for economic, ideological and political purposes. As we have during the 2016 US presidential election, threat actors have tried to amplify social disputes, like Black Lives Matter. As noted in The Canadian cyber threat assessment, “they try to emphasize friction in democratic societies around political, economic issues, and other values such as human and liberty. They adapt their dialogues to the new context and change their strategies” (Government of Canada, 2020). They even pay journalists to write biased articles to give more credibility to the messages; we have seen that in the past.

According to this (Martin & Shapiro, 2019), Russia has been the leading country to use cyber influence strategies around the world since 2017. Indeed, the Russian army, the Kremlin, largely finances these activities. Their goal is to erode the liberal model, by exploiting the internal division within liberal parties in Europe and US and amplifying social division.

Even the US is the most targeted country with 38% of cyber influence cases since 2013 (Martin & Shapiro, 2019), this state used well cyber influence to gain economic and ideological purposes. For examples noted in De Rocheonde and Tenenbaum (2021), cyber influence can be used to ban extremist ideologies as Islamist organizations. It could also be used to disrupt an adversary’s activities as was the case, in 2016, when the US wanted to confuse and influence the perceptions of Islamic State fighters.
