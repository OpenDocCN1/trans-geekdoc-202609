
## 2. Network Layer

It is the layer that connects the perceptual and middleware layers, as the name implies. It uses networking technologies like 3G, 4G, UMTS, Wi-Fi, infrared, and others to obtain data from the perception layer and send it to the middleware layer. Because it is responsible for communication between the perception and middleware layers, it is also known as the communication layer. All data transfers are done securely, with the collected information remaining confidential.

## 3. Middleware Layer

This includes sophisticated functionality such as storage, calculation, processing, and action-taking. It saves all data and sends it to the relevant device depending on the device’s address and name. It may also make judgments based on computations performed on sensor data sets.

## 4. Business/Support Layer

Any device’s success is determined not only by the technology employed in it but also by how it is presented to its users. These responsibilities are managed by the device’s business layer, which includes creating flowcharts, graphs, analyzing data, and determining how the device can be enhanced, among other tasks.

## 5. Application Layer

All application activities are handled by the application layer using data obtained from the middleware layer. This application can be used to perform a variety of functions, including sending emails, setting alarms, turning on or off security systems, wearables, and smart agriculture.

## 1.4 Medical Imaging Sources

In order to diagnose, monitor, or treat medical disorders, the term “medical imaging” refers to a number of various technologies that are used to visualize the human body. Each technology type provides distinct information on the part of the body being investigated or treated, in relation to potential illness, damage, or the efficacy of medical care. The connectivity of the IoT allows medical imaging equipment to operate more efficiently and effectively, while reducing costs. This section provides details on the various sources of medical imaging and how the IoT contributes to illness detection through medical imaging, as depicted in [Figure 1.3](#ch1.xhtml#fig1_3). The limited sources of medical imaging used by the medical community to diagnose diseases are also detailed in this section \[[29](#ch1.xhtml#ref1_29)\].

### 1. Ultrasound Imaging (Sonography)

High-frequency sound waves are used in ultrasound imaging, also known as sonography, to look within the body. Real-time ultrasound imaging allows for the display of blood flowing via blood vessels as well as movement of the body’s interior organs. Contrary to X-ray imaging, ultrasonic imaging does not expose patients to ionizing radiation.

A transducer (probe) is positioned either on the skin or within a bodily orifice during an ultrasonic examination. The skin is covered with a small layer of gel to allow the ultrasonic waves to pass through the gel and into the body.

[Figure 1.4](#ch1.xhtml#fig1_4) depicts the probe (transducer) used during an ultrasound examination. The ultrasound picture is created as a result of the waves’ reflection off the body’s structures. The information required to create a picture is provided by the intensity (amplitude) of the sound signal and the duration of the wave’s passage through the body.

### 2. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

A medical imaging process called MRI creates pictures of the inside body structures. To create pictures, MRI scanners employ radio waves (radiofrequency energy) and high magnetic fields. Protons found in water and fat molecules make up the majority of the signal in MR images.

An electric current is sent via coils of wire during an MRI test to produce a transient magnetic field inside the body of the patient. A transmitter and receiver in the device broadcast and receive radio waves, and these signals are utilized to create digital photographs of the body part being scanned. Depending on the portion of the body being examined, a normal MRI scan might take anywhere between 20 and 90 minutes \[[30](#ch1.xhtml#ref1_30)\].

### 3. X-Ray Imaging for Pediatrics

The identification and management of a variety of pediatric medical disorders have improved due to medical X-ray imaging. Pediatric patients are those who are under the age of 21 at the time of their diagnosis or treatment, according to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). They are typically divided into several categories based on age ranges (neonates, infants, children, and adolescents). The size of the juvenile patient is even more crucial to consider for medical X-ray imaging than the patient’s age, as the patient’s size affects the amount of radiation required to produce a high-quality medical image \[[31](#ch1.xhtml#ref1_31)\].

### 4. Medical X-Ray Imaging

Numerous medical disorders in both children and adults can now be diagnosed and treated more accurately, thanks to medical imaging. Medical imaging processes come in a variety of forms, or modalities, and each one employs a unique set of tools and methods. Radiography, including mammography, CT, and fluoroscopy, uses ionizing radiation to provide pictures of the body. However, the chance of developing cancer over a person’s lifetime may increase if they are exposed to ionizing radiation, which has enough energy to potentially harm DNA.

### 5. X-Ray (X-Radiation)

The utilization of radiation allows for the sharp, precise imaging provided by X-rays. X-rays create images of the internal structure by penetrating it with electromagnetic radiation, which is useful for diagnosing and identifying anomalies within the body. In their study, Kieffer et al. \[[32](#ch1.xhtml#ref1_32)\] describe the use of X-rays in medical imaging.

To enhance the contrast of certain MRI scans, intravenous (IV) medications such as gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) are used. GBCAs, which are rare earth metal-based, are typically administered through an IV in the arm.

### 6. Computed Tomography (CT)

The non-invasive medical treatment known as CT, sometimes referred to as “computerized tomography” or “computed axial tomography” (CAT), creates cross-sectional images of the body using specialized X-ray equipment. Similar to the slices in a loaf of bread, each cross-sectional image displays a “slice” of the individual being examined. These cross-sectional images have various diagnostic and therapeutic applications.

CT scans can be performed on any part of the body for multiple purposes, such as diagnosis, treatment planning, intervention, or screening. The majority of CT scans are conducted as outpatient procedures.
