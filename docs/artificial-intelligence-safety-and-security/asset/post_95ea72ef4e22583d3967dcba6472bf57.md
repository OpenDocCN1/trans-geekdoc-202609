
## INTRODUCTION

In October 1962, during the Cuban Missile Crisis, humanity came close to a devastating war, enabled and caused by the menacing side of nuclear technology. We are now faced with the question of whether artificial intelligence (AI), a more powerful and promising technology, presents us with perhaps even more potent existential threats. After all, nuclear weapons don’t decide to launch themselves; humans, and perhaps soon, AI entities (AEs), do. More troubling could be the myriad of routine but pervasive little decisions that AI would slowly take over or influence, like whom to hire or what views to propagate. Our own history, as well as our everyday experience, tells us that intelligent beings can be quite dangerous to other intelligent beings, both at the individual and group levels.

The dangers posed by AI appear dauntingly broad *and existential* in nature. Understandably, some are calling to curtail AI severely. Historically, however, we have never been able to prevent progress. The real question is how to manage progress to keep ourselves safe. Given the speed with which AI is growing and infiltrating our society, there is also the question of whether realistic measures can be conceived and implemented before it is too late; that is, before an existential threat is faced. Moreover, the transition itself from where we are now until AI matures, and which will be dominated by “inferior” AI, presents its own dangers.

Let us first make a distinction between two aspects of AI. As an *automation* technology, which has mainly economic impacts, AI is not really new. We have absorbed many waves of automation before, to great benefit in the long run. But AI automation will evolve faster and deeper than the previous waves. So, in the short term, there will be economic disruption, which although not existential in nature, can be quite painful and can lead to upheaval and conflict [1,2]. If AI is strictly viewed as “capital,” it may also lead to an imbalance between capital and labor [3] that can be dangerous. We may find that the disruption and wealth brought by AI may require foundational changes in our societies. Nevertheless, we will not concentrate on the economic impacts of AI in this chapter. Instead we will examine the other aspect of AI, as a fundamentally new creature posing existential threats.

Improvising haphazardly after the fact against a high and broad risk profile is unwise, so laying the intellectual groundwork for AI Safety and Security (AISS) is necessary. Currently, AISS is mostly a forward-looking, volunteer-based, technical discipline of interest to people with deep backgrounds in technology, which seems sensible and natural.

In this chapter, however, we present a somewhat contrarian view, not only to present feasible solutions but also to stimulate new to how they perform in an ecosystem. HI, on the other hand, has both “memory and desire”; it does not only learn and adapt based on *past* events, but it can also actively make decisions that incorporate explicit *desired future* states. In other words, HI can actively *define* as well as seek goals. In addition, HI is communicative. As such, HI and cognitive AI will increasingly share many similarities inherent in their nature.

We should also not forget that the human brain inspires AI. Many advanced forms of AI use neural processing and employ knowledge representation that mimics that of the human brain (as we understand it). In fact, some definitions of “cognitive systems” refer to the ability of these systems to simulate or mimic the human brain [4]. The conclusions above still mostly hold even with other forms of AI that use very different hardware than the one we use today, or that depend on new architectures that do not mimic the human brain. In his book *Out of Control*, Kevin Kelly [5] described a series of experiments that strongly suggest that intelligence, self-learning, and even creativity seem to be implied characteristics of certain evolving information systems, whether they are systems of genes undergoing evolution or learning computational devices.

## Threat Profiles of Human, Gene, and Artificial Intelligence

For convenience, we state two conjectures on which our approach is based. The first states that the AI risk profile is similar to that of HI because both intelligences are similar at a fundamental level. We’ll call that the **HI conjecture**. The second conjecture states that control and resiliency mechanisms implied in GI can be reused to achieve stability in a system that has both HI and many species of AI. We’ll call that the **GI conjecture**. The latter is harder to justify, since GI is not communicative or inherently goal seeking. However, we note in particular that GI constructed a *huge* ecosystem that is largely in equilibrium with no *conscious* intelligence behind it. So, GI offers control mechanisms that are in a layer more fundamental and of a different nature than autonomous intelligence like that of HI or AI. Notice that GI is also a source of threats to humans (e.g., viruses), but we will ignore that in this chapter.

If one accepts these two conjectures, or at least the first one, one can conclude that the risks of AI are not entirely new and that we already have experience in managing many of them. **In fact, the entire human history can be interpreted as a struggle by HI to harness its own potential while containing the risks due to its own limitations, faults, prejudices, and pathologies, both at the individual and group levels.**

This is somewhat reassuring. All intelligences seem to be inherently dangerous, but the human ability to contain the dangers of HI and to employ (sometimes unwittingly) controls used by GI suggests that we can contain the dangers of AI. We will need to adapt and reuse much of the structures, institutions, ethics, etiquettes, processes, common sense, and even paranoias that we have devised over centuries to contain the risks of HI to apply them to AI.

There are two concerns that can be stated at this point. *First*: the proposed approach may appear to be a top-down architecture requiring mostly engineering, and may not require significant investment from nontechnical disciplines. Our view is that there must be a rebirth of humanities, ethics, law, and business and government policies to reinterpret our own traditions to harness this emerging form of intelligence and guarantee our own place in the world.
