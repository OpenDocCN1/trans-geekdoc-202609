### 11.2.5 Ingestible Sensors

Proteus Digital Health and its ingestible sensors represent another scenario of how smart medication can monitor adherence. According to the World Health Organization in 2003, half of prescriptions were not taken as desired. Proteus' framework represents one effort to reduce such figures. The organization has designed pills that break down in the stomach and generate a signal that can be detected by a sensor worn on the body. Afterwards, the information is passed to a mobile phone application, confirming that the patient has taken the medication as directed.

Proteus has so far tested this system with pills for treating uncontrolled hypertension, Type 2 Diabetes, and antipsychotic medication. In late 2017, ABILIFY MYCITE—an antipsychotic drug manufactured by Proteus and Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co.—became the first FDA-approved product with a digital ingestion tracking system. Similarly to connected inhalers, ingestible sensors can track and improve how often patients take their medication, while enabling them to have a more informed dialogue with their doctor regarding treatment. While ingesting pills with a sensor may seem intrusive, the architecture is designed with respect to patients, and it can stop sharing certain types of data or stop the program during clinical use.

Therapeutic smart contact lenses represent a good application of the Internet of Things in a healthcare setting. Although the concept has great potential, the science has not yet determined how to fulfill such goals. In 2014, Google Life Sciences (currently called Verily, a subsidiary of Google's parent organization Alphabet) announced that it would develop a smart contact lens that can measure tear glucose and provide an early warning system for diabetics to alert them when their blood glucose levels are falling or rising or exceed a certain threshold. It also partnered with Alcon, the eye care division of Novartis, for its development.

In any case, the project drew significant skepticism from physicians who believed that measuring blood glucose levels through tears was not scientifically sound. Eventually, they proved correct. After a long period with no real news regarding project developments, in November 2018 it was announced that the project was being shelved. Moreover, first developed in 2010, Triggerfish, which is now CE-marked and FDA-approved, is approved for marketing and sales in Europe and the United States, and was approved for sale in Japan in September 2018.

### 11.2.6 The Apple Watch Application That Screens for Depression

Wearable technology does not normally need to be designed with medical use in mind to provide healthcare benefits. Takeda Pharmaceuticals USA and Cognition Kit Limited, a platform for measuring mental health, collaborated in 2017 to explore the use of an Apple Watch application for monitoring and assessing patients with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). The results from the exploratory study were presented in November 2017 at the CNS Summit pharmaceutical and biotech conference. The research established a high level of compliance with the app, with participants using it daily to track mood alongside cognition.

Participants' daily assessments were also found to correlate with more in-depth and objective cognitive tests and patient-reported outcomes, indicating that psychological tests delivered via an app can still be robust and reliable. While the study was only exploratory, it demonstrated the potential for wearable technology to be used to assess the effects of depression continuously. Similar to smart medical devices that collect data, the Apple Watch app can provide more insight to patients and healthcare professionals while enabling relevant collaboration.

### 11.2.7 Coagulation Testing

In 2016, Roche launched a Bluetooth-enabled coagulation system that enables patients to check how quickly their blood clots. It represents the first device of its kind for anticoagulation patients to self-test. It appears to help patients stay within their therapeutic range and reduce the risk of stroke or bleeding. The ability to transfer results to healthcare providers indicates reduced visits to the clinic. The device also allows patients to add comments to their results while reminding them to test.

### 11.2.8 Apple's ResearchKit and Parkinson's Disease

Apple added a 'Movement Disorder API' to its open-source ResearchKit framework in 2018, allowing Apple Watches to monitor Parkinson's disease symptoms. Typical symptoms are assessed by doctors at clinics via physical exams, while patients are encouraged to keep a journal to provide broader insight into symptoms over time. The API aims to automate and standardize that process.

An application on an associated iPhone can display the information in a chart, providing daily and hourly breakdowns, as well as minute-by-minute symptom changes. Apple's ResearchKit has also been used in various health studies, including an arthritis study conducted in collaboration with GSK. Apple is quick to tout the potential for its applications to assist with medical research and care, and with that in mind, in 2017 it launched CareKit, an open-source framework intended to help developers create apps for managing health conditions.

Unlike HealthKit, which is geared more toward general wellness and well-being, CareKit can be used to build apps with a specific medical purpose—thereby opening this space to more medical innovations that use iPhone and Apple Watch technology.

### 11.2.9 ADAMM Asthma Monitor

ADAMM is a wearable smart asthma monitor designed to detect asthma attack symptoms before they begin, allowing the wearer to manage it before the attack worsens. It vibrates to alert the wearer of an impending asthma attack and can send a text message to a designated contact at the same time. Key features include inhaler detection—the device can detect and track inhaler usage if the patient cannot remember whether they have used it—and voice journaling to record changes, feelings, and behaviors. It also features baseline estimation technology that reveals what is 'normal' for the wearer over time, enabling it to better detect when anything has changed.

ADAMM works with any iOS and web-based interface, helping asthma patients set medication reminders, visualize data from the device, and adhere to their treatment plan. The device was initially expected to receive FDA clearance and be released to consumers by the end of 2017, but has not yet been cleared, showing that such devices can sometimes take a long time to reach the market even after development. On the other hand, a study on patient health monitoring platforms that integrate IoT devices published in July 2018 states that ADAMM is "expected to receive FDA clearance soon" [3].
