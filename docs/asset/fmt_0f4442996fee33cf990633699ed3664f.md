# 12.1.4 Impact of Autonomous Vehicles on the Automotive Industry

It is precisely because autonomous vehicles have produced unprecedented impacts that they are causing huge changes in the automotive industry. Relevant studies indicate that with the emergence of autonomous vehicles, highway safety will be greatly improved, and air pollution and traffic congestion will be effectively alleviated.

## 12.1.4.1 Improving Highway Safety

Today, highway accidents are a major problem plaguing the world. According to statistics, in the United States, about 35,000 people die in car accidents each year; in China, about 260,000 people die in car accidents each year; in Japan, about 4,000 people die in highway accidents each year. According to World Health Organization data, the global annual death toll from highway accidents reaches 1.24 million.

Statistics show that annual losses caused by fatal car accidents reach 260 billion US dollars, losses caused by car accident injuries reach 356 billion US dollars, and annual losses caused by highway accidents are about 625 billion US dollars. According to a survey by the RAND Corporation, among various factors leading to fatal traffic accidents, drunk driving accounts for 39%. In this regard, the widespread application of autonomous vehicles will effectively reverse this situation, reducing or even avoiding car accident casualties. In addition, according to data released by transportation departments, 60% of all traffic accidents in China are caused by collisions between pedestrians, bicycles, electric bicycles and cars, trucks. Human error leads to about 94% of motor vehicle accidents in the United States.

Research by the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) in the United States shows that with the installation of automatic safety devices (collision braking systems, collision warning systems, lane departure warnings, blind spot detection, etc.), driving on highways will be safer, and highway accident deaths can be reduced by 31% — that is, about 11,000 traffic accident deaths can be prevented each year.

## 12.1.4.2 Alleviating Traffic Congestion

Traffic congestion is an "epidemic" in large cities. In the United States, each driver spends about 40 hours per year stuck on the road due to traffic congestion, and the cost of traffic congestion is about 121 billion US dollars. In large cities such as Moscow and Rio de Janeiro, the time wasted due to traffic congestion is even more severe, with each driver wasting more than 100 hours per year in traffic congestion. In China, there are about 35 cities with car ownership exceeding 1 million, and 10 cities exceeding 2 million; about 75% of roads in urban areas of large cities are in a congested state. The number of private cars has reached 126 million, an increase of 15% over the previous year.

Donald Shoup's research found that 30% of traffic congestion in urban areas of large cities is caused by drivers circling back and forth looking for parking spaces. This is precisely a factor leading to traffic congestion and air pollution, because 30% of carbon dioxide emissions come from automobiles, and carbon dioxide is considered the culprit of climate change. In addition, it is estimated that 23%–45% of traffic congestion occurs at intersections. Because traffic lights and stop signs are fixed, and time intervals are preset, they cannot take into account traffic flow from all directions, making it difficult to truly play a role in regulating traffic flow.

As the proportion of autonomous vehicles in traffic flow increases, on-board sensors will be combined with intelligent transportation systems to optimize traffic flow at intersections. The time intervals of traffic lights will be automatically adjusted according to traffic flow from all directions, thereby improving vehicle passage efficiency at intersections and providing effective solutions to traffic congestion problems.

## 12.1.4.3 Reducing Air Pollution

Automobiles are a very important source of air pollution. According to research by the RAND Corporation (by James M. Anderson, Nidhi Kalra, Karlyn D. Stanley, Paul Sorensen, Constantine Samaras, Tobi A. Oluwatola et al. [www.rand.org/t/RR443-2](http://www.rand.org/t/RR443-2)), compared with manual driving, autonomous driving technology can improve fuel efficiency by 4% to 10% through smoother acceleration and deceleration. According to surveys, haze in industrial areas is closely related to the number of automobiles. Some studies prove that exhaust emissions from cars forced to stop at red lights or in traffic congestion are 40% higher than during normal driving. The increase in the number of autonomous vehicles can alleviate air pollution to a large extent.

Autonomous vehicle sharing systems may help achieve energy conservation. Researchers at the University of Texas at Austin studied carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, volatile organic compounds, fine particulate matter, and greenhouse gases, and found that autonomous vehicle sharing systems can save energy and reduce emissions of various pollutants.

Uber found that 30% of passengers in Los Angeles and 50% of passengers in San Francisco choose carpooling. Globally, about 20% of passengers choose carpooling. Whether autonomous vehicles or traditional vehicles, the more passengers choose carpooling, the better the environmental benefits, and the more traffic congestion is alleviated. Because carpooling changes the "one person per car" travel mode, air quality has been effectively improved.

An autonomous vehicle is an intelligent vehicle, also known as a wheeled mobile robot. It mainly relies on the intelligent driver inside the vehicle to make autonomous driving a reality.

#### 12.2.2.1 Foreign Autonomous Driving Technology Development

Western developed countries began research on autonomous vehicles in the 1970s, achieving major breakthroughs in feasibility and practicality. By the late 1980s to early 1990s, due to overly complex expected goals and immature technical conditions, many countries withdrew from autonomous vehicle research projects and turned to highway civilian vehicle assisted driving projects. Autonomous vehicles during this period belonged to semi-automated driving vehicles, which still had a large gap from truly autonomous vehicles.

It was not until 1999 that an autonomous vehicle developed by Carnegie Mellon University in the United States successfully crossed parts of the eastern and western United States. On the 5,000-kilometer interstate highway, the vehicle drove autonomously at a speed of 50-60 km/h for more than 96% of the mileage, marking the success of the autonomous vehicle project.

In 2000, Toyota Motor Corporation developed an autonomous bus and built an automatic driving system composed of road guidance, anti-rear-end collision, platooning, and operation management. In 2007, Germany launched the autonomous vehicle Lux, which was modified from an ordinary car and used laser sensor technology, intelligent computers, and global positioning devices. The autonomous Lux could cope with complex road conditions and drive automatically in urban highway systems.
