# Genetic-Epigenetic Interplay Discovery

# 4.1 A biological question — the genetic-epigenetic interplay pattern discovery problem

The DNA methylation is a common process which covalently modifies DNA bases and adds a methyl group to the cytosine or adenine DNA bases. It has been widely studied in many species including bacteria, plants, and mammalian cells [Capuano, et al., 2014]. Though methylation can occur at both CpG and non-CpG islands, most studies have focused on the methylations at CpG islands in the promoter regions and the gene bodies [Jones and Liang, 2009; de Carvalho, et al., 2010; Lou, et al., 2014]. The fact that methylation modification can be inherited across generations has investigated influences into various cellular functions and disease development [Law and Jacobson, 2010; Boyko, et al., 2011; Iqbal, et al., 2011; Cedar and Bergman, 2012; Wagner, et al., 2014; Meng, et al., 2015].

The non-reversible property of methylation has led to the studies of how methylation influences disease development [Jones and Liang, 2009; Iqbal, et al., 2011; Chambaz and Neuvial, 2016]. The methylation strength is measured by the beta value which is defined as the ratio of methyl signal over total methylation and non-methylated signals [Chari, et al., 2010]. The methylation strength is classified as the hypo- or hyper-methylation representing less or more methylation depending on the magnitude and the sign of differential methylation ratio for comparing an experiment sample against a control sample [Ehrlich, 2002; Chari, et al., 2010]. The hypo-methylation has been found in relationships with tumour genesis [Ehrlich, 2002], gene expression profiling [O'Neil, et al., 1998; Walsh, et al., 1998; Morgan, et al., 1999; Tao, et al., 2000]. The hyper-methylation can also affect gene expression profiling, leading to some diseases [Network, 2011; Fujiwara-Igarashi, et al., 2014; Bryan, et al., 2009].

The combined or integrative study of the epigenetic and the genetic signatures can be used to discover more biological insight than using a gene expression data set alone. For instance, several studies have found that the interplay between the epigenetic and the genetic signatures is one of the most important mechanisms leading to cancer development and cellular functions [Chang, et al., 2003; Chari, et al., 2010; Baylin and Jones, 2011; Sandoval and Esteller, 2012; You and Jones, 2012].
