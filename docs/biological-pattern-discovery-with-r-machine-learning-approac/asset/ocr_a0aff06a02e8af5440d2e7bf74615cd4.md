# 1.1 The responsive gene discovery problem

One major objective of a biological investigation through an experiment is to identify genes with some desired functions. When a species or an organism has experienced a stress and some phenotypic phenomenon has been observed, it is desirable to study which genes have responded to the stress. A gene which shows a significant response to a stress in an association with a phenotypical phenomenon or phenomenon change is called a responsive gene [Alves, et al., 2016]. Responsive gene discovery in a stress-related experiment is called a genotype-phenotype mapping process [Bassel, 2019; Bolker, 2019; Koch, 2019; Lian, et al., 2019; Kawasaki, et al., 2020; Kowalko, 2020; Morris, et al., 2020].

The main objective of a genotype-phenotype mapping process is to establish either a qualitative or quantitative associative model. Based on such a model, it is aimed to discover and describe the dependency relationship between the genotype data and the phenotype data in an experiment. Afterwards, either the interpretation of the existing phenomena or the prediction of future events is followed.

A responsive gene is also called an essential gene or a differentially expressed gene depending on an experiment. Normally, the proportion of responsive genes is small in most biological experiments. This provides a traditional basis for simplifying, but not completely ignoring the complexity of a biological system to focus focal points of interest [Lai, et al., 2008; Chen, et al., 2011; Zeng, et al., 2013; Alves, et al., 2016; Liu, et al., 2016; Antolovic, et al., 2019; Sidorenko, et al., 2019; Trolet, et al., 2019].

There are mainly two types of approaches for the genotype-phenotype mapping study. The two types depend on how an experiment has been carried out.
