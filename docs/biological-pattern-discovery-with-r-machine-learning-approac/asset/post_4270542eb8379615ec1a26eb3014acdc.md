## 6.6 Dual-scale Gaussian model for small replicate data DEG discovery

Biological data often has a problem of having insufficient replicates, which causes the difficulty of robust DEG discovery. This section will introduce an alternative called the dual-scale Gaussian model (DSG) which can model a gene expression data set without replicate or with a few replicates (Everson, 2014; Al watban, 2015).

## 6.6.1 The dual-scale Gaussian model

### 6.6.1.1 The working principle of DSG

DSG is a mixture model composed of two Gaussian components with an identical centre for both Gaussian components but different variances as shown in Figure 6.47 (Bertschinger, 2001; Billings, et al., 2007; Kramer, et al., 2007). A vector $x = \{z_i\}_{i=1}^n$ is assumed to follow a mixture of two Gaussians. One of them has a smaller variance which referred to as the null density $f_0$ and other a greater variance referred to as the alternative density $f_1$. Figure 6.47 demonstrates the working principle of DSG. The null density $f_0$ has a smaller variance, hence a sharp peak at the centre zero. The alternative density $f_1$ has a greater variance, hence a flattened curve extending towards two extremes. The identification of a DEG depends on the magnitudes of $f_0$ and $f_1$. For instance, a gene marked by the pair of the dots in the figure is identified as a non-DEG because of the relationship $f_0 > f_1$. However, a gene marked by the pair of the triangles is identified as a DEG because of the relationship $f_0 < f_1$.

A standard DSG density function $f(z|\theta)$ given the model parameters $\theta = \{\mu_0, \mu_1, \sigma_0^2, \sigma_1^2, w_0, w_1\}$ has the form shown below,

$f(z|	theta) = w_0\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_0, \sigma_0^2) + w_1\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_1, \sigma_1^2) \quad (6.43)$

where $w < 1$ is the mixing coefficient or weight of the $m^{th}$ Gaussian and $w_0 + w_1 = 1$

$\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_m,\sigma_m^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_m}\exp\left(\frac{(z-\mu_m)^2}{2\sigma_m^2}\right) \quad (6.44)$

In such a model, $\sigma_0^2$ is the variance of the null density and $\sigma_1^2 \gg \sigma_0^2$ is the variance of the alternative density. When this condition is satisfied, it can be seen that the alternative density will be much flatter. This model is also called a two-component Gaussian mixture model. In this model, $\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)$ models the informative subset of the data. In this subset, the data has a little variation from zero, hence uninformative. Non-DEGs will belong to this category, hence the null data. The other component $\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ models the informative subset of the data. The data points (genes) in this subset have a great deviation from zero, hence very informative. A small $\sigma_0^2$ naturally leads to a sharp peak centred at $\mu_0$. When $\sigma_1^2 \gg \sigma_0^2$, $\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ approximates a uniform distribution in the extreme case and the identification of the null data is straightforward. The likelihood function of such a model is defined below,

$P(Z|\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(z_i|\theta) \quad (6.45)$

A conjugate inverse gamma distributed priors on the variances included in the model,

$IG(\sigma_m^2|a_m, b_m) = \frac{b_m^{a_m}\sigma_m^{-2(a_m+1)}}{\Gamma(a_m)} \exp\left(-\frac{b_m}{\sigma_m^2}\right) \quad (6.46)$

The mixing coefficients are modelled using the non-informative priors and each of $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$ is assigned a Gaussian prior with a zero mean and a small deviation $\tau_m$ which is a hyper-parameter (the inverse of a variance),

$\mu_m \sim \mathcal{G}(0, \tau_m) \quad (6.47)$

Moreover $|\tau_m| \to 0$ indicates the null data. $\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ is estimated from data such that $|\mu_0| \to 0$ by consistency. The priors are set to zero based on the observation close to zero and both $\tau_m$ to be 0.5. Suppose $\alpha = \{a_0, a_1, b_0, b_1, \tau_0, \tau_1\}$ and $\beta_m = \sigma_m^{-2}$. The posterior of DSG can be written as,

$P(\theta|Z, \alpha) \propto P(Z|\theta)P(\alpha) = \left[ \prod_{i=1}^{n} \{w_0\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_0, \beta_0^{-1}) + w_1\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_1, \beta_1^{-1})\} \right] \quad (6.48)$

$\prod_{m=0} \frac{b_m^{a_m}\beta_m^{a_m}}{\Gamma(a_m)} \mathcal{G}(0, \tau_m)$

The log-posterior can be written as,

$\log P(\theta|Z, \alpha) \propto \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f(z_i|\theta) + \sum_{m=0}^{1} \log B_m \quad (6.49)$

Here $B_m$ is defined as,

$B_m = (a_m + 1)\log\beta_m - b_m - 0.5\mu_m^2 \quad (6.50)$

The model parameters are estimated by maximising the posterior (Bishop, 2006), giving the iterative variance update below,

$\sigma_m^2 = \frac{\sum_i \vartheta_{i,m}z_i + 2b_m}{\sum_i \vartheta_{i,m} + 2a_m} \quad (6.51)$

where $\vartheta_{i,m}$ is defined as,

$\vartheta_{i,m} = \frac{w_m\mathcal{G}(z_i|\mu_m, \sigma_m^2)}{f(z_i|\theta)} \quad (6.52)$

The iterative update rule for the mixing coefficients can be written as,

$w_m = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \vartheta_{i,m} \quad (6.53)$

and the iterative update rule for each centre is, where $\mu_{0} = 0$,

$\mu_m = \frac{\beta_m\sum_i \vartheta_{i,m}z_i + 2\mu_{m0}}{\beta_m\sum_i \vartheta_{i,m} + 2} \quad (6.54)$
