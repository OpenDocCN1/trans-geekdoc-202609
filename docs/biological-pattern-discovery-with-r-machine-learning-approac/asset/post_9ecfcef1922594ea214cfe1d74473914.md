## 4.1 A biological question — the genetic-epigenetic interplay pattern discovery problem

The DNA methylation is a common process which covalently modifies DNA bases and adds a methyl group to the cytosine or adenine DNA bases. It has been widely studied in many species including bacteria, plants, and mammalian cells [Capuano, et al., 2014]. Though methylation can occur at both CpG and non-CpG islands, most studies have focused on the methylations at CpG islands in the promoter regions and the gene bodies [Jones and Liang, 2009; de Carvalho, et al., 2010; Lou, et al., 2014]. The fact that methylation modification can be inherited across generations has investigated influences into various cellular functions and disease development [Law and Jacobson, 2010; Boyko, et al., 2011; Iqbal, et al., 2011; Cedar and Bergman, 2012; Wagner, et al., 2014; Meng, et al., 2015].

The non-reversible property of methylation has led to the studies of how methylation influences disease development [Jones and Liang, 2009; Iqbal, et al., 2011; Chambaz and Neuvial, 2016]. The methylation strength is measured by the beta value which is defined as the ratio of methyl signal over total methylation and non-methylated signals [Chari, et al., 2010]. The methylation strength is classified as the hypo- or hyper-methylation representing less or more methylation depending on the magnitude and the sign of differential methylation ratio for comparing an experiment sample against a control sample [Ehrlich, 2002; Chari, et al., 2010]. The hypo-methylation has been found in relationships with tumour genesis [Ehrlich, 2002], gene expression profiling [O'Neil, et al., 1998; Walsh, et al., 1998; Morgan, et al., 1999; Tao, et al., 2000]. The hyper-methylation can also affect gene expression profiling, leading to some diseases [Network, 2011; Fujiwara-Igarashi, et al., 2014; Bryan, et al., 2009].

The combined or integrative study of the epigenetic and the genetic signatures can be used to discover more biological insight than using a gene expression data set alone. For instance, several studies have found that the interplay between the epigenetic and the genetic signatures is one of the most important mechanisms leading to cancer development and cellular functions [Chang, et al., 2003; Chari, et al., 2010; Baylin and Jones, 2011; Sandoval and Esteller, 2012; You and Jones, 2012].

There are currently two types of integrative studies for the interplay between the epigenetic signatures and the genetic signatures. The first type analyzes the epigenetic signatures and the genetic signatures separately followed by a consistency validation about how the discovered differentially expressed genes and the discovered differentially methylated sites are correlated in the relationship with a disease. For instance, this approach was used to discover aberrant genes in association with breast cancer [Chari, et al., 2010]. The cluster analysis algorithms have also been used to examine whether the epigenetic signatures and the genetic signatures show similar abundance pattern in relation with diseases [Kresse, et al., 2012].

The second type of approach models the epigenetic-genetic interplay directly. For instance, the correlation analysis was used to examine how the DNA copy number and the methylation ratio interact with the gene expression based on the deep sequencing transcript expression for a breast cancer [Sun, et al., 2011]. When studying the sperm motility, a mixture model has been constructed based on the pooled methylation data and the gene expression data [Pacheco, et al., 2011]. The correlation between the methylation and sperm motility was examined using regression models, where the positive regression coefficient was mapped to a hyper-methylated site and the negative one hyper-methylated [Sms, 2004]. In one study, 62 primary fibroblast samples were used to analyse how the epigenetic signatures influence the genetic signatures for the species, where the gene expressions were regressed on the neighbouring methylation sites [Wagner, et al., 2014]. Another study also employed only local and few epigenetic signatures to examine their contributions to the regulation of genes [Ping, et al., 2015].

The objective of regression analysis is to establish a proper way to regress observations to their mean. In this case, $x_n$ is the data, $\sigma^2$ stands for the variance, $\mu$ is the unknown truth, the likelihood is defined as below,

$$\mathcal{L} = (2\pi)^{-N/2} \prod_{n=1}^{N} \exp\left(-\frac{(x_n-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \quad (4.1)$$

Applying the negative logarithm to this likelihood leads to the following equation,

$-\log\mathcal{L} = \sum_{n=1}^{N}(x_n-\mu)^2 + C \quad (4.2)$

Its derivative is shown below,

$$\frac{\partial(-\log\mathcal{L})}{\partial\mu} = \sum_{n=1}^{N}x_n - N\mu \quad (4.3)$$

Zeroing this derivative leads to the estimated mean as shown below,

$$\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_n \quad (4.4)$$

Just having a number of the regressed means may not be of great interest. In fact, if these regressed means are interpolated, a continuous regression function can be generated. This is what the magic regression analysis can do. An example is shown in Figure 4.2. Based on the observed data, which were the filled dots in the plot and treated as the corrupted observations, a regression process was used to find their true values, which are named as the regressed means. Based on the regressed means, an interpolation process is used to generate a regressed mean (regression) function. The regressed (regression) function is continuous in most regression analysis models. The most important feature of a continuous regression function is its capability for the inference on novel data.
