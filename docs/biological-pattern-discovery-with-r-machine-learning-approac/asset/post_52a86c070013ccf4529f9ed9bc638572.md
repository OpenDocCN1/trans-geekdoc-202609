# Gene Expression Pattern Discovery

Differentially expressed genes are treated as the major signal to understand how a stress results in a phenomenon change in a species. Scientists hypothesised that it is the genetic signature that ultimately causes the observed phenomenon change. The major objective of gene expression pattern discovery is to discover differentially expressed genes. In this chapter, the approaches for gene expression pattern discovery will be introduced for two major types of data, i.e., the microarray gene expression data and the RNA-seq sequencing count data. Gene expression pattern discovery may encounter the challenges of the outlier problem or the subpopulation problem. Therefore this chapter will also introduce the alternatives for gene expression pattern discovery. Moreover, insufficient replicate number is the common issue in gene expression pattern discovery. The dual-scale Gaussian mixture algorithm for discovering differentially expressed genes in a data set with insufficient replicates will be introduced in this chapter.

## 6.1 Differentially expressed genes

In most situations, an experiment with two conditions is conducted to discover which has caused a phenomenon change in a species for it to respond to a stress. These two experimental conditions normally have different settings. One is called the control condition, in which no stress is applied and the other is called the case condition, in which a stress is applied.

In a biological/medical research, the genetic signature space is composed of thousands of genes, which are examined simultaneously using either the microarray technology or the sequencing technology nowadays. The output of the former technology is called the microarray gene expression data, and the latter is called the sequencing count data. The phenomenon change can be observed in the output data of either technology by comparing two conditions. The main objective of gene expression pattern discovery is thus to search in the genetic space to discover a small subset of genes which have significantly different responses to a stress across experimental conditions.

An important understanding of a living system is that such a system will not use all its genes to respond to a stress [Liu, et al., 2016; Antolovic, et al., 2019; Siderenko, et al., 2019]. This is why the subset of genes, which have significantly different responses to a stress across two experimental conditions, is normally of a small size. To discover differentially expressed genes (DEGs) given two experimental conditions, it is required to compare genes one by one using a significance measurement. A measurement should be unified across different experiments or experimental data sets. Two commonly used significance measurements are the biological significance and the statistical significance [Chagoyen and Pazos, 2010; Mingue and Dopazo, 2011; Chagoyen, et al., 2017; Miller, et al., 2019].

### 6.1.1 The biological significance

The biological significance used to discover DEGs is the so-called fold change or the base two logarithm fold change. It has been exercised in biology since 1960s [Pauly, 1960; Hill and Sussman, 1964; Gilbert and Ehrenstein, 1969]. Suppose a case is denoted by $A$ and a control condition is denoted by $B$. The case normally represents the experimental group in which a stress is applied or a phenomenon change has been observed. The control condition normally represents the group in which no stress is applied or no phenomenon change has been observed. The fold change below is defined as the ratio between two mean expressions ($\mu_{g,A}$ and $\mu_{g,B}$). Note that $\mu_{g,A}$ and $\mu_{g,B}$ stand for the mean expressions of the $g^{th}$ gene.

$F_g = \frac{\mu_{g,A}}{\mu_{g,B}} \quad (6.1)$

The base two logarithm fold change is defined as below,

$\log_2(F_g) = \log_2\left(\frac{\mu_{g,A}}{\mu_{g,B}}\right) = \log_2(\mu_{g,A}) - \log_2(\mu_{g,B}) \quad (6.2)$

When a fold change is two, the base two logarithm fold change is one, meaning that the case expression is the double of the control mean expression. When a fold change is half, the base two logarithm fold change is negative, meaning that the control mean is double of the case mean expression. For short, the base two logarithm fold change is expressed as the fold change in the following discussions. A gene with a negative fold change is called a down-regulated gene and a gene with a positive fold change is called an up-regulated gene. The gene regulation direction is from a control to a case condition. If the fold change of a down-regulated DEG is less than a pre-defined negative threshold, the gene is named as a down-regulated DEG. If the fold change of an up-regulated gene expression is missing in the fold change space, the gene is named as an up-regulated DEG.

Figure 6.1 shows the distribution of the fold changes of all genes in a microarray gene expression data, in which the letrozole drug was used to treat the breast cancer patients [Miller, et al., 2007; Miller, et al., 2010]. The data set is composed of two conditions, i.e., before and after using the drug. The condition population was named as baseline before using the drug. The fold change threshold was one, 22 down-regulated DEGs and six up-regulated DEGs were identified. All the down-regulated DEGs were on the left side of the estimated density function of the fold change. Meanwhile, all the up-regulated DEGs were on the right side of the estimated density function of the fold change.

However, the fold change may have little information regarding the statistical significance.

### 6.1.2 The statistical significance

To examine whether a variable displays statistically significant difference between experimental conditions, the t-test is used. The null hypothesis is $H_0: \mu_A = \mu_B$.

$t = \frac{\hat{U}_A - \hat{U}_B}{\sigma(x_A, x_B)} \quad (6.3)$

A p value will be derived from the t-statistic and is used for accepting or rejecting the null hypothesis. Figure 6.2 shows two genes of letrozole data [Miller, 2007; Miller, et al., 2010]. The expressions of the gene NEK2 show a significant difference between two conditions and hence its p value is $2.28e-11$. Therefore, the null hypothesis is rejected and this gene is certainly a DEG in terms of statistical significance. However, the expressions of the gene SLC25A30 in two conditions overlapped leading to a large p value, which is almost one. Therefore the null hypothesis cannot be rejected and this gene is certainly not a DEG.

### 6.1.3 The Type I and Type II errors

A p value stands for a tolerated error when making a decision. For any decision made, there might be two types of errors. They are the Type I error and the Type II error. They are important subjects in all statistical significance analysis based on the use of p values [Markel, 1991; Yarandi, 1996; Biau, et al., 2010]. For a commonly critical p value 0.05, the error rate is 5% among the denied null hypotheses. This means that at least five of 100 null hypotheses, which have been rejected, may be true.
