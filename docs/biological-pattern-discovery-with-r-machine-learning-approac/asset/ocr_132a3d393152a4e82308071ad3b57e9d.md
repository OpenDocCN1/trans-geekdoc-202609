A standard DSG density function $f(z|	heta)$ given the model parameters $	heta = \{\mu_0, \mu_1, \sigma_0^2, \sigma_1^2, w_0, w_1\}$ has the form shown below,

$f(z|	heta) = w_0\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_0, \sigma_0^2) + w_1\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_1, \sigma_1^2) \quad (6.43)

where $w < 1$ is the mixing coefficient or weight of the $m^{th}$ Gaussian and $w_0 + w_1 = 1$

$\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_m,\sigma_m^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_m}\exp\left(\frac{(z-\mu_m)^2}{2\sigma_m^2}\right) \quad (6.44)

In such a model, $\sigma_0^2$ is the variance of the null density and $\sigma_1^2 \gg \sigma_0^2$ is the variance of the alternative density. When this condition is satisfied, it can be seen that the alternative density will be much flatter. This model is also called a two-component Gaussian mixture model. In this model, $\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)$ models the informative subset of the data. In this subset, the data has a little variation from zero, hence uninformative. Non-DEGs will belong to this category, hence the null data. The other component $\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ models the informative subset of the data. The data points (genes) in this subset have a great deviation from zero, hence very informative. A small $\sigma_0^2$ naturally leads to a sharp peak centred at $\mu_0$. When $\sigma_1^2 \gg \sigma_0^2$, $\mathcal{G}(z|\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ approximates a uniform distribution in the extreme case and the identification of the null data is straightforward.
