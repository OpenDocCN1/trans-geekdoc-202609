## 6.5 Gene expression bimodality pattern discovery

In addition to the heterogeneous gene expression pattern discovery problem, the gene expression bimodality pattern discovery problem is another important subject in gene expression pattern discovery. This is due to the fact that many human-related diseases as well as cancers can demonstrate genetic responses to stress, especially in some therapeutic exercises [Farahani et al., 2007]; Ertel et al., 2010; Randade et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2010; Krejci and Grim, 2011; He et al., 2011].

The problem of inconsistent responses across replicates or subpopulation formation has been well studied in the biomed area along with the development of high-throughput expression technologies. To understand and interpret the issue of genetic response within a population of patients, the subpopulation formation is this critical subject to study [Wappert et al., 2016].

The main step in gene expression bimodality pattern discovery is the bimodality test for population. Among many statistical approaches, one of the most popularly used is the Hartigan test. It tests the bimodality for a population through maximising the difference between two clusters [Hartigan 1985; Hartigan and Hartigan 1985]. Its null hypothesis is unimodality.

To study the subprocess in biomedical practices, a gene expression bimodality pattern discovery can be based on a single-object approach or dual-object approach. The dual-object approaches assume the cooperation between two genes such as oncogene and suppression gene relationship to disease development [Wappert et al., 2016]. The single-object approach takes one gene for the study. Most single-object approaches assume that the expressions of a gene follow a mixture of two distributions [Hellwig et al., 2010].

The measure used by the PACK algorithm is similar to the Kurtosis test [Teschendroff et al., 2006], where the bimodality of a gene is tested by kurtosis profile, which is either negative or positive. A positive measure stands for super-Gaussian and a negative measure represents sub-Gaussian. The bimodality index approach, on the other hand, assumes that the expressions of a bimodal gene follow a mixture of two Gaussians [Wang et al., 2009]. The relative measure is also based on the assumption of a Gaussian mixture [Besserova et al., 2010]. By formulating a ratio between a bimodal distribution and a unimodal distribution, the likelihood ratio between them is used for bimodality pattern discovery [Hu, 2008]. In addition to the use of measure, cluster analysis has also been used to discover a bimodal distribution [Gormley and Tozeren, 2008].

### 6.5.1 The likelihood ratio test

The likelihood ratio test (LR) assumes that gene expressions follow a Gaussian distribution and the bimodality only occurs in the case population [Hu, 2008]. Suppose a gene expression vector is denoted by z. It is a joint vector of a control expression vector x and a case expression y, i.e., z = x ∪ y', where y' stands for a sorted vector of y. The z vector is shown below, where n is the number of the control expressions, m is the number of the case expressions, $x_{i}$ is the $i^{th}$ control expression and $y_{k'}$ is the $k^{th}$ sorted case expression,

```
$z = (x_1, x_2, ..., x_n, y_1, y_2, ..., y_m) \quad (6.32)$
```

A cumulative S is defined as below:

```
$S_r = \sum_{i=1}^{r} z_i \quad (6.33)$
```

Based on the S statistic, the likelihood ratio statistic is defined as below:

```
$\lambda = \max_{n<i<n+m} \frac{iS_{n+m}/(n+m) - S_i}{\sqrt{i(1 - i/2)}} \quad (6.34)$
```

An approximate significance level will be calculated for the likelihood ratio [Hu, 2008].

### 6.5.2 The bimodality index test approach

The bimodality index test approach assumes that the expressions of a bimodal gene follow a mixture of two Gaussians. It also joins the control replicates and the case replicates together into one vector for the test. The mixture model for the bimodality index test is shown below, where $z_{i}$ stands for the $i^{th}$ expression in $z$, $\mu_{1}$ and $\mu_{2}$ stand for the mean values of Gaussians, $\sigma_{1}^{2}$ and $\sigma_{2}^{2}$ stand for the variances of two Gaussians,

```
$z \sim \pi G(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2) + (1 - \pi) G(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2) \quad (6.35)$
```

The bimodality index simplifies the model using a uniform variance, i.e., $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$. A standardised distance is defined by the bimodality index by $\mathcal{H}_0: \delta = 0$:

```
$\delta = \frac{|\mu_1 - \mu_2|}{\sigma} \quad (6.36)$
```
