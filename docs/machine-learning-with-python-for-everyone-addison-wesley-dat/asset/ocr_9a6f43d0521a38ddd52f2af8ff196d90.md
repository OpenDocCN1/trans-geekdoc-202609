Let me squeeze in a few quick notes on terminology. If there are only two target classes for output, we can call a learning task binary classification. You can think about {Yes, No}, {Red, Black}, or {True, False} targets. Very often, binary problems are described mathematically using {-1, +1} or {0, 1}. Computer scientists love to encode {False, True} into the numbers {0, 1} as the output values. In reality, {-1, +1} or {0, 1} are both used for mathematical convenience, and it won't make much of a difference to us. (The two encodings often cause head-scratching if you lose focus reading two different mathematical presentations. You might see one in a blog post and the other in an article. I'll be sure to point out any differences in this book.) With more than two target classes, we have a multiclass problem.

Some classifiers try to make a decision about the output in a direct fashion. The direct approach gives us great flexibility in the relationships we find, but that very flexibility means that we aren't tied down to assumptions that might lead us to better decisions.
