# 9.2.1 Q1: What Am I Actually Inferring?

If we're going to build a phone placement classifier, the first question to ask is what are the possible classes that we want to infer. In other words, “Where do people actually keep their phones?” We need to know what the possible labels are so that we can collect the appropriate data. We could try to enumerate this list on our own, but then we’re subjecting the classifier to be biased toward our own experiences. I’m one particular type of phone user, and if I enumerate this list myself, it’s subject to the bias of my own experience. Another way to answer this question is to try to find an answer in the literature. If the literature does not have an answer, it’s worth the time and effort to come up with an answer that is based on more than just the experience of myself and my collaborators. In this project, we did that with interviews, surveys, and *in-situ* data collected through the experience-sampling method (ESM, see Larson and Ciskzzentmihalyi [2014]).

We began our investigation of phone placement with 50 short, semi-structured interviews with desk-based office workers in their usual office setting at our company. We approached participants in their offices and if they agreed to participate we asked:

+   1. What was the current location of their mobile phone?
+   
+   2. Where they normally keep the phone at work, at home while sleeping and awake, in the car, and while out walking around?
+   
+   3. How they decide where to put their phone?
+   
+   4. In how many of ten pre-identified places had they ever put their phone, and which of those places had the phone been in during the past 24h?

For question 4, we used past literature to suggest places where people might keep their mobile devices [Ichikawa et al. 2005, Patel et al. 2006, Dey et al. 2011]. In addition, we administered a modified version of this same survey (omitting the “right now” question) to participants in our ESM and lab studies described below (some participated in both and were only interviewed once). We had a total of 93 (37 female) office worker participants (OWork).

To understand the broader generalizability of our results, and avoid any possible bias from an internal office worker population, we deployed a survey based on our interview questions on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) to 600 participants (242 females) located in the United States (U.S.), see Egelman et al. [2014] for a good overview of using crowdsourcing platforms to collect study data.
