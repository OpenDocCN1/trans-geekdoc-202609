
First, consider if there are systematic holes in the dataset with respect to a particular person. For example, if you’re depending on user-provided data labels, are there situations when the user might not provide any labels (i.e., while driving, while in a meeting, while watching TV). Next, consider how representative your training set participants are with respect to the broader population. What type of diversity might matter? Does the person’s job, age, living situation, culture, gender, or something else matter for this inference? While always important to consider, this is especially important when the classifier will ultimately be deployed in a production context. These types of oversights do happen in production environments. For example, the man in China who was wrongfully charged with jaywalking because his face appeared in an advertisement on the side of a bus, a situation that the training data had failed to account for¹. Another example occurred when an automatic image tagging solution offered by Amazon classified images of members of the US Congress (particularly people of color) as criminals². In this case, better training data might have helped the developers see that this was a model that should not have been developed at all. These are just two high profile examples of terrible situations that could have been avoided if the training data used to develop those models accurately reflected the diversity of potential users and contexts, but there are countless examples. There are many types of diversity in the pool of potential users and the responsibility is on you (the developer) to consider what types of diversity your particular model should account for.

## 9.1.2.5 Q5: What Are the Privacy and Ethical Implications of Developing this Classifier?

Further building on the previous point, it is essential to consider the privacy and ethical implications of developing a model or a classifier. Just because we are not especially good at foreseeing the negative consequences of our work, does not mean we should not try. As a field, computing is coming up against the reality that the technology we develop can be used for purposes other than those we initially intended.

On an even more basic level, users are unlikely to be able to anticipate what you can do with their data. For example, an application can ask for access to the user’s accelerometer, which seems pretty innocuous. But if that application uses that data as an input into a “how intoxicated are you?” classifier [Gharani et al. 2017], that data just became *much* more sensitive. In these situations, it is important to give users both notice (i.e., explaining what you will do with that data) and choice (i.e., an option not to use that feature). Even this can be complicated and can lead to inequity and other issues.

## 9.1.2.6 Q6: A General Classifier or Personal Classifiers?

Next, are you going to train one classifier that generalizes across all users, or a classifier that is personalized to each user? These are two extremes: the generalized classifier is ready out of the box, but can never adapt or change to the particular situation of the user. However, the completely personalized classifier will not work until it has enough data. In these cases, something in the middle may be preferred. One approach is to launch a service based on a generalized classifier, but to collect training data and retrain the model once it has been deployed to the user.

## 9.1.2.7 Q7: What Happens When the Classifier Is Wrong?

Finally, it is important to consider what will happen when your classifier infers context incorrectly, which it inevitably will do at least some of the time. Does the application misbehave in these cases? Does it give the user the ability to fix the incorrect inference? Or to disable the functionality that is connected to the classifier? It is important to consider what is the worst case scenario for an incorrect inference from your classifier, and what steps can you take to address its negative impacts?

## 9.1.3 Summary

The questions outlined provide a guiding framework to consider different projects focused on inferring personal context from mobile phones. The following two sections describe two research projects at different places on the continuum in Figure 9.1 from this perspective.

## 9.2 Example: Inferring Phone Placement

One opportunity for inferring personal context from a mobile phone is to infer specific aspects of context about the phone, which in turn can be relevant for personal context. One such example is to infer where the phone has been placed. This can be useful as an aid to a flexible authentication system [Hayashi et al. 2013], it can be a useful context for interpreting the output of another sensor [Singla et al. 2018], or as a factor in a dynamic notification scheme [Weber et al. 2016, Komninos et al. 2018]. The example described in this section was published in Wiese et al. [2013], and reuses some of the text published in that paper.

### 9.2.1 Q1: What Am I Actually Inferring?

If we're going to build a phone placement classifier, the first question to ask is what are the possible classes that we want to infer. In other words, “Where do people actually keep their phones?” We need to know what the possible labels are so that we can collect the appropriate data. We could try to enumerate this list on our own, but then we’re subjecting the classifier to be biased toward our own experiences. I’m one particular type of phone user, and if I enumerate this list myself, it’s subject to the bias of my own experience. Another way to answer this question is to try to find an answer in the literature. If the literature does not have an answer, it’s worth the time and effort to come up with an answer that is based on more than just the experience of myself and my collaborators. In this project, we did that with interviews, surveys, and *in-situ* data collected through the experience-sampling method (ESM, see Larson and Csikszentmihalyi [2014]).

We began our investigation of phone placement with 50 short, semi-structured interviews with desk-based office workers in their usual office setting at our company. We approached participants in their offices and if they agreed to participate we asked:

- 1. What was the current location of their mobile phone?
- 2. Where they normally keep the phone at work, at home while sleeping and awake, in the car, and while out walking around?
- 3. How they decide where to put their phone?
- 4. In how many of ten pre-identified places had they ever put their phone, and which of those places had the phone been in during the past 24h?

For question 4, we used past literature to suggest places where people might keep their mobile devices [Ichikawa et al. 2005, Patel et al. 2006, Dey et al. 2011]. In addition, we administered a modified version of this same survey (omitting the “right now” question) to participants in our ESM and lab studies described below (some participated in both and were only interviewed once). We had a total of 93 (37 female) office worker participants (OWork).

To understand the broader generalizability of our results, and avoid any possible bias from an internal office worker population, we deployed a survey based on our interview questions on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) to 600 participants (242 females) located in the United States (U.S.), see Egelman et al. [2014] for a good overview of using crowdsourcing platforms to collect study data.

We captured a broad variety of places that people place their phones, which we further categorized into bag, car, out, and pocket (see Table 9.1 for an exhaustive list).
