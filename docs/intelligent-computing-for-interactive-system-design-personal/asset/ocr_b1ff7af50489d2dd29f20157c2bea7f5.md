# ## 9.3 Inferring Social Relationships from Communication Behavior

The previous section explored one, fairly low-level, type of personal context. Another very different type of context is the context of a social relationship. For a particular contact in your phone, how do you know that individual and how close are you to them? The context of a social relationship can be used for filtering the information that is presented in an interface, prioritizing email messages, or even for automatically determining privacy settings [Wiese et al. 2011]. In this work, our goal was to develop models to infer social closeness and life facet from call logs, SMS message logs, and the contact list available on smartphones. The example described in this section was published in Min et al. [2013] and Wiese et al. [2015] and reuses some of the text published in that paper. Again, we start by asking the core questions.

# ### 9.3.1 Q1: What Am I Actually Inferring?

There are two types of information about a social relationship we wanted to infer in this project. First was life facet: where you know somebody from, or what part of your life they fit into now. Second was tie strength: how close you feel to that person.

For life facet, we started with 11 different categories, which we then sorted into three life facets: family (immediate family, extended family, and significant other), work, and social (neighborhood, religious, family friend, knowing through somebody else, school, hobby, and others). We selected the names of these sub-categories by combining several sources [Hill and Dunbar 2003, Spencer and Pahl 2006, Kelley et al. 2011, Wiese et al. 2011], aiming to strike a balance between comprehensiveness (for richness of data) and simplicity (to prevent errors and minimize burden for participants). Ultimately, we decided that we would infer the three life facets rather than the sub-categories. Part of this decision was because of the amount of training data we had for different sub-categories, but also because of the complexity and low performance we would expect from an 11-class classifier.

The nature of tie strength poses a challenge for building this model. Tie strength could be treated as a numeric class value based on the answers to the tie strength questions. However, the difference between a rating of 1 and 2 is not necessarily equal to the difference between a rating of 2 and 3. Therefore, we used the tie strength levels of very strong tie, medium strong tie, and weak tie as nominal class values in these models.
