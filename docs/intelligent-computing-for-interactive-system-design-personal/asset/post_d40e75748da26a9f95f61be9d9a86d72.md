## 9.3 Inferring Social Relationships from Communication Behavior

The previous section explored one, fairly low-level, type of personal context. Another very different type of context is the context of a social relationship. For a particular contact in your phone, how do you know that individual and how close are you to them? The context of a social relationship can be used for filtering the information that is presented in an interface, prioritizing email messages, or even for automatically determining privacy settings [Wiese et al. 2011]. In this work, our goal was to develop models to infer social closeness and life facet from call logs, SMS message logs, and the contact list available on smartphones. The example described in this section was published in Min et al. [2013] and Wiese et al. [2015] and reuses some of the text published in that paper. Again, we start by asking the core questions.

### 9.3.1 Q1: What Am I Actually Inferring?

There are two types of information about a social relationship we wanted to infer in this project. First was life facet: where you know somebody from, or what part of your life they fit into now. Second was tie strength: how close you feel to that person.

For life facet, we started with 11 different categories, which we then sorted into three life facets: family (immediate family, extended family, and significant other), work, and social (neighborhood, religious, family friend, knowing through somebody else, school, hobby, and others). We selected the names of these sub-categories by combining several sources [Hill and Dunbar 2003, Spencer and Pahl 2006, Kelley et al. 2011, Wiese et al. 2011], aiming to strike a balance between comprehensiveness (for richness of data) and simplicity (to prevent errors and minimize burden for participants). Ultimately, we decided that we would infer the three life facets rather than the sub-categories. Part of this decision was because of the amount of training data we had for different sub-categories, but also because of the complexity and low performance we would expect from an 11-class classifier.

The nature of tie strength poses a challenge for building this model. Tie strength could be treated as a numeric class value based on the answers to the tie strength questions. However, the difference between a rating of 1 and 2 is not necessarily equal to the difference between a rating of 2 and 3. Therefore, we used the tie strength levels of very strong tie, medium strong tie, and weak tie as nominal class values in these models.

We assigned those class labels as follows, based on previous work [Zhou et al. 2005]:

- strong tie—the top group (rank 1–4)

- medium tie—the middle group (rank 5–19)

- weak tie—the remaining contacts

We further decided to explore both binary and ternary classifiers for the tie-strength problem:

- 3-class: classifies as strong, medium–strong, or weak

- 2-verystrong: binary classifier that combines medium–strong and weak ties into one class

- 2-mediumstrong: binary classifier that combines strong and medium–strong ties into one class

### 9.3.2 Q2: What Is the End Goal for this Classifier?

Similar to the work described in Section 9.2, the primary goal of this project was research: the plan was not to develop a model or system and deploy this to the wild, but rather to just characterize the problem space and show that it was possible and how well it performed. However, if the work was particularly successful, the classifiers trained here might facilitate additional research to explore some of the hypothesized applications (e.g., following up the previous work we had been doing on the association between these dimensions of relationships and privacy preferences [Wiese et al. 2011]).

### 9.3.3 Q3: What Data Do I Need, What Data Do I Want?

To develop these classifiers, our plan was to use call logs, text message logs, and the contact list on phones running the Android operating system. Unfortunately, at the time that we were doing this study there were some limitations to this data. In particular, by default Android (Alphabet Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA) phones limited the call log to the last 500 calls and typically have a default limit of 200 SMS messages per contact. This resulted in broad differences in how many days the logs represented (range: 21–369; median: 80; mean: 108). This represents a serious limitation to the dataset, but it would be difficult to have collected more data without running a longer-term data collection. Another challenge was that contact lists have many fields, but in practice the data in the contact list is fairly sparse. To protect privacy, we did not collect the content of SMS messages. However, we did collect descriptive information such as email domain name, first six digits of phone numbers, and city/state/zip code.

Another aspect of deciding which data to collect was to determine which of a participant's contacts to get ground truth labels for. The vast majority of any individual's contacts will be weak ties, and so random sampling is unlikely to turn up medium and strong ties. However, in this work, we wanted to maximize the likelihood of collecting information on strong ties. To accomplish this, we asked participants to generate a list of contacts that fit specific social categories, regardless of their appearance in the phone contact list. Participants listed five people in each of the following categories: immediate family, extended family, people they live with, coworkers, people they feel close to, and people they do hobbies with. We selected these groups based on past qualitative work that suggests these categories will contain an individuals' strong ties, and people from different life facets [McCarty 2002, Spencer and Pahl 2006, Wiese et al. 2011]. In the end, each participant ended up with a list of 70 contacts that were a mixture of the names they gave us, contacts they had contacted frequently in the data we collected, and names chosen randomly.

In addition to the communication log and contact data and the class labels described, we decided to collect some additional data from our participants, including some data we thought might be easily collectable in the future. Participants provided demographics for each contact in the 70-person list, such as sex, age, and relationship duration.

Finally, because of the difficult-to-define nature of tie-strength, participants also answered four questions about their relationship with each contact, adapted from Marin and Hampton [2007]:

- 1. How close do you feel to this person?

- 2. How strongly do you agree with the statement “I talk with this person about important matters”?

- 3. How strongly do you agree with the statement “I would be willing to ask this person for a loan of $100 or more”?

- 4. How strongly do you agree with the statement “I enjoy interacting with this person socially”?
