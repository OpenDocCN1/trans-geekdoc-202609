
Based on these categories, we chose each of the following classification problems:

- **Enclosed or Out:** whether the phone is out and potentially visible to a user, or if it is put away. Applications for this model include preventing pocket dial, and knowing whether or not visual notification mechanisms (e.g., flashing light or screen) will be noticed.

- **On Person or Not:** whether or not the phone is on the user. Applications for this model include dynamic notification preferences (a primary concern from survey respondents, e.g., whether or not to vibrate) and progressive authentication (whether the user has to reauthenticate, based on whether they could have been separated from the phone since last authenticated).

- **In Bag, In Pocket, Out, or In Hand:** where the phone is at a finer granularity. This is useful for the *find my phone* functionality (e.g., “your phone has been in a bag since 11:22 am” a concern expressed in survey responses) and for sharing the user/phone’s context to provide social awareness.

## 9.2.2 Q2: What Is the End Goal for this Classifier?

While we can imagine several end goals for this type of classifier, in this project the goal is just to show that this classification is possible, and to begin to characterize how well a phone-placement classifier might work if deployed in a real-world setting.

## 9.2.3 Q3: What Data Do I Need, What Data Do I Want?

When we began this project, we brainstormed data that we thought might be useful for this classification. We decided that *accelerometer data* was essential for our classifier. In practice, this decision was strongly influenced by the fact that we knew that this was the data that we would be able to collect from a deployment.

However, we hypothesized that additional data would also be useful, and after we conducted the in-the-wild data collection, we added the following data sources in our lab study:

- **Proximity sensor and light sensor:** These sensors already existed in many mobile devices. It would have been preferable to collect this data in the field, but a combination of technical limitations for accessing those sensors and the variety of configurations that lacked these sensors on the device made data collection during the ESM study infeasible. In the lab study, we collected the data using a development phone that gave us access to all the sensors on the device.

## 9.2.4 Q4: Where Will My Training Data Come from, and Is It Good Enough?

There are many factors to consider when collecting training data. One major trade-off was between the external validity of the data (collecting real data from a range of people in real situations) and the ability to explore data sources that are not available on consumer devices (described already). Thus, we decided to do two different data collections—one in-the-wild and one in-the-lab. This allowed us to explore the classification problem from two different angles. Collecting and labeling the data in-the-wild is a significant challenge. We describe both data collections here.

### 9.2.4.1 Collecting In-situ Labeled Data

To collect sensor data in situ we used the ESM (see Larson and Csikszentmihalyi [2014] for a thorough description) to collect accelerometer data from 32 participants’ personal smartphones in conjunction with a ground-truth label describing the phone’s placement. These in-situ labels complement the retrospective self-report data from the phone placement interviews. While interview participants were undoubtedly as complete as possible, in-situ survey data enriches our understanding of where users choose to keep their phones.

We built a data collection application, Where’s Your Phone? (WYP) on the Windows Phone 7.5 platform (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). WYP collects 10s of accelerometer data (three dimensions at 50Hz) roughly every 30min. It then prompts the participant to answer “Where was your phone at XX:XXpm?” Below this question, participants were given six choices including Other and I Don’t Know. Each category had a few sub-categories (see Table 9.1 for all 23 sub-categories). We chose categories based on the phone placement interview responses to minimize the number of situations when ESM participants would need to enter free-text Other responses.

Unanswered surveys expired when the next set of accelerometer data was collected and unlabeled accelerometer data were deleted. Participants specified a bedtime at which WYP turned off and a wake time at which it resumed. Participants could also “snooze” WYP for up to 2h at a time. Survey responses and corresponding accelerometer data were automatically uploaded to a server.

We recruited 32 participants (16 males, 16 females) from a Windows Phone 7.5 email list. Fifty-nine percent of participants were between 20 and 39 years old and the rest were older. Our participants submitted 5,524 ESM responses, although the number submitted varied across participants (median [med]: 147.5, minimum [min]: 29, maximum [max]: 358). The total number of responses from male (52%, 2,847) and female (48%, 2,677) participants was similar. It is important to acknowledge the potential limitations in the comprehensiveness of the ESM responses; times when phones are away from their owner would be under-reported.

### 9.2.4.2 Collecting Data in the Lab

Using the three devices described in Section 9.2.3 (development phone, capacitive sensing prototype, and multi-spectral sensor), we collected data from 15 participants (six females). Seventy-three percent of participants were between 20 and 39 years old and the rest were older.

We asked participants to bring anything that they might normally put their phone into including jackets, purses, or other bags. We collected data from each participant in any of the following places that they report having kept their phone: front trousers pocket, back trousers pocket, jacket pocket, bag/purse, and hand. We chose these based on our phone placement survey responses. To increase the external validity of this study, we did not have participants put our sensing devices in places that they would not normally put their phone. While this meant that we would capture more data for some participants than for others, it also maximizes the realism of our collected data.

For each place participants normally keep their phone, they completed a series of activities to simulate the various conditions that the device might encounter in everyday life while in the placement. For all placements except bag/purse, the activities were: sitting on a couch, sitting on a desk chair, standing in place, and walking around. We did not have participants sit for the back trousers pocket placement. For the bag/purse we had participants let the bag sit on the floor, stand while holding the bag, and walk while carrying the bag. Participants spent 20s on each activity. Because each device had at least one sensor that was directional (e.g., toward leg in a trouser pocket), we collected all data for each placement with each device facing both inward and outward. We collected from two devices at a time to minimize the number of trials.

Unlike the other placements, most of the variation in placing a device on a surface involves finding a variety of surfaces. Thus, we collected the surface-specific data ourselves, following a similar process to the other placements: we recorded data from each device in the two orientations of facing-up and facing-down. In total we collected data for 18 surfaces, including tables, desks, chairs, couches, on and in a metal filing cabinet, and even on a pillow.
