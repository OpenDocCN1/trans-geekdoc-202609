First, consider if there are systematic holes in the dataset with respect to a particular person. For example, if you’re depending on user-provided data labels, are there situations when the user might not provide any labels (i.e., while driving, while in a meeting, while watching TV). Next, consider how representative your training set participants are with respect to the broader population. What type of diversity might matter? Does the person’s job, age, living situation, culture, gender, or something else matter for this inference? While always important to consider, this is especially important when the classifier will ultimately be deployed in a production context. These types of oversights do happen in production environments. For example, the man in China who was wrongfully charged with jaywalking because his face appeared in an advertisement on the side of a bus, a situation that the training data had failed to account for¹. Another example occurred when an automatic image tagging solution offered by Amazon classified images of members of the US Congress (particularly people of color) as criminals². In this case, better training data might have helped the developers see that this was a model that should not have been developed at all. These are just two high profile examples of terrible situations that could have been avoided if the training data used to develop those models accurately reflected the diversity of potential users and contexts, but there are countless examples. There are many types of diversity in the pool of potential users and the responsibility is on you (the developer) to consider what types of diversity your particular model should account for.
