
# Can Cryptos Replace The Centralized Financial System?

Before we address this issue, we must consider how centralized financial systems came to be. This would enable us to assess the function of central financial institutions and determine if cryptos might eventually replace them.

## Central Banks: Their History

In 1844, the English developed central banking with the Bank Charter Act, a legislative initiative that established the foundation for an organization with exclusive authority over currency issuance. The notion was that a bank with so much power might contribute to the financial system's stability during times of crisis. It's a notion that many experts believe played a role in averting catastrophe during the 2007-2008 financial crisis and the subsequent Great Recession.

## The Evolution of Central Banking Roles

Over time, central banks have developed. For example, the Federal Reserve utilizes monetary policy to attain full employment and price stability, assure the nation's banking and financial systems are secure and sound and allow customers to get credit, stabilize the financial system in times of crisis, and contribute to the oversight of the nation's payment systems. The Federal Reserve and other central banks may adjust interest rates and create or destroy money to accomplish these goals.

## Monetary Policy Tools and Effects

For instance, if the economy seems to be expanding too fast and prices for goods and services become unaffordable, a central bank may raise interest rates to make borrowing more costly. Additionally, a central bank may deplete the economy of money by decreasing the quantity of money it makes accessible to other banks for lending reasons. Because most funds reside on computerized balance sheets, just pressing delete may cause it to vanish. Thus, the quantity of money available to buy products is reduced, presumably resulting in a decrease in pricing.

Naturally, every action has a corresponding response. While demonetization may result in lower costs, it makes it more difficult for companies to borrow money. As a result, these companies may become more cautious, hesitant to spend, and reluctant to recruit new employees.

Central banks may lower interest rates or create money if an economy needs to develop more rapidly. Reduced interest rates make borrowing money less costly and more accessible and attractive to businesses and individuals. Similarly, central banks may expand the funds available to banks for lending.

Additionally, central banks may attempt to influence economies in other ways. These measures may involve the open market purchase of securities (bonds) to create demand. High demand results in reduced interest rates since borrowers no longer need to provide a higher rate because the central bank offers a willing buyer.

## Risks and Unintended Consequences

Attempts by central banks to guide economies toward prosperity are loaded with danger. Inflation may become a concern if interest rates are kept too low. The economy may stall when prices rise and people need help to afford their desired goods. When interest rates are too high, borrowing is restricted, and the economy is harmed.

Low-interest rates (compared to other countries) encourage investors to withdraw money from one country and transfer it to another, providing a better return rate. Consider the predicament of retirees whose income is dependent on rising interest rates. When interest rates are low, their buying power and capacity to pay their expenses are directly impacted. Sending money to a nation that provides a higher rate of return is a sensible move.

Interest rate and money supply manipulation also directly impacts the value of a nation's currency. A high currency makes exporting products more costly for local businesses. This has the potential to result in domestic unemployment. A depreciating dollar raises imported products' prices, such as oil and other commodities. This may increase the cost of imports for consumers and domestic producers of products that depend on imported components or materials. A weak dollar benefits a slowing economy needing a boost, while a strong dollar benefits consumers.

Because there is a lag between when a central bank initiates a policy adjustment and when that move affects a nation's economy, central banks are always looking forward. They aim to implement policy changes now that will allow them to accomplish future objectives.

## The Case Against Central Banks

Now let's look at the other side of the coin. The inherent complexity of national and global economies provides ammunition for the claim that these economies are too unpredictable to be effectively controlled via the manipulation practiced by central banks. This argument, advanced by adherents of the Austrian School of Economics, may justify the adoption of a peer-to-peer currency like Bitcoin that does away with central banks and their intricate systems.

Since their creation, modern central banks have been a source of contention. And the causes for dissatisfaction are many and diverse. On the one hand, the idea of monopolistic power is very unsettling to many individuals. On the other hand, the presence of an autonomous, opaque organization that can influence an economy is much more alarming.

Numerous individuals (including economists and politicians) think that central banks make errors that significantly affect citizens' lives. Among these errors are the following: increases in the quantity of money (creating inflation and hurting consumers by raising prices for the goods and services they purchase); interest rate hikes (hurting consumers who wish to borrow money); the development of measures that maintain an abnormally low rate of inflation (resulting in unemployment); abnormally low-interest rates (creating asset bubbles in real estate, stocks, or bonds).

In this vein, no less an authority than former Federal Reserve Chair Ben Bernanke blames the Great Depression of 1929 on central bank manipulation (which increased interest rates).

In an era where technology enables consumers to conduct trade without the intervention of a central authority, central banks are obsolete. This argument is strengthened by a more comprehensive study of the financial sector.

## Scandals and Public Distrust

The Great Recession and a slew of scandals were precipitated by banking sector corruption. Bankers have wreaked havoc in Greece and other countries. Several organizations, like the International Monetary Fund, have been accused of prioritizing profits above people. And on a more local level, bankers earn billions of dollars by acting as intermediaries in individual transactions. In this climate, many individuals find abolishing the financial system attractive.
