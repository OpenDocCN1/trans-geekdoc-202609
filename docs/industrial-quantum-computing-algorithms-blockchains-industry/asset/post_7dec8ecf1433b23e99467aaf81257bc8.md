Ziegler et al. [2] described the journey toward 6G connectivity with the successful initiation of architectural research, while commercial 5G deployment is ongoing worldwide. Over the next 8 years, the evolution of 5G architecture will continue, paving the way for exciting opportunities in real-time communication and synchronization between the physical, digital, and biological worlds. This will be facilitated by a hot cloud platform, convergent RAN-CORE, new data and information architecture, specialization of the network, and intent-based automation, laying the foundation for diverse applications across consumer and vertical industries.

Akyildiz et al. [3] surveyed key techniques for future wireless networks, emphasizing wireless ubiquity as a driving force for 6G and beyond. Liu et al. [5] discussed the rapid advancement of IoDT properly toward digital twin integration, with envisioned applications such as synesthesia Internet and super-transportation. Key network performance requirements for 6G, including peak rates and ultra-high-precision positioning, are discussed alongside proposed features like on-demand fulfillment and AI, aimed at addressing lessons learned from 5G's development. ## 5.3 Cybersecurity threats in IoT

Similarly, the speed of 6G allows innovation that we could not imagine yet, but may put us at greater risk of cybersecurity threats. The threats have evolved from the unique fabric of IoT IoT technologies. It is important to realize these weaknesses because tomorrow’s interconnected world would not be secure without them.

## 5.4 Millimeter-wave mayhem

6G is based on frequencies (mmWave) aimed for high-speed communication. Unfortunately, these shorter wavelengths are more susceptible to signal breach compared with earlier generations:

- Signal interception and jamming: It has been reported that 5G networks, without doubt, more vulnerable than the 1 billion vehicles predicted in 2040.
- Beamforming hijacking: Attackers could exploit the directionality of mmWave beams to mislead data streams, eventually leading to partial or complete compromise of sensitive information involved in communication between devices. A survey undertaken in 2021 established that 74% of IT practitioners have concerns about this.

## 5.5 AI-powered peril

However, the speed of 6G is much dependent on AI for network management and device performance. This dependence introduces new attack vectors:

- AI poisoning: In this way, malicious actors can create biases in AI models by manipulating the training data, leading to inaccurate conclusions and chaos. A recent report from Carnegie Mellon University discovered that AI models charged with managing traffic can be manipulated, which ultimately cause accidents.
- Model manipulation: Hackers would be able to manipulate AI models using vulnerabilities to control or influence their outputs, which would lead to data breaches and manipulation of critical infrastructure. A survey by Gartner indicated that 70% of CIOs are worried about the security risks involved.
- Adversarial attacks: Actors with malicious motives can engage in scenarios to mislead or take advantage of AI systems, which may cause system failure. Research by the University of California Berkeley found that it could be possible to mislead autopilot systems into making faulty decisions by adding black stickers.

## 5.6 Supply chain spectre

The multilayer global supply chains for manufacturing and distributing IoT devices offer attackers several tactics, providing an opportunity to insert malicious code or hardware backdoors at various stages:

- Hidden backdoors: Meanwhile, malicious actors can put stealthy backdoors in the firmware or hardware when manufacturing items, which may give them access to devices after the supply chain. As reported by the National Intelligence and Security Center in 2020, supply chain attacks are among the major threats to national security, with 80% of organizations experiencing at least one.
- Counterfeit components: Instead of the original, incorporating malware into counterfeit components can open doors to system-wide vulnerabilities. A study by the International Trade Commission in 2021 shows that the amount of counterfeit electronics was estimated at $230 billion worldwide, pinpointing this problem at a high level.
- Software tampering: Malicious entities could modify software updates delivered during distribution, introducing vulnerabilities and compromising numerous devices like a virus. The 2021 SolarWinds attack is a good example of the negative consequences for a company that has been made to update its software using compromised methods.
- Edge computing exposure: 6G's great focus on edge computing, which means that data is processed close to the devices [15], presents a new set of security challenges.
- Distributed attack surface: The greater number of entrance endpoints increases the security risk, making it harder to protect and monitor every entry point. Sources indicate that 6G could produce 1 trillion connected devices by 2030, which would increase the number of vulnerabilities by a large factor [17].
- Limited resource constraints: Edge devices are usually affected by resource-constrained attacks because these devices have limited processing power and memory. Research carried out by the University of Oxford in 2022 has shown that resource depletion attacks may make compromising edge devices used in smart homes possible, which could result in gaining unauthorized access or manipulating data.
- Data privacy concerns: The edge computing environment causes concern as to the safety of data and how it could be misused by unauthorized access. Pew Research Center finds that 72% of Americans worry about companies collecting their data without permission. This shows that data privacy regulation is crucial for edge computing.
