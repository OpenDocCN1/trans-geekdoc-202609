As the commercialization and dominance of quantum computing technologies get closer, it is crucial to consider possible applications that may use these devices. ML emerges as a potential statistical framework to tackle issues for which precise algorithms are difficult to build. Examples of such difficulties include autonomous systems, image and voice recognition [13, 14], medical applications, biology, and artificial intelligence (AI) [15–17].

Classification, Gaussian models, vector quantization, principal analysis [19–23], and other techniques utilized often by ML practitioners have been the topic of research in this area. These methods will not be useful for QCms shortly. With devices ranging from 100 to 1,000 qubits, these approaches are less attractive as potential killer applications in QAML, notwithstanding their popularity. Due to scalability and algorithmic efficiency in handling massive datasets, it would take millions or billions of qubits to achieve useful applications at an industrial scale, notwithstanding promises of polynomial or exponential algorithmic speed. This benefit will be irrelevant when working with real-world datasets and the few thousand-qubit devices by Valdex and Melin [24].

We explain in that the only way to significantly accelerate ML jobs would be a revolutionary transformation, like the recent advances in hybrid classical-quantum algorithms. In this chapter, we highlight three methods that we believe will increase the likelihood of discovering game-changing applications on QCms soon:

- Concentrate on challenging and unsolvable issues in ML, such as generative models in unsupervised learning.
- Pay attention to datasets with inherent quantum-like correlations, which would make QCs necessary. These datasets will provide a model representation that is both compact and efficient, and there will be a substantial quantum advantage even at the level of 50–100 qubit devices.
- Hone in hybrid algorithms that use a quantum procedure to solve the intractable problem in conventional ML.

Experimental implementations on existing quantum hardware are still a way off, and each job has unique difficulties. We provide insights into the key difficulties and potential solutions in this rapidly expanding study area based on our prior work using QAML algorithms on current quantum hardware. We want to provide broad ideas and problems relevant to different quantum computing paradigms, such as the gate model, using examples from our previous work and quantum ante-demonstrations.

## 2.3 Quantum machine learning

The objective of ML methods with QCms is to surpass the speed and effectiveness of their traditional computer counterpart by exploring the development and execution of quantum software. Sharma and Khatal Sunil [28] stated, "QML algorithms resolve some problems more effectively than their equivalent convolutional approaches." Various QML techniques, such as quantum neural networks (QNNs), quantum support vector machines, quantum principal component analysis, and quantum reinforcement learning (QRL), have been presented. Existing discoveries indicate the presence of QML methods that demonstrate quantum speedup. QRL involves a quantum agent interacting with the environment and receiving rewards based on performance by Woolnough et al [29].

A reinforcement strategy may be developed using quantum measurement and Grover operation, representing the various states and actions. Based on theoretical evidence, QRL can surpass traditional reinforcement learning by a factor of 4. Researchers have shown that employing a quantum communication channel with the outside world may greatly decrease the time it takes for an agent to learn. In addition, Pradeep and Sharma used QRL to explain the decision-process in humans [30].

The underlying principle of this technique is that quantum states of qubits may be represented as vectors in a complex vector space with dimensions. Furthermore, matrix computations on extremely complex vector spaces play a crucial role in quantum mechanics. Several QML approaches have used Harrow, Hassid, and Lloyd as a subroutine by Sharma and Rokade [31]. QNNs aim to develop quantum versions of deep learning and neural networks, making them a fascinating approach to QML. Quantum information processing enables innovative quantum deep learning, incorporating novel models and features like entanglement and coherence. This can surpass current deep learning methods [32].

For instance, Balyan et al. [33] suggest that quantum coherence could greatly reduce the sample size for training a quantum Boltzmann machine to do a certain task. Another recent advancement is the thorough examination of variational quantum algorithms. These algorithms are designed to use the capabilities of near-term QCms to get a quantum advantage. These methods use a conventional optimizer to train the parameters.
