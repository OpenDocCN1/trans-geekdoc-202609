# 7.3 Quantum computing fundamentals

Quantum computing is a significant breakthrough in the theory and practice of computation. It uses the principles of quantum mechanics to calculate results beyond what standard computers can perform. It has its foundation in qubits, which are quantum informational elements, exhibiting unique features like superposition and entanglement. Superposition enables qubits to exist in more than one state simultaneously, allowing quantum computers to manage high-resolution domains once. This is central to quantum algorithms that give exponential speedup for specific problems over classical algorithms [2]. Additionally, entanglement creates connections between separated qubits irrespective of their distance in space. This phenomenon facilitates computation and information processing by allowing quantum computers to cooperate with one another on qubits. Grover's unstructured search algorithm and Shor's integer factorization algorithm are two quantum methods that highlight the potential of computation to solve computational problems with unexpected speed. The use of quantum mechanics is accompanied by numerous obstacles, including qubit coherence, gate errors, and capping. Quantum error correction codes and fault-tolerant architectures are frequently employed to solve such issues and provide quantum security. In conclusion, quantum computers are a novel technique of information processing that uses quantum mechanics to achieve unparalleled memory and capacity. Quantum computing embodies a revolution in computational science, and the quest for practical, scalable quantum computers continues at a frantic pace. Numerous advancements have been made in theoretical quantum computing, as well as in the experimental realm. Indeed, it is possible to solve problems rapidly that classical computers would take millions of years to solve. Quantum computing is based on the utilization of quantum mechanics principles to manage qubits. The use of a qubit in a superposition state increases the computational power and operational efficiency of a quantum computer.

# 7.3.1 Quantum bits (qubits)

Classical computing transmits information encoded as bits when each bit portrays 0 or 1. Instead of that, quantum computing uses qubits, which can represent 0, 1, or a superposition of both. Superposition multiplies the computational field by allowing a quantum computer to execute different operations simultaneously. In addition, qubits can entangle, a situation where one qubit is inextricably related to another, despite the separation between them. Entanglement provides a mechanism for solving various computational issues, enabling quantum computers with the skill to undertake numerous calculations like a classical computer.
