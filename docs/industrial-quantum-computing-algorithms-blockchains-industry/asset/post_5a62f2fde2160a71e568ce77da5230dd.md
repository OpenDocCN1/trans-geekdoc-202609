
## 9.1.1 Quantum basics

Quantum computing is based on the principles of quantum mechanics to process data and perform calculations. While regular computations use bits as the basic unit of information (0 or 1), quantum computing employs qubits. In quantum mechanics, states like photon polarization, electron spin, or states of atoms can be seen as qubits, which are the foundation of quantum computing. These qubits represent and manipulate information in 0 and 1 states simultaneously, a phenomenon called superposition. One significant difference between classical and quantum systems is how their parts combine. In classical systems, each part's state defines the system's state. However, in quantum systems, most states cannot be explained by just looking at the parts. These are called entangled states, where qubits are dependent on each other despite their distance. This results in better performance, where quantum computers can perform faster in situations like analyzing and running simulations.

Quantum measurement gives specific outcomes, even though there are many possible states. After measurement, the system ends up in one of these possible outcomes, chosen probabilistically, and changing the states, which means you cannot measure an unknown state without affecting it. Also, you cannot perfectly copy an unknown quantum state due to a principle called the no-cloning principle. The various elements of quantum computing are discussed in Table 9.1.

## 9.1.2 Elements of quantum computing

### 9.1.2.1 Superposition

In quantum computing, a quantum bit is the basic unit of information. If we talk about a classical bit, it can only be in one state, state 0 or state 1, but a quantum bit can be present in both states, i.e., state 0 and state 1 at the same time, and this is known as superposition. Qubits exist in a superposition of their two distinguished states, 0 and 1. That means superposition shows how quantum systems have the ability to be in multiple states at the same time [1, 2].

### 9.1.2.2 Entanglement

Entanglement is an important element of quantum computing. The state of a classical system can be completely characterized by the state of each of its component pieces. A surprising and counterintuitive aspect of quantum systems is that most states can not be described in terms of the system's components. Such states are called entangled states. Qubits can be entangled, meaning that their positions become correlated even when separated by large distances [3].

### 9.1.2.3 Quantum measurement

Quantum measurement involves observing a quantum system, causing it to collapse into one of its possible states, with probabilities determined by its initial superposition. Before measurement, a quantum system can be in a superposition of multiple states [4]. This means it can be in a fusion of different states simultaneously, unlike classical systems, which are typically in one definite state:

- Probabilistic nature: When you measure a quantum system, you "collapse" from superposition into one of its possible states. However, you cannot predict with certainty which state it will collapse into. Instead, you get a probabilistic outcome. The probability of each outcome can be calculated by the coefficients of the superposition. The measurement forces the system to choose one of its states. For example, if you measure the spin of an electron along a certain axis, it will collapse into either a "spin-up" or "spin-down" state along that axis.
- Irreversible process: Quantum measurement is typically irreversible. Once the system's state is measured, it remains in that state. This is in contrast to the reversible operations performed by quantum gates during computation.
- Entanglement: Measurement can also reveal correlations between entangled particles. For example, measuring the spin of one entangled particle instantly determines the spin of the one, no matter how distant they are. This phenomenon is known as entanglement, which is a main feature of quantum mechanics.

## 9.2 Quantum machine learning (QML)

QML is an enhanced version of ML that acquires some properties of quantum computing. In recent years, researchers have explored how quantum technology can enhance ML algorithms. The detailed mechanism of QML is shown in Figure 9.1.

In the first step, users need to convert classical data into quantum data or a group of qubits. In order to make classical data fit into quantum computing, this process involves quantum feature mapping, which changes classical data into an infinite-dimensional space, i.e., Hilbert space. Choosing the right quantum gate and arranging them in order is very important for designing a quantum circuit that can process data and provide the desired output. A quantum circuit is useful for specific ML tasks such as clustering, regression, and classification. The complexity and structure of the circuit differs from problem to problem and depends on the desired output. To increase the efficiency of the model, quantum algorithms should minimize cost functions, which can be calculated as the difference between the prediction by the model and the actual value. Further, quantum algorithms use different techniques, where changes can be made to the quantum circuit based on feedback provided by quantum machines. Once the quantum circuit has processed the input data, results can be retrieved through the measurements of qubits. The classical output is decoded and interpreted to provide insights or predictions concerning the original problem.

## 9.2.1 Quantum neural networks (QNN)

QNN is a computational ML framework that blends artificial neural networks with quantum technology to build more efficient algorithms. QNNs follow the theory of quantum mechanics. “Subhash Kash and Ron Cris published the first concepts on quantum neural network in 1995. They discussed the theory of QNN, which claims quantum effects have an impact on cognitive functioning of models.

In recent research, QNN involves combining classical artificial network models with the benefits of quantum computations. It's challenging to train classical models on large amounts of data. However, quantum computing features, such as quantum parallelism, interference, and entanglement, are potential resources for improving neural network efficiency. Most of the QNN models remain theoretical proofs, awaiting proper implementation in actual experiments [2].
