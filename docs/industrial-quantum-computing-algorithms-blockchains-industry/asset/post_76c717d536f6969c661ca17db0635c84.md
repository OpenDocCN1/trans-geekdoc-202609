
## 13.3 Fundamental concepts of quantum computing

Quantum physics is based on different foundational concepts of quantum mechanics. These are some of the key fundamentals or concepts of quantum computing:

- Superior position: Quantum physics and quantum mechanics suggest that particles may assume states at once. In quantum computing, you can supervise qubits on 0s and 1s, allowing multiple operations to be processed simultaneously in parallel.
- Entanglement: The phenomenon occurs when two or more particles' states entangle. In quantum computing, qubits exhibit correlations that allow for faster computations than are possible with classical methods.
- Uncertainty principle: This states that it is hard to accurately measure the position of particles and momentum at the same time. That idea holds true for qubits, whose fundamental errors allow measurements to cause state changes.
- Measurement: In quantum computing, measurement is an important part because it collapses a particle's superposition into a single state. In the context of quantum computing, measurements cause qubits to lose their quantum coherence by upsetting the superposition state.

## 13.5 Emergence of quantum physics

The base of quantum physics can be said to have emerged in the early twentieth century. The most advanced mechanics in quantum computing were discovered by well-known scientists like Max Planck, Albert Einstein, and Niels Bohr, who formed the groundwork for the development of quantum mechanics.

Richard Feynman introduced the idea of using quantum phenomena to substantially improve classical computing in 1982. David Deutsch evolved the theoretical basis for quantum computing between 1984 and 1985, building on this idea. The breakthrough came in 1994 when Peter Shor introduced Shor's algorithm, which demonstrated the potential power of quantum computing by efficiently factoring large numbers using quantum polynomial time.

Additionally, significant advancements occurred in quantum complexity concepts, with the definition of quantum Turing machines and the establishment of quantum complexity classes. The fast database search technique, the algorithm of Grover, developed in 1999 by Lov Grover, added to the potential of quantum technologies.

In 1996, the initial quantum computer prototypes began to appear, setting the stage for practical uses. Advanced developments in quantum error correction techniques, pioneered by Bell Labs and IBM in 1997, addressed the difficulties of preserving quantum states against environmental interactions.

The physical awareness of quantum computers includes the progress of a three-qubit machine in 1999 and a seven-qubit machine in 2000, marking significant milestones in the evolution of quantum computing systems.

## 13.6 Demands for quantum computing

Quantum technology could tackle any type of logical or conceptual challenge that traditional computers can handle. As per the Church-Turing theory, the reverse is also true: standard computers can solve all the issues that quantum technology can. This means they do not offer any added benefits in the context of computers. However, there are complex and seemingly hard issues that cannot be solved by regular computers in a practical timeframe. These require more physical power. Quantum mechanics can solve hard issues much faster, significantly reducing complexity, a phenomenon called quantum supremacy [18].

In 1993, Peter Shor introduced the idea that quantum mechanics can solve certain issues much faster, even within seconds, without overheating. He designed algorithms that can quickly factor large numbers. This is because quantum computers operate on the probabilities of the states of atoms before they are observed. Furthermore, according to studies, a practical quantum computer might be capable of cracking the technology used for encryption, exposing personal and sensitive information. The benefits of quantum computing are noteworthy, though more investigation is necessary.
