Unsupervised ML aims to create models and algorithms that can extract structures and information from datasets without labels. However, what is its significance? Unsupervised approaches are crucial for scientists to extract significant spatiotemporal properties from unlabeled data since they cannot choose patterns to look for in advance. Pattern recognition is a functional aspect of scientific inquiry.

Unsupervised approaches often provide viable, high-dimensional data representations with desirable qualities like sparsity and simplicity. By combining unsupervised tools with supervised techniques such as regressors and classifiers, the amount of labeled data required to attain a certain level of generalization performance may be greatly decreased. Di Meglio et al. [43] emphasize the connections and linkages between reinforcement learning and other specialized applications.

The term "generative model" frequently describes an unsupervised method that learns the combined likelihood of all the input variables. The term is derived from the fact that actual and conditional distributions may be inferred, leading to fresh data that closely resembles the training set. Using the classification scheme by Meyer et al. [44], we categorize generative models as explicit or implicit concentration.

Probabilistic enrichment, trust networks, and autonomous encoders are all members of the extensive family of models known as density estimators. The model features dictate the learning method, which might be variational estimates using "Monte Carlo sampling." Implicit concentration estimators essentially accomplish the generative objective by recasting the issues as a classification job by locating the Nash equilibrium as the state of game [45]. Generative models' intriguing property is their ability to gain insight into multimodal patterns over highly dimensional information. These models include several layers of unseen stochastic variables, commonly known as hidden variables.

The model's capacity to generalize is enhanced as the number of layers increases, as each layer is shown as directed or undirected networks with stochastic nodes. Regrettably, even for the simplest topologies, precise inference remains unsolvable. Performing the insurmountable inference step for each data point's observed point and each learning iteration makes learning the model parameters significantly harder in practice. The computational barrier is computing the anticipated values of several parameters under the model's complicated distribution [46]. Traditional methods rely on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approaches, which are notorious for their slow-mixing issue. When significant-sized low-density patches divide the distribution modes, it is difficult for the Markov chain to transition between them.

The ML community is fascinated by the possibility of QCs efficiently generating and sampling targeted probability distributions. Instead of MCMC methods, we propose using quantum devices to generate samples from quantum or conventional Gibbs distributions. This sampling technique can potentially enhance the efficiency of inference and training in traditional and quantum generative models, including classical and quantum Boltzmann machines. Furthermore, the sampling procedure may be hindered by the scarcity of qubits and the constraints of the current technology, making it impractical for real-world ML applications [47].

From this vantage point, we argue that generative modeling of real-world datasets is possible with noisy distributions. For this reason, it is necessary to operate in environments where only partial knowledge of the hardware-implemented functions is known. This kind of situation is known as a gray box. We further contend that hybrid classical-quantum designs are well-suited to near-term uses, where the classical component is used to circumvent hardware constraints. It is called a quantum-assisted method by Malik and Verma [48]. As pointed out by ML specialists, the significance of unprivileged learning is anticipated to outstrip that of strictly supervised learning. To be more precise, previous efforts in deep learning's generative models mostly used the computationally expensive MCMC step, which made scaling to big datasets challenging. More efficient sampling strategies may help inference and learning in graphical models, which we addressed by Kulkarni et al. [49].

Despite the obstacles, QML offers much more. A major opportunity is the possibility of training to be completed more quickly. Faster training times and improved optimization are both made possible by QCs' exponential speed advantage over conventional computers in operations. There is some evidence that the ability to process massive volumes of data and intricate algorithms can greatly improve forecasting and decision-making [50].

## 2.6 Challenges in QML

Quantum computing possesses the capacity to bring about a significant transformation in numerous industries; however, it is not exempt from encountering various challenges and limitations. Businesses need to be cognizant of current and future restrictions to make informed decisions and evaluate the feasibility of quantum capabilities [51]:

- **Maturity of technology:** Unlike traditional computers, quantum technology is in its early stages. Although impressive, the most cutting-edge QCs on the market today, known as noisy intermediate-scale quantum, are not error-free because of the system's intrinsic noise. The accumulation of these mistakes may create substantial difficulties for real-world implementations [52].
- **Quantum decoherence:** The environment affects qubits. Decoherence is the loss of quantum characteristics of qubits caused by external effects like electromagnetic radiation or temperature changes [53]. It is essential to guarantee longer coherence durations for practical and scalable quantum processing, even if progress has been made in isolating qubits.
- **Quantum-to-classical transition:** Converting a perfectly accurate quantum solution to the more common classical system may be challenging and error-prone. Given the problem's complexity, techniques combining quantum and traditional computing are essential.
- **Algorithms and quantum programming:** The way QCs work is different from how conventional computers work. This calls for fresh approaches to programming and new types of algorithms. Although Shor's and Grover's algorithms have made great gains in solving some issues, numerous real-world challenges still do not have efficient quantum algorithms [54].
- **Hardware diversity issues:** QCs offer diverse hardware options for utilization. Topological qubits, trapped ions, and superconducting qubits are a few instances of this. Each has distinct advantages, disadvantages, and periods of development. Due to the wide range of options, businesses face a challenge when determining the most suitable approach to invest in.
- **Skill gap:** Because of complexity, the quantum world necessitates a multidisciplinary approach combining knowledge of mathematics, computer science, and physics. Professionals with the knowledge to plan, construct, and run quantum systems are in short supply. For enterprises to widely use quantum technology, it is crucial to close this.
- **Cybersecurity concerns:** QCs threaten the stability of encryption protocols, raising cybersecurity concerns. Concerns about data security and privacy have been raised by the possibility that algorithms such as Shor's might crack popular encryption methods [55]. A major worry is the application of quantum-safe cryptography algorithms in a corporate setting, even if these approaches are being investigated.
- **Business case validation:** Due to the immaturity of quantum technology, many companies find it difficult to provide a convincing rationale for investing in it. It is difficult to quantify the return on investment and guarantee that quantum solutions provide a clear benefit over alternatives [56].
- **Quantum dominance:** The phrase “quantum supremacy” is prone to misunderstand. The groundbreaking work that Google did not prove that QCs are inherently better than conventional ones. According to Jäger and Krems [57], both tools have advantages and disadvantages.
- **Representing complex ML datasets for immediate anticipation:** Continuous observables serve as an alternative to binary observables (qubits) by Campos et al. [58] for encoding quantum information. The latter route has been explored in quantum ML by Abdulsalam et al. [59]. Nevertheless, many extant QCs rely on qubits, akin to traditional computing. Many variables present in datasets used in industrial applications are not binary. For instance, images might consist of millions of pixels, where “entry” denotes a numerical value for saturation and “pixel” signifies a three-dimensional vector.
