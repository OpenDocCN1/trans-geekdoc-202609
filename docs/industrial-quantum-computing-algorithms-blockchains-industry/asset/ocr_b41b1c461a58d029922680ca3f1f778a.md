# 9.1.2 Elements of quantum computing

# 9.1.2.1 Superposition

In quantum computing, a quantum bit is the basic unit of information. If we talk about a classical bit, it can only be in one state, state 0 or state 1, but a quantum bit can be present in both states, i.e., state 0 and state 1 at the same time, and this is known as superposition. Qubits exist in a superposition of their two distinguished states, 0 and 1. That means superposition shows how quantum systems have the ability to be in multiple states at the same time [1, 2].

# 9.1.2.2 Entanglement

Entanglement is an important element of quantum computing. The state of a classical system can be completely characterized by the state of each of its component pieces. A surprising and counterintuitive aspect of quantum systems is that most states can not be described in terms of the system's components. Such states are called entangled states. Qubits can be entangled, meaning that their positions become correlated even when separated by large distances [3].

# 9.1.2.3 Quantum measurement

Quantum measurement involves observing a quantum system, causing it to collapse into one of its possible states, with probabilities determined by its initial superposition. Before measurement, a quantum system can be in a superposition of multiple states [4]. This means it can be in a fusion of different states simultaneously, unlike classical systems, which are typically in one definite state:

+   - Probabilistic nature: When you measure a quantum system, you "collapse" into superposition into one of its possible states. However, you cannot predict with certainty which state it will collapse into. Instead, you get a probabilistic outcome. The probability of each outcome can be calculated by the coefficients of the superposition. The measurement forces the system to choose one of its states. For example, if you measure the spin of an electron along a certain axis, it will collapse into either a "spin-up" or "spin-down" state along that axis.
- Irreversible process: Quantum measurement is typically irreversible. Once the system's state is measured, it remains in that state. This is in contrast to the reversible operations performed by quantum gates during computation.
