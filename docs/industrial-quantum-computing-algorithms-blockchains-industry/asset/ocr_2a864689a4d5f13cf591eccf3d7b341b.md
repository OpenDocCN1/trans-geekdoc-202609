# 5.4 Millimeter-wave Mayhem

6G is based on the frequencies of mm-wave (mmWave) for high-speed communication. Unfortunately, these shorter wavelengths [8] are more susceptible to:

+   1 Signal interception and jamming: It has been reported that 5G networks, without doubt, more vulnerable to the breach of signal (10x more in comparison with earlier generations). mmWave's narrower beams aimed targeted attacks that can compromise infotainment and similar car-connected services, with more than 1 billion vehicles predicted in 2040 to increase the risk.
2 Beamforming hijacking: Attackers could exploit the directionality of mmWave beams to mislead data streams, eventually leading to partial or complete compromise of sensitive information or components involved in communication between devices. A survey undertaken in 2021 by the Poemon Institute established that 74% of IT practitioners have concerns about data interception over 5G networks, showing this anxiety is growing with the vendor's mmWave technology.

# 5.5 AI-powered peril

However, the speed of 6G is very much dependent on AI for network management and device performance. However, this dependence introduces new attack vectors:

+   1 AI poisoning: In this way, malicious actors can create biases in AI models by manipulating the training data and missing with the system, leading to inaccurate conclusions and chaos. In a recent report from Carnegie Mellon University, researchers discovered that AI models charged with managing traffic lights can be manipulated, which can ultimately cause accidents.
2 Model manipulation: Hackers would be able to manipulate AI models using vulnerabilities to control or influence their outputs, in the end, would lead to data breaches and manipulation of critical infrastructure. A survey by Gartner indicated that 70% of CIOs in AI systems are worried about the security risks involved.
3 Adversarial attacks: Actors with malicious motives can engage in scenarios that mislead or take advantage of AI systems, which may cause unintended results or system failure. Research by the University of California Berkeley found that it could be possible to mislead autopilot systems into making faulty decisions by adding black stickers.
