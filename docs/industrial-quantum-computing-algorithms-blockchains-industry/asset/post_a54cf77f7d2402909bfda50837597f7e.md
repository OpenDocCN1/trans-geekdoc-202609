## 14.3.2 Hardware constraints

The qubit [quantum-bit] is the basic information storage unit in quantum computers, just like a bit in classical computers. There are many physical devices in nature that host and can be used to perform qubit operations. The fundamental building block of these qubits, however, are the superconducting Josephson Junctions. They are nonlinear capacitors and can form a Quantum-Well. Due to Quantum Mechanics, in such a potential well – and in contexts similar to this as an atomic shell when electrons orbit the nucleus of an atom – only certain energy levels are allowed for the electrons [32].

These levels together with the states that the electrons can have within their [spin|orbital] form a two-level system. These states are labeled as quantumstates and one of them is used as 0, and the other as 1. Quantum states in superconducting qubits are the macro-states, and they are not individual electrons, in fact they are collective quantum-phenomena, entangled Cooper-pairs. This presence of many Cooper-pairs in a bi-currents drives the inductance of the system which can make the state observable as a macro-state, and they are indeed the states of the system [33].

Quantum Computing [QC] is the use of quantum-mechanical phenomena like entanglement and superposition to perform computation [17]. We are currently in the “Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum” (NISQ) era of QC, which we have in sufficient qubits, noisy qubits, and short circuit-depths to replace classical computers in the works they do. Constructing modern QC is a big challenge because of the requirements of factors such as isolation from external electric fields, isolation from thermal disturbances, a need for expansion joints, and an ultra-high vacuum in the immediate vicinity of the qubits and the chip that houses them. QC is known to be error robust to the coding and gates that it can use error-correct its outputs, but there is a threshold for that error robustness known as the Breakthrough error rate, and as inspired by the Breakthrough noise in vacuum tubes [34, 35].
