# 7.3.2 Quantum gates and circuits

Quantum computers, like classical computers, use logic gates to apply operators to bits. Unlike classical circuits, quantum gates adhere to the rules of quantum mechanics and can incorporate superposition, entanglement, and phase shift. The quantum gate often involves a Hadamard gate that generates superpositions, as well as Pauli-X superposition states that are gateless and entangling two qubits. By planning circuits utilizing these entryways, one may hence utilize the quantum system's one of a kind highlights to achieve advanced computations.

# 7.3.3 Quantum algorithms

The usage of quantum systems' stability and complexity opens a doorway to a new set of algorithms. The best known are Grover's and Shor's algorithms. Grover's algorithm is an unstructured search quadratic speedup, helping to solve most search issues much faster than classical search techniques. Shor's algorithm, on the other hand, uses polynomial time to decompose large numbers into probable values, which is impossible for standard computers. These two concepts showcase the revolutionary value of quantum computing for solving multiple computational dimensions.

# 7.3.4 Quantum supremacy and challenges

Quantum supremacy is the extent to which a quantum computer outperforms the most powerful classical computer over specified tasks. Quantum supremacy is critical in the progress of quantum computing by certifying that quantum computing is superior at certain problems. Nonetheless, quantum supremacy faces monumental obstacles to overcome. Quantum systems are unstable, and decoherence can occur at any time. Since qubits lose their quantum energy as they collaborate with the environment, making qubits consistent and reducing mistakes are vital in the creation of a quantum computer.
