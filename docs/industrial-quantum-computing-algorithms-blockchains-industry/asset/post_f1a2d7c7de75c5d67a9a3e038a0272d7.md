## 6.3 Big data in health

Every second, health systems collect huge amounts of data, and the term “big data” in health is a very real one with multiple challenges regarding storage, analysis, interpretation, and use.

In the past, data of gigabytes or terabytes were considered high, today we can say that petabytes have become basic units of measurement for health data, registering in just a few years astronomical to the level of exabytes.

According to studies, in 2013, data totaling 153 exabytes were produced at the level of health systems (1 exabyte = 1 billion gigabytes), and in 2020, the estimated value increased exponentially by about 48%, to 2,314 exabytes [9].

The increase in global health systems is driven not only by new devices, which are able to store information electronically, but also by the increased number of details recorded. At this point, medical equipment can provide highly detailed information, which automatically translates into larger amounts of data. Also, at the patient level, the volume of stored data increases with each presentation to the doctor, hospitalization, or treatment performed with electronic medical records recording personal history.

Medical imaging produces the highest percentage of the volume of data generated by medical equipment, which can be explained by the increase in the use of MRI and CT machines that are becoming more efficient, providing extremely detailed images, with 3D and 4D images becoming benchmarks in the industry. With the increase in the number of digital scanners, hospitals store hundreds of millions of digital images, which are used for a short period of time to establish diagnosis and treatment, and then only kept on different storage media.

Also, data transfer has increased exponentially, especially under the impact of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, with telemedicine has exploded worldwide, with available solutions and applications increasing, this has been reflected in an increasing volume of transmitted medical data.

The sources generating data in health are multiple, from patient health records, data collected and processed by health management and monitoring bodies, to hospitals, medical and genetic laboratories, pharmaceutical research companies, or various medical devices (Figure 6.1).

As of 2014, hospitals produced more than 50 petabytes of data per year, including medical records, laboratory tests, medical images, sensor data records, genomics, operational and financial data, for health systems' management bodies. However, according to studies, 97% of this information is unused or summarily analyzed, being mainly stored on different systems [10].

Such a large volume of data is impossible for humans to analyze and process in an efficient way and with positive results. Therefore, dedicated software solutions have been developed, which can replace human work and automate analysis and processes by providing centralized results.

As mentioned earlier, medical imaging generates the largest volumes of data in the medical system, which also means a high percentage of errors in image recording, especially X-rays, with their percentage reaching up to 25% [11].

Researchers at the University of Washington, Seattle, have developed an application dedicated to errors in X-ray scans that manage to reduce the percentage of error from 20.6% to 6.9%. This is being done automatically, and the application manages to replace about 7 h of work required by a specialist to complete the same activity [12].

This shows us that big data can no longer be selected, analyzed and processed to provide useful results for humans without the help of computers and digital applications.

In order for digital data to be used and the information contained to be used to its full value, it is necessary that it is stored certain elements to save optimization, classification of data according to predetermined criteria, creation of structured databases, insertion of contextual elements that can help perform analysis and processing, and the use of globally standardized codes so that data can be comparable.

## 6.4 How is health information used?

The evolution of health systems is a major one, with changes related to both existing infrastructure and facilities, as well as new models of treatment for diseases data collected at the level of populations or individuals.

Applying analytics to data collected in health can save lives by analyzing the evolution or prevention of pandemics, revealing trends or even optimizing health costs.

At the individual level, the existence of a digital health record, which includes the patient's medical history over several years, can easily show what future health trends are, with the possibility of detecting a disease at an early stage or even anticipating measures to prevent it.

Screening activities can detect cases of illness early, and their treatment is easier when detecting advanced forms of the disease. Both activities fulfill at least two positive aspects: maintaining a high level of health and at the same time reducing health expenses. The costs of treating a disease or preventing it are significantly lower than those detected at an advanced stage, where, most of the time, advanced medical procedures or state-of-the-art treatments are needed [13].

The importance of data processed in health is given by the results obtained, which can predict future developments and even solve problems by anticipating trends in health, rapid evaluation of treatments and methods used, efficient cost management by optimizing expenses, monitoring the health status of patients, and engaging them in activities beneficial to health.

The amount of data obtained by healthcare workers is increasing, and the underlying issue being not only its quantity but also the quality of the information stored, as well as how compatible the different data sources are with each other.

Each healthcare provider has implemented its own applications dedicated to collecting and processing information about patients. Often, these applications are not standardized, being created according to their own requirements and needs, compatibility sometimes only in terms of generating reports for authorities.
This leads to a digitalisation of data, making it difficult to communicate between different providers, requiring additional data processing, a significant consumption of resources, and for significant amounts of data this is unproductive.

The advancement of current technology allows relatively easy data collection, as well as processing and creating useful reports that can be used to provide better care services.
The use of information collected at the level of entities active in the field of health is countless and may include predictions and planning, telemedicine, electronic health records, real-time alerts, optimization of expenses and logistics, prevention, risk analysis, medical imaging analysis, and development of new therapies (Figure 6.2).
