
## 15.3.1 Quantum algorithms to revolutionize electrical structure computations

Electronic structure computations are needed to understand chemical reactions, materials, and molecules. Computer chemistry and materials science use them. Quantum algorithms for calculating electronic structures are described in this article, along with their benefits and revolutionary potential. Classical structure approaches have issues with standard basis sets not describing electronic correlations and the high processing cost of solving Schrödinger equation. We examine how quantum algorithms can uniquely simulate molecular and material structures on quantum computers. Quantum subspace estimation (QSE), quantum phase estimation (QPE), and variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) are methods for computing electronic structures [38]. These approaches efficiently and accurately determine molecular energies, electronic wavefunctions, and characteristics using quantum computers. Along with DFT and wavefunction-based electronic approaches, we consider quantum algorithms. This allows a hybrid technique that combines classical and quantum computing. Quantum algorithms for electronic structure calculations are also examined for their pros and cons. This includes developing efficient quantum algorithms for large molecular systems, cleaning up quantum hardware, and comparing quantum conclusions to experimental data. We also see if quantum algorithms can tailor electrical devices, boost catalytic efficiency, and speed up material discovery.

## 15.4 Drug design and quantum computing

Quantum computer power can help quantum machine learning systems predict molecular features. Such algorithms include variational eigensolvers (VQE) and quantum kernel techniques. These algorithms use quantum data representation and processing to examine large molecular datasets and gain insights. Quantum algorithms can accurately predict molecular electronic energies, geometries, and spectroscopic properties. These quantum mechanics simulate chemical systems to reveal molecular stability, reactivity, and other properties for drug development and materials science [8–10]. Hybrid quantum-classical algorithms predict molecular properties using conventional and quantum methods. Quantum algorithms are combined with quantum chemistry and classical molecular simulations to understand molecular behavior. Quantum algorithms explore the configuration space of molecules to improve their properties. Quantum optimization methods like quantum annealing and variable algorithms identify the best chemical compositions for specific properties.

### 15.4.1 Quantum computing for molecular modelling advances

Quantum algorithms make more accurate predictions and reduce computing load in molecular modeling. These advances enable researchers to model more intricate and large-scale molecular processes, improving drug discovery, materials design, and chemical synthesis. Quantum computing helps evaluate treatments and forecast their interactions with biological targets to speed drug discovery. Quantum algorithms [11] help researchers create new materials for electronics, catalysis, and energy storage. Despite progress, quantum computing still challenges hardware restrictions, noise, and error correction. To widely use quantum computing to anticipate chemical properties, scalability, accuracy, and reliability of quantum algorithms for predicting molecular attributes is the goal of current research.

Quantum algorithms accurately simulate molecular properties using quantum mechanics. These algorithms accurately mimic quantum phenomena like electron correlation and entanglement, which conventional methods struggle with. Traditional methods like Hartree-Fock (HF) theory and density functional theory (DFT) accurately predict molecular properties. However, they may struggle to capture complex quantum processes, resulting in discrepancies between theoretical and experimental predictions.

Quantum algorithms may accelerate some computer operations exponentially relative to classical methods. They can travel large sets of configurations and perform complex computations in a polynomial timescale, reducing computational resources and simulation time [12–15]. Traditional methods require a lot of processing power, especially for large molecular systems or electron systems with strong correlations. Although classical algorithms have been optimized for years, they may still have scalability and computational cost issues for certain applications. ### 15.4.2 Analysing chemical data with quantum machine learning

Quantum machine learning models use quantum states to represent and process chemical data. These models can depict intricate quantum correlations and entanglement, enabling more precise chemical system descriptions. Classical Machine Learning (CML) models analyze chemical data using classical methods. They can analyze enormous datasets, however they may miss quantum events in chemical systems. Quantum Machine Learning (QML) models can encode chemical data using states and circuits for better feature extraction and parallel processing. Complex molecular structures properties benefit from quantum representations. Traditional chemical ML models [16] employ multiple IDs and structures. Classical models have significant uses, but they may miss the smallest quantum effects needed for precision similarities and inference. Because QML models require quantum simulators or hardware for training their scalability and accessibility are constrained. QML model training uses kernel or quantum variational circuits. Because CML models can be learned and using standard computing, many academics and professionals can use them. Decision trees, SVMs, and classic neural networks train CML models [17–20].
