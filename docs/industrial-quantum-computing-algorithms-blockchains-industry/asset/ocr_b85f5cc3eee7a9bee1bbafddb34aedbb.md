# 13.6 Demands for quantum computing

Quantum technology could tackle any type of logical or conceptual challenge that traditional computers can handle. As per the Church-Turing theory, the reverse is also true: standard computers can solve all the issues that quantum technology can. This means they do not offer any added benefits in the context of computers. However, there are complex and seemingly hard issues that cannot be solved by regular computers in a practical timeframe. These require more physical power. Quantum mechanics can solve hard issues much faster, significantly reducing complexity, a phenomenon called quantum supremacy [18].

In 1993, Peter Shor introduced the idea that quantum mechanics can solve certain issues much faster, even within seconds, without overheating. He designed algorithms that can quickly factor large numbers. This is because quantum computers operate on the probabilities of the states of atoms before they are observed, allowing them to process exponentially massive amounts of data. Shor's work also suggests that a workable quantum computer could potentially crack the codes used in cryptography, secure communication, exposing private and sensitive information. However, the benefits of quantum physics are significant and deserve further research. The benefits of this emerging technology are substantial and warrant continued exploration toward a brighter future.

Any computing task that traditional machines can do might be completed by quantum computers. The opposite is also true, according to Church-Turing theory: classical computers can solve every issue that quantum computers are capable of. This indicates they do not provide any extra advantages. Nonetheless, some difficult and unsolvable issues cannot be resolved in a reasonable amount of time by modern-day computers. These demand greater processing power. “The quantum victory” refers to the fact that quantum computers can solve tasks substantially faster and with exponentially less time complexity.
