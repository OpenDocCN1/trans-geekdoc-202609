# 11.5.10 Quantum generative adversarial networks

**Quantum generative adversarial networks** are an intriguing frontier for ML, where a growing field of quantum computing meets the established power of generative adversarial networks. A classical GAN is a type of ML model that consists of two networks locked in an adversarial competition. This network strives to create new data like images, text, or music that closely resembles real-world data from a training set. The generator acts as a critic and claims to distinguish real data from the generator's creations (discriminator).

The qGANs use the properties of qubits, which enable them to generate more intricate, realistic, and diverse data compared to classical GANs. The potential for parallel processing in quantum computers could lead to faster training times for qGANs compared to classical GANs.

# 11.5.11 Quantum autoencoders

Novel approach to the application of quantum principles in ML is the idea of **quantum autoencoders (QAEs)** [1]. They impart quantum principle properties, such as quantum states, which are used as the systems compressed and encoded information, potentially superior compared to traditional compression techniques.

These are neural networks that are able to learn optimal, low-dimensional representations of data in a higher-dimensional space through the encoding stage (which is how the data compression is achieved) and the decoding stage, where the original data is attempted to be reconstructed from the compressed representation [1].

The QAEs borrow their inspiration from classical autoencoders but operate in the quantum realm:

+   *Encoding*: A quantum circuit takes quantum state as input and enters it into a lower dimensional quantum state. The most important information from the original input is captured in this shortened state.
*Decoding*: From a compressed representation, another quantum circuit tries to reconstruct the original quantum state.

# 11.5.12 Quantum kernels

**Quantum kernel (QK)** methods are a set of pattern analysis algorithms that utilize kernel functions to operate in a high-dimensional feature space [1]. This includes techniques like SVMs. The primary goal of SVMs is to identify the decision boundaries that can separate a given dataset into distinct classes. The data spaces of these classes are not linearly separable, SVMs can leverage the use of kernel functions to discover these decision boundaries.
