### 8.1.1 Problem Statement

Many prescriptive approaches to ISO 27002 already exist, for example, ISO 27003, which is the official standard with guidelines for ISO 27001 (Talib et al., 2012). Several steps to implement the management framework provided in ISO 27001, called ISMS, are presented. However, a practical methodology for implementing an information security policy does not exist.

Many organizations do not have the resources or expertise to conduct a risk analysis and implement an ISMS (Mintz et al., 2003). Therefore, they could not know what aspects of security might be relevant to them. Instead of doing a comprehensive risk analysis, an organization could also look to its peers. What are they doing? Although it is not as good to follow your peers as it is to do an extensive risk analysis, it is certainly better than setting up controls for no reason.

Modern times require different approaches to problems. Nowadays, mobile phones and tablets are common problems. Employees are supposed to work everywhere. Information is quickly shared via social media. How do companies manage these new issues – what controls they do put in place and how do they select?

To date, there was no literature on practical research using ISO 27000, Google Scholar and SCOPUS. Almost all results had a very limited number of references. However, some were useful. The following paragraphs describe the documentation used for research. There are few scientific papers on ISO 27002, and none found research as described in the background section. This chapter is considered exploratory: The data collected in this research may be used to formulate hypotheses for other projects.

### 8.1.2 Research Question/Approach

The research question in this chapter is: What relevant ISO 27002 controls do security managers choose to implement and why are they chosen? The approach taken in this research is qualitative research, using interviews with experts. Expert interviews are a good way to explore an area of research. Experts often know a lot about the topic. It is possible to know whether there is consensus or still debate on subjects. Both results could be used in future research.

Unlike quantitative research, qualitative research focuses more on ‘why’ and ‘how’ questions. As a result, qualitative research generally takes smaller samples than quantitative research. Qualitative research often does not have a clear hypothesis instead, we need an open question. The selection is not made by chance. When interviewing information security officials, the objective is to get an overview of what controls they choose and why they have been chosen.

### 8.1.3 Purpose

As there is very little scientific literature on the practical use of ISO 27002, this research is exploratory. The purpose of this chapter is to investigate what controls are commonly used and selected for the implementation of information security in large organizations in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) (Calder & Watkins, 2010), with a specific focus on a practical framework for implementation.

### 8.2 BACKGROUND

Policy has been conceptualized as a form of strategic management (Mintzberg, 1983). Two perspectives make up how strategy is made: deliberate formulation and emergent formation (Mintzberg, 1983). The classical approach advocated by Quinn is grounded in military strategy used for thousands of years. This type of strategy advocates the use of deliberate plans to win battles and wars. Prominent historical figures in military strategies such as Sun Tzu, Napoleon, and Machiavelli have contributed to the classical strategy in its modern form. Rees et al. (2003) stepped away from this rigid approach to business strategy by advocating an emergent approach. In this, an organization's realized strategy is a combination of the organization's deliberate strategy with an evolving emergent strategy. This emergent strategy is identified by a pattern of actions.

Two perspectives of strategic management can be used to investigate the research behind IS security policy. One strategy is grounded in the classical approach, while the other is in the emergent approach. The following section will utilize each of these perspectives in examining the literature behind IS security policy formalism.

Continuing with the classical perspective, Kuhnhauser (1999) expounded on planning a multi-domain policy system. These are defined as systems with a multitude of independent security domains in which an individual IS security policy is enforced on the applications. Joshi et al. (2001) performed a logical analysis to produce a formal model of policy. Research has also examined the issue of multi-policy systems by investigating the digital government (Knapp et al., 2007). A sequence of solutions to the issues of multi-domain environments are presented, including ad hoc approaches, formal approaches, model-based methods, agent-based methods, architectural methods, and the database federation approach. However, policy enforcement does not highly correlate with policy effectiveness (Baskerville & Siponen, 2002).

The classical perspective has also welcomed a call for a security meta-policy (Wilson, 2002). It is noted that existing IS security policy approaches do not pay attention to policy formulation itself. In other words, the actual creation of the policy is done in an ad hoc manner. Calling for a meta-policy implies that the best strategy or policy is through rational planning.

On the emergent side of the strategic paradigm, researchers have examined how problems are dealt with creating an IS security policy. It has been noted that 52% of all logistical and physical security breaches arise from the activities of personnel within the organization (Ahmad & Ruifer, 2003). Research has sought to determine the most optimal control method to handle these breaches. IS security policy formally defines security requirements, outlines the main security objectives, and allocates resources. To maximize the probability of compliance, the enlightenment of staff to their responsibilities as outlined in the IS security policy is one potential solution.

Also, from the emergent perspective, there has been a call for the improvement of audit management to allow administrators to configure the software to reflect an organization's security needs as defined in the IS security policy. This demonstrates a dynamic approach to the policy in that it can be reactive to how an audit trail affects an information system. Changing the configuration from the status quo bottom-up approach to a policy-centric top-down approach would help the configuration closely match an organization's security goals (Coyne & Kluksdahl, 1994).

While not explicitly approaching the issue from an emergent perspective (Coyne & Kluksdahl, 1994), failing a IS security policy implementation demonstrates an analysis from an emergent perspective. The implication remains in how elements could have adapted to how the actual was different from the rational plan. They found that compliance-based approaches are more prone to failure than risk-based approaches. De facto compliance-based policy led to the reaction of all security-related matters being answered (Walsham, 1993).

This research was conducted via an interpretive study in the Information Technology Department of a large university in the south-eastern portion of the United States. The interpretive tradition perceives that the knowledge of reality is a social construction by human actors (Howcroft, 2005). In contrast to the assumptions of objective science, this knowledge of reality applies equally to researchers. It leads to the perception that there is no objective reality researchers can discover. This perspective is also described whereby interpretive research provides in-depth insights into social, cultural and historical contexts wherein particular events and actions are described and interpreted as the authentic experiences of the people studied (Reich & Benbasat, 2000).

Approximately 45 employees worked for the department under study. Of these, 20 participated in the interviews. The subjects involved in the formulation and implementation of the IS security policy included the Chief Information Officer, Security Officer, and a group of operational level employees who were members of a Security Planning Team (SPT). SPT members included systems analysts, web developers, a database administrator, two school administrators, and three faculty members. The employees within the department who did not participate in the study included those who were not stakeholders in the IS policy formulation and implementation process.
