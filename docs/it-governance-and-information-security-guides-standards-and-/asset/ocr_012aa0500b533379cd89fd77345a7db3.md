Many organizations continue to struggle with fundamental governance practices, as select-ing, managing, and evaluating IT governance processes (Heier et al., 2007; McKay et al., 2003).

From an anecdotal perspective, COBIT's size and multifaceted and complex structure making a framework of this magnitude in its entirety too medium and large task (De Haes et al., 2013; Warland & Ridley, 2005). This is also echoed by statements that view the COBIT framework as too extensive to be applied entirely and proposed to move to a less approach to defining and selecting controls. Prominent researchers in the domain (De Haes & Van Greenberg, 2005; Peterson, 2004; Webb et al., 2006; Weill & Ross, 2005) all put forth converging views of IT governance that recognizes the importance of all structural, process, and relational mechanisms. Although the value of user involvement in various aspects of IT governance has long been recognized (Gremberg, 2004; Posthumus et al., 2010), human behavior of IT governance has received less attention from academics (El-Makway et al., 2015; Lengnick-Hall et al., 2011).

The importance of IT governance and the relevance of frameworks provide the content for this study, which also focuses on the factors underlying the adoption of IT govern-frameworks. The intentions and intentions of the adopters are explored to shed light on the factors influencing adoption.

Several studies have endeavored to tailor and adapt the COBIT framework for a specific organizational context. For example, by Nugro (2014) examined COBIT 5 as an IT governance tool in higher education institutions in Indonesia. The author concluded that IT governance must consider its specific situation to define its own set of governance processes as it sees fit, as long as all necessary management and governance objectives are covered. Similarly, Hierraiz (2012) conducted a evaluation using eight high-level controls from COBIT to determine the IT governance maturity of the information systems (ISS) department within a single university in Indonesia. Along the same line, by Wood (2010) adopted a case study design based on nine of the COBIT high-level control as a modified framework to evaluate the IT governance maturity of the city of San Marcos in the United States. Similarly, the implementation of COBIT as an IT governance framework was examined in an educational institution in Portugal by Gomes and Ribeiro (2009) and two Australian institutions of higher education by Bhattacharjee and Chang (2010).

In a similar effort to derive an abbreviated list of processes for creating an integrated IT governance framework in the Malaysian Ministry of Education, Azizi Ismail (2008) noted that the focus on IT governance differs the organization. For example, the Plan and Organize domain was the main focus at the ministerial level, whereas the Monitor and Evaluate domain was the highest emphasis at the school's level. Their study determined 20 high-level controls that were considered to be most important in one organization. Similarly, Bragas (2015) adopted COBIT the framework's goals cascade mechanism to pick a specific set of primary and secondary processes that relate to two IT-related goals: compliance with external regulations and security of information, processing infrastructure, and applications.
