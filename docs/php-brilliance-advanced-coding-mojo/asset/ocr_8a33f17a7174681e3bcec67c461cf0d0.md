The long and short of it is this: don't even think of chaining traits together in this manner. If you have a definite need for traits, try to ensure that the hierarchy is only two levels deep: The consuming class and the consumed trait. Given that we have the ability to use many traits as necessary, if those traits are each only a single level deep and each trait has a sensible name that clearly its intent, then the developer who encounters traits in a class should at least be able to infer what's going on. If they can't, they need to go and look. Checking a trait to see what's going on should *not* result in the need to mentally solve a “Towers of Hanoi” puzzle game just to work out what the end result will be.

# Summary

On the whole, this may have seemed quite a negative chapter by illustrating just how many pitfalls crop up from using traits. Even so, let this knowledge be a means of empowering our wisdom on the matter. Let it put us at a distinct advantage of having been equipped with sufficient knowledge to foresee how we might avoid traits creeping into our applications. Whenever we learn something as a developer, we enrich our mental toolbox.

Knowing just a few of the things in here will help deliver better judgement when it comes to traits. In the same way all of the preparations for the Millennium Bug made the Millennium Bug a non-event, we can avoid the pain of time spending the bugs in our own code when those bugs never occur in the first place.

Conclusion! Traits should be used sparingly and used wisely. Keep the focus of your intent both contained and laser sharp. Then reap the rewards from a carefully managed but powerfully effective system of code reuse. High five for the win!
