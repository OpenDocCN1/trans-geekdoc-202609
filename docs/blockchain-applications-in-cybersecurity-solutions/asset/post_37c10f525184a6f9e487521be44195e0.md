### Flawed Key Generation

In December 2014, a hacker known as Johoe got access to secret keys by using flaws in the key creation. During a code upgrade, a coding mistake resulted in inadequate randomization of inputs for creating public user keys, resulting in the attack. Despite the fact that the weakness was promptly patched, the ECDSA algorithm still has a vulnerability.

### Attacks on Cold Wallets

These wallets can be hacked as well. For example, researchers used flaws in the Nano S Ledger wallet to launch an Evil Maid attack. Researchers received the victims' secret keys, PINs, recovery seeds, and passwords, as a result of this incident.

Cryptocurrency exchange UPbit began moving funds to a cold wallet in 2019, which was one of the most recent cold wallet hacks. When you are expecting a cyberattack, this is a standard approach to freeze crypto. The hackers were able to take 342,000 ETH because they were aware of the transaction's timing [31].

### Attacks on Hot Wallets

Hot wallets are web-based applications that store private cryptographic keys. Though cryptocurrency exchange owners maintain that their users' data is kept in wallets that aren't connected to the internet, a $500 million attack on Coincheck in 2018 demonstrated that this is not always the case, though [32].

An attack against GateHub in June 2019 resulted in the theft of crypto assets and unauthorized access to dozens of native XRP wallets. Due to a system weakness, Bitcoin, a Singapore-based cryptocurrency exchange, was also exposed to a hot wallet attack around the same time. As a result, hackers were able to take XRP, valued at over $4.5 million and ADA, worth $237,500.

## c. Smart Contract Attacks

Smart contract development and penetration testing are two areas where Apriorit has teams working. We have extensive experience detecting and preventing problems in smart contracts built on the Ethereum, EOS, and NEO platforms. Errors in the source code, the virtual machine on a network, the smart contract execution environment, and the blockchain are the most common vulnerabilities associated with smart contracts. Let's take a look at each of these attack vectors one by one.

### Vulnerabilities in Contract Source Code

The parties signing the smart contract are at risk if the contract's source code is defective. For example, errors found in the Ethereum DAO cost its owners $80 million in 2016. The re-entry attack, which is one of the most common vulnerabilities in Solidity, allows untrusted functions from other smart contracts to delegate control to them. Contract A calls a function from contract B, whose behavior during this attack is undefined. Contract B, on the other hand, may use the function of contract A for harmful purposes [33].

However, if there are errors in the code of a smart contract, they cannot be fixed either. As with the DAO attack, there is a risk that hackers could detect and steal ether or create a new fork, and take advantage of programming faults.

## d. Transaction Verification Mechanism Attacks

### Double Spending Attack

Blockchains, unlike financial institutions, only confirm transactions after approval by all nodes in the network. The transaction is considered unverified until the block containing the transaction is verified. On the other hand, verification takes time, making it an ideal vector for cyber attacks. A typical blockchain attack using a transaction validation mechanism is double spending. To be valid, all blockchain transactions need to be validated by users, which takes time. System latency can be used by attackers to mislead the system into utilising similar crypto-coins or tokens in repeated transactions [35].

### Finney Attacks

When the transaction is converted to a block, and another identical transaction is performed prior to that block being published prior to network propagation, the other same transaction becomes invalid.

### Race Attacks

When an attacker makes two mismatched transactions, it is called a race attack. The money is sent to the victim in the first transaction (and ships the merchandise, for example) before the transaction is verified. At the same time, the conflicting transaction is sent to the network, causing the attacker to return the same amount of bitcoins, effectively canceling the original transaction.

### Vector76

Vector76 is the result of combining two prior attacks. A malevolent miner constructs two nodes in this situation; one is just connected to the exchange node, while the other is connected to the blockchain network's well-connected peers. After that, the miner creates two transactions, one with a high value and one with a low value. The attacker makes preemption and then holds the lock from an exchange service that contains a high-value transaction. After an accident announcement, the attacker quickly delivers the pre-mined block to the operator's service. He and some miners will confirm this transaction by treating the pre-mined block as the main chain. Therefore, this attack takes advantage of the fact that one part of the network sees the transaction that the attacker has inserted into a block, while the other does not see the transaction [36].

The attacker transmits a low-value transaction to the main network after the exchange service approves the high-value transaction, which is eventually rejected. As a result, the amount of the high-value transaction is credited to the attacker's account. Though this form of attack has a high possibility of succeeding, it's not widespread because it requires a hosted e-wallet that takes payments after one confirmation and a node that receives incoming transactions.

### Alternative History Attacks

An alternate history attack, also known as a blockchain reorganisation assault, can occur even when there are numerous confirmations, although it needs a significant amount of computer power on the part of the hacker. A malevolent user in this example sends a transaction to a recipient while simultaneously mining an alternative fork with another transaction that yields the same currency. Even if the recipient accepts the transaction after n confirmation and delivers a product, if the attacker releases a longer chain and collects the coins, the recipient may lose money.

In August 2020, one of the most recent blockchain rearrangement assaults occurred when a miner employed obsolete software and lost internet access for a period of time while mining Ethereum Classic. When two versions of the blockchain contended for legitimacy from network nodes, a restructuring occurred, resulting in around a 3000-block insertion. It was first published on [37].

### 51% or Majority Attacks

A majority assault happens when a hacker gets control of 51% of the network's hash rate and launches a new fork that eventually replaces the existing forks. This attack was the only known blockchain vulnerability at the time, and it appeared to be unachievable in the near future. Verge, ZenCash, Monacoin, Bitcoin Gold, and Litecoin Cash are among the cryptocurrencies that have previously been targeted at a 51 percent attack. Cybercriminals amassed enough hashing power in each of these situations to hack the network and take millions of dollars [38].

In August 2020, a 51 percent assault on Ethereum Classic (ETC) resulted in the double-spending of around $5.6 million worth of ETC coins. The hacker apparently had an extensive understanding of the ETC protocol and mined 4,280 blocks over the course of four days before the platform detected an attack. ETC was hit by a second 51 percent attack just five days after the first, in which a miner reorganised the network by 4,000 blocks.
