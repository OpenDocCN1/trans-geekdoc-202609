
## 2.7 CONCLUSIONS

This work shows that efficient learning is possible in high-dimensional settings when the target function admits a compositional structure – specifically, when it can be expressed as a sum of constituent functions, each depending on a smaller subset of variables size $t$. By using a kernel that incorporates this structure with $s$-dimensional constituents, the learning curve exponent is independent of $d$ and governed by $s \ge t$, optimal for $s=t$ and null if $s < t$.

In the context of image classification, these results are related to the “Bag of Words” interpretation. Consider images composed of $M$ features, each consisting of $t$ adjacent pixels, where classes correspond to specific (possibly overlapping) subsets of such features. If features can be located anywhere in the image, then data lie on a $2M$-dimensional manifold. We expect a one-hidden-layer convolutional network with filter size $s \ge t$ to learn the task efficiently with a learning curve exponent governed by $s$ and independent of $M$. In contrast, a fully-connected network operating in the lazy training regime would exhibit poor generalization in this setting for large $M$ due to the curse of dimensionality.

Our analysis does not account for the hierarchical compositional structure of real data, where large features are composed of smaller sub-features. It is intuitively clear that depth and locality together are well-suited for such data structure [Bie21; Pog17a]. In the next chapter, we will extend the present framework to this case.

# THE ROLE OF DEPTH AND SPATIAL ADAPTIVITY

The previous chapter established how compositionality and locality allow kernel methods and shallow neural architectures to overcome the curse of dimensionality. We showed that when the target function is a sum of local components, the generalization performance is governed by the size of the receptive field rather than the input dimension. However, real-world data rarely exhibit flat structure. Instead, they are widely believed to possess a hierarchical organization, where features at higher levels are composed of sub-features at finer scales.

Although many works have this idea [Bie87; Pog+17a; KT18; ZXS18; Dez+20; KKW20; PBL19; SH20; FSH21; GRSH22], we miss a qualitative understanding of how hierarchy affects the learning curve $\beta$ – which characterizes the decay rate of generalization error $\mathcal{E}$ with the number of training samples $P$. Specifically, there are no theoretical predictions for $\beta$ in the context of deep networks on tasks with varying degrees of locality or a hierarchical structure.

In this chapter, we address this gap by developing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in the overparameterized regime. In this limit, the width of each hidden layer tends to infinity, and the network's output converges to that of a corresponding kernel method [JGH18; Lee+19]. While real-world networks do not generally operate in such a regime, the correspondence with kernel regression provides powerful theoretical tools for computing the decay of the learning curves. Namely, as discussed before, given an infinitely wide neural network, its generalization performance depends on the spectrum of the induced kernel [CDV07; BCP20].

The central challenge, becomes the characterization of the kernel spectrum, especially when kernels exhibit complex structure and are defined recursively [Aro+19]. This characterization is the primary outcome of this chapter, along with the subsequent study of generalization in deep CNNs.

## THE ROLE OF DEPTH AND SPATIAL ADAPTIVITY

More specifically, we investigate the generalization properties of deep CNNs with non-overlapping patches and no pooling (defined in Figure 2 for an illustration). These networks are trained on a target function $f^*$ via empirical minimization of the mean squared loss. We consider the infinite-width limit of the networks (Section 3.3), where the parameters change infinitesimally over training, thus the network coincides with the corresponding Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK). Thus, generalization is fully characterized by the spectrum of the integral operator of the kernel: in simple terms, the projections on the eigenfunctions with larger eigenvalues can be learned — to error with fewer training points (e.g., Bach [Bac21]).

**SPECTRA (Theorem 3.3.1).** Due to the network topology, hidden neurons in each layer depend only on the scale of the input referred to as the neuron's receptive field (highlighted by colored boxes in Figure 2, left panel). We show that the NTK eigenfunctions of a CNN of depth $L+1$ can be organized into sectors indexed by the layer index $l=1,...,L$ (Theorem 3.3.1). Each sector consists of eigenfunctions depending only on the receptive fields of the neurons of the hidden layer. By $d_{eff}(l)$ the scale of the receptive fields of neurons in the $l$-th layer, the eigenfunctions of the $l$-th sector are functions of $d_{eff}$ variables. We characterize the asymptotic decay of the eigenfunctions in the polynomial degree of the corresponding eigenfunctions (Theorem 3.3.1), and show this decay is governed by $d_{eff}(l)$. Consequently, the eigenfunctions with the largest eigenvalues — the easiest to learn — are those that are supported on small, localized subsets of the input and have low polynomial degree. This spectral structure is our main technical contribution, and all of our conclusions follow from it.

**SPATIAL ADAPTIVITY (Corollary 3.4.0.1).** We leverage the spectral characterization to prove that deep CNNs can adapt to the scale of the target function (Section 3.4). By applying rigorous bounds from the theory of kernel ridge regression [CDV7] (reviewed in the first paragraph of Section 3.4), we prove that when learning with the kernel of a CNN and optimal regularization, the error depends on the effective dimensionality of the target. In particular, if $f^*$ depends only on a localized group of $d_{eff}$ input variables, then the generalization error $\mathcal{E}(P) \sim P^{-\beta}$ with $\beta \ge \mathcal{O}(1/d_{eff})$ (Corollary 3.4.0.1, see Figure 2 for a typical representation). This is consistent with non-rigorous results from the replica method for the ridgeless case [BCP20; Lou+21]. (Section 3.5).
