The dynamics in this regime is no longer linear. Instead, it can be described in terms of a time-evolving density over parameters, yielding a hydrodynamic description although interacting particle systems under a potential. This feature-learning regime captures richer behavior, including data-dependent representation learning. Crucially, it offers a path toward understanding how networks can overcome the curse of dimensionality through ‘discovering’ structure from data.

Theoretical progress in this direction has been primarily limited to two-layer networks [MMN18; RVE18; CB18; SS20]. Recent work has extended this framework to deeper architectures [Ngu19]. Yet, generalization in this regime remains difficult to analyze and typically requires stronger, data-dependent assumptions.

# 1.2.3 Role of data structure in high-dimensional learning

As discussed in Section 1.1, learning in high dimensions is, in general, statistically intractable. Under minimal regularity assumptions – as Lipschitz continuity – the generalization error decays only as $E(P) \sim p^{-1/d}$ [LB02; Wai] becomes prohibitive as the dimension grows. Nevertheless, modern networks are able to learn high-dimensional tasks with remarkable efficiency. This apparent paradox suggests that real-world data distributions are far from generic and possess rich internal structure.

**GLOBAL SMOOTHNESS** A classical structural assumption is that the function $f$ to be learned belongs to a Sobolev space – that is, it has $m$ square-integrable derivatives. In this case, generalization rates scale as $E(P) \sim p^{-m/d}$ (e.g., [Bac21]). However, this scaling overcomes the curse of dimensionality when $m \ll d$, a condition that is implausible in practice.

**MANIFOLD HYPOTHESIS** An alternative assumption posits that data lie on a $d_M$-dimensional manifold embedded in $\mathbb{R}^d$, with $d_M \ll d$. In this case, the sample complexity depends on $d_M$ rather than $d$, e.g., [Kpo11; SGW20; HS21]. However, empirical studies show that the intrinsic dimension $d_M$ can remain large in practice – in the tens or hundreds for visual data [Pop21]. Thus, the manifold hypothesis alone does not fully explain the efficiency of deep learning.
