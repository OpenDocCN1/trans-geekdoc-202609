If the noisy observation $\mathbf{x}$ is produced by a forward diffusion process, sampling from $p(\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x})$ is equivalent to integrating exactly (i.e., for an infinite number of time steps) the backward diffusion process starting from $\mathbf{x}$ and using the score function. In fact, BP can also be used to compute the score function, which is proportional to $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x}}[\mathbf{x}_0]$, to having access to a neural network achieving perfect generalization (see Section D.1.1.3 for a comparison between BP sampling and backward diffusion with the score function). This is a different situation with respect to real data, like images and text, where the score is estimated by training a neural network.

DIFFUSION PROCESSES IN THE RHM For the RHM data, we consider two different processes.

+   *e-process* is this simplified process where one considers any data $x_0$ that can be generated by the RHM, and ensures there is some level of uncertainty on each visible token (see Section D.11.2 for details). One uses BP to compute the probability that the true initial data was $x_0$. The noisy process is controlled by a noise-to-signal ratio $\epsilon \in [0,1]$, which plays the role of $t$ in the standard diffusion processes, such that the $\epsilon = 0$ at $t=0$ and $\epsilon = 1$ at $t=T$. From an RHM data $x_0$, we include the noisy observation at the leaf priors. Before, BP computes the marginals $p(h^{(l)}|x_c)$ and samples from $p(x_0|x_c)$. We theoretically this process in Section 5.2.1 using a mean-field approximation, neglecting fluctuations of the marginal probabilities and averaging over the disorder of the RHM.

+   *Masking diffusion with an absorbing state* This is the diffusion process described above for discrete data, which is commonly used in practice. We study numerically with BP in Section 5.2.2.
