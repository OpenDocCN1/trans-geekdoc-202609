## 2.1 RELATED WORK

Several recent studies have focused on the role of compositional structure in data. When the structure is hierarchical, deep convolutional networks have been shown to possess significantly greater power than shallow architectures [Pog17; Dez20]. From a training perspective, Malach and Shalev-Shwartz [MSS21] demonstrated that convolutional networks can achieve target errors in polynomial time that depend on consecutive bits of d-dimensional input, with $s=O(\log d)$. Conversely, fully-connected networks do not share this advantage. [Bietti21] explored the impact of locality through the lens of kernel methods, using deep convolutional kernels introduced by Mai6 and [BM19], and characterized their Associated Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). However, for isotropic kernels, smoothness constraints might become increasingly restrictive as the dimensionality $d$ grows. By contrast, for kernels exhibiting locality, smoothness constraints depend on the filter size $s$, rather than the dimensionality $d$ [Bietti21]. Additionally, Mei et al. [MMM21] recently established that weight sharing, without locality, provides only a modest improvement in generalization performance of shift-invariant kernels. In contrast to the above studies, we specifically consider nontrivial learning curve events within a framework where locality and shift invariance properties of the kernel do not match the target class being learned. Notably, in our setup, our finding that student's filter size $s$ determines the learning curve—rather than the teacher's filter size $t$—reflects the inability of kernels to capture anisotropy both in worst-case [Bac17] and typical scenarios [PSW21].

## 2.2 SETUP

### KERNEL RIDGE REGRESSION

Kernel regression is a method for learning a target function $f^*$ from observations $\{(x_i, f_i\}_{i=1}^n$ where the inputs $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are i.i.d. random variables drawn according to a probability distribution $p(d^x)$, and $f_i := f^*(x_i)$. Given a positive-definite kernel $\mathcal{K}$ and its corresponding Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS), a Gaussian process of covariance $\mathcal{K}$ is never an element of the RKHS associated with the same kernel, see e.g. [Kan18]. The kernel regression estimator $\hat{f}$ is defined by:

$$\hat{f} = \text{argmin}_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \left( \frac{1}{p} \sum_{\nu=1}^p (f(x_{\nu}) - f^*)^2 + \lambda \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 \right)$$

where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the RKHS norm and $\lambda$ is the ridge regularization parameter. The ridgeless case $(\lambda \rightarrow 0)$ corresponds to the minimum-norm interpolation solution. The optimization problem in Equation 21 is convex and its unique solution is

$$\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{p} \sum_{\nu=1}^p K(x_{\nu}, \mathbf{x}) \left( \frac{1}{p} K_p + \lambda I_p \right)^{-1}_{\nu} f^*_{\nu}$$

where $K_p$ is the Gram matrix defined as $(K_p)_{\mu\nu} = \mathcal{K}(x_{\mu}, x_{\nu})$, and $I_p$ is the identity matrix of dimension $p$. Our objective is the generalization error, defined as expected squared error over the data distribution $p(d^x)$ and the target $f^*$, i.e.,

$$\mathcal{E}_{X,f^*} = \left[ (f(\mathbf{x}) - f^*(\mathbf{x}))^2 \right]$$

### STATISTICAL MECHANICS OF GENERALIZATION IN KERNEL REGRESSION

In general, theoretical understanding of the generalization error is still an open problem. Recent works [BCP20; CBP21] derived approximate expressions for $\mathcal{E}$ using the decomposition of the target function in the eigenbasis of the kernel. Mercer's theorem allows any positive-definite kernel $\mathcal{K}$ to be expressed in terms of its eigenvalues $(\lambda_{\rho})$ and eigenfunctions $(\phi_{\rho})$ as:

$$\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{\rho=1}^{\infty} \lambda_{\rho} \phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{y}), \quad \int \mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) p(d^y) = \lambda_{\rho} \phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})$$

Defining the kernel features $\phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{\lambda_{\rho}} \phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})$, since the kernel's eigenfunctions form a complete basis, the target function and estimator can be decomposed as:

$$f^*(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\rho} w^*_{\rho} \phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{x}), \quad f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\rho} w_{\rho} \phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})$$
