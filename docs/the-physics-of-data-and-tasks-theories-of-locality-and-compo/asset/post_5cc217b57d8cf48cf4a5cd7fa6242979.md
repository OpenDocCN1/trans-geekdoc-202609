
## 5.2 CORRELATIONS OF TOKEN CHANGES

In this section, we characterize the statistics of how the input tokens change in the forward-backward experiments. Let $x_{0,j}$ denote the $i$-th input token, $i \in [d]$, and $\hat{x}_{0,j}(t)$ the same token after undergoing a forward-backward experiment with inversion time $t$. We seek to compute the changes between the tokens as a function of the inversion time $t$. For each token position $i$, we introduce a variable $\sigma_i(t)$ characterizing the dynamics.

**Definition 5.2.1** (Token change). If $\sigma_i(t)$ takes values in a discrete variable, then $\sigma_i(t)$ is a spin variable defined as

$$\sigma_i(t) = \begin{cases} +1, & \text{if } x_{0,j} \neq \hat{x}_{0,j}(t); \ -1, & \text{if } x_{0,j} = \hat{x}_{0,j}(t). \end{cases} \quad (75)$$

**Definition 5.2.2** (Dynamical correlation function). Given the $\sigma_i(t)$ defined above, the dynamical correlation function between the changes of tokens at positions $i$ and $j$, relative to the initial point $x_0$, is defined as

$$C_{x_0,ij}(t) = \langle\sigma_i(t)\sigma_j(t)\rangle - \langle\sigma_i(t)\rangle\langle\sigma_j(t)\rangle, \quad (76)$$

$\langle\cdot\rangle$ denotes averaging over different stochastic trajectories. The **average dynamical correlation function** is defined as $C_{ij}(t) = C_{x_0,ij}(t)$, where the overline indicates averaging over the initial point $x_0$.

Given the correlations, we compute the **dynamical susceptibility** $\chi(t)$, a quantity used to study the dynamics in physical systems [Don+02; Ton+05].

**Definition 5.2.3** (Dynamical susceptibility). Given the average correlation function $C_{ij}(t)$ of Definition 5.2.2, the dynamical susceptibility is defined as

$$\chi(t) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}\sum_{j=1} C_{ij}(t)}{\sum_{i=1}\sum_{j=1} C_{ii}(t)}, \quad (77)$$

where we normalized by the sum of auto-correlations.

Intuitively, the susceptibility measures the volume of the blocks of tokens that change together. In the case of the $\epsilon$-process for the RHM, where $\hat{x}_0(\epsilon)$ is sampled from $p(|\mathbf{x}||)$, the same definitions hold for $C_{ij}(\epsilon)$ and $\chi(\epsilon)$. In the case of continuous embeddings, where the tokens $x_{0,j}$ and $\hat{x}_{0,j}$ are continuous vectors (see Section 5.3 for image diffusion), the same definitions for $C_{ij}(t)$ and $\chi(t)$ hold by redefining $\sigma_i(t)$ as $\sigma_i(t) = ||x_{0,i} - \hat{x}_{0,i}(t)||$.

### 5.2.1 Mean-field theory of the $\epsilon$-process of the RHM

The average correlation $C_\ell(\epsilon)$ can be computed by the $\epsilon$-process of the RHM through a mean-field approximation. This mean-field approach consists of computing the average BP messages at each layer $\ell$ which is performed over the possible realizations of the RHM rules. Let's consider two leaf nodes $h_i^{(0)}$ and $h_j^{(0)}$ connected to the common ancestor $h_\ell^{(\ell)}$ through the nodes $h_l^{(\ell-1)}$ and $h_m^{(\ell-1)}$ (see Figure 11). Their spin variables are therefore $\sigma_i^{(0)}$, $\sigma_j^{(0)}$, $\sigma_l^{(\ell-1)}$ and $\sigma_m^{(\ell-1)}$, where we omit the $\epsilon$ to lighten the notation. Given the tree structure, the joint probability $P(\sigma_i^{(0)}, \sigma_j^{(0)})$ can be written as

$$P(\sigma_i^{(0)}, \sigma_j^{(0)}) = \sum P(\sigma_i^{(0)}|\sigma_l^{(\ell-1)}) P(\sigma_j^{(0)}|\sigma_m^{(\ell-1)}) P(\sigma_l^{(\ell-1)}, \sigma_m^{(\ell-1)}) \quad (78)$$

Each element in the sum of Equation 78 can be written in terms of BP messages, and its value can be computed by averaging over the realizations of RHM rules. The average of $P(\sigma_i^{(0)}|\sigma_l^{(\ell-1)})$ and $P(\sigma_j^{(0)}|\sigma_m^{(\ell-1)})$ can be represented as a $2 \times 2$ matrix $\mathbf{T}^{(\ell-1)}$ depending on layer $\ell-1$. Similarly, the average of the joint probability $P(\sigma_l^{(\ell-1)}, \sigma_m^{(\ell-1)})$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix $\mathbf{C}^{(\ell-1)}$. In the mean-field approximation, we neglect fluctuations of these quantities. Therefore we compute the average probability $P(\sigma_i^{(0)}, \sigma_j^{(0)})$ by substituting elements in the product of Equation 78 with their means. For spin variables $i$ and $j$ at distance $\ell$, we have

$$C(\sigma_i^{(0)}, \sigma_j^{(0)}) = \mathbf{T}^{(\ell-1)} \mathbf{C}^{(\ell-1)} \quad (79)$$

All the expressions for the above quantities are reported in Section D.1. With a similar procedure, we can compute the average marginal probability $P$. From these, we obtain the correlation function $C_\ell(\epsilon)$ at each noise level $\epsilon$.

### 5.2.1.1 Dynamical correlation length

In what follows, we present our main result predicting power law divergence of dynamical correlation length at the phase transition. In the mean-field approach, the average belief $p_\ell$ of a latent variable at layer $\ell$ can be computed through the iterative map

$$p_\ell = F(p_{\ell-1}),$$

where the functional form of $F(p)$ was derived in Chapter 4 and the initial condition $p_0$ depends on noise level $\epsilon$ as $p_0 = 1-\epsilon$. In the large limit $D \to \infty$, the probability $p$ of reconstructing the class is given by the fixed points of $F(p)$. For RHM such as $p_\ell$ undergoes phase transition, $F(p)$ has three points: two attractive ones, corresponding to $p=1/v$ and $p=1$, and a repulsive one, corresponding to the non-trivial solution $p^*=F(p^*)$ with $p^* \in (0,1)$. $p^*$ corresponds to critical noise level $\epsilon^*=1-p^*$.

In the vicinity of $\epsilon^*$ and the limit $L \to \infty$, we can estimate typical distance over which changes occur by computing the number of layers $\ell$ after which probability of reconstructing the latent variables $p_\ell$ approaches one of the two fixed points $p=1$ and $p=1/v$. This corresponds to the number of layers required to escape the repulsive fixed point $p^*$.

Given the iterative map of Equation 80, we can linearize it around fixed point $p^*$ and iterate for $\ell$ layers,

$$\Delta p_{\ell} = \left( \frac{dF(p)}{dp} \right|_{p^*} \right)^{\ell} \Delta p_0,$$

where $\Delta p_\ell = p_\ell - p^*$. We have $|\frac{df(p)}{dp}|_{p^*} > 1$ and we use shorthand notation $F'^* = |\frac{df(p)}{dp}|_{p^*}$. We want to compute the correlation length $\ell$ after which $F^{*}^\ell |\Delta p_0| = \mathcal{O}(1)$. In terms of corruption noise $\epsilon$, we have $F^{*}^\ell |\Delta \epsilon| = \mathcal{O}(1)$, where $\Delta \epsilon = |\epsilon - \epsilon^*|$. Here, $\ell \sim -\log |\epsilon - \epsilon^*| / \log F'^*$. From $\ell$, we compute the correlation length as

$$\xi^{\ell} \sim s^{\ell} = |\epsilon - \epsilon^*| \text{ with } v = \frac{\log s}{\log F'^*},$$

that diverges at the critical point: $\epsilon_c = \infty$. This divergence of the correlation length at the transition indicates that large blocks of tokens change in concert. In fact, these large correlated changes are caused by modifications of deeper latent variables near the transition (see Figure 13 for an illustration). At smaller and larger noise levels, the correlation length decays.
