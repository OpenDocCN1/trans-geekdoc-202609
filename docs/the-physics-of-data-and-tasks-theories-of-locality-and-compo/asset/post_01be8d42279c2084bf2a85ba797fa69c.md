### 1.2.4 Questions

Despite the empirical success of convolutional networks, our theoretical understanding of why they outperform connected networks and how different architectural components contribute to this advantage remains incomplete. A central challenge in machine learning is to quantify how different forms of structure – both in the model and in the data – affect sample complexity.

How much does locality contribute to generalization? Can locality alone change the curve exponent $\beta$, allowing convolutional networks to escape the curse of dimensionality? How do these gains compare to those induced by other structural priors, such as translational invariance and weight sharing?

In the NTK regime, FCNs are biased toward low-frequency functions. What is the spectral bias of convolutional kernels? How do locality and weight sharing reshape the kernel spectrum, and what classes of functions become easier (or harder) to learn as a result? Does it privilege classes of functions – e.g., those with localized or multiscale structure?

For FCNs in the NTK limit, increasing depth is known to have no impact. Yet in practice, deep CNNs substantially outperform shallow ones. Does depth lead to improved generalization in wide CNNs as well? Is this advantage attributable to the presence of hierarchical receptive fields, which mirror the compositional organization of many real-world signals? More broadly, is the sample complexity of learning hierarchical or compositional target functions with gradient-based methods?

## INTRODUCTION

Beyond the fixed biases of CNNs, a deeper question is whether networks can learn useful structure from data - such as locality, hierarchy, or variance - without being hard-wired into the architecture. What kinds of data structures allow neural networks to overcome the curse of dimensionality through feature learning, and how does this manifest in practice? Can models automatically discover modular or compositional patterns in their inputs, and if so, how does this affect their generalization efficiency?

## DEEP GENERATIVE MODELING

### 1.3 unsupervised learning 

The goal of unsupervised learning is to uncover the underlying structure of data without access to labels or supervision. This is particularly relevant in generative modeling, where we aim to learn a model that captures the probability distribution of natural data and can generate new, realistic samples from.

Real-world data distributions are complex and usually unknown. A common approach is to approximate the true data distribution $p_{data}(x)$ with a parameterized model $p_{\theta}(x)$, typically a deep neural network. The parameters $\theta$ are learned by maximizing the likelihood of the data:

```
$$\int p_{data}(x) \log p_{\theta}(x)dx. (12)
```

In practice, we approximate $p_{data}$ by the empirical distribution over a dataset, $\hat{p}_{data}(x) = P^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\delta(x-x_i)$, yielding a training objective that seeks to assign high probability to observed samples. Once trained, such a model can be used to sample new data.

### 1.3.2 Score-based diffusion models

A breakthrough in generative modeling comes from score-based diffusion models, which draw inspiration from non-equilibrium physics [SD+15; HJA20; SEI19; Son+20].

These models define a *forward process* that progressively corrupts data, transforming an unknown distribution into a simple, known one by adding noise. For instance, in the case of images, this can be implemented as an independent random walk per pixel, gradually turning the image into noise. A *backward process* is then defined as the reversal of this trajectory: starting from pure noise, the model learns to recover naturalistic data.

Formally, the diffusion process is defined by a stochastic differential equation (SDE):

```
$dx = f(x,t)dt + g(t)dw,$ (13)
```

where $w$ is a Brownian motion (Wiener process), $f(\cdot, t): \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is the *drift*, and $g(\cdot)$ is the diffusion coefficient. Denoting with $p_t$ the probability density of $x(t)$, we define the marginal $p_0 := p_{\text{data}}$ as the data distribution and $p_T$ as terminal (or prior) distribution, which is simple and easy to sample. To generate data, we reverse this process. The reverse-time SDE, derived from Fokker-Planck equation [And82], reads:

```
$dx = [f(x, t) - g(t)^2\nabla p_t(x)]dt + g(t)dw,$ (14)
```

where $dt$ is an infinitesimal time-step, $w$ is a Brownian motion running backward in time, and $\nabla \log p_t(x)$ is the so-called *score function*. Thus, learning to sample from the distribution reduces to estimating the score at each time.

The score is learned by training a neural network $s_{\theta}(x, t)$ via matching [Hvy09], e.e., minimizing

```
$\mathbb{E}_{x(0)}||s_{\theta}(x(0), t) - \nabla \log p_t(x)||^2. (15)
```

For affine drift $f(-, t)$, the transition kernels are Gaussian, allowing for analytical expressions of the score.

 DIFFUSION PROBABILISTIC MODELS (DDPMs) In practice, the continuous-time SDE is discretized. In DDPMs [HJA20] — we define $f(x) := -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x$ and $g(t) :=\sqrt{\beta(t)}$, the forward process with noise schedule $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$ that typically increases linearly with time or follows a cosine law. The backward process reverses this trajectory using a learned score network. Notice that the conditional score at each step is given by:

```
$x_t = \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}z_{t-1}, \quad z_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I}), (16)
```

and averaging over the distribution, the score is

```
$\nabla_x \log p_t(x_0) = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}, \quad \bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{i=1}^t (1-\beta_i),$ (17)
```

```
$\nabla_x \log p_t(x_t) = \mathbb{E}_{x_0|x_t}[\nabla \log p_t(x_t)] = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}. (18)
```

Thus, one can train a diffusion network to directly predict $\mathbb{E}[x_0|x_t]$.

### ARCHITECTURES A common architectural choice for the score net is the U-Net [RFB15], a convolutional neural network featuring symmetric downsampling and upsampling paths, each comprising multiple resolution blocks. Skip connections blocks operate at the same resolution, helping to retain fine-grained details that might be lost during the downsampling process. More recently, transformer-based architectures have also been introduced as alternative backbones for diffusion models [PX23].

## INTRODUCTION

Diffusion models can be extended to text and other discrete modalities.

DISCRETE DIFFUSION. To handle data — such as text or molecular graphs — diffusion models can be generalized to discrete spaces via Markov jump processes by time-varying transition matrices [Hoo21; Aus+21]. These processes corrupt data by randomly flipping coordinates to uniformly random ones (uniform diffusion) or by masking (absorbing diffusion). The reverse processes require estimating the conditional expectation $E[x_0|x_t]$, to construct the original signal [Aus+21], similarly to the continuous case. At the time of writing this thesis, diffusion large language models are beginning to achieve competitive performance, particularly on generation tasks, and the first commercial implementations are being released.

### 1.3.3 Questions

As discussed above, sampling via time-reversal of a diffusion process amounts to estimating the score function $\nabla \log p(x)$. Hence, in the worst case, the sample complexity of this task would explode exponentially with the data dimension. Nevertheless, different diffusion models learn to synthesize high-resolution images and long passages from finite datasets. Existing generation guarantees hinge on smoothness assumptions that rarely hold in practice [OAS23]. A central open question is: What latent structure in $p_{data}$ enables efficient learning?

A compelling hypothesis is that diffusion models exploit the hierarchical and compositional structure of data: these models might learn a ‘library’ of reusable parts and the rules for composing them — allowing for the generation of novel samples. This raises important questions: Do diffusion models synthesize novel data based on compositional principles? If so, how many samples are needed to learn such rules? What role do architectural choices, such as the U-Net, play?

Furthermore, to what extent do diffusion models generalize their training data versus simply memorizing, functioning as ‘stochastic parrots’? In theory, perfect minimization of the objective would lead models to reconstruct the empirical distribution exactly, i.e., to memorize the training data. Recent work has demonstrated that large, overparameterized models can and often do memorize [Som+22; Car+23; Yoo+22; Kai+23a]. How, then, diffusion models avoid memorization in practice? Is this due to architectural or optimization-related inductive biases, or merely a consequence of under-parameterization? Can overparameterized diffusion models still generalize?

We will cover these processes in more detail in Part III.
