As we illustrated in the main text, to solve this constraint on the hypothesis space, one has to consider deep convolutional architectures. In particular, consider the same teacher of the previous paragraph (left) learned by a depth-four convolutional student (right).

![](img/86150bd6d44cb578b97e3eba954b7be2_213_0.png)

![](img/86150bd6d44cb578b97e3eba954b7be2_213_1.png)

Remarkably, this student is able to learn the teacher without being cursed by input dimensionality. Indeed, as the number of patches diverges, the error asymptotes to $P^{-1/s_1}$. This rate is slightly worse than the one obtained by the student matched with the teacher, which is proven to be the Bayes-optimal case cursed. Intuitively, this fast rate is obtained because the student engine contains a single patch, correspond to large eigenvalues and bias the learning dynamics towards $s_1$-local functions. Yet, this student is also able to represent functions that are considerably complex.

Now consider a depth-three teacher (left) learned by a depth-four student (right).

![](img/86150bd6d44cb578b97e3eba954b7be2_213_2.png)

![](img/86150bd6d44cb578b97e3eba954b7be2_213_3.png)

As highlighted in orange, the output of the teacher is a linear combination of a composition of non-linear functions acting on patches and coupling them. In this setting, the error decay is controlled by the effective dimension of the second layer. In fact, when the number of patches diverges, the error decay asymptotes to $P^{-1/d_{eff}(2)}$, this behavior is a result of what we called ‘adaptivity’ to the spatial structure of the target.

Finally, consider both teacher and student with the complete hierarchy, i.e., the receptive fields of the neurons in the penultimate layers coincide with the full input.

![](img/86150bd6d44cb578b97e3eba954b7be2_213_4.png)

![](img/86150bd6d44cb578b97e3eba954b7be2_213_5.png)

In this case, we show that the error decays as $P^{-1/d_{at}(3)}$, i.e. the rate is cursed by the input dimension. The physical meaning of this result is that the hierarchical structure we are considering is still too complex and cannot be learned efficiently. In other words, hierarchical convolutional networks are excellent students, since they can adapt to the spatial structure of the task, but bad teachers, since they generate global functions that are too complex to be learned efficiently.

## B.7.4 Extensions to different normalizations and overlapping patches

This section investigates the robustness of our results to the input distribution, i.e. for data outside the multisphere $M^{S-1}$, and relaxes the non-overlapping patches assumption.

**Inputs in $\mathbb{R}^d$.** While our analysis requires that each of the input data is normalized to a unit sphere, this normalization is not the standard one used for neural networks. Therefore, in this section, we investigate the robustness of our predictions to data distribution. In particular, we consider data uniformly distributed in the hypercube, $x \in [0,1]^d$, and data with standard Gaussian distribution, $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$. First, we extend the definition of the RFK and NTK to inputs in $\mathbb{R}^d$.

**Definition B.7.1** (RFK and NTK of hierarchical CNNs for inputs in $\mathbb{R}^d$). Let $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Denote tuples of the kind $i_1, i_2, \dots i_m$ with $i_{l \to m}$ for $m \ge 1$. For $m < l$, $i_{l \to m}$ denotes the empty tuple. For each tuple $i_{2 \to L+1}$ and $s$ a divisor of $d$, denote with $\theta_{i_{2 \to L+1}}$ the angle between the $s$-dimensional patches of $x$ and $y$ by the same sample, i.e.

$$\frac{x_{i_{2 \to L+1}}^T y_{i_{2 \to L+1}}}{\|x_{i_{2 \to L+1}}\| \|y_{i_{2 \to L+1}\|}\quad (273)$$

For $1 \le l \le L$, denote with $\{x_{i_{2 \to l+1}}, y_{i_{2 \to l+1}}\}$ the sequence of patches obtained by letting the indices of the tuple $i_{2 \to l}$ vary in their respective range. Consider a hierarchical CNN with filter sizes $(s_1, \dots, s_L)$, $P_L \ge 1$ and all the weights $w_l^{(1)}, w_l^{(2)}, \dots, w_l^{(L)}$ initialized as Gaussian random numbers with zero mean and unit variance.

RFK. The corresponding RFK (covariance kernel) can be obtained recursively as follows. With $\kappa_1(t) = (\pi - \arccos t) / t + \sqrt{1-t^2} / \pi$,

$K_{RFK}^{(1)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}) = \|x_{i_{2-l+1}}\| \|y_{i_{2-l+1}}\| \kappa_1(i_{2-l+1});$
$K_{RFK}^{(l)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}_{i_{2-l+1}}) = \frac{1}{|S_l|} \sum_{i \in S_l} |x_i|^2 - \frac{1}{|S_l|} \sum_{i \in S_l} |y_i|^2$
$\times \kappa_1 (\frac{1}{|S_l|} K_{RFK}^{(l-1)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}_{i_{2-l+1}}) - \frac{1}{|S_l|} \sum_{i \in S_l} |x_i|^2 - \frac{1}{|S_l|} \sum_{i \in S_l} |y_i|^2)$

NTK. The NTK of the same hierarchical CNN can be obtained recursively as follows. With $\kappa_0(t) = (\pi - \arccos t) / \pi$,

$K_{NTK}^{(1)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}) = \|x_{i_{2-l+1}}\| \|y_{i_{2-l+1}}\| \kappa_1(i_{2-l+1})$
$K_{NTK}^{(l)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}_{i_{2-l+1}}) = K_{RFK}^{(l-1)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}_{i_{2-l+1}})$
$+ +\frac{1}{|S_l|} \sum_{i \in S_l} K_{NTK}^{(l-1)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}_{i_{2-l+1}})$
$\times \kappa_0 (\frac{1}{|S_l|} K_{RFK}^{(l-1)}(\{x_{i_{2-l+1}}, y_{i_{2-l+1}}\}_{i_{2-l+1}}) - \frac{1}{|S_l|} \sum_{i \in S_l} |x_i|^2 - \frac{1}{|S_l|} \sum_{i \in S_l} |y_i|^2)$
