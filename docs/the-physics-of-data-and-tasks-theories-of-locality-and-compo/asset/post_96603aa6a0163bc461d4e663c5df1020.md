## 3.6 CONCLUSIONS

We have proved that CNNs can adapt to the spatial scale of the target function, beating the curse of dimensionality if the target depends only on local groups of variables. Yet, if considered 'teachers', they generate functions that cannot be learned efficiently in high dimensions, yet in the Bayes-optimal setting where the student is matched to the teacher, the architectures we considered are not good models of the hierarchical structure of real data, which are efficiently learnable.

# A PHASE TRANSITION IN THE DIFFUSION PROCESS

In this part of the thesis, we study compositionality in the context of deep generative models, specifically diffusion models, and explore how they might leverage the compositional and hierarchical nature of data. These models learn to generate novel, complex data by composing and assembling features learned from examples, much like a writer combines words to form sentences. While the idea is intuitive, its formal and empirical validation presents a significant scientific challenge.

This chapter present evidence that diffusion models [SD+15; Hja20; SE+20] do operate compositionally, generating images by assembling features across different hierarchical levels during the diffusion process. We first provide quantitative, empirical evidence of these compositional effects in the denoising diffusion process of natural images. We then develop a theoretical framework, based on synthetic compositions and hierarchically structured models of data, to explain these observations.

Diffusion models operate by progressively adding noise to data as time increases (the forward process) and then learning to reverse this to generate new classes. By adding a finite amount of noise to an image and then denoising, we observe that: (i) at low noise levels, only low-level features of the image are modified; (ii) at a critical noise threshold, the probability that the denoised output belongs to the same class of the original data drops sharply to the level of chance; (iii) beyond this critical point, high-level features from the original image can surprisingly persist and recombine to compose elements of entirely new classes.

While the first observation is intuitive and has been noted previously [Hja20], the sharp phase transition and the subsequent recombination of elementary features are surprising. We will demonstrate that these phenomena can be precisely theoretically explained using synthetic generative models of data with a built-in hierarchical and compositional structure, inspired by concepts from formal grammars and statistical physics [Cag24].
