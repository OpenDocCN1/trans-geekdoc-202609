# 1.1.2 Theoretical puzzles

Despite the remarkable empirical success of deep neural networks, our theoretical understanding of how high-dimensional tasks remain limited. A central question is: How many data points are needed for a model to learn a task with good precision? That is, does the generalization error $\mathcal{E}(P)$ decrease as the number of training examples $P$ increases? Remarkably, empirically, for many high-dimensional tasks, $\mathcal{E}(P)$ is fitted by a power-law decay:

```
$$\mathcal{E}(P) \sim P^{-\beta},$
```

where the exponent $eta$ captures how efficiently a model learns from data [Hes17; Kap+20; Hot+22]. These empirical scaling laws show that $eta$ depends on the dataset, the task, and the algorithm. General works suggest that $eta$ would be vanishing for large input dimension $d$, implying that learning would be practically impossible in settings where the dimension is large, which is the case in practice (e.g., images where $d$ is the number of pixels and color channels). This is the essence of the curse of dimensionality. In high-dimensional spaces, volume grows exponentially with $d$, and the typical distance between nearest-neighbor points decreases very slowly: $d \sim P^{-1/d}$. Consequently, only weak regularity assumptions on the task — like resembling a 1-Lipschitz function $f$, where $|f(x) - f(x')| \le |x - x'|$ — lead to $eta \propto 1/d$ [LB04; Wai19]. For large $d$, this means that the number of samples $P$ needed to generalize almost extremely, even exceeding the number of atoms in the observable universe.

The empirical success of deep learning in high-dimensional tasks, despite the curse of dimensionality, highlights a fundamental puzzle. If high-dimensional data can be learned efficiently, they must possess under structure: symmetries, invariances to transformations, or other forms of regularity that make the problem tractable. This leads to several critical questions: *What is the nature of the exponent $\beta$?* *How do neural networks harvest this structure through architectural choices — such as depth, convolution, or weight sharing — that encode inductive biases?* Addressing these questions is among the most fundamental and practical problems in deep learning, as it directly impacts the sample requirements for achieving a given precision and, influence model training and scaling.
