
U-Net with the skip connections between the encoder and decoder parts mimics the upward and downward iterations of belief propagation, where the downward process mixes upward and downward messages. In fact, building on the present work [Mei24] U-Nets are capable of effectively approximating the belief propagation denoising algorithm. Investigating whether the function learned by U-Net approximates BP is a promising avenue for future work. Our analysis focused on score-based diffusion, which holds for more recent generative frameworks like flow matching [Lip+22] and stochastic interpolants [ABVE23]. These methods rely on a continuous transformation from data to noise, and the phase transition remains uncovered as a signature of the data's latent hierarchical structure, rather than of specific noise paths.

In the present work, we used the internal representation of networks as a proxy for the hierarchical structure of images. An interesting direction for future work will be using deep hierarchical segmentation techniques [Arb+10; Ge+23; Xie+21; ZM20] to extract latent variables, so as to test our predictions on their evolution in forward-backward experiments. Finally, future work can test theoretical predictions on other modalities successfully handled by diffusion models, such as language and biological structures.

The interplay between the hierarchy in feature space and time revealed here may help understand the puzzling success of diffusion methods, including the number of data needed to train such methods, or why they generalize and not simply memorize the empirical distribution on which they were trained [Som+22; Car+23; Yoo+23]. More generally, our results put forward hierarchical generative models as tools to understand open questions for other methods, ranging from the emergence of new skills by the composition of more elements in foundation models to that of transferable representations in self-supervised learning.

# 5 PROBING HIDDEN HIERARCHIES IN DATA

In the last chapter, we introduced *forward-backward experiments*, where a controlled level of noise is added to a starting image and then removed to generate a new one [HJa20; SFW25; BC23]. For small amounts of noise, low-level features of the image change. Passed a transition point, the class is likely to change, but remarkably, some of the low-level features of the original image are still retained, as predicted in simple hierarchical models of data structure. However, this analysis is limited to image modality. Moreover, the geometrical structure of the changes occurring in such a process is not known.

In this chapter, we derive the length scale associated with changes occurring in the forward-backward protocol with the RHM, and we experimentally show that our predictions hold in both language and image datasets. Specifically, in the generative model of hierarchically structured data, using a mean-field description of the forward-backward diffusion process, we show theoretically that the tokens are correlated over a length scale that diverges at the transition. This phenomenology is a signature of the hierarchy in the data structure, indicating changes in deep latent variables.

We validate our theoretical predictions by performing numerical experiments on our stochastic diffusion process used in discrete data, showing the same phenomenology predicted by our theory. To do so, we measure the *dynamical susceptibility*, an observable to study the dynamics in physical systems.

We perform forward-backward experiments with state-of-the-art masked diffusion language models (MDLM) [Sah+24] on WikiText. We show the presence of a peaking correlation length in the token changes at a finite inversion time, consistently with our theoretical model. We perform the experiments with Denoising Probabilistic Models (DDPM) [ND21] on ImageNet. We tokenize the resulting images using the patch embeddings of a contrastively pre-trained vision encoder [Rad+21] and show that the correlations of token changes display a qualitative agreement with our analysis.

Overall, our results show how changes in latent variables affect visible data, and directly support the idea that a hierarchical structure is central to both language and vision modalities. Moreover, our work puts forward the forward-backward protocol as a tool to probe the latent hierarchical structure of real data.

# 5.1 PRELIMINARIES

### 5.1.1 Discrete diffusion models

For discrete data, like text, $x_0$ consists of a sequence of tokens $x_{0,i}, i \in [d]$, each corresponding to a symbol belonging to vocabulary $V$. In this case, we consider *masked diffusion* with an absorbing state by introducing a mask token [Aus+21]. At time step $t$, a non-masked token stays unchanged or transitions to a masked state with some probability $\eta_t$. Using a one-hot encoding representation of $\{V\}$, the forward transition matrix $Q_t$ is:

$Q_t = (1 - \eta_t)I + \eta_t e_M$

with $I$ the identity, a vector of ones and $e_M$ the one-encoding vector corresponding to the mask symbol. The element indicates the probability of $x_t$ transitioning from state $k$ to $l$, i.e., $[Q_t]_{kl} = q(x_t=l | x_{t-1}=k)$. At the final time $T$, tokens are masked, $x_T = \text{mask}$ for every $i \in [d]$. In the following, we consider the noise schedule $\eta_t = (T - t + 1)^{-1} p(x_t | x_0) = t/T$.

## BAY-OPTIMAL DENOISING OF THE RHM USING BELIEF PROPAGATION

We consider the Random Hierarchy Model [Ag+24], and use the notation $h^{(\ell)}$ to indicate the value at level $\ell$ and position $i \in [d]$. The leaf nodes $h^{(1)}, \dots, h^{(L)}$ correspond to visible tokens, while upper-level nodes represent latent variables. We define the distance $t$ between two visible tokens as the number of edges between them and their lowest common ancestor. Their corresponding distance $r$ is $r = s^{-t}$. Because of the hierarchical structure generating the data, the visible tokens have non-trivial correlations, which depend on their tree distance $t$.

As discussed in Chapter 4, knowing the production rules and tree structure of the RHM, the probabilities of latent variables, conditioned on some observation, can be reconstructed exactly using Belief Propagation (BP) [MM09]. Specifically, if an RHM datum $x_0$ is corrupted by some noise, e.g., by masking a fraction of tokens, resulting in noisy observation $x_t$, then BP can be used:

- compute the marginal probabilities of any latent or visible variables, conditioned on the noisy observation $x_i: p(h^{(i)}|x_i)$;
- sample directly from the posterior $p(x_0|x_i)$.

If the noisy observation $\mathbf{x}$ is produced by a forward diffusion process, sampling from $p(\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x})$ is equivalent to integrating exactly (i.e., for an infinite number of time steps) the backward diffusion process starting from $\mathbf{x}$ and using the score function. In fact, BP can also be used to compute the score function, which is proportional to $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x}}[\mathbf{x}_0]$, having a neural network achieving perfect generalization (see Section D.1.3 for a comparison between BP sampling and backward diffusion with the score function). This is a different situation with respect to real data, like images and text, where the score is estimated by training a neural network.

## DIFFUSION PROCESSES IN THE RHM

For the RHM data, we consider two different processes:

- *epsilon-process*: this simplified process where one considers any data $x_0$ that can be generated by the RHM, and ensures there is some level of uncertainty on each visible token (see Section D.1.2 for details). One uses BP to compute the probability that the true initial data was $x_0$. The noisy process is controlled by a noise-to-signal ratio $\epsilon \in [0,1]$, which plays the role of $t$ in the standard diffusion processes, such that $\epsilon = 0$ at $t=0$ and $\epsilon = 1$ at $t=T$. From an RHM data $x_0$, we include the noisy observation at the leaf priors. Before, BP computes the marginals $p(h^{(l)}|x_c)$ and samples from $p(x_0|x_c)$. We theoretically analyze this process in Section 5.2.1 using a mean-field approximation, neglecting fluctuations of the marginal probabilities and averaging over the disorder of the RHM.

- *Masking diffusion with an absorbing state*: this is the diffusion process described above for discrete data, which is commonly used in practice. We study numerically with BP in Section 5.2.2.
