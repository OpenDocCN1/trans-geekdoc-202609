## 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

### 2.1. AI and Job Performance

The concept of artificial intelligence was first introduced in 1956 by John McCarthy, the “father of AI” (Benko & Lányi, 2009). It is defined as the capacity of machines to learn from experience, adapt to new inputs, and accomplish human tasks (Brooks, 1991; Duan et al., 2019). AI can be categorized into analytic AI (it shows only features consistent with cognitive intelligence), human-inspired AI (it includes elements of both cognitive and emotional intelligence), and humanized AI (it characterizes all kinds of competencies such as cognitive, emotional, and social intelligence) (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019). A growing body of literature has emerged that aims at introducing the concept of artificial intelligence and examining its role. Indeed, there is disagreement among researchers about the role of AI. Many contributions (Abusalma, 2021; Agrawal et al., 2019; Edwards et al., 2000; Larivière et al., 2017) have highlighted that artificial intelligence can replace human capital in the performance of some tasks (i.e., adaptive tasks, contextual performance, and organizational performance). More specifically, AI systems may perform an increasing number of tasks without the control or supervision of humans (i.e., autonomous machines that make decisions on their own, provide customers with personalized offers, find patterns in the data that humans would never look at, and manage employees) (Kumar et al., 2019; Shaw et al., 2019; Vrontis et al., 2022; Złotowski et al., 2017). Meanwhile, other studies (Jarrahi, 2018; Miller, 2018; Shaw et al., 2019; Wilson & Daugherty, 2018) have found that AI should complement human capital, not replace it. For instance, AI has been used for decision-making (i.e., strategic, tactical, and operational decisions) (Duan et al., 2019; Edwards et al., 2000) and as a decision support tool, not to replace the role of decision makers (Duan et al., 2019). In this context, Shaw et al. (2019) added that artificial intelligence lacks empathy. More specifically, AI systems may not be able to comprehend or respond to emotions in the same way as humans.

It is well-documented that artificial intelligence affects job performance (Abusalma, 2021; Larivière et al., 2017; Miller, 2018; Prentice et al., 2020; Wijayati et al., 2022; Wilson & Daugherty, 2018; Younus, 2021). Abusalma (2021) used a sample of 319 managers to investigate the effect of artificial intelligence on job performance in Jordan’s banking sector. Their findings showed AI influenced job performance. For instance, artificial intelligence assists bank managers in administrative decisions and ensures lower transaction costs. In the same vein, Wilson and Daugherty (2018) concluded that AI allows employees to make better decisions, enhance their analytical and decision-making skills, and increase their creativity. They added that companies could benefit from optimizing “human-artificial intelligence collaboration” by enabling employees to work effectively with human-machine interfaces. It claims to be able to develop “fusion skills”. Similarly, Jia et al. (2023) interviewed employees to investigate whether artificial intelligence increased their creativity. Their results showed that AI mainly increases the creativity of higher-skilled employees. Compared to lower-skilled employees, higher-skilled employees are more capable of generating innovative scripts and developing positive emotions at work. Wijayati et al. (2022) conducted a questionnaire survey with 254 employees in the service and banking sectors in Indonesia. They analyzed the impact of artificial intelligence on employee performance and engagement, including the moderating role of leadership in change. They found that AI has a significant positive impact on employee performance and engagement at work. Furthermore, they added that change leadership can positively mediate the impact of AI on the performance of the employee and their work engagement. Larivière et al. (2017) developed a conceptual framework to capture the essence of Service Encounter 2.0 and concluded that technology can complement/replace service workers and facilitate network connectivity. Prentice et al. (2020) stated that artificial intelligence plays a significant mediating role in the performance of employees. Younus (2021) stated that artificial intelligence improves staff efficiency and effectiveness (i.e., improves performance, to reach desired goals). Zhang et al. (2022) added that AI enhances leadership, improves workplace flexibility, and ensures autonomous knowledge acquisition. Miller (2018) stated that a new level of human-machine symbiosis is required to augment the capabilities of individuals.

However, it is also important to be aware of the potential negative impacts of AI on job performance. In this context, many studies have expressed concern that robots and machines could take over jobs in many sectors (Cooper, 1984; Dormehl, 2018; Findlay et al., 2017; Koo et al., 2021; Li et al., 2019; Prentice et al., 2019; Spencer, 2018). Frey and Osborne (2017) argued that 47% of jobs are in danger in the United States. Findlay et al. (2017) found that artificial intelligence not only has positive effects on worker performance but also negative effects. They explained that AI can lead to fewer chances for collaboration and job rotation. Therefore, employees may feel isolated, insecure, and demotivated. In fact, the development of AI may reduce the need for human labor in certain industries. Li et al. (2019) suggested that hotel staff members who know more about artificial intelligence and robots have a higher intention to quit. However, they also added that this relationship between AI and robotics awareness and turnover intention is mitigated when staff members feel that their company values them and that they work in a psychologically competitive environment. Cooper (1984) emphasized that even the introduction of computers in the workplace may lead to an increase in occupational stress. Koo et al. (2021) argued that AI may result in employment instability for hotel staff in two ways:
- AI can replace much of the work that hotel employees currently perform, and
- due to the quick speed at which technology is developing, hotel staff may find it challenging to stay current on the knowledge and abilities required to use AI technologies.
Therefore, we make our main hypothesis:

H1: Artificial intelligence has a negative effect on job performance.

### 2.2. Gender and Job Performance

Job performance, which refers to the extent to which workers perform their jobs within the rules set by the institution (Al-Makhaita et al., 2014), depends on many factors such as age, marital status, education, experience, and gender (Abusalma, 2021; Banjo & Olufemi, 2014; Khan et al., 2022; McDaniel et al., 1988; Quiñones et al., 1995).

There are conflicting results regarding the effects of gender on job performance. Actually, it is difficult to compare the performance of men and women in identical activities (Rydstedt et al., 1998). This may be explained by differences in gender characteristics or the biological differences between men and women (Banjo & Olufemi, 2014). Abusalma (2021) indicated that gender statistically influences job performance in Jordanian commercial banks. In detail, men perform better than women. This could be due to the fact that men are perceived as more competent and successful, as they are more inclined to take risks. In addition, Khan et al. (2022) found that gender significantly moderates the relationship between transformational leadership and job performance. They claimed that male employees are more likely to benefit from transformational leadership compared to female employees. In fact, men and women may react differently to the many facets of transformational leadership.
