The introduction and application of technologies developed in this phase of healthcare allowed the creation of a smart and interconnected healthcare community (Li & Carayon, 2021).

Still in the context of Health Care 4.0, it is important to highlight that critical data security issues arise due to the digitization and interconnection of healthcare systems, including the risk of sensitive information leakage, unauthorized access, data integrity, and interoperability challenges. To ensure patient safety and privacy, measures such as encryption, user authentication, access control, threat monitoring, training, regulatory compliance, and maintenance are widely implemented, aiming to protect confidential medical information and ensure data integrity. In the case of regulatory compliance, it is essential to have laws that guarantee the privacy of patients’ health data, and privacy implies that only authorized individuals have permission to view patient health data, and it defines the circumstances under which this information can be accessed, used, and shared with third parties. For example, to ensure this regulation, laws such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States are in place (Box & Pottas, 2013; Hathaliya & Tanwar, 2020).

## E-HEALTH AND M-HEALTH

According to Antoja et al. (2019), new scenarios in many sectors are being shaped by the rapid advancement of technologies, which have the potential to change people’s lives, assuming a special value. Healthcare systems have been facing a scenario of changes in the way healthcare services are delivered, whether due to the aging population or the increased need for more complex disease management. It is a fact that a new service model is needed to connect the system with those who need it, thus generating and adding value (Kuziemsky & Vimarlund, 2018).

As an example of these changes, different measures have been taken worldwide due to the need to increase consumer involvement in healthcare. Such measures, which can be framed within the concept of e-Health, aim to focus on the development of new digital services to increase well-being or address some social challenges, such as decreasing resources and the lack of qualified personnel (Vimarlund, 2017). Through Pagliari et al.’s (2005) research, it can be observed that most definitions of e-Health conceptualize it as “a wide range of medical informatics applications to facilitate the management and delivery of healthcare” (p.10).

ICTs, exemplified by computers, smartphones, and the Internet, have revolutionized how the search and exchange of information enrich the lives of those who use them, as well as changed the way people communicate (World Health Organization, 2010). The use of technology in health apps, as long as it provides security for its users, plays an essential role in improving the lives of those who use it (European Commission, 2014).

In fact, e-Health has come to facilitate the daily lives of healthcare professionals and allow patients to be more up-to-date about their own health. Along with the use of mobile devices, such as tablets and smartphones, access to information and communication has improved, elevating the quality of healthcare provision and enabling efficient treatment of patients. The use of apps contributes to patient autonomy, as they can manage their health to better understand processes and treatments, as well as have solutions for tracking and monitoring their medical histories, medication use, and medical appointments (European Commission, 2014).

In recent years, the development of ICTs, as well as mobile Internet, which ensures connectivity anywhere and anytime, has played a fundamental role in healthcare solutions. In this context, mobile Health (m-Health) offers healthcare services that overcome geographical, temporal, and even organizational barriers. M-Health solutions address emerging health challenges, including the growing number of lifestyle-related chronic diseases, the high cost of existing national healthcare services, the need to empower patients and families to take care of themselves and their health, and motivations to provide direct access to healthcare services, regardless of time and place (Oldenburg et al., 2015; Silva et al., 2015).

M-Health technologies, such as health apps and wearable devices (which connect to cloud computing), make continuous digital monitoring of citizens’ health data possible. This includes monitoring vital signs such as heart rate, blood glucose levels, blood pressure, body temperature, brain activity, and other indicators. Portable devices, sensors, and smartphone apps allow people to collect significant amounts of medical, biological, and physiological information, as well as information about their lifestyles and daily activities. This wealth of data enables the capture of unique information about each individual’s behavior, genetics, and environment, facilitating the delivery of highly personalized and patient-centered healthcare, with an emphasis on accuracy (Aydin & Silahtaroglu, 2021; Belliger & Krieger, 2018; Demiris et al., 2019; Scholz & Teetz, 2022).

The digital transformation in the healthcare sector, driven by the adoption of apps and mobile devices by patients, has brought about significant changes in people’s attitudes and behaviors, deeply impacting citizens. They have become active participants in managing their own health, having an active voice in interactions with healthcare institutions. M-Health has enabled an evolution in the role of the patient, transitioning from a passive recipient of diagnosis and treatment to an active, well-informed partner responsible for their own health. Mobile health solutions allow patients to make informed decisions and take preventive measures based on their own health metric monitoring. This gives citizens greater control over decisions related to their healthcare, while also holding them accountable for staying informed about their health conditions in order to remain healthy (Aydin & Silahtaroglu, 2021; Belliger & Krieger, 2018; Schnurr et al., 2018; Scholz & Teetz, 2022; Vial, 2019).
