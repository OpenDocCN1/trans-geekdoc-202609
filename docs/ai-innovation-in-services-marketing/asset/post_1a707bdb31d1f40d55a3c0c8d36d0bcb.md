
## 2. METHODOLOGY

The method used to identify the foundational and base articles related to service innovation, strategies, and potential future research areas is discussed in this section. The first step in gathering articles on innovation and service innovation was to access search engines and databases like EBSCO, Google Scholar, etc. For the researchable concept of “Service Innovation,” a list of keywords was created, including terms like “Innovation,” “services,” “barriers,” “factors,” “product,” “process,” “process typologies,” “organization,” “innovation and performance,” “dimensions,” and “new service development,” among others. The list of keywords was combined in any way that was conceivable to conduct the search.

All of the articles related to the keywords that were available were downloaded and separated based on their relationships. The content was gathered from a variety of Journal publishers, including Sage Publications, Scopus Elsevier, Emerald, Business Communication Quarterly, Routledge, Taylor & Francis, Oxford University Press, Wiley Online Library, Inderscience Publications, World Scientific Publishers, David Publications, Scientific Research, etc. All articles released between 1970 and 2023 were included in the search. Using the SPAR 4SLR framework for inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 380 articles were filtered, and by deleting the duplication, the final list was reduced to 247. Out of those, 84 of the articles dealt with service innovation approaches, and 73 of them dealt with typologies, dimensions, service innovation measurement, empirical studies involving outcome variables and service innovation, and conceptual studies involving the measurement of service innovation. According to Tranfield et al. (2003), a systematic review should be conducted by multiple reviewers. This study adhered to this recommendation, and the authors carefully analyzed the articles to identify any gaps in the literature.

disruptive innovation is an innovation that creates a new market and new value network, that is, the innovation that improves the service or product that already does not exist in the market and is designed for the first time. Whereas sustaining innovation does not create a new innovative market, rather it improves the value of the existing one. Similarly, researchers classified measurement based on the degree of novelty as radical and incremental innovation (Ettlie et al. 1984; Tushman et al. 1985; Dewar and Dutton 1986; Orlikowski 1991). Radical innovation is nonlinear paradigmatic changes that depart from existing knowledge or practice. On the other hand, incremental is a linear cumulative change that implies a minor improvement or adjustment in existing knowledge or technology.

Product innovation, process innovation, marketing innovation, input innovation, and organizational innovation are the five key categories of service innovation (Gadrey et al., 1995; Tejada and Moreno, 2013). It majorly relies on market capability that encompasses the customer orientation (Akter et al., 2023). In relation to this, the authors need to consider some typologies, such as marketing innovation, input typology, and organization innovation, while measuring service innovation is the main issue identified. On the other hand, several authors added additional typologies to the current service innovation typologies, including administrative innovation, strategic typology, and technology innovation. The measurement elements in these typologies were mostly related to product innovation, whether typologies were included or excluded when measuring service innovation. Later arguments were made against the inclusion of technological innovation in the typologies that were developed to measure service innovation by authors such as Barras (1986), Edvardsson (1997), Sirilli and Evangelista (1998). This allowed academics to mix several product and service innovation typologies to develop comprehensive business models (Gallouj and Weinstein 1997; Coombs and Miles 2000).

Miller and Friesen (1982) explored the measurement of service innovation by dimensions, and the dimensions considered were: (a) New product or service innovation; (b) Methods of production or provision of services; and (c) Risk taken by senior executives in search of unique and creative solutions. Hertog (2000) provided a four-dimensional model of service innovation that was similar to this, with the dimensions being (a) the service concept, (b) the client interface, (c) the service delivery system, and (d) technological options. Later, Alvonitis and colleagues (2001) highlighted several factors, such as (a) the Newness of the delivery process; (b) Adjustments to the service; (c) the Newness of the service to the market; and (d) the Newness of the service to the company. The work of Sidhu et al. (2007), which indicates the complexity of service and represents these complexities by three dimensions, including (a) Supply-side, (b) Customer-side, and (c) Geographical/Institutional, has recently been embraced by various studies. Customers-focused innovation, service-dominant innovation, conventional innovation, and collaboration-based innovation are a few dimensions listed by Nam and Lee (2010). Cheng and Krumwiede (2012) recently adopted a three-dimensional measurement, with the dimensions being (1) Types of innovation, (2) Radicalness of innovation, and (3) Frequency of innovation. Although researchers aimed to measure the same notion, it can be seen from the review on the measurement of service innovation by dimensions that they needed a consistent phrase for measuring dimensions, with the name of the dimension frequently changing from one author to another. The Hertog (2000) dimensions were widely adopted by several authors in the investigations (Miles 2008 and Lin 2012). It is also clear from the review that the dimensions were largely the same from one author to the next; only the terminology was different.
