New technologies have the potential to transform the field of preventive health, allowing people to monitor their individual health effectively through more accurate information about their physical and mental conditions, so that they receive personalized preventive treatment, enabling the prolongation of human life (Jiang et al., 2022). However, over the years, there has been a reluctance in the healthcare industry to pave the way for preventive medicine. As mentioned by Akhmetov & Bubnov (2015) and Terkola et al. (2017), there are specialists who point out that there is a lack of research or solid evidence that convincingly shows how the adoption of preventive medicine can affect the economy of resources, such as reducing medical costs and expensive treatments. This resistance affects the implementation of preventive technologies that could improve the population’s health in a personalized and effective way, as there is still a need for more studies to prove their financial benefits and their effectiveness in managing healthcare resources.

## FINAL CONSIDERATIONS

Health apps play a crucial role in implementing preventive healthcare. They act as intermediaries between AI, healthcare professionals, and patients. To understand the acceptance of health apps, it is necessary to understand how their users perceive them, considering the perspectives of both consumers who will use the apps in their daily lives and healthcare professionals who will use the apps to complement and assist their work. The perception of patients regarding health apps can vary greatly depending on various factors, such as their previous experience with these tools, their familiarity with technology in general, their specific health needs, and even their age and level of education (Peng et al., 2016).

For some consumers, health apps can be seen as a convenient and affordable way to monitor their health and manage chronic conditions, allowing them to have more control over their well-being and enabling them to record and track behaviors and goals (Alkaldi et al., 2023; European Commission, 2014; Peng et al., 2016). These individuals appreciate the ease of access to health information and online resources, such as healthy eating tips, exercise routines, and medication reminders. They also believe that through apps, they can control their diet and exercise routines, track their progress in weight loss cases, stay updated on their health preventively, ensuring that tests and appointments are always up to date (Chakraborty & Paul, 2023; Dennison et al., 2013; Peng et al., 2016).

On the other hand, there are consumers with different profiles who may be more skeptical about health apps and may have concerns about the privacy and security of their health data, as it may be accidentally exposed or disclosed to unauthorized third parties (Dennison et al., 2013; Stephen et al., 2022). Additionally, some people may feel that health apps are not as effective as consulting a doctor in person, or may not have access to the necessary technology to use these apps (Mills et al., 2020).

Another barrier to the adoption of these health apps would be the cost of accessing this technology. According to research, some users prefer free apps because they believe that they already deliver enough to meet their needs, but this does not mean that people are not willing to pay for the service. Some people would pay a small amount if they found an app worth buying, that is, only if the app had exclusive features and functions that other free apps do not have (Alqahtani & Orji, 2020; Aydin & Silahataroglu, 2021; Peng et al., 2016).

In general, the patient's perception of health apps depends heavily on their personal experience and individual health needs, as well as the quality and reliability of the available apps. It is important for health app developers to consider these factors and work to provide digital health tools that are useful, accessible, and secure for all patients. This is what the vast majority seek, whether young or old (Lagan et al., 2021; A. Nunes et al., 2019; Stoyanov et al., 2016).

Health apps have become increasingly popular and accessible in recent years and offer a wide range of features to help people manage their health and well-being. From the perspective of healthcare professionals, these apps can have various importance, namely:

- Improve patient communication and engagement: Health apps can help improve communication between patients and healthcare professionals, allowing patients to track and share real-time health data. This can lead to better patient engagement and greater collaboration between the patient and the healthcare professional (European Commission, 2014).

- Facilitate screening and management of chronic diseases: Many health apps are designed to help people manage chronic diseases such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and asthma. These apps allow patients to monitor their symptoms, medications, and daily lifestyle habits, which can assist healthcare professionals in assessing treatment progress and adjusting the treatment plan if necessary (Krishnan & Selvam, 2019).

- Increase the efficiency and accuracy of diagnoses: Some apps are designed to assist medical professionals in diagnosing and treating diseases. For example, an app that uses AI to analyze medical images can help healthcare professionals identify abnormalities more quickly and accurately (Silva et al., 2015).

- Promote a healthy lifestyle: Many health apps aim to help people lead healthier lifestyles by providing guidance on exercise, diet, and sleep. This can help healthcare professionals promote disease prevention and health maintenance (Balapour et al., 2019; Bondaronek et al., 2018).

Healthcare professionals have varied opinions about health apps, depending on the specific context of use. In general, many professionals recognize the potential of apps as useful tools to improve the quality of healthcare and engage patients in their own care. However, there are some concerns regarding the reliability, security, and privacy of personal health information collected by the apps. Additionally, some healthcare professionals may be concerned that health apps may replace personal medical care rather than complement it, but we see that this should not be a concern, as technology can be considered as a support tool, and professionals continue to be essential in the healthcare process (Antoja et al., 2019; Arni & Laddha, 2017; Chakravorty et al., 2018; European Commission, 2014). The results obtained from the literature presented demonstrate perspectives that complement each other through very similar perceived benefits and barriers. The study presents a limitation as it would be necessary to deeply understand these perspectives through interviews or questionnaires.
