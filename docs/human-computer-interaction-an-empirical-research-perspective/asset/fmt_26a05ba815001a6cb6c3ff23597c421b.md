
Generally, a low SPC implies efficient input. MacKenzie and Felzer's scanning ambiguous keyboard (SAK), for example, has SPC = 1.56 for English text entry [\[311, Figure 6\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1555). One implementation is BlinkWrite, which uses eye blinks for the input switch (see Chapter [2](#Chp002_31-91_B9780443140969000087.xhtml), [Fig. 2.26](#Chp002_31-91_B9780443140969000087.xhtml), p. 59).

Scansteps per character (SPC), although useful as a point of comparison, must be viewed in a larger picture that also considers the cognitive and physical demands on the user. Other design issues for scanning keyboards abound [\[32](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0160),[252](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1260),[279](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1395),[340](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1700),[434](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2170),[472](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2360),[480\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2400). See as well the letter arrangement in [Fig. 5.16](#Chp005_203-242_B9780443140969000117.xhtml)a (p. 234) and student exercise [3-1](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#o0010) at the end of this chapter.

Single-switch scanning can also serve as an interaction method for general purpose target selection. Raynal and MacKenzie [\[408\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2040) describe TBS³, two-bar single-switch scanning, a technique using horizontal and vertical lines that traverse a display with their intersection marking a point for selection. The method is slow but interaction does not require operating a pointing device such as a mouse or touchpad. Input is through a single input switch. In the empirical evaluation, participants used the Spacebar on the system's physical keyboard to select targets in a 2D Fitts' law target selection task. See [Fig. 3.17](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0090). Fitts' law is presented in greater detail in Chapter [7](#Chp007_293-360_B9780443140969000130.xhtml).

### 3.1.6 Gesture input

Gestures are movements to communicate. They are germane to human communication, whether through eye, face, or head movement during conversation or through finger, hand or arm motions to provide context or emphasis.

Described so broadly (*movements to communicate*), gestures encompass all human interactions with computers.^([^(50)](#fn50))

An example from 1980 of gestures is the "Put That There" demonstration by Bolt [\[35\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0175). The user makes mid-air gestures, moving their hand to point to objects or locations on a large display and then issues voice commands. Bolt's system was a room with a large projection screen and a instrumented chair for the user. The system included voice recognition for simple commands and a Polhemus 6 DOF tracker mounted on the user's hand or wrist. It was, perhaps, the first VR "Cave." An "x" cursor appeared on the display as visual feedback for pointing. Because of the cursor, Bolt's system is an example of indirect pointing; the cursor is a intermediary between the user's hand position and a position on the display. But, the pointing movements are gestures, specifying a location or object as the target for a voice command.

Often, the intent or purpose of a gesture is a matter of the gesture location and shape. Classic non-computing examples are the marks a copy editor puts on a document. Kurtenbach and Buxton's GEdit extended the idea to gestures for graphics editing using a tablet and stylus [\[264\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1320). See [Fig. 3.18](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0095). The operations, such as copying or deleting objects, normally require the use of both hands with a combination of keyboard and mouse or stylus input. Notably, GEdit gestures only require stylus input and so only involve the use of one hand.

For text entry, there are Goldberg and Richardson's Unistrokes for English letters [\[148\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0740). Each letter is formed by a single gesture stroke; thus, the "segmentation problem" is avoided and eyes-free input is possible.^([^(51)](#fn51))

Sometimes the meaning or purpose of a gesture depends on articulation points within the path of the gesture. There are many examples, including Quikwriting [\[393\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1965), EdgeWrite [\[524\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2620), Cirrin [\[339\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1695), WatchMI [\[537\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2685), and MojiBoard [\[7\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0035). A widely-used example is *shape writing* where words are entered on a soft keyboard by gesturing through the keys bearing the letters in the word [\[256](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1280),[547](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2735),[548\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2740). The gesture starts on "e", transitions through "a" and "s", and finishes on "y". Note in the figure that word prediction is used with "way" and "ready" offered as alternatives. Shape writing gestures for text entry are also possible for entire sentences [\[503\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2515) or on other keyboard layouts, such as an ambiguous phone keypad [\[258\]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1290).
