
[Figure 3.53](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#cf1245) The Rockin'Mouse works like a mouse but also senses side-to-side (*θ*[*z*]) rotation (shown) and forward-backward (*θ*[*x*]) rotation [[18]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0090). Rotation about the *z*-axis is not sensed (sketch courtesy of Bartosz Bajer).

The prototype operated on the surface of a tablet. A cordless sensor was mounted in the centre of the Rockin'Mouse. The sensor was extracted from the tablet's stylus. From the tablet's perspective, the Rockin'Mouse is a stylus. From the user's perspective, it looks and feels like a mouse, except for the rounded bottom. The tablet senses and reports the *x* and *z* positions of the sensor as well as tilt (*θ*[*x*]![Image](images/B9780443140969000099/si12.png) and *θ*[*z*]![Image](images/B9780443140969000099/si11.png)). Balakrishnan et al. [[18]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0090) evaluated the Rockin'Mouse in a 3D positioning task using tilt for the up-down movement of the object. In the mouse comparison condition, participants had to select a handle on the object to switch movement modes. Overall, the Rockin'Mouse was about 30% faster than the mouse.

Evans et al. [[120]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0600) in 1981 anticipated a three-axis trackball that senses rotation about each of the *x*, *y*, and *z* axes. See [Fig. 3.54](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0275)a. Even though the usual operation of a trackball is *x*-*y* planar control of a pointer, the ball freely rotates; so, the labelling of the degrees of freedom in [Fig. 3.54](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0275)a is appropriate. A device like this has potential to perform well in controlling the view of the 3D surface plot in [Fig. 3.50](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0255). Spatial congruence is clearly possible between the three-axis rotation of the ball and similar rotation of the surface plot. Of course, it is relatively easy to include modes, allowing mouse-emulation.

There is more to degrees of freedom than simply counting them. It is important to consider the relationship between device properties, such as degrees of freedom, and task requirements. Jacob et al. [[217]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br1085) speak of the *integrality* and *separability* of degrees of freedom. With a regular mouse, controlling one degree of freedom in isolation of the other is difficult. Consider drawing a horizontal line with a mouse using the drawing mode of an application. The task is difficult because the *x* and *y* degrees of freedom are integrated. Of course, degrees of freedom can be separated. A classic example for *x*-*y* positioning is the children's toy Etch-A-Sketch introduced in the 1950s by the Ohio Art Company. See [Fig. 3.55](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0280). There are separate 1 DOF controllers to move the drawing instrument along the *x* and *y* axes. Drawing a horizontal line is easy. Drawing a diagonal line is a challenge, however. See Buxton [[50]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0250) for additional discussion on input using the Etch-A-Sketch and other toys.

![Figure 3.55 Etch-A-Sketch children's toy with separate controls for the x and y degrees of freedom.](images/B9780443140969000099/gr055.jpg)

[Figure 3.55](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#cf1295) Etch-A-Sketch children's toy with separate controls for the *x* and *y* degrees of freedom.

Another example is the wheel mouse, released in 1996 as Microsoft's IntelliMouse. See [Fig. 3.56](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0285)a. The rotating wheel gives the mouse an extra – and separate – degree of freedom. A less successful version was Mouse System's ProAgio, released about a year earlier. See [Fig. 3.56](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0285)b. Even earlier was Venolia's Roller Mouse [[499]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2495), presented at the annual ACM SIGCHI conference in 1993. See [Fig. 3.56](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0285)c. The Roller Mouse had two wheels. The intended use was controlling degrees of freedom in 3D graphics applications.

Additional, but separate, degrees of freedom are evident in Balakrishnan and Patel's PadMouse [[20]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br0100). See [Fig. 3.57](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0290)a. A touchpad was added to the surface of a regular mouse. The intent was to support pie menu access with gestures on the touchpad while the mouse operated as usual, controlling an on-screen pointer. Clearly, combining interaction in this manner requires separating the degrees of freedom for pie menu access and pointer control.

Silfverberg et al. [[453]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2265) describe a display screen that allows the displayed image to be zoomed and panned by two touch sensors positioned underneath the device ([Fig. 3.57](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0290)b). Occlusion is avoided since the fingers act below the display. As well, the 1 DOF zooming and 2 DOF panning operations are separated and, thus, do not interfere.

Villar et al. [[504]](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#br2520) describe a variety of mouse configurations instrumented to detect touch, including multi-touch. The devices use IR transmitters that inject light into the edge of an acrylic surface. An IR camera below the surface receives an image of the surface. When fingers contact the surface, the light is scattered at the points of contact. Image processing software determines the location and pressure of the points of contact. Some of the device configurations are shown in [Fig. 3.58](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0295). The devices support multi-touch gestures as well as regular mouse operations.

One configuration is the CapMouse (second from left in [Fig. 3.58](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0295)). It uses a capacitive sensor, rather than an IR transmitter and camera. The setup is similar to Balakrishnan and Patel's PadMouse ([Fig. 3.57](#Chp003_93-160_B9780443140969000099.xhtml#f0290)a), except the touch-sensing surface is contoured and seamlessly integrates with the device chassis.
