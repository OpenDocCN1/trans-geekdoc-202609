
With a small number of hidden neurons, an `MLP` \[multilayer perceptron\] is a parametric model that provides a useful alternative to polynomial regression. With a moderate number of hidden neurons, an `MLP` can be considered a quasi-parametric model similar to projection pursuit regression … An `MLP` with one hidden layer is essentially the same as the projection pursuit regression model except that an `MLP` uses a predetermined functional form for the activation function in the hidden layer, whereas projection pursuit uses a flexible nonlinear smoother. If the number of hidden neurons is allowed to increase with the sample size, an `MLP` becomes a nonparametric sieve that provides a useful alternative to methods such as kernel regression and smoothing splines. `MLPs` are especially valuable because you can vary the complexity of the model from a simple parametric model to a highly flexible, nonparametric model.

Differences between the two methods also include the fact that parameters in a neural network are typically estimated *simultaneously* where in projection pursuit regression they are usually fit *successively* for each hidden unit ([Klinke & Grassmann, 2000](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000192)). Though not an exact analogy, the optimal number of hidden units in a neural network can be likened to the number of terms to add in a projection pursuit.

Thus, projection pursuit is often considered an historical and conceptual “precursor,” especially pedagogically, to understanding neural networks, and according to Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)), projection pursuit was not widely used at the time of its inception largely due to computational requirements for obtaining a solution, computational “roadblocks” that have long been overcome with modern computing advances. Perhaps had more computing power existed back in the day of projection pursuit, these models would have a more significant legacy. Pedagogically, however, projection pursuit is very valuable in putting neural networks and other models (e.g., `PCA` and generalized linear models) into proper context. Indeed, a generalized version of `PPR`, aptly named `Generalized Projection Pursuit Regression` ([Roosen & Hastie, 1993](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000307)) has been proposed that is similar in structure to the generalized linear model paradigm of McCullagh and Nelder ([1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000243)) in its use of link functions and that allow for binary responses.

Lastly, it can be shown that as the number of smoothing functions increases without bound to infinity, for an appropriate set of functions in `f[j]`, as was the case for neural networks, projection pursuit regression can approximate virtually any continuous function and is considered a *universal approximator*. How many smoothing functions to include in the approximation? There are different recommendations regarding this. For example, [Friedman et al. (1984)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000113) found that adding more terms than necessary then using a backward selection algorithm helps avoid getting stuck in local minima. [Roosen and Hastie (1993)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000307) have found that adding terms to maximize generalized cross-validation then pruning back somewhat may be an effective strategy.

Having compared projection pursuit to neural networks, it is also useful to recap where we began this discussion, in that projection pursuit can be understood as a higher-order and more general framework to principal components analysis. Such emphasizes that the latter is but only one *type* of projection, not *the* unique projection or dimensionality reduction of data. As we have seen throughout our discussion of dimension reduction techniques, what one technique might reveal from the data another technique may do better. There is no such thing as finding the *unique* dimensions of one’s data; *what you find is as much to do with what tool you use to do the finding*, which, incidentally is why some dislike statistics in the first place as it is perceived to provide no “hard” truths, a misguided perception based on an incomplete understanding of the subject.

*Principal components analysis is only one type of projection of the data. Projection pursuit (and by extension, neural networks) makes this clear – how you project your data will, to a large extent, determine what you find, and thus “see” from that data.*

Projection pursuit in R can be performed using the R package `tourr` (Wickham et al., [2011](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000382)). It can also be performed using `ppr()` in R.

## 15.13 Summary, Warnings, and Caveats of Neural Networks

Neural networks can be easily misunderstood by the uninitiated. Their very name can encourage newcomers to give them powers they may not have, or at minimum, exaggerate the powers they actually do have. Neural networks are tremendously useful, but in learning them for the first time, it is important to understand exactly what they *are* and what they are *not*. Throughout this chapter 559we have issued many caveats and warnings about neural networks. We summarize, and in some cases extend on those caveats and warnings in what follows.

### 15.13.1 Neural Networks Are Not Biological Nor Are They Machines

A first obvious point is that though neural networks were motivated by psychological and biological entities in the brain, they themselves have nothing to do with psychology or biology! They have everything to do with *abstract mathematics*. What is more, though they, like virtually all other statistical or machine learning methods are run on high-speed computers, there is nothing about neural networks themselves that have anything to do with computers or other “machines.” A neural network can, at least in theory and with enough time, be fit by pencil and paper just as any other mathematical technique. “Enough time” is more lifetimes than anyone might ever have, hence the benefit of using computers.

Why is this point so relevant? It is so because too often some users get into the habit of drawing conclusions from data based on the *jargon* of the statistical or machine learning method. As we discussed earlier in the book, in social statistics, for instance, all too often researchers will surprisingly conclude that such and such variable *mediates* or *moderates* another and impose an *action quality* on those words when no action is necessarily supported by the research design or the *organicity* of the variables used in the modeling. As emphasized throughout this book, *physical action of any sort can never be implied or concluded from the application of a statistical model alone, whether those models are of mediation, moderation, or neural networks*. The statistical or machine learning model never “knows” the kind of data or research design to which it is being applied; all models are able to do is process the data fed to them. *Models are entirely abstract entities*. So-called *action statements* must come from the *design* or *science*, not the *statistics*.

### 15.13.2 Neural Networks Need Not Be Nonlinear

As we have discussed, neural networks are typically *nonlinear* models. However, they need not necessarily be so. The special case, as we have seen, is the *linear* activation function. Of course, imposing a linear activation function minimizes the power of neural networks, and hence most neural networks are nonlinear. However, to say that they *necessarily* need to be nonlinear, or to *necessarily* associate nonlinearity with neural networks is a pedagogical shortcoming, as a linear neural network also, technically, counts as a neural network. Part of the confusion is simply in how writers and researchers choose to define a neural network, often as nonlinear. However, newcomers should be aware of this distinction.
