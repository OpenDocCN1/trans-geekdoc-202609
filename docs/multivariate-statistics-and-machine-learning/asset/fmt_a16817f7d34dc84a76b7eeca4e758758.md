
KNN is the prototype method of *local learning*, since it approximates the target function using only nearby data points. This also makes the technique an example of what is referred to as *instance-based learning* as it is not imposing a model as in *model-based learning* (e.g., linear regression). That is, it does not attempt to look at the “big picture” or to data points that are not in the *neighborhood*. Because KNN focuses only on *local* information, it can give a very misleading conclusion if outside of the neighborhood the more “global” structure is quite different than nearby. It would be akin to predicting the home price of a particular house based solely on a few of the nearby homes instead of the homes a bit further down the block.

As we have seen in this book, however, the above is not only true for KNN; it is true for virtually all methods that prioritize *local structure* over *global structure*. Looking more locally has advantages, but the cost is potentially ignoring the bigger picture. This can be good or bad, depending on how you look at it. To use our analogy of home prices, perhaps the house you are buying is worth the asking price because its “mini neighborhood” consists of mansions, even if its “extended neighborhood” features many less expensive houses. In the end, it’s a judgment call based on what is most important to the investigator.

KNN is also considered a *memory-based learner* because it focuses only on the training data and nearby points. What is remarkable about nearest-neighbor decision rules is that half of the classification information in an infinite sample set is contained in the nearest neighbor ([Cover & Hart, 1967](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000070)). That is, “this simple rule has a probability of error which is less than twice the Bayes probability of error, and hence is less than twice the probability of error of any other decision rule, nonparametric or otherwise, based on the infinite sample set.” ([1967](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000070), pp. 21–22) For a theoretical justification for how nearest-neighbor methods converge on risk, see [Cover and Hart (1967)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000070), who justify that half of the available information in an infinite collection of classified samples is contained in the nearest neighbor (p. 27).

As mentioned, such instance-based learning algorithms are sometimes referred to as “lazy” learning methods because they delay processing until a new instance must be classified. One advantage of this kind of delay is that instead of estimating the target function one time for the entire instance space, such methods can estimate it *locally* and differently for each new instance that needs to be classified. Recall that locally weighted regression is one type of method that accomplishes something similar to KNN. Not all learners that are instanced-based are also lazy. For instance, support vector machines are considered instance-based yet are not considered a part of the class of lazy learners.

KNN can also be operationalized from a Bayesian perspective which incorporates class priors in the classification of new points based on a sphere of data denoted as the nearest neighbors. For details, see [Hastie et al. (2009)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145). The history of nearest-neighbor rules seems to go back to at least Fix and Hodges ([1951](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000103)), with further developments by [Cover and Hart (1967)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000070).

The data point `x` will be classified to group “1” because its nearest neighbors are mostly values of 1. That is, there are 3 *ones* and 2 *zeroes*. KNN thus focuses on *local behavior* of nearby data points to determine classification. In running a KNN, we are required to select a value for `k`, which in this example is equal to 5, designating the nearby points to consider when making the classification. Of those 5 nearest neighbors, 3 of them have values of 1, and hence `x` is classified to group “1.” For cases that are equally as close to other points, random selection can be used to determine which is the “nearest neighbor” (Kroese et al., [2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000202)).

With `k` set to 3, we can see that 2 out of 3 of the nearby objects have values of 1, and hence the new case `x` is classified once more to the group “1.”

Whereas least-squares assumes the underlying function `f(x)` is well-approximated by a global linear function, the `k`-nearest neighbors approach assumes `f(x)` is well-approximated by a *locally constant function* ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). This fact is operationalized in KNN as the nearby points. While the KNN approach may appear more flexible, the sheer size of the dimensionality problem (i.e., the *curse of dimensionality*) makes assumption-laden approaches such as OLS regression often more attractive.

When `k` = 1, then each data point has a single nearest neighbor, and hence the classifier will fit very well; however, this will likely result in *overfitting*. Specifying `k` = 1 results in what is known as a *Voronoi tessellation* of points ([Figure 8.10](#c8.xhtml#f8.10)), where each point has its own neighborhood of a single data point (think of the skin on a giraffe).347

Clearly, setting `k` = 1 is not very useful and will usually overfit. We seek instead to set `k` to a larger value for most problems. Incidentally, when `k` = 1, KNN is sometimes simply referred to as `NN` (“nearest neighbor”). What distinguishes KNN from `NN` is the option of specifying values for `k` greater than 1. Of course, if we set `k` equal to `n`, the total number of data points, then KNN will simply select the most common class of all data points.

The other requirement of KNN in addition to setting `k` is specifying a *distance metric*. Concluding that a data point is in the “neighborhood” is fine, but what denotes a “neighborhood?” New York City is in the neighborhood of Washington DC if we consider eastern cities only, but is not in the neighborhood if we adopt a different metric or different context. As is true of most statistical and machine learning methods, findings can be heavily influenced by how we choose to model things, and those decisions often come down to decisions such as the nature of the *metric* we choose to use.
