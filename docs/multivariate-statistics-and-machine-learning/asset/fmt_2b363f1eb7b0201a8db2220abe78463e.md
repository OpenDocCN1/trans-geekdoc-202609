
$$\\lim\\limits\_{x\\rightarrow a}f\\left( x \\right) = f\\left( a \\right)$$

(2.116)

in which we recall again that the *limit* of a function is the number *approached* by that function, and not necessarily defined *at that function*. For example, in the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, the limit of the sequence is 1 regardless of whether the sequence ever attains 1. A sequence that has a limit is referred to as *convergent*. Limits can be computed quite easily in Python. For examples, see [Saha (2015)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000319).

Elementary as we have portrayed continuity to be above, it is in fact a very deep concept and took years for mathematicians to develop and rigorize. In modern mathematics, continuity is usually conceptualized using epsilon-delta definitions (see [Denis, 2021a](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000087) for a brief and simple overview of how these definitions work). Of course, continuity is not first and foremost a mathematical idea. It is first and foremost a *concept*. The *idea* of continuity in the consciousness of human beings has, just as is true of many essential mathematical concepts, likely existed since the dawn of humanity. It is not difficult to imagine early civilizations planning travel routes based on the continuity of one route instead of taking an alternative route that would be *discontinuous* and have “gaps” or “jumps” in the route (perhaps a river that needed to be crossed). The fact that mathematicians and philosophers have defined these concepts *rigorously* using precise notation should not be taken to imply that such concepts were “born” in mathematics. If anything, like most things, they were likely born in early philosophical “nonspecialist” thinking.

## 2.58 The Derivative

The reader will recall from calculus that to differentiate a function, `f(x)`, we first compute the change in `y` (`δy`) relative to the change in `x` (`δx`):

$$\\frac{\\delta y}{\\delta x} = \\frac{f\\left( x + \\delta x \\right) - f\\left( x \\right)}{\\delta x}$$

(2.117)

The above is called the *difference quotient*, and represents the average rate of change of a function. To get the *derivative*, we compute the change in the function relative to the change in `x` as in (2.117) but with one very important addition; we take the change in `y` relative to the change in `x to the limit`, where now `df` corresponds to change in `y` (or change in the *function* more generally) and `dx` corresponds to change in `x`, represented now by `h` in the ratio (i.e., `h` = `δx`):

$$\\frac{df}{dx} = f'\\left( x \\right) = \\lim\\limits\_{h\\rightarrow 0}\\frac{f\\left( x + h \\right) - f\\left( x \\right)}{h}$$

(2.118)

That is, the derivative `f'(x)` is the limit of the difference quotient as the change in `x`, represented by `h`, goes to zero. Be sure to note that the derivative is a *slope*, but it is only the slope in the limit as `h`→0. In other words, it’s the *instantaneous rate of change*. Without this qualifier of “*limit as* `h`→0,” the derivative has no meaning and is not defined. Differential calculus (and integral, for that matter) is all about *limits*. The derivative is easily visualized in the graph in [Figure 2.16](#c2.xhtml#f2.16) for two dimensions, where we see the tangent to the curve as well as an approximating secant line (to be discussed shortly).153

Note that we could have drawn a number of slopes around the given point and all would be slightly different from the slope in [Figure 2.16](#c2.xhtml#f2.16). How is the *tangent* defined at that particular very small point? This is again where the concept of a limit comes in. For `h` > 0, we can fit many different secant lines (i.e., lines intersecting the curve closer and closer to the eventual tangent line). However, as `h`→0, we *zero in* on the tangent line, the slope of which we will call the *derivative*. That is, *the derivative is the slope defined by the limit of the secant lines as* `h`→0. In this respect, the derivative is not simply the slope of the line *touching* the point at the given location; it is the *limiting (secant) line that touches the point*, even though informally we will often simply say “touching the point” or “tangent to the point” or “slope at the point” when describing the derivative.

It stands that if the derivative of a function is equal to 0 at each point along some interval of `x`, then this implies that the function is *constant* on that interval. That is, if `f'(x)` = 0 at each point, then this implies `f(x)` = `c` at each point, where `c` is a constant. If a function does have a limit at a given point, that limit is *unique*. In higher dimensions, much more difficult to visualize, instead of a *tangent to the* *curve*, we will have a *plane tangent to the surface*. The vocabulary has changed but the concept is the same. Higher dimensions can be intimidating, but are less so if one has a good grounding in understanding concepts in lower dimensions.

[Hubbard and Hubbard (2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000161), p. 126) summarize the goal of differential calculus beautifully, providing great insight into its purpose:

> The object of differential calculus is to study nonlinear mappings by replacing them with linear transformations: we replace nonlinear equations with linear equations, curved surfaces by their tangent planes, and so on. Of course, this linearization is useful only if you understand linear objects reasonably well. Also, this replacement is only more or less justified. Locally, near the point of tangency, a curved surface may be very similar to its tangent plane, but further away it isn’t. The hardest part of differential calculus is determining when replacing a nonlinear object by a linear one is justified … nonlinear is much too broad a class to consider. Dividing mappings into linear and nonlinear is like dividing people into left-handed cello players and everyone else.

We can also distinguish derivatives into left and right. The left-derivative is the limit, not surprisingly, from the left,154

$$f\_{-}^{'}\\left( x \\right) = \\lim\\limits\_{h\\rightarrow 0^{-}}\\frac{f\\left( x + h \\right) - f\\left( x \\right)}{h}$$

(2.119)

where the right-derivative is given by

$$f\_{+}^{'}\\left( x \\right) = \\lim\\limits\_{h\\rightarrow 0^{+}}\\frac{f\\left( x + h \\right) - f\\left( x \\right)}{h}$$

(2.220)

It can be shown that the derivative of a function at a point only exists if both the left and right derivatives exist and are equal to one another.

In Python, we can find derivatives of functions as follows:

```
from sympy import Symbol, Derivative

St = 5*t**2 + 2*t + 8
Derivative(St, t)
Out[68]:
Derivative(5*t**2 + 2*t + 8, t)
```
