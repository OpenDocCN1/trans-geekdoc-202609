
As an example of what we are talking about, consider an airplane system. Probabilistic independence is a sought-after goal when conceptualizing and manufacturing aircraft components and running failure analysis scenarios. Engineers purposely design airplane systems to be as independent as possible so that *a failure in one system does not change the probability of a failure in another system*. Why is this so important? Well, consider the possibilities when flying on an airplane; it would be a terrible thing if the probability of a failure of one engine increased the probability of a failure of a second engine, or if the probability of the landing gear detracting was not independent of the probability of the hydraulic system working. In these cases, it would take but one failure to influence other failures, and hence a single failure could, in theory, take down the airplane through a chain reaction of events. Hence, as much as possible, system events on airplanes are designed to be probabilistically independent. The probability of one engine failing should, ideally, not have an effect on the probability that the plane experiences a decompression, for instance.

But how do these experts “know” the systems are independent? They don’t! Well, not for *certain*, anyway. However, rational argument, a *plausible argument*, knowledge of the systems under consideration, increasing empirical evidence, and making assumptions that align with such knowledge typically lead such professionals to conclude that operating the flaps on the wings, for instance, is independent of the ability of the fuel tank to hold fuel, just as a failure of the landing gear to retract is independent of the plane’s engines to generate power. Of course, these seem like “obvious” assumptions to make, but the point is that they are often nonetheless *assumptions* (assumptions often supported by increasing amounts of evidence, in this case). The goal of science is to reject “obvious” assumptions with newly established empirical evidence that potentially counter or challenge existing assumptions.

For instance, it was once assumed that a simple cigarette could never cause any harm, just as it was once assumed that chemicals used in paint (e.g., benzene) could never pose a threat to our health and the health of our pets. Social media was never thought to be associated with depression and anxiety in our children. Science is about cutting through “established” beliefs with evidence that suggests such “establishment” should be rejected. Newtonian mechanics was once assumed correct until Einstein’s “unlikely” theory led us to believe differently. The point is that all scientific “fact” is tentative to new evidence and revision, and the idea of probabilistic independence is no different. We can assume events to be independent to get on with business (and fly the plane, for instance), but we must always allow the possibility that they are not, subject to new evidence. It was once assumed that a thin layer of icing on wings could never influence the capacity of the plane to stay airborne. We now know differently, largely due to experiencing (i.e., data) planes fall from the sky because of a thin layer of such ice. Such has encouraged us to “update” our belief about the impact of ice on wings, which is why that 20-minute delay we are so impatient about as travelers (while de-icing equipment clears our wings) could be saving our lives. The 20-minute delay is a “good thing” and has been informed by prior deaths and tragedies, that is, *experience*.

## 2.7 Marginal vs. Conditional Distributions

As we have seen, the concept of conditional probabilities is vitally important to both statistics and science. However, we have also discussed that conditional probabilities are often applied to statistical *models*, not individual probabilities. With these two elements, we can generalize concepts 70of conditional probabilities to that of *joint* and *conditional distributions*. Further, for a continuous distribution, the marginal *probability density function* (PDF) over all possible values of `y` can be obtained from the joint PDF by integrating out all other `p` variables that may be present and summing (via integration) over all possible values of `y`,

`f(x) = ∫[-∞]^( +∞) f(x,y) dy`

(2.13)

The PDF integrates to 1, that is,

`f(x) = ∫[-∞]^( +∞) f(x,y) dy = 1`

(2.14)

In the discrete case, we can simply visualize summing up or down a 2 × 2 table across *categories* of the second variable. As was the simpler case for the rule of independence for individual probabilities, random variables are *independent* if the joint PDF is equal to the product of marginal PDFs. If the joint PDF is not equal to the product of marginal PDFs, then the two random variables are said to be *dependent*.

## 2.8 Independence Implies Covariance of Zero, but Covariance of Zero Does Not (Necessarily) Imply Independence

As we will discuss in more detail later, the covariance between variables is a measure of the extent to which the variance of one variable is *linearly* associated with the variance of another. The covariance is given by

$$Cov\_{xy} = \\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( x\_{i} - \\overline{x} \\right.} \\right)\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)}{n}$$

(2.15)

For any two variables that are statistically independent, this necessarily implies a covariance equal to 0. However, the converse is not necessarily true. That is, a covariance equal to 0 does not necessarily imply that two events are independent. They may turn out to be independent, but the covariance equal to 0 is not enough to imply it. This is because the relationship between the two variables may simply be one that is *nonlinear*. *The covariance between two variables is a measure of linear association or relationship only*. Hence, two variables may be strongly dependent or related to one another, yet still have a covariance (and hence, also *correlation*, though as we will see later the two are not equivalent) equal to 0. This is one reason why interpreting a measure of covariance or correlation should never be done without also inspecting a scatterplot of the association. *Always plot your data!* This is worth repeating:

> **Always plot your data!**

Numerical summaries are never enough.
