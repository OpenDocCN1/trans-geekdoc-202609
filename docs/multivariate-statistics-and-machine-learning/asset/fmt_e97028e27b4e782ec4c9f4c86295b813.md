## 10.4 Committee Machines

As mentioned at the outset of this chapter, “committee machine” is a general term referring to combining weaker learners to generate a stronger learner. By far the most popular and useful of committee machines are those of *bagging* and *boosting*.

## 10.5 Overview of Bagging and Boosting

We have so far in this chapter discussed decision trees and have focused on their construction. We have emphasized that a single tree, especially if not pruned, likely significantly overfits the data. That is, it is easily subject to random perturbations in the data such that if we changed a few data points here or there, the outcome of the tree would be different. In statistical learning theory, as we know by now, this is referred to the method having *high variance*. Note carefully this is not the *data* having high variance. We are referring here to the *method*. The data are what they are. We are more interested in evaluating the *method* used to evaluate the data. Unstable methods are usually not preferred to more stable ones. Decision trees are not the only statistical method that is unstable, but in what follows, we will use them as an example in our discussion of bagging and boosting.

How can we reduce the variance of an otherwise unstable method? Though there are many ways of accomplishing this reduction in variance, two methods are often used: *bagging* and *boosting*; however, boosting prioritizes reducing *bias* over reducing variance. Bagging, which is short for *bootstrap aggregating*, is random and probabilistic. By essentially averaging bootstrapped samples, one could get different bagging results depending on the given sampling procedure. Boosting, on the other hand, is not random or probabilistic. It is instead *systematic* or *deterministic*. Boosting generates predictable non-random perturbations of the training data by successive reweighting of the training set, where the most current and updated weights depend on misclassification history. We survey boosting shortly. First, we more fully develop the idea of bagging.

### 10.5.1 Bagging

Bagging is essentially a procedure of taking many bootstrapped samples and then aggregating (averaging) them. By averaging results, the goal is to reduce variance. For example, as already discussed, in a typical decision tree, any *particular* tree likely suffers from high variance, which basically means that a given tree is subject to random fluctuations in the data. That is, if we perturbed the data somewhat, the resulting tree would be different. By averaging trees (or whatever method we are using), the hope is to reduce the overall variance. In fact, the success of bagging actually depends on how originally unstable the data are; *the more unstable, the more perturbing the data set causes a change in prediction, the more advantageous it is to bag to improve accuracy* ([Breiman, 1996](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000047)). Be sure to understand what we have just said. We emphasize that *the more unstable a learning method, the more it stands to benefit from aggregating*. If the learning method is stable, bagging will, in general, be less efficient. In fact, for relatively stable methods, bagging has been found to work less well and has been found to actually *degrade* the performance of stable procedures ([Breiman, 1996](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000047)).

### 10.5.2 A Familiar Example: Bagging Samples and the Variance of the Mean

The simplest conceptual example of bagging would be to designate a sample of say, 100 observations, bootstrap 10 samples, on each compute a sample mean, then average those means. The average of those averages $\\mu\_{\\overline{y}}$ will have less variance than any of the individual averages, $\\overline{y}$. *Averaging (in this case, of means) reduces variance*. If each of the subset samples has a variance equal to *σ*², then the variance of those means, as is well known, is equal to *σ*²/*n*, with standard error $\\sigma/\\sqrt{n}$.
