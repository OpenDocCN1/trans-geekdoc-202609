
In the statistics textbook market, there are numerous kinds of books for sale. Some are very technical books on the theory of statistics and their mathematical elements, the field of so-called *mathematical statistics*. These books usually feature virtually no scientific or software applications. Their emphasis is primarily on theoretical aspects of statistics and proving theorems. While at first glance these books might appear “deep,” they are often only deep *mathematically* and not in the development of *statistical ideas*, though a mathematical statistician would likely say the ideas are *in* the mathematics, which at that more advanced level is entirely correct. Software books on the other hand give minimal explanation and theory, assuming the reader already “knows what they are doing,” as it were, as it pertains to understanding the material. For example, those big bulky SAS manuals have traditionally been of this sort in that they “recap” the concept somewhat and then go on to show the reader how to implement a methodology in software. Lastly, the “cookbook” is even more incomplete in that such books simply tell the reader *what to do* via software instruction without telling them *why*. The most ideal of books at an introductory level is probably one that contains *some theory*, *some application*, and *some software*. The book you hold in your hands contains that mix.

### 0.4.2.1 Watered-Down Statistics
The study of statistics can be terrifying for most who are not aspiring quantitative specialists. It’s scary for the specialist as well, the only difference is that they have learned to push through the fear and swim among the sharks daily. All of this is for good reason, *statistics is difficult*, the complexity of which is only fully realized once one begins working with statistical formulae and applying them to data, or reading more advanced literature on statistics. As an example, I was recently asked by a researcher whether Cohen’s `d` values can be compared across data sets. That is, can we say one study with `d` equal to 0.6 had a larger effect than another study with `d` equal to 0.5? The answer is, as are so many answers in statistics, *yes and no* and more complex than first thought would suggest. A few perturbations of data points in one study and the effects could be reversed; a slight change in the standard deviation can have a rather substantial effect on the standardization of the mean difference. So, yes, they can be compared across data sets, *but not without a keen awareness of what went into each computation and knowledge of each data set*. Many answers are of this nature in statistics, such that the answer usually comes with *caveats*, sometimes many of them. For reasons such as this, when students actually embark on understanding statistics, it is rarely black and white, 8and gray zones can, well, make one feel as though they have bees in their heads. *The seemingly simple problem can become quite complex very fast*. *Understanding statistics and how it should and should not be applied and interpreted on data is hard*. Figuring out optimal experimental designs can also be exceedingly challenging.

So, what’s the solution? Make it easier of course! Anyone can write an “easy” statistics book, just as easily as one can *completely misrepresent the discipline and the inherent complexity of statistics and quantitative science more generally*. Books like that fly off the shelves and are sold mostly to individuals who do not want to study nor learn statistics in the first place and are merely looking for an *escape route*. Consequently, they learn “some” statistics, but at risk of it being terribly misunderstood. A little knowledge is a dangerous thing. Memorizing that the mean “is a measure of central tendency” with a formula showing the user how to compute it is dangerous. How so? For one, the *arithmetic mean* is only one of many *possible* means and it often doesn’t adequately indicate the center of the distribution! “Incomplete” statistics books (e.g., software and cookbook manuals) are fine for those who are already aware of the complexities of statistics because they can navigate around the shortcomings in such books, but they are nothing short of *malpractice* if used in teaching as *primary* (not *secondary*) sources for students coming to the discipline for the first time. Without unpacking and working with statistical formulae to truly understand what they reveal vs. what they do not, statistics teaching is more likely to *mislead* than it is to properly *educate*. Alleviating anxiety by teaching “easy statistics” is unethical as such teaching completely misrepresents the subject. *These students are not learning statistics. They are learning something else*. The “easy books” can certainly be useful in a course, but should never replace a *primary* and more rigorous resource.

## 0.4.3 Prerequisites to Reading This Book
The prerequisites to reading this book are modest. We assume the reader has had at least one introductory statistics course at the undergraduate level. Preferably, the student will have also had an introductory course at the college level in calculus and linear algebra, or at least some *exposure* to these areas. The introductory course in statistics is mandatory, while the calculus and linear algebra courses are optional but preferred. The necessary prerequisites are reviewed in [Chapter 2](#c2.xhtml#c2) and as needed throughout the book, but at a quicker pace than would be the case in a truly introductory course in these subjects. For instance, we may refresh the reader on why we are passing to the limit, but we assume the reader has had exposure to limits and derivatives before. Likewise with concepts from linear algebra, we discuss the *why* behind the concept and may re-introduce it briefly, but it would be beneficial if the reader has seen the concept before. From a generally applied perspective, it suffices that the reader has a sense of where the mathematics used “fit” in the bigger picture instead of presenting the mathematics as a sole topic as would be the case in an appendix or simply referring the reader to a book on pure linear algebra devoid of statistical application, for instance.
