
The reader will also likely remember being taught that converting to `z-scores` standardizes a distribution such to have a mean of zero and standard deviation equal to one. Perhaps such was the extent to which this concept was explored. If you look at the `z`-score more closely, however, what is it *exactly*? We see that it is a *ratio*, a ratio of `y` − `μ` to `σ`. But what is `y` − `μ`? It is a *deviation*, a deviation from the mean to be more precise. What is `σ`? It is another deviation, this one the *standard deviation*. Hence, we see the `z`-score is a comparison via a ratio of *two deviations*, one that you are computing based on `y - μ` to one that is “average” or “standard” in the data, that of `σ`. Hence, if the observed deviation is about as large as the average deviation, then `z` is equal to approximately 1. If it is much larger, then `z` will be much greater than 1. This is one interpretation regarding what a `z`-score is actually communicating.

As much as possible throughout the text, space permitting, we seek to *unpack* the meaning behind equations to appreciate what they are communicating. Equations and formulas are, after all, *concepts*, and concepts should be explored for all of their meaning.

### 0.5.5 The Power of Problems
It is often said that in learning any technical area such as statistics, working out problems is the way to mastery. Tackling problems is not the only way to learn, but it definitely helps focus energy on elements of what you are learning. One can read on a topic to no end, but until you come across problems that take time, energy, brainstorming, and experience to solve, the learning may remain latent. That is, it hasn’t consolidated yet, akin to preparing for a life event yet not truly knowing if you are truly prepared until the event actually arises. I have taught many students who brazenly believed they understood statistics, that is, until asked to explain even basic statistics at a dissertation defense. As is true with life in general, *reality is the true test of one’s knowledge*. “Training” will only get you so far, as every athlete knows.

Each chapter in the book contains a brief set of problems, most are open so that the student can think more globally about the concepts learned to see where it takes her. Most are of the “discussion type.” Intellectual exploration of ideas is encouraged throughout this book, and problem sets attempt to foster this activity. The selection of problems is relatively arbitrary and we encourage instructors to expand or customize existing problems to suit the needs and scope of the course they are teaching. In the author’s own teaching, problem sets are usually improvised or customized to each lecture or class, and we encourage instructors to do the same. An “open problem” can easily turn into a more rigorous closed-answer analytical one with just a few additional elements. The problems in the book should be considered nothing more than good “starting points” to *initiate discussion* and are relatively few in number given space considerations for the book. If the reader or instructor would like more targeted and specific problems of a technical or analytical nature, they should feel welcome in contacting the author who would be more than happy to provide problem sets to suit the needs of their specific course.

### 0.5.7 Notation Used in the Book
Half of the battle with reading a book on multivariate statistics or machine learning can be making out the notation used. Even if you are experienced in the area, you can spend a great deal of your time trying to figure out how the author is using this or that notation. What a waste of time! We avoid that as much as possible in this book. Mathematics is a beautiful subject in its own right and to those who dislike it, it is nonetheless a necessary evil to understanding multivariate and machine learning, but *abuse* of mathematics is all too rampant; otherwise great texts ruined because the author does not explain what the mathematics mean or the symbols used in describing such mathematics. We try to avoid those pitfalls the best we can in this book, though we no doubt do so imperfectly.

Our use of subscripts is purposely non-consistent throughout the text. At times, we subscript *variables*, whereas other times we do not. To use a simple example of this approach, a `z`-score can be defined as

$$z = \\frac{y - \\mu}{\\sigma}$$

or like this,

$$z = \\frac{y\_{i} - \\mu}{\\sigma}$$

where `y` is now purposely indicated as a variable, `y[i]`. Both `y` and `y[i]` denote a variable in this context, and both forms are acceptable for this and other statistics and equations featured throughout the text. As well, the transpose of a matrix `A` can be denoted `A^T` or just as well simply as `A′`. In places using `A^T` seemed more “natural” than `A′`, whereas in other places `A′` was preferred as it is a bit less cumbersome. Random variables versus *realizations* of those random variables is also not always consistent. For example, formally, for a given random variable `X`, a realization of that random variable could be notated as `x[i]`, such that `X = x[i]`, however using uppercase throughout the text we found would be too cumbersome and overall preferred the smaller notation `x` or `x[i]` even when potentially referring to a random variable. Lastly, in a regression context, `βx` or `βx[i]` was, in general, deemed preferable to `βX` simply because again we favored the smaller `x` notation regardless of whether or not `x` was considered fixed or random.
