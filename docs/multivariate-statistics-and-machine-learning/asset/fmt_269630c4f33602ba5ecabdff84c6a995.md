
First, we need to make a distinction between *structure* vs. *model*. The term “multilevel” is a *structure*, it is not actually a “model,” though it is referred to as such in the literature (i.e., *multilevel modeling*), for reasons that will become clear shortly. It is best to reserve the word “multilevel” to describe structure, though in what follows we ourselves at times adopt the conventional phrase “multilevel modeling” as well and make no distinction. So long as one understands and appreciates the distinction, no harm is done. However, to the newcomer to multilevel models, a great deal of confusion can arise from not understanding what a *structure* is compared to a *model*. As we will see, multilevel structures can be analyzed in a host of different ways, only one of which is via random and mixed effects.

Second, *a multilevel structure need not necessarily imply a mixed model, nor does a multilevel structure need to incorporate any random effects*. They *usually* do include such random effects, but the definition of what is a multilevel (structure) does not absolutely require it. In reading the literature, however, the newcomer to these models is rarely made aware of this. Multilevel models are automatically assumed to consist of random effects. This implied association is not a necessary one. *As we will see, for a multilevel model to have one or more random effects (other than the naturally occurring error terms), this must be assumed or imposed by the researcher*. *No multilevel structure, by itself, necessarily implies or must otherwise contain random effects*.

As an example, consider the classic case once more of ten students per classroom in which there are five classrooms. Students are nested within classrooms, as students in one classroom will likely share features or common experiences over students in another classroom. They will be more *similar* than students in a competing classroom. Statistically, error terms of students within classroom will not be independent of one another. *We can assume their errors will be correlated*. In practice, if we desired, we could account for the multilevel *structure* by calculating separate regressions on each classroom. We could then compare the regressions by way of computing a product term that incorporates classroom. Notice that there is no random effect implied here and yet we are dealing with the multilevel structure just the same. *The multilevel structure is still intact without requiring any random effects*. It may not be the most “ideal” of solutions in modeling the multilevel structure, but the point is that addressing the multilevel structure does not *necessarily* imply, nor require, the modeling of random effects.

The purest or most accurate definition of a multilevel model often given or implied in the literature is that it *allows for residual analyses at each level of the hierarchy*. And while this is true, there is nothing in a multilevel *structure* that absolutely requires this level of analysis. The emphasis should be on “allows” in the previous phrase, not “necessitates.” Again, notice that we could quite easily analyze each regression or mean structure separately by classroom and assess residuals within each classroom. We do not *require* any random effects to do so. Including random effects, however, is often a very good idea, as we now discuss.

### 4.4.2 Why Do Multilevel Models Often Include Random Effects?

If a multilevel *structure* by itself does not necessarily imply the inclusion of random effects, how do random effects therefore come to rise in a multilevel model? The answer is that the researcher has to *impose* them on the multilevel structure through an assumption of the modeling. Take note again that *we are imposing the random effect(s); they are not necessarily implied in the multilevel structure*. Again, and referring to our previous example, *observed variance in intercepts or slopes in exploratory analysis of data cannot serve as justification alone to model such as random effects*.

To emphasize this important point, consider an excellent description of multilevel modeling by one of the chief leaders in this area:206

> *Given that the intercept and slope coefficients vary randomly over group …*

The above is completely correct, but what does it mean? The key point to recognize from the above is that it is *us* who decides whether such coefficients “vary randomly” or not. The fact that there is *observed variation* in them is not enough. We have to decide to model the intercept and slope as fixed or random based on the *assumptions* we are imposing on the model. If there is no observed variation, we probably would not model them as random effects, but in theory, we still could.

Consider another typical presentation of the multilevel model:

> *Because multilevel models contain a mix of fixed effects and random effects, they are sometimes known as mixed-effects models.*

The problem with the above is that, as mentioned, not only do multilevel structures *not* necessarily require the inclusion of random effects, but that even if they did, they do not *need* to be modeled using a *traditional* mixed-effects modeling approach. A structural equation modeling approach, for instance, could be just as fruitful. Once more, *a multilevel structure does not necessarily imply a fixed, random, or mixed model*.

In summary then, the intercepts and slopes typically found in hierarchical (or “nested” designs) do not know where they “came from.” We have to make those decisions as part of the assumptions we are imposing on our analysis. In writings about multilevel models, authors too often imply that because there is observed variation in intercepts and slopes, it necessarily implies the corresponding effect should be modeled as a random effect. This is simply not the case. Observed variation in statistics, whatever they may be (e.g., intercepts, slopes, means, standard deviations, etc.), is insufficient reason for modeling such as random effects. The decision to model as a random effect has all to do with how the observed realizations are *conceptualized to have originated and how such statistics would likely behave in theoretical repetitions of the experiment*. These are decisions made by the investigator.

> *A multilevel structure does not necessarily imply the presence of random effects. Random effects arise in such structures because the researcher decides to model them (intercepts and slopes) as such, but a multilevel structure need not necessarily imply the inclusion of these effects. Intercepts and slopes could, in theory, even when exhibiting much variation, be estimated as fixed effects.*
