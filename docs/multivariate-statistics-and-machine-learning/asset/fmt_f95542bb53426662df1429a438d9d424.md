
### 5.10.1 Classic Mediation

*Figure 5.3* Classic mediation model where “`M`” is a mediator of the relationship between `X` and `Y`.[Return to text.⏎](#c5.xhtml#re_f5.3)

The conditions for “classic” or traditional mediation typically include the following ([MacKinnon et al., 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000232)):

1. `X` should bear a relationship to `Y`.
2. `X` should bear a relationship to `M`.
3. `M` should be related to `Y` when both `X` and `M` are predictors of `Y`.
4. The relationship of `X` to `Y` should *diminish* (in absolute value) when `M` is included in the model, presumably as a result of `M` “taking up” the correlation that would have otherwise been “transmitted” (in necessarily some *statistical* sense only) from `X` to `Y`.243

A more complex combination of mediator models is illustrated in [Figure 5.4](#c5.xhtml#f5.4) where we now have three mediators, `z[1]`, `z[2]`, and `z[3]`, and where `ε[1]`, `ε[2]`, and `ε[3]` are errors.

While including more than a single mediator may seem initially a good idea, it comes with many caveats. The models are only as valid as the assumptions imposed on those models. Indeed, as again noted by Pearl ([2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000283), pp. 2–3), whom we paraphrase:

> The validity of these two methods depends of course on the *assumption* \[added emphasis\] that the error terms … are uncorrelated, otherwise, some of the structural parameters would produce biased results. In randomized trials, where error can be identified with the randomization device, we are assured that error is uncorrelated with other errors and so the regressional estimates will be unbiased … However, randomization does not remove error from `e2` and `e3`.

There is no end to how complex we can formulate a mediation model. For example, [Figure 5.5](#c5.xhtml#f5.5) features a mediation model with a confounder that predicts the treatment, mediator, and outcome. Other than the confounder, the model is a classic three-variable mediation model.

Other than for rather simple mediation models, models such as these are often best analyzed as *structural equation models*, and hence we will revisit them to some degree later in the book when we survey SEM models in more detail. Estimation in mediation models includes the traditional *coefficient product estimator* ([Hayes, 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000147)). Simulation-based estimators have also been proposed as well as semi-parametric estimators such as the *inverse probability weighting estimator* ([Zhang, 2442023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000403)). The *multiply-robust estimator*, as well as the *double-machine learning for mediation estimator*, have also been proposed. For details, see [Abécassis et al. (2023)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000002).

### 5.10.2 Statistical Moderation

As mentioned at the outset of our discussion of mediation and moderation, moderation analysis is nothing more than analyzing interactions. That is, quite literally, “moderation” is a fancy word for statistical *interaction*. When you conduct an analysis of variance and model the interaction, you are performing a moderation analysis. However, there is so much “hype” about moderation analyses in fields such as psychology and others that it is often advertised as being able to accomplish so much more than it can. Again, to reiterate, nowhere in the *statistical* procedure of moderation analysis is anything necessarily “moderating” anything else in a *physical* or *substantive* sense unless the variables you are working with and your theory suggest such a “physical” process. *Statistical moderation alone does not afford that process in any way whatsoever*. In fact, in many cases, statistical moderation can be rather anticlimactic.

As a basic example of moderation, consider the plot in [Figure 5.6](#c5.xhtml#f5.6).

![Figure 5.6 Illustration of moderation for two groups; slopes are nonparallel.Return to text.⏎](images/fig5.6.jpg)

*Figure 5.6* Illustration of moderation for two groups; slopes are nonparallel.[Return to text.⏎](#c5.xhtml#re_f5.6)

As a result of the slopes being different (*nonparallel*), we have a moderation effect. Of course, in the *sample*, there will virtually *always be a moderation effect*. Do you really think slopes could ever be *exactly* parallel *in the sample*? The real question is whether such an effect can be inferred toward the population, which is the inferential question we are ultimately interested in. We will survey an example of moderation later in the chapter using software.

### 5.10.3 Moderated Mediation

Just as in the test for any other interaction effect, one may be interested in learning whether an entire mediation model holds across values of a moderating variable. More specifically, we may be interested in learning whether an *indirect effect* (i.e., the effect of going “through” the mediator, so to speak) is conditional on values of a moderating variable. This is referred to as *moderated mediation* and more generally is found under the name of *conditional process models* ([Hayes, 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000147)), or again, the search for *conditional indirect effects*. That is, the indirect (or “mediating”) effect is conditional upon some other third variable, illustrated in [Figure 5.7](#c5.xhtml#f5.7).245

![Figure 5.7 Three-variable mediation model conditional upon w.Return to text.⏎](images/fig5.7.jpg)

*Figure 5.7* Three-variable mediation model conditional upon `w`.[Return to text.⏎](#c5.xhtml#re_f5.7)

We see in [Figure 5.7](#c5.xhtml#f5.7) the classic three-variable mediation model, however conditional upon the `w` variable as indicated by the `x*w` interaction. Note that both `w` and the interaction term `x*w` are included due to the *principle of marginality* ([Fox, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106)) which holds that an interaction term should not be included without also including the individual terms that make up the interaction, which in this case is simply the `w` variable.

A *mediated moderation* model is when an interaction between an independent variable and a moderator variable “affects” (statistically) a mediator variable that in turn “affects” (again, only necessarily *statistically*) the outcome variable. According to Hayes ([2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000147), p. 465), “mediated moderation is neither interesting nor meaningful” and ends up being mathematically equivalent under most conditions to moderated mediation. As such, Hayes recommends that questions of mediated moderation should be recast as *moderated mediational hypotheses*.
