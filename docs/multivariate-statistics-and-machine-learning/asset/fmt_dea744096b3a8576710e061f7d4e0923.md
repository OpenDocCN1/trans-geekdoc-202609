In practice, it will often be the negative of the log-likelihood that is *minimized* instead of the log being maximized,

All of the above is quite general. For instance, we can unpack these elements for the multivariate situation specifically in which we desire maximum likelihood estimators. Hence, suppose we seek maximum likelihood estimates of `μ`, the multivariate mean, and `Σ`, the covariance matrix, that will maximize the likelihood of the data. In the multivariate setting, the aforementioned estimates are found by

$$\\hat{\\mu} = \\overline{y}$$

for the multivariate mean vector, and98

$$\\left. \\hat{\\Sigma} = \\frac{1}{n}{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right.} \\right)\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)'$$

which can also be given by $\\frac{n - 1}{n}S$, where `S` is the sample covariance matrix. For justification for these particular maximum likelihood estimators, see Rencher and Christensen ([2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295), p. 99). Maximum likelihood requires us to select the parameters that *maximize* the joint density evaluated at the observations ([Johnson & Wichern, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173)). The maximizing parameter values, as before, are called *maximum likelihood estimates*. That is, the maximum likelihood estimates of `μ` and `Σ` are those values, `μ̂` and `Σ̂` that maximize the likelihood function `L`(`μ`,`Σ`). In practice, as in the univariate case, often the negative of the log of the likelihood is computed. As mentioned, maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing error ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)).

### 2.27.1 Why ML Is So Popular and Alternatives

Maximum likelihood estimators possess many favorable properties. It is the default estimation for many if not most statistical and machine learning problems. For instance, it is commonly used in neural networks (Goodfellow et al., [2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)).

Despite its popularity, maximum likelihood is by no means the *only* method of estimation in statistical modeling and machine learning. Numerous other methods have been advanced to accommodate data situations for which assuming multivariate normality is unrealistic. For instance, *quasi-maximum likelihood* (`QML`) is a method of estimation advanced in structural equation modeling featuring nonlinear terms such as quadratic effects ([Klein & Muthén, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000188)). *Two-stage least-squares* estimation for nonlinear SEM models has also been advanced ([Holst & Budtz-Jorgensen, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000156)). *Adaptive Gaussian Quadrature* (`AGQ`) has also been proposed for estimating nonlinearity in SEM models. The *method of moments* is an alternative approach to ML estimation when the solution is either unavailable or computationally difficult ([Murray, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). *Online recursive estimation* is yet another possibility (Murray, 2022). All of these estimation methods are complex and discussions of them could fill an entire chapter or even book.

The key takeaway from this discussion is that when it comes to estimation methods, though maximum likelihood is probably the most popular method, there are numerous alternative methods which are already in existence and others which are being developed in statistics and machine learning. Hence, when one comes across the term “estimation,” one should be curious to learn what *type* of estimation is being referred to as it will not always be maximum likelihood.

### 2.27.2 Estimation and Confidence Intervals

In classical statistics, we can either attempt to estimate a particular point in a distribution, such as the arithmetic mean, or we can attempt instead to estimate an *interval* of values. Theoretical statistics tells us that the expected value of the sample mean, $\\overline{y}$, is equal to the population mean, `μ`. That is, $E\\left( \\overline{y} \\right) = \\mu$. However, we also know that the probability of “hitting” the precise value of the population mean has a limiting value of zero. Hence, we seek usually to build an *interval* of values around the sample estimator and instead of seeking *point predictions*, we will use an *interval estimator*, the most common of which is the confidence interval.

The *confidence for the mean* is given by

$$\\overline{y} \\pm z\_{\\alpha/2}\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}$$

(2.58)

where `z`[`α`/2] is the critical value of `z` given the desired accuracy for the confidence interval. For a 95% interval, that value is 1.96, and so we have99

$$\\overline{y} \\pm 1.96\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}$$

Recall that seldom do we actually know the value of `σ` and must instead estimate it based on sample data. When we use the sample standard deviation `s` as an estimator of the population standard deviation `σ`, the mean is no longer distributed as `z`, but instead is distributed as a `t`\-statistic, and hence the confidence interval becomes:

$$\\overline{y} \\pm t\_{\\alpha/2}\\frac{s}{\\sqrt{n}}$$

(2.59)

Confidence intervals are by no means limited to means. Interval estimators can be used for a variety of statistics. In many cases, analytical solutions tell us how to construct the standard errors for such intervals. In other cases, standard errors are intractable and difficult or even impossible to calculate. When we cannot compute standard errors directly, one option is to *bootstrap* samples of observations and determine interval bounds via repeated sampling. We discuss the bootstrap shortly.

Be sure to note that as was true for the point estimator, an interval estimator is the random variable. The parameter that is being estimated is not the random variable. While there are a few different ways of interpreting confidence intervals in practice, the most correct way is to refer to the *procedure* for computing such intervals of which one interval has been calculated. Hence, the most correct interpretation of a computed 95% interval on data becomes

> *The probability that the given interval, based on the procedure used to compute the interval, covers the parameter, is 0.95. Repeating the procedure of calculating intervals this way would result in 95% of them covering the true parameter.*

There is a whole literature on the correct interpretation of confidence intervals and how researchers presumably always get it wrong. Yes, formality matters here, but the above is not the only way a confidence interval can be conceptualized, especially in a real research setting. The above interpretation is rather awkward when it comes to interpreting real research, but is arguably near the most theoretically correct interpretation. For a brief discussion on this point and a brief argument for why competing ways of interpreting confidence intervals may be useful in practice, consult [Denis (2020)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000086).

## 2.28 The Bootstrap: A Way of Estimating Nonparametrically

Perhaps more than any other statistical technique, the bootstrap is the one that has benefited the most from the computer age revolution and the speed and facility that such computation provides. Though theoretically the bootstrap would still be possible without computers, it would be so laborious and impractical computationally that it is fair to say the technique would have likely forever been abandoned and have never risen to fame. Thanks to computation, however, the bootstrap has, in the world of statistics and machine learning, become a “household name.”
