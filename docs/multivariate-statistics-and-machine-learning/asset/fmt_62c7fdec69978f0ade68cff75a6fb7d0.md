
where `θ̂[g]` is the maximum-likelihood estimate of `θ[g]`, `v[g]` is the number of estimated parameters associated with the model with `g` components ([Baudry et al., 2010](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000024)), and `n` is the sample size. Note that in this case, greater values for `BIC` are preferred over smaller values as the goal is to maximize the first part of the equation, log `p(x|g,θ̂[g])`. The penalty or correction factor is in the second term, $\\frac{v\_{g}}{2}\\log\\left( n \\right)$, which includes the number of parameters `v[g]` required to obtain the given maximum for given `g` components. To the extent that more parameters `v[g]` are required over less, $\\frac{v\_{g}}{2}\\log\\left( n \\right)$ will be increasingly large and hence we are subtracting an increasingly larger number from log `p(x|g,θ̂[g])`. Hence, as usual with `BIC`, more parsimonious models are typically preferred. Cut-off criteria here are generally discouraged and what denotes a “good” clustering solution will depend on the research area and user judgment. Note as well that there are different versions of `BIC` and related model selection statistics for mixed models in cluster analysis. What we have presented here is 528only one possibility. It is good practice to always verify how the given author or software is defining the particular model selection statistic featured before interpreting it.

The package `mclust` (Scrucca et al., [2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000331)) can perform mixture modeling in `R`.

## 14.13 Cluster Validation

Having surveyed the most essential algorithms and approaches to cluster analysis which have included `k-means`, hierarchical, density-based, and mixture models, we close out this chapter with a brief discussion and recap of the cluster validation problem and revisit criteria for choosing the number of clusters.

In general, there are two types of validation one may perform on a cluster solution: 1) internal validation criteria, and 2) external validation criteria. The most preferred of these is, of course, external validation, since externally validating a cluster solution puts us back in touch with the empirical world akin to cross-validation of a model on test data helps us know how the model or algorithm will perform on data it has not yet seen. Internal validation approaches can be useful however when external validation criteria are not available or otherwise difficult to come by.

Internal methods of validation include sums of square distances to centroids, which is, as the name suggests, a measure of variability of cases from their corresponding centroids. A better than not cluster solution generally has a smaller sums of squares distance than a poorer solution. This measure can be used with `k-means` but not with density-based clustering such as `DBSCAN`. A ratio of the intracluster distance (distance within) to the intercluster distance (distance between) can also be computed ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005), p. 196) where smaller values than not are suggestive of a better solution. Other measures of internal validity include the silhouette coefficient as well as probabilistic measures. See Aggarwal ([2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005), pp. 196–197) for details. Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie ([2001](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000359)) have also proposed the aforementioned “gap” statistic which in some cases can be useful as well. The gap essentially compares total within-cluster variation for a given cluster solution (e.g., `k`-cluster solution in `k-means`) to what would be expected under a null distribution. An increasingly large gap is thus indicative of a more refined cluster solution.

For mixture models, as we have seen, log-likelihood statistics can be computed as well as `BIC` for helping determine the number of components to the mixture model. It should be noted that mixture models are of the few clustering methodologies that generally allow for significance tests to be performed since they are probabilistic. A device called the clustergram has also been proposed (see [Fleischmann, 2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000104) for details).

External methods of cluster validation are most useful when knowledge of the actual clusters, sometimes called the ground truth, is known a priori or beforehand to performing the cluster analysis (such as in the case of clustering the iris data). The benefit of using external methods of validation over internal ones is that they are not biased by the particular algorithm doing the clustering ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)). To externally validate a cluster solution, something as simple as a confusion matrix, as the reader will recall from discriminant analysis, can be generated to learn how well the clustering solution “mimics” reality, or ground truth. While simple visualization of smaller confusion matrices may be sufficient, for especially larger confusion matrices measures of cluster purity can be computed as we did earlier when we clustered the iris data. As might be suspected, while these and other measures are useful, final decisions on the number of clusters usually come down to substantive interpretation and should be highly influenced by how pragmatic the cluster solution proves to be given the research or scientific context.529

## 14.14 Cluster Analysis and Beyond

In this chapter we surveyed the most common and foundational techniques of cluster analysis and data segmentation. These have included techniques such as `k-means` and other `k`-representatives, hierarchical and agglomerative approaches, density-based methods such as `DBSCAN` and mixture models. The reader should be aware that the current chapter has literally only scratched the surface when it comes to the literature on cluster analysis. New clustering techniques are continuously being developed in the statistical and machine learning literatures. However, as is the case with other methods surveyed in this book, if the reader is strong on the foundations of the techniques surveyed here, they will be in a reasonable position to generalize that knowledge to future techniques as they come across them.

Where to go from here? The extensions of the methods surveyed are almost limitless. The `k-means` clustering approach in this chapter can be extended to the kernel `k-means` algorithm. The `DENCLUE` algorithm is based on kernel-density estimation ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)). Ensemble methods can also be used in choosing a cluster solution, since different methods may provide conflicting solutions but combining them may paint a clearer picture. `K-means` is used in vector quantization related to data compression, often used in the analysis of images (see [Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032), p. 428 for details).

Cluster analysis can also be applied to categorical data, but with more difficulty; `k-modes` clustering is one option for categorical analysis. A hierarchical algorithm that has been found useful for categorical data is that of `ROCK`, which stands for Robust Clustering Algorithm for Categorical Attributes. See [Aggarwal (2015)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005) for details. Semi-supervised methods of cluster analysis are also available.

Other options for cluster analysis include spectral clustering which features an eigen analysis of a similarity matrix ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)) as well as biclustering which involves clustering rows or columns of a data matrix. See Murray (2022, p. 731) for details.
