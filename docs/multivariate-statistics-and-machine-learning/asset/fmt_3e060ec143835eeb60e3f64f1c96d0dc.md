
## 11.4 Probabilistic PCA
The principal axes of probabilistic PCA can be derived as maximum-likelihood parameter estimates computed by the usual eigen-decomposition approach. There is no closed-form solution and hence parameter estimates for `W` and `σ²I` must be obtained iteratively ([Tipping & Bishop, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000361)). We can also approach the construction of components via an iterative, computationally efficient expectation-maximization approach. For details, see [Murphy (2022)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259). For an excellent introduction to the probabilistic PCA model from the ground up starting with classic non-probabilistic PCA, see [Tipping and Bishop (1999)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000361) who provide a very good overview.

### 11.4.1 Motivation for Probabilistic PCA
Why seek out a probabilistic approach to PCA in the first place? Why add a noise term similar to how factor analysis includes an error term? There are at least a couple benefits to doing so over the simpler and more conventional PCA approach:
1. Using a probability model allows us to extend the scope of conventional PCA and may also be useful in cases of missing data. It also allows for a comparison with other probabilistic approaches which dominate statistics, facilitating statistical testing and allowing for the application of Bayesian approaches ([Tipping & Bishop, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000361)).415
2. Probabilistic PCA can be utilized as a constrained Gaussian density model ([Tipping & Bishop, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000361)). This allows for the efficient computation of maximum-likelihood estimates directly from the data principal components.

## 11.5 PCA for Discrete, Binary, and Categorical Data
Up to now, we have assumed that the data subjected to PCA is numerical and relatively continuous in nature. This is fine, so long as the data in one’s study adheres to this nature. However, oftentimes data come in a variety of forms that are hardly continuous. For example, binary (e.g., yes vs. no) data are common in polling, and categorical data (e.g., A, B, C) are quite popular as well.

Can PCA be used for data of different types? According to Jolliffe ([2002, p. 339](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000174)), the answer is “yes”:
> When PCA is used as a descriptive technique, there is no reason for the variables in the analysis to be of any particular type … the variables could be a mixture of continuous, ordinal or even binary (0/1) variables. It is true that variances, covariances and correlations have especial relevance for multivariate normal data `x`, and that linear functions of binary variables are less readily interpretable than linear functions of continuous variables. However, the basic objective of PCA – to summarize most of the “variation” that is present in the original set of `p` variables using a smaller number of derived variables – can be achieved regardless of the nature of the original variables.

While PCA can be conducted on a mixture of variables, *multiple correspondence analysis* (MCA) is usually the preferred technique for dimension reduction using categorical data, as PCA is most often used for dimension reduction featuring numerical measurements. [Kalantan and Alqahtani (2019)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000179) also feature a method of PCA for mixed data. Murphy ([2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259), p. 670) gives the case of PCA for categorical data.418
