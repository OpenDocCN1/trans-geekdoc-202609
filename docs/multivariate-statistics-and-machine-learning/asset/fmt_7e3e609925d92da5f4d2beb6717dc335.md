# 1 First Principles and Philosophical Foundations
DOI: [10.4324/9781003376941-3](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003376941-3)
In this first chapter, we introduce and survey “big picture” topics that are pertinent to the fields and evolution of statistics and machine learning. These areas of study, just as is true of virtually all areas of inquiry, rest on deep *philosophical assumptions*. To get “deep” is to get philosophical. To the uninformed, the word “philosophy” might bring to mind someone casually questioning the meaning of life or similar pursuit whether in a university classroom or on a Sunday afternoon hammock. Even worse, to the naïve the word might bring to mind something one does if one is determined to not gain employment! To the educated, however, philosophical foundations are everything and are at the very core of virtually all knowledge. *Anything studied enough, questioned enough, critically evaluated enough, will find its way to philosophical assumptions and foundations, into “mushy” territory where distinctions are made not in terms of symbols such as in mathematics, but in pre-symbolic forms in terms of “ideas” that often cannot be easily operationalized. Transforming the “mushy” into “rigorous” is, in many cases, brutally difficult*.
## 1.1 Science, Statistics, Machine Learning, and Artificial Intelligence
Attempting to distinguish between what exactly *statistics*, *machine learning*, and *artificial intelligence* are can seem like a trivial waste of time for knowledgeable researchers. However, for the student, a comment on these distinctions, if there are any, is very important as they are the ones coming to these fields for the first time and do not have the luxury of seeing things in the perspective of a rearview mirror to be able to note which distinctions are important and which are indeed, trivial; that is, which features are overlapping versus which are unique to their respective areas.
Historically, numerous attempts have been made to define just what *is* artificial intelligence, and none of them are truly satisfactory. None can be said to have truly “captured” the concept, in large part because whenever one tries to define something so precisely one usually misses the mark, akin to trying to get a hold of a single observation on a continuous scale – *the more you zero in on it, the more elusive it appears*. Hence, as is true of most things, artificial intelligence is a *concept* that cannot be truly captured by precise definitions and vague language. Even defining precisely what mathematics is can take up a dissertation! The best one can do is get a general *sense* of what it is and what it is not and go from there. Still, we may be able to settle on the following imperfect definition:
> *Artificial intelligence is the science of machines, usually computers, carrying out tasks that would typically require something called “intelligence” if the same task had been performed by a human being.*21
Artificial intelligence (AI) can hardly be considered a “science” in the spirit of laboratory science. Proponents of *weak AI* hold that a machine is intelligent if we can get it to carry out sophisticated tasks. Proponents of *strong AI* hold that artificial intelligence is the ability of machines to “mimic” human intelligence. Of course, strong AI may not even be achievable, but weak AI is possibly so, especially with the rise of machine learning.
*Machine learning* is often defined as the ability of computers to learn from data without having to be explicitly programmed. Machine learning is related to artificial intelligence because an intelligent system should, at minimum, be able to adapt to changes in its environment, which is precisely what machine learning algorithms purport to do. While machine learning is largely associated with computer science, much of its history can also be found in cybernetics and control science. More recently, the origins of machine learning are often associated with the psychologist Frank Rosenblatt who essentially conceived of a learning machine that we would today call a basic *neural network* ([Fradkov, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000107)). It was work on the backpropagation algorithm in the 1980s that truly initiated modern interest in neural networks more specifically and when eventually combined with the cheap availability of parallel computing and memory, the stage was set for the “gold rush” of machine learning in the 2000s ([Fradkov, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000107)).
Where does *statistics* fit into all this? How closely is machine learning related to statistics? The following are a couple of quotes from experts in the area that highlight the closeness of statistics and machine learning:
> Machine learning is essentially a form of applied statistics with increased emphasis on the use of computers to statistically estimate complicated functions and a decreased emphasis on proving confidence intervals around these functions.
>
> ([Goodfellow et al., 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131), p. 95)
> If the statistics field had incorporated computing methodology from its inception as a fundamental tool, as opposed to simply a convenient way to apply our existing tools, many of the other data related fields \[such as machine learning (ML)\] would not have needed to exist – they would have been part of statistics.
>
> ([Friedman, 1997](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000110), p. 27)
