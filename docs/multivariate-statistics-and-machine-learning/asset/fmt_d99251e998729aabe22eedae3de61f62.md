
Where a random variable “is going” is often of theoretical interest. That is, we often would like to know what value a random variable *converges to* in the limit. As sample size increases without bound to infinity, that is, `n→∞`, we would like to know properties of this convergence.

We say a sequence of random variables *converges in probability* to a random variable `x` if:

$$\\lim\\limits\_{n\\rightarrow\\infty}p\\left\\lbrack \\middle| x\_{n} - x \\middle| \\geq \\varepsilon \\right\\rbrack = 0$$

(2.19)

In passing, note we have used the word *infinity* above, and do so as well in other places in the book. And while it seems convenient to use it and the spirit of what is meant by statements such as `n→∞` is quite easily understood, the concept of infinity itself is quite rich historically and comes with it many philosophical difficulties and challenges, many of which are still ongoing today. Our inclusion of it in no way is meant to trivialize the depth of the object. Indeed, a dissertation with the title *The History and Theory of Infinity in Philosophy and Mathematics* would be no easy undertaking! Some hold that concepts of infinity, coupled with abstraction and proof, open the gateway to advanced mathematics ([Stillwell, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000349)). Make no mistake as well, by our mention of *philosophy of mathematics*, we are in no way meaning to communicate anything less than extremely rigorous. An in-depth study of infinity would not only require deeper mathematics, but also deeper philosophy. Whether such philosophy utilizes symbols (as does mathematics) has no bearing on the depth required to investigate such a topic.

## 2.14 Probability Density Functions

A *probability density function* is a function `f` that is associated with a continuous random variable `x` and that integrates to one:78

`p(a≤x≤b)=∫[a]^(b)f(x)dx=1`

The probability of any particular value in a *continuous probability density* has a limiting value equal to 0. That is, `p(x=x[i])=0`. Continuity is defined very precisely and rigorously in mathematics using epsilon-delta definitions; however, intuitively, continuity can be thought of as drawing a curve without lifting one’s pencil from the page. Because it is a probability density function, its CDF is also regarded as *absolutely continuous*, where absolute continuity implies, among other things, that we can integrate for all values of `a`, `b`. The distinction here between absolutely continuous vs. simply continuous has no bearing on the work we do in the current book. However, to the real analyst in mathematics, such a distinction is very important.

At first consideration, this may seem like an odd result. *How can any particular value in a continuous distribution have (limiting) probability equal to 0?* To make this more intuitive, consider the probability between any two particular values, `a` and `b`, to equal a number greater than 0. As the distance `a−b` decreases, the probability of that interval gets smaller and smaller, until in the limit the distance `a−b` is such a thin slice that the probability of that thin slice is, theoretically, equal to 0. Numerically, one can think of this as the impossibility of grabbing hold of a number with an infinite number of decimals; only an *interval* of numbers can be seized; any particular number in that interval is elusive. One can also liken the idea to that of *Zeno’s paradox*, in which as one approaches an object, the distances are continuously “halved” on route to that object, until one gets so close to that object that the distance between the person and the object has a limiting value of zero.

If the probability distribution is of a *discrete* rather than a *continuous* variable, then it usually goes by the name of a *probability mass function*. In such distributions, we can assign a positive probability of a given value of the random variable, `p(x=x[i])≠0`, unless of course the probability of a particular value of `x` is, in actuality, zero. The point, however, is that the probability of any particular value does not *need* to be zero as was the case for a continuous distribution. In a discrete distribution, we can assign probabilities to specific events. In a continuous distribution, we can only assign probabilities to *intervals*. That is, in a continuous density, the “events” are, in actuality, the interval “cuts” along the real line.

## 2.15 Normal (Gaussian) Distributions

Normal distributions and densities in statistics are everywhere. They are often called “Gaussian” because of their innovator, Friedrich Gauss. Not only do many physical phenomena approximate normality, but assuming normality theoretically provides a great deal of convenience in deriving new statistics and sampling distributions. Normality of a single variable is referred to as *univariate normality*, for two variables is *bivariate normality*, and for greater than two, *multivariate normality*. We survey each of these densities below.

### 2.15.1 Univariate Gaussian

The univariate normal density is given by

$$f\\left( x \\right) = \\frac{1}{\\sigma\\sqrt{2\\pi}}e^{- {(x - \\mu)}^{2}/2\\sigma^{2}}$$

(2.20)

There are two parameters in the univariate density, that of `μ` and `σ²`, where `x` is simply the “input” value and `π` and `e` are constants. The density (or crudely, “area”) under the normal distribution integrates to 1,

$$\\int\\limits\_{- \\infty}^{\\infty}{N\\left( x \\middle| \\mu,\\sigma^{2} \\right)dx = 1}$$

If we set `μ`=0 and `σ²`=1, the normal density is called the *standard normal density* and is the classic `z`-distribution that appears at the back of most introductory statistics texts and serves to reveal various areas (proportions) under the curve. Having a single “standard” distribution to refer to provides a single “scale” from which other distributions with different `μ` and `σ²` can be transformed via `z`-scores. When we compute a `z`-score,

$$z = \\frac{x - \\mu}{\\sigma}$$

(2.21)

we are transforming the given distribution to have a mean equal to 0 and standard deviation equal to 1 where `(x−μ)²/σ² = (x−μ)(σ²)^(−1)(x−μ)`. Hence, for a given `μ` and `σ²`, the “action” of the univariate density is a function of its exponent. If we take the reciprocal of `σ²`, that is, `1/σ²`, we obtain the *precision* of the distribution ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032), p. 24). As we will see later, writing the exponent in the way of `(x−μ)(σ²)^(−1)(x−μ)` will translate nicely in going from univariate to multivariate dimensions.
