
Given that many methods come down to dimensionality reduction, it is well worth noting at this point that dimensionality can be extraordinarily difficult to `tame`. Nonparametric methods, as we have discussed, often attempt to model data at a very `local` level. It would at first seem that these methods are well-suited for handling the dimensionality problem. However, as the number of dimensions increases, modeling local behavior becomes unwieldy. [Hastie et al. (2009)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145) describe the `curse of dimensionality` with reference to local regression, a technique that models the regression surface at a more local level than typically can be accomplished by ordinary least-squares:

> Local regression becomes less useful in dimensions much higher than two or three … It is impossible to simultaneously maintain localness (low bias) and a sizeable sample in the neighborhood (low variance) as the dimension increases, without the total sample size increasing exponentially … When the dimension to sample-size ratio is unfavorable, local regression 54does not help as much, unless we are willing to make some structural assumptions about the model.
> 
> (pp. 200–201)

To paraphrase, `locality` is a relative term and not simply a function of a distance metric. As we will revisit when we discuss `k`-nearest neighbors, as the dimensionality of the space we are working in grows, even to capture a relatively local neighborhood of that sizable dimensionality often involves covering a significant quantitative range of the variable(s) in the model. Considered another way, as dimensionality grows, it becomes virtually impossible to account for all possible “cells” in a multidimensional matrix, and hence the very definition of `locality` becomes less meaningful unless `sample size` grows substantially to account for the increasing volume of the space.

To use a `k`-nearest neighbor analogy that we will refer to when considering that method in more detail later in the book, if your `nearest neighbor` mathematically is a house on the planet Mars, such fails to convey the true spirit of locality. That is, it’s hard to argue a house on Mars is a neighbor of yours. It may be a neighbor `mathematically`, but such is only because of the huge dimensionality of the problem in which there is a necessarily insufficient sample size to account for all “cells” in the data from your house to the house on Mars. In other words, `that there is no house on the moon should not, by itself, make Mars, by default, a nearest neighbor`. Mars being a neighbor is more an artifact of the dimensionality of the space you are working in, not because Mars is truly a neighbor. Increasing sample size would eventually, and most likely, put a house on the moon, and the moon would be your neighbor now instead of Mars. In brief, as the dimensionality of the problem increases, that Mars may be your “nearest neighbor” becomes a much less meaningful statement than if we were to truly identify your nearest neighbors more `locally`, that is, in a smaller volume of space.

## 1.20 Distance

Perhaps even more rudimentary of a “unifier” of the methods and models of this book is that of `distance`. Every approach and method, whether in multivariate statistics or machine learning, in some way assesses distance between objects ([Johnson & Wichern, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173)). For instance, “patterns” and “clusters” can only be discerned if we are able to measure distance between observations. Which objects are similar? Which are different? One important point to keep in mind, however, is the following:

> `Similarity, measured by a distance function, does not necessarily imply “alikeness.”`

For example, a weight of 10 pounds is alike a height of 10 inches, but only in terms of measured distance, not in terms of substantive material. As is the case with most statistical procedures, we must be acutely aware of the distinction between numerical representations of data and the scientific nature of the material underlying the numbers. As William James once said, `we must not confuse data with the abstractions we use to analyze them`.

We discuss the concept of distance further in the following chapter on mathematical foundations, there highlighting the most common measure of distance, that of `Euclidean distance`.

## 1.21 Error Minimization

Error minimization is yet another unifying theme of most of the methods in this book. In all methods we seek to, in a sense, hit a nail straight on the head as much as possible and minimize error 55in doing so. This idea of minimizing error occurs in virtually every method, either implicitly or covertly behind the scenes.

As an example, consider once more principal components analysis. Though typically performed using eigen analysis or `SVD` (`singular value decomposition`) and reproducing all the original variance in the variables, the most sought-after result of a `PCA` is that of a single component accounting for all the variance. Hence, the target can be considered a single component. The error function is the extent to which we require other components to make reasonable sense of the data, or, depending on the application, to reproduce the given image via data compression if the substantive application is that of image compression. Likewise, in structural equation modeling we seek to reproduce the covariance matrix; the extent to which we are unable to `exactly` is the extent to which we are in error. `Minimizing error when imposing some kind of functional or near-functional form onto data is a goal of many statistical and machine learning methods`.

## 1.22 Training vs. Test Error

When we consider `f(x)`, this is regarded as the `true` unknown function. We typically estimate `f(x)` using `f̂(x)`, where `f̂(x)` is the `estimated function`. Do we care whether the function is a “black box” or something we can “unpack” and get to know better? If we do, then we would like to know exactly what `f(x)` is; if we do not, then black-box predictability may be of primary interest without actually getting to know the functional form. Perhaps `Amazon.com` simply wishes to know what product you will most likely buy next; it may not care `why`; it simply cares to know which one.

When we `train` a model, we fit it to sample data. However, as we will discuss later in the book, `training error` (i.e., lack of fit) is usually not of primary interest. Rather, we instead wish to know how well the model fits on data it has `not` yet seen before. This is known as `test error` and is usually the final verdict on how well a model fits, at least for the given instance of fitting it to sample data. A useful analogy is that of a boxer training for a fight; we care less about how the boxer does on sparring practice opponents and instead care more about how he or she does against a new fighter. “Fight night” is the `true test` of the boxer’s skill, not the training sessions in preparation for the fight.

## 1.23 Cross-Validation and Model Selection

Cross-validation is a major “theme” of statistical learning and machine learning that differentiates itself somewhat from “classical” statistics of the past. When we come across a research paper that applies a statistical method to data and reports a measure of fit, the magnitude of the fit statistic is usually `optimistic` because by fitting the model to the sample data we may have capitalized on random or chance factors. As we have already discussed, we may have `overfit` the model. That is, the `R-squared` reported of say, 0.80, may be much too high of a realistic assessment of model fit. Yes, it is accurate on the `training data`, but as we have emphasized, we are much more interested in how the model performs on the `test data`.
