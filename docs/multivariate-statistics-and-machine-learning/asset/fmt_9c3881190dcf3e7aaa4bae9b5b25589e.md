
# Multidimensional scaling

Multidimensional scaling ([Kruskal, 1964](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000203); [Torgerson, 1952](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000362), [1958](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000363)) is a nonparametric technique used for dimensionality reduction with the goal of preserving pairwise distances between observations. It is perhaps the simplest type of manifold learning (Murray, 2022). Recall that the problem of visualizing data in high dimensions is essentially to reduce the dimensionality and visualize resulting projections in those lower dimensions. In this sense, dimension reduction, as it pertains to visualization, is a way of answering the question:

> *What would this high-dimensional data look like if the points existed in a much lower dimension?*

This is the goal of many dimension-reduction techniques, to learn how high-dimensional data “live” or “exist” in lower dimensions. As an example, suppose we consider the distances between cities in miles. We then put these distances into a matrix and perform multidimensional scaling on that matrix. What we will obtain is the *coordinate map* in two dimensions with the city distances preserved as much as possible.

As summarized by [Chatfield and Collins (1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 189]:

> Multidimensional scaling is the term used to describe any procedure which starts with the “distances” between a set of points (or individuals or objects) or information about these “distances,” and finds a configuration of the points, preferably in a small number of dimensions, usually 2 or 3.

Or, as remarked by Venables and Ripley ([2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000376), p. 306),

> The most obvious of the distance methods is multidimensional scaling (MSD) which seeks a configuration in `R^(d)` such that distances between the points best match (in a sense to be defined) those of the distance matrix.

Most generally, MDS takes a distance matrix of observed variables and seeks to find a lower-dimensional representation of those distances. It takes a distance matrix of say, ten dimensions, 430and asks the question “*Can we represent this ten-dimensional matrix in terms of only a few underlying dimensions, and if so, what are the coordinates that best give rise to the observed distances/dissimilarities*?” In this sense, MDS seeks to accomplish a similar (though not *exact*) goal as PCA and other dimension-reduction techniques. In fact, classical multidimensional scaling with a Euclidean distance metric is equivalent to plotting the first `k` principal components, without rescaling to correlations ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057); Murray, 2022; [Venables & Ripley, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000376)). A related interpretation of MDS is to uncover underlying dimensions such that the observed distances between points are maintained or “preserved” as closely as possible (Meila & Zhang, [2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000247); [Rencher & Christensen, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295)). Most embedding algorithms do not preserve Euclidean distances, instead resulting in a distorted geometry to at least some extent (Meila & Zhang, [2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000247), p. 19).

## Classical scaling

Depending on whether we have a distance matrix already computed, classical MDS can begin with a matrix of raw data, `X`, or the distance matrix `D`. Beginning with `X`, we compute a matrix `B`, which is the matrix of sums and squares and cross-products,

`B = X X'`

(11.12)

It stands that the matrix `B` can be converted into a matrix of distances, `D`, where we can obtain the squared Euclidean distances between rows of `X` as a function of the elements of `B`:

`d[i j]^2 = b[i i] + b[j j] - 2 b[i j]`

(11.13)

These are squared Euclidean distances ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 198), yet in the above formulation, it can be difficult to appreciate what is being computed and it is helpful to recall what a Euclidean distance actually is,

$$\\begin{array}{l} {d\\left( x,y \\right) = \\sqrt{\\left( x\_{1} - y\_{1} \\right)^{2} + \\left( x\_{2} - y\_{2} \\right)^{2} + \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot \\left( x\_{p} - y\_{p} \\right)^{2}}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\sqrt{\\left( x - y \\right)'\\left( x - y \\right)}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

where `d(x,y)` is the distance between vectors `x` and `y`. It should be emphasized that the distance matrix `D` does not *need* to contain Euclidean distances. Many other options are available, which we briefly survey when we discuss cluster analysis later in the book. What *is* important for `D` is that the distances featured satisfy basic essential properties of distances `d[i j]` where `i` and `j` correspond to points `i` and `j`. These properties include `d[i j] >= 0`, `d[i i] = 0`, and `d[i j] = d[j i]` ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057)). Beyond such essential properties, MDS does not really “care” where `D` comes from. Though, of course, *the scientist should*. Distance measures (sometimes called *similarity coefficients*) can also be obtained for binary data (see [Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 194 for details).

Given the correspondence between `B` and `D`, an eigenanalysis can then be computed on `B`, which, assuming `B` is of full rank, will yield as many eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs as there are rows or columns of `B`. The resulting eigenvectors are new dimensions from which the data can be projected. As in PCA, we can then evaluate the size of respective eigenvalues relative to the sum of eigenvalues to determine the “importance” of the given dimension, where proportions greater than 0.8 are suggestive of an at least reasonable fit ([Everitt, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000097), p. 95), however such “cut-off criteria” should not be taken rigidly and like PCA will depend on the nature and context of the problem. To emphasize, the matrix of raw data in `X` was not actually required had we already had `D`. That is, MDS only requires a distance matrix, it does not operate directly on the raw data 431([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). Of course, in most cases, raw data is all the investigator has, and hence such will usually be the starting point to computing an MDS, that of computing the matrix `D` from `X`. But theoretically, one can perform an MDS having only knowledge of the distance matrix, similar to how one can perform a PCA having only knowledge of the covariance or correlation matrix. How these matrices “arise,” MDS cares little about.

## Kruskal-Shepard scaling

As mentioned, MDS comprises several related techniques and versions. The method surveyed above is generally known as *classical scaling*. A competing version of MDS, called *Kruskal-Shepard scaling*, seeks values or points `p[1]`, `p[2]`, ..., `p[n]` that minimize the following so-called *stress function*:

$$S = {\\sum\\limits\_{i,j}\\left( d\_{ij} - \\middle| \\middle| p\_{i} - p\_{j} \\middle| \\middle| \\right)^{2}}$$

(11.14)

where `d[i j]` is the distance between two data points `i`, `j` in the original data and `||p[i] - p[j]||` is the corresponding “distance” `i` to `j` between reconstructed or “embedded” data points `p` based on the dimension reduction. It should be noted these latter distances may not represent actual *distances* and may not agree with classical properties of distances; in the case of nonmetric MDS (to be discussed shortly), they are only used to help determine the monotonicity of the original distances, `d[i j]` ([Rencher & Christensen, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295)). On the other hand, in a version of metric MDS in which `d[i j]` represent more than an ordinal ranking, interpreting `||p[i] - p[j]||` as the distance between observed and reconstructed points is more acceptable.

An alternative *scaled* way of defining the stress function is,

$$S = \\frac{\\sum\\limits\_{i,j}\\left( d\_{ij} - D\_{ij} \\right)^{2}}{\\sum\\limits\_{i,j}d\_{ij}^{2}}$$

(11.15)
