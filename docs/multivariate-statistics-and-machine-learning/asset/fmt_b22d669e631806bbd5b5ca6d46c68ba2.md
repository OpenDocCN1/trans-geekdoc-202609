## 5.8 Collinearity

### 5.8.1 Collinearity

Collinearity can have serious and deleterious effects on a regression solution. If the columns of `X` are not linearly independent, this equates to `X` not being of full rank, which implies that `X^T X` is singular, the determinant is equal to 0, and the least-squares coefficients, though they theoretically can be estimated, are not *uniquely* defined. Hence, collinearity in regression is a serious problem and we need a way to address it.

#### 5.8.1.1 Variance Inflation Factor

There are a few common ways of assessing collinearity. The first we survey is probably the most popular, that of computing a statistic called the variance inflation factor, or “`VIF`” for short. To understand `VIF`, we must first consider how we can write the variance of the i*th* partial regression coefficient (Cohen et al., [2003](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000064), p. 423):238

$$s\_{\\beta\_{i}}^{2} = \\frac{s\_{y}^{2}}{s\_{x\_{i}}^{2}}\\left( \\frac{1 - R\_{y.12...p}^{2}}{n - p - 1} \\right)\\left( \\frac{1}{1 - R\_{i.12...(i)...p}^{2}} \\right)$$

(5.29)

where *s*[*β*[*i*]]² is the variance of the *β*[*i*] coefficient, *s*[*y*]² is the variance of the response, *s*[*x*[*i*]]² is the variance of the given predictor *x*[*i*] corresponding to *β*[*i*], and *R*[*y*.12...*p*]² is the coefficient of determination for the entire model with all predictors *y*.12...*p*. The variance inflation factor is the last term in (5.29):

$$\\left( \\frac{1}{1 - R\_{i.12...(i)...p}^{2}} \\right)$$

(5.30)

where *R*[*i*.12...(*i*)...*p*]² represents the explained variance of the predictor in question regressed on the rest of predictors in the model. The extent to which *R*[*i*.12...(*i*)...*p*]² is large is the extent to which the denominator 1 − *R*[*i*.12...(*i*)...*p*]² goes toward zero, and hence is the extent to which `VIF` will be larger than not. That is, the extent to which the predictor in question shares variance with other predictors in the model is the extent to which the variance inflation factor increases. The variance inflation factor, as the name suggests, measures *the factor by which the variance of the given regression coefficient increases due to its collinearity with other variables in the model*.

A second popular way of assessing ill-conditioning is through the condition number. The condition number `k` is the square root of the ratio of the largest to the smallest nonzero eigenvalues, that is, $k = \sqrt{\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}}$, where `lambda_1` is the largest eigenvalue and `lambda_2` is the smallest ([Fox, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106)). If this ratio is large, `X` is considered *ill-conditioned*. It makes sense that a comparison of eigenvalues here can be used as an indicator of ill-conditioning. Consider what it means to have a very wide range between the largest and smallest eigenvalues. It implies that an increasing amount of variance is in one “component” (i.e., *principal component*) only in the data, which implies that there exists a large degree of correlation among variables “coagulating” into that component. Hence, the condition number can serve as an aid in detecting collinearity.

### 5.8.2 Collinearity Necessarily Implies Redundancy Only in Terms of Variance

In our discussion of collinearity thus far, it should be emphasized that two variables that are nearly perfectly collinear are redundant only in terms of *shared variance*, and not necessarily in terms of what they are actually individually measuring. A common misunderstanding when considering collinearity is to assume that two collinear variables are “getting at the same thing” on a substantive level simply because they are highly correlated. And while in some cases this may be true, it is not necessarily so across all cases.

For example, consider the classic case of correlating height and weight. Suppose the correlation is near 0.90. The shared variance between variables is relatively high, but we would all agree that weight is not the same thing as height. More generally, *a statistical measure, whatever the kind, in no way* ***necessarily*** *reflects substantive reality*. Whenever we apply an arbitrary statistical function to any “real object,” it is never a one-to-one relationship. *The statistics are never enough; you have to know what the statistics are being applied to in order to draw the proper scientific conclusion*. A lack of understanding of what statistics *can* vs. *cannot* tell you about empirical data is probably the greatest pitfall for users of statistics who have not considered these wider and deeper methodological and philosophical issues.

## 5.9 Variable Selection Methods: Building the Regression Model

Previously we discussed model fit and model selection measures, such as `R^2`, `R_adj^2`, `AIC`, etc. While we discussed how to potentially choose the best model, we did not discuss *how* to select 239variables for a given model. This latter concern falls under the category of *variable selection* rather than the previously discussed topic of *model selection*. One way to conceive of variable selection in a very general sense is as suggested by [Breiman (2001)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000049), such that a variable is important *if dropping it from the model seriously affects prediction*. And while variable selection may at first glance appear to be simple, it is an extremely complex problem, just as complex as model selection overall. Most variable selection procedures are quite unstable as well, such that tweaking or perturbing the original data even slightly can have a rather drastic effect on the final model ([Breiman, 1996](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000047)).

Variable selection methods include `forward`, `backward`, and `stepwise` techniques, where stepwise can be considered a hybrid version of forward and backward. Stepwise and selection algorithms often go by the name of `wrapper models` in the data-mining literature ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)). Versions of backward elimination algorithms are sometimes referred to as `recursive feature elimination algorithms` in the data and machine learning environments, and though there are distinctions between these types of algorithms, they pretty much get at the same thing in the end.

Other techniques include `all-possible-subsets`, `forward-stagewise regression`, and others. Both `ridge` and `lasso` regression, as we will see in the next chapter, can be considered in some sense variable selection techniques as well, especially the `lasso` since coefficients are explicitly driven to values of zero, while for `ridge` coefficients they are simply “shrunk” toward zero. For this reason, `ridge` is often not considered a “pure” variable selection procedure. However, nothing inhibits the user from simply dropping shrunken coefficients as they near zero or are much less in magnitude than other coefficients in the model. We will return to this discussion when we discuss both techniques in the following chapter.
