
`Naïve Bayes`, as the name would suggest, is yet another method that attempts to approximate the `Bayes classifier`. As we will see, it accomplishes this approximation by making the simple assumption of `conditional independence`, one that is unlikely to ever be met in practice.

## 8.16.1 What Makes Naïve Bayes “Naïve?”

To understand and appreciate what makes Bayes “naïve,” we must first revisit the fact that when we conduct methods such as `linear discriminant analysis`, we assume a great deal when implementing the model. For instance, we assume that the density function is `multivariate normal`; we assume equal covariances in the case of `linear discriminant analysis` (not so in `QDA`). All of these assumptions make the task of fitting a model relatively “simple,” in the sense that we can cross many things off our “checklist” and assume them to be `constant` rather than `variable`. That is, by making assumptions in models, we can focus on the change we want to focus on and consider the other elements “fixed” and not fluctuating, analogous to how an experimentalist holds empirical conditions constant except for that which is under investigation, the so-called `experimental manipulation`.
As another example, `linear regression` is a great model under the assumption that the relationship is linear. How great are parametric assumptions in facilitating the task! They “prep the ground,” so to speak, so that we can take the next step and estimate the functional form. We are starting off with the bed half-made. Much of statistical modeling is, in actuality, as is true with most theories and narratives, `the specifying of assumptions a priori` ([Shao, 2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000334)).
This is where `Naïve Bayes` differs from methods such as `linear discriminant analysis`. With `Naïve Bayes`, we typically do not make assumptions about the parametric form, nor about parametric densities such as imposing a `multivariate normal` distribution. This is not to say we do not make `any` assumptions, but the assumptions we do make are rather limited. Here is what we usually assume with `Naïve Bayes`:
> `Within the kth class, the p predictors are independent.`
But what is the above, really? Recall the independence assumption from basic probability theory for events `A` and `B`,
`p(A∩B) = p(A)p(B)`
which reads that the probability of the joint event `A` and `B` is equal to the product of their unconditional probabilities. Recall that if the two events were not independent, then the joint event would involve a `conditional` probability:
`p(A∩B) = p(A)p(B/A)`
Under independence of `A` and `B`, it stands that `p(B) = p(B/A)` and so we obtain,
```
\begin{array}{l}
{p\left( A \cap B \right) = p\left( A \right)p\left( B/A \right)}\\
{\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, = p\left( A \right)p\left( B \right)}\\
\end{array}
```
(8.39)
Equality (8.39) is also generally known as the `product rule for independent events` in probability theory. We will generalize this assumption to `densities` instead of variables, and state
`f[k](x) = f[k1](x[1]) ⋅ f[k2](x[2]) ⋅ ... ⋅ f[kp](x[p])`
where the different densities are now indexed by `k` for `p` variables. `Naïve Bayes` assumes that for a given class, group or category, `predictors are independent` ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). If we choose to incorporate the group or category directly in our statement of independence, then the statement of independence becomes that of `conditional independence`:
`p(A,B/C) = p(A/C)p(B/C)`
(8.40)
where `C` is now the class membership stated `explicitly` in the probability statement instead of needing to “attach” the membership statement in our earlier definition of conditional independence. That is, conditional independence simply generalizes the concept of independence to conditional probabilities. Though the `Naïve Bayes` assumption is technically about conditional independence, it makes more sense, as many other authors do, to speak about the assumption of `independence` rather than continually state `conditional independence.` That is, we make it implicit in this discussion that we are referring to independence `within a particular class`.
Now, stop and think for a moment – `Are two events A and B ever truly independent?` That is, can we realistically imagine any two `real` events to be completely independent? Probably not, no more than we can think of two events that are totally uncorrelated. All variables are correlated to `some` degree at least, just as pretty much all events are dependent upon one another to some degree, at least `probabilistically`. That your colleague found a correlation between his or her variables is `expected`. The `unexpected` result is finding a correlation of precisely 0. Likewise for probabilistic dependence, the probability of winning the lottery is likely `not` independent of the probability of a mouse living in your house. We often `assume` they are independent (and for good reason, pragmatically they `are`), but probabilistically they are (probably) not so, even if the ensuing probability is so remote to be trivial. `Correlations and probabilities are not necessarily reflective of empirical processes`, especially given that probabilities are typically measured on a continuous scale.
Even given the reality of empirical independence virtually never being satisfied, the `assumption` of independence will be a useful, pragmatic, and simplifying one to make, just as the assumptions we employed for things like `LDA` were useful (and simplifying) as well. Hence, `Naïve Bayes` does not totally jettison the making of assumptions, but one might say the assumptions it does make are rather `simple` and `easy`, such as that for conditional independence. Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize the presence of `some` assumption here, even if quite simple and basic. As has been emphasized throughout this book, very few procedures are devoid of `any` assumptions, even if they are advertised as making few. Even in nonparametric techniques we will usually find hidden assumptions, though they are typically much less in importance or influence than in their competing assumption-laden parametric counterparts.
The assumption of conditional independence in `Naïve Bayes` is made for the purpose of `utility`. We know in advance it will likely not be true. It is made for `convenience` and `pragmatism`, similar to the idea of assuming Gaussian distributions in `LDA`. Will `Naïve Bayes` estimate correctly? Probably not, and hence, it is `biased`, but with added bias comes a reduction in variance, similar to how using `ridge regression` allows for an increase in bias but with a reduction in variance. Recall once more the fundamental bias-variance trade-off:
`E(y[0]-f̂(x[0]))² = Var(f̂(x[0])) + [Bias[f̂(x[0])]]² + Var(ε)`
In the case of `Naïve Bayes`, `Var(f̂(x[0]))` typically decreases at the expense of an increase in bias, `[Bias[f̂(x[0])]]²`.
`Naïve Bayes` is simple to understand because it is more easily understood as an extension of `Bayes theorem`, which once more recall is the following:
```

```
(8.41)
