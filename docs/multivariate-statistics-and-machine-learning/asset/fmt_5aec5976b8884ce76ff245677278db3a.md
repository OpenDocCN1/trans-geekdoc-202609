
For example, suppose that in the model of (8.7), `β[1]` were equal to 0.8; what would be the interpretation? In classic least-squares regression, the interpretation, as we recall, would be for a one-unit increase in `X`, we expect `Y` to increase by 0.8 units, given the model under test. In logistic regression, the interpretation is the same, only that the “units” now are not natural units of the original variable, but instead are units of the `logit`, and hence, our interpretation would be for a one-unit increase in `X`, we expect the logit of `Y` to increase by 0.8 logits, given the model under test.

### 8.3.1 Odds and Odds Ratios
Recall that we said the odds were defined as the probability of an event occurring relative to the probability of the event not occurring, that is,
$$\\frac{p\\left( x \\right)}{1 - p\\left( x \\right)}$$
The *odds ratio* is, as the name suggests, a *ratio of odds*, comparing the odds for one variable, `x[1]`, or category of a response, to the odds of another variable, `x[2]`, or complement category to `x[1]`:
$$OR = \\frac{\\left( \\frac{p\\left( x\_{1} \\right)}{1 - p\\left( x\_{1} \\right)} \\right)}{\\left( \\frac{p\\left( x\_{2} \\right)}{1 - p\\left( x\_{2} \\right)} \\right)}$$
(8.8)
For example, if the odds are the same for each variable or category, then the odds ratio is equal to one. An odds ratio greater than one implies that the odds for `x[1]` are greater than the odds for `x[2]`. Conversely, an odds ratio of less than one implies that the odds for `x[1]` are less than the odds for `x[2]`.

## 8.4 Generalized Linear Models and Neural Networks
