
We have discussed thus far the architecture of neural networks, but we have not yet discussed why, theoretically, they are so appealing. Why bother with building a neural network? The key motivation behind neural networks, just as is the wish for any statistical method, is that they are remarkably good at *approximating functions*. This concept is housed in the *universal approximation theorem* and is the key to understanding the more theoretical side of neural networks. The theorem, as stated by Hornik ([1991](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000159), p. 359) word for word, is the following:

> Standard multilayer feedforward networks with as few as one hidden layer using arbitrary squashing functions are capable of approximating any Borel measurable function from one finite dimensional space to another to any desired degree of accuracy, provided sufficiently many hidden units are available. In this sense, multilayer feedforward networks are a class of universal approximators.

This is a very profound and remarkable result! For instance, [Cybenko (1989)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000074) has shown that a two-layer feedforward neural network with a sufficient number of hidden units can approximate any continuous function to any degree of accuracy. The approximation theorem also holds for a great deal of activation functions except for the polynomial ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)). See [Hornik (1991)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000159) for approximation capacities for arbitrary bounded and nonconstant activation functions.

Typically, the closer the approximation desired, to reiterate, the larger the number of hidden nodes required ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)), but the key point to emphasize is that we are able in the first place to approximate functions as closely as we would like using neural networks. The theorem, due to [Kolmogorov (1957)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000199) tells us that any continuous high-dimensional map can be represented as the function composition of much simpler one-dimensional maps. Again, this is a very powerful idea, as [Izenman (2008)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167) summarizes very well:554

> The universal approximation theorem is an existence theorem: it shows, theoretically, that one can approximate an arbitrary continuous function by a single hidden-layer network. Unfortunately, it does not specify how to find that approximation; that is, how to determine the weights and the number, `t`, of nodes in the hidden layer (a problem known as network complexity). It also assumes that we know the continuous function being approximated and that the available set of hidden nodes is of unlimited size. Furthermore, the theorem is not an optimality result: it does not show that a single hidden layer is the best-possible multilayer network for carrying out the approximation.

> (p. 334)

What does it take for the theorem to work? It turns out that the only thing that is required to realize the approximation are mild assumptions on the activation function. And while the theorem typifies modern neural networks, its mathematical origins can be traced back to Hilbert’s 13th problem or conjecture, which held that *all high-dimensional continuous functions are a combination of two-variable continuous functions* ([Kratsios, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000201)). In machine learning, more generally, universal approximation is about “a model class’s ability to generically approximate any member of a large topological space whose elements are functions, or more rigorously, equivalence classes of functions” ([Kratsios](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000201), 2020, p. 2). For a highly theoretical read of the approximation theorem, see [Kratsios](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000201) (2022).

### 15.11.1 Visualizing the Universal Approximation Theorem

Approximating functions has a long history in mathematics. Indeed, as [Steffens (2006)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000346) has remarked regarding the centrality of approximation in mathematics, “Whenever one computes, one *approximates*” (p. vii). But what exactly does *function approximation* mean? Perhaps the easiest way to understand it is to liken it to what occurs in differential calculus as the prototypical elementary example of function approximation. For example, consider the function in [Figure 15.10](#c15.xhtml#f15.10), that of a parabola along with its graph (left):

![Figure 15.10 Inverted parabola (left) and concept of approximating a function at a point (right).Return to text.⏎](images/fig15.10.jpg)

*Figure 15.10* Inverted parabola (left) and concept of approximating a function at a point (right).[Return to text.⏎](#c15.xhtml#re_f15.10)

`f(x) = - x² + 3`

Suppose we would like to approximate the function at a value of `x` = -1. Of course, we could compute the slope of the line at `x` = -1 which would give us the derivative, shown in [Figure 15.10](#c15.xhtml#f15.10) 555(right). The derivative, as the reader will recall, is the *slope of the tangent to the curve* (or, more precisely, *the limit of the slope of secant lines approaching the given point*). That is, the derivative is *approximating the function* at that particular point. Indeed, if we computed derivatives at all infinitesimally small and infinite numbers of points along the `x`-axis and summed them up, we would have approximated the actual function of the inverted parabola.

As may seem obvious to the reader, the more derivatives we use the better the polynomial approximations become. They get better as well if we use higher-order polynomials to better capture the curve. These ideas suggest a much more general result which is that of the *Taylor polynomial* of degree `n` (`P[n](x)`), which `x` for near `x0` is given by,

$$P\_{n}\\left( x \\right) = f\\left( x\_{0} \\right) + f'\\left( x\_{0} \\right)\\left( x - x\_{0} \\right) + \\frac{f''\\left( x\_{0} \\right)}{2!}\\left( x - x\_{0} \\right)^{2} + \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot + \\frac{f^{(n)}\\left( x\_{0} \\right)}{n!}\\left( x - x\_{0} \\right)^{n}$$

(15.16)

and for which (15.16) is called the `n`th Taylor polynomial for `f` at `x0`. The approximation for points *near* `x0` is given by `x` − `x0`, and the approximation is rarely perfect, yielding a remainder term `f` − `P[n]` (i.e., the actual function minus the approximation). If we allow the degree `n` of the polynomial to go to infinity, that is, `n`→∞, then the Taylor polynomial becomes simply a *Taylor series*. That is, a Taylor series is simply a Taylor polynomial of infinite degree. Put differently, a Taylor series is the limiting sum of a Taylor polynomial as `n`→∞.

Though a bit more complicated and involved than the simple Taylor series featured here, the essential idea is analogous in neural networks. Approximating functions is what neural networks, in the end, actually *do*. Often these functions are unknown and must be “learned” from the data. This is, in part, the *nonparametric* element of neural networks.

## 15.12 Neural Networks and Projection Pursuit

There is often a link drawn in the literature between neural networks and a nonparametric technique called *projection pursuit* ([Friedman & Tukey, 1974](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000112); [Friedman & Stuetzle, 1981](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000111); [Kruskal, 1969](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000204)). Understanding the latter can provide a window into better understanding neural networks. As the name suggests, in projection pursuit we are “pursuing” the best means by which to make a projection. The primary qualification for the pursuit is that the projection be in some sense, *interesting*. The original conception of projection pursuit was formulated by Kruskal ([1969](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000204), [1972](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000205)).
