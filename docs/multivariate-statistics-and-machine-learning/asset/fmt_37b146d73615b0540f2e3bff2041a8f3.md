
As the story goes, in *supervised learning* we have prior-established designated labels on the response. Supervised learning is sometimes called *learning by teacher* because we have already defined the classes we are trying to predict. For example, if we are using education level to predict salaries *equal to or greater than* vs. *less than* $100,000, we have clearly identified the labels ≥100*k* and <100*k*; thus, the problem is supervised. Methods such as linear regression and discriminant analysis are generally regarded as supervised learning. We know what we are trying to predict or model.

In *unsupervised learning*, on the other hand, we do not have prior-established classes or labels. That is, we are not attempting to predict or classify into categories because the categories are currently unknown, or, at minimum, unused. Dimension-reduction techniques such as *principal components analysis* *(PCA)* or *cluster analysis* are often regarded as unsupervised. Unsupervised methods are often considered more *exploratory* in nature than their supervised counterparts.

There is also a third category, that of *semi-supervised learning*, which is when we do not have all of the data and can alleviate the need for labeled data by capitalizing on the unlabeled data ([Mohri et al., 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000253)). Semi-supervised learning approaches are beyond the scope of this book.

### 1.6.1 Fuzzy Distinctions

Despite the traditional arguments for the distinction between supervised vs. unsupervised, if forced to choose one over the other for all methods, we would likely conclude that most techniques are *supervised* in some sense and that unsupervised techniques really do not exist. To do this requires us to generalize our idea of “supervised” somewhat to a bit more than simply denoting labels on the response. It requires us to accept that most if not all methods are, in one way or another, *user-driven* at least to some extent, even if minimal in some cases.30

Consider that the moment the user is required to make any decisions regarding the method, he or she is either directly or implicitly supervising the approach. Consider principal components as an example, which is usually assumed to be an unsupervised method. As we will see later in the book, there are many different ways of conducting PCA. Choosing among these versions is an act of supervision, just as are choices regarding whether to standardize data and analyze the covariance or correlation matrix as input. Further, once the dimension reduction has been performed, the decision of how many components to retain is a supervised *act*. The situation is even more supervisory in a related so-called unsupervised method, that of *factor analysis*, where the user often must decide *a priori* how many factors to extract from the covariance or correlation matrix before even running the procedure. Telling the computer how many factors to compute is an extremely “supervisory” decision based on our revised version of what supervisory means.

As another example, consider *cluster analysis*, another so-called unsupervised technique. Such a procedure requires *a priori* for the user to select a metric by which distances will be operationalized. Once these inputs have been made, we allow the algorithm to “do its thing” in a non-supervised way, but the choices that govern the use of these methods are quite similar to the choice made in, for instance, the type of regression one performs in a supervised learning context. Should the user go with ordinary least-squares for a given problem or weighted least-squares? How about least-angle regression? In the end then, though the distinction between supervised and unsupervised is useful, when considered in a deeper way and with a bit of tinkering with the definitions, we arrive at the conclusion that *all methods are supervised, some less so than others, and some not as explicitly as others*.

It should be emphasized that many distinctions such as these in multivariate statistics or machine learning, when *critically challenged*, become quite *fuzzy* and blurry to the point where the distinction has trouble holding water, as it were. This is perfectly fine and does not mean the distinction isn’t useful or wrong; it simply means we should not think too *rigidly* about any of these definitions. Definitions such as these are useful, but they are often weakened when we put them under a microscope and tweak them slightly. Never *memorize*. Instead, *conceptualize*.

## 1.7 Theoretical vs. Empirical Justification

In philosophy, there is a difference between *theoretical* vs. *empirical* justification. We illustrate these differences with a couple of powerful and recent examples.

### 1.7.1 Airplanes and Oceanic Submersibles

The airplane lifting off the runway is an empirical demonstration that *airplanes fly*. It is only one demonstration of the idea. If many such airplanes successfully lift off the ground, we gain confidence in the idea that “airplanes fly.” That is, the certainty that airplanes fly is due to empirical justification, empirical “proof” we might crudely say.

The above is different from theoretical justification. The theoretical justification comes from an analytical argument, usually based on mathematics in this context, that airplanes *should* fly. That is, if the theory of flight is correct based on precise engineering aeronautic principles, then the airplane should fly. As much as possible, scientists should analytically justify what they seek to observe, or even have observed, *empirically*.

Why is this important? Recall the OceanGate expedition of 2023. The Titan oceanic submersible was successful in delving to the bottom of the ocean in visiting the *Titanic* many times such as to suggest the idea that it was a safe vessel to embark on such excursions. However, as critics noted after the submersible went missing (and was later found destroyed due to implosion), theoretical justification for the suitability of the vessel was lacking. That is, the vessel was not *analytically 31justified* for such excursions. Empiricism may reveal something to “work” a few times, but only *theoretical justification* can give us increased certainty that such will work in general for a large number or even infinite number of trials.

### 1.7.2 Will the Bridge Stay Up If the Mathematics Fail?

A classic example of the distinction between theoretical vs. empirical justification is perhaps the construction of a bridge. If one day the mathematics that led to the construction of the strong bridge suddenly were found faulty, would the bridge still stay up, or would it immediately fall? Of course, it would likely stay up, *at least for a little while, maybe even longer*. However, it is the theoretical justification of the bridge’s construction that we typically rely on to ensure its long-term reliability and sturdiness. Theory, or rational argument, makes the knowledge on which the bridge is constructed more *certain*; not *guaranteed*, since the theory itself is tentative, but simply more certain.

Theoretical and empirical justification must work hand in hand, however, and serve to inform each other, but one should never take a few empirical demonstrations to “justify” a theory that has not been justified *a priori* independent of those empirical demonstrations. This is the strength of sound mathematics and proofs, or more generally, rigorous *philosophical argument*. Such serves as a rational “guarantee” that the way we are thinking about constructing the bridge is sound.
