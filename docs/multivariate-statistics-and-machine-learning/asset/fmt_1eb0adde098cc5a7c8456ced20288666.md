
## 15.9 Preventing Overfitting
One approach to preventing overfitting is to simply stop the model short of fully iterating. This is known as the *early stopping* solution to mitigating overfitting. The longer a network is trained, the more it capitalizes on *noise or chance factors* and thus fits the training data quite well, but at some point will increasingly generalize poorly to validation data. The complexity of the system is increased with training and hence generalizability suffers ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)). In other words, the network might fit really well on the training data, but error increases substantially on new data because, essentially, what the network was so “good” at on the training data past a certain point was in effect capitalizing on chance or random noise. The case study analogy once more works well here, such that the network becomes an “expert” on the current case, but the theory (i.e., in the form of optimized estimated weights) developed on that case does not do nearly as well predicting the outcome of new cases. If one purposely stops the network early from iterating fully, one can possibly mitigate overfitting.

### 15.9.2 Dropout Method
A second method to dealing with overfitting is that of the *dropout approach*, which prevents *complex adaptation* of the hidden units ([Srivastava et al., 2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000345)). The method purposely drops some of the units out on each iteration and hence each unit learns to perform well even if some other units are missing. This approach estimates what is known as a *noisy version* of the weights since dropping weights equates to adding noise to units. As a result of the method randomly dropping weights, it is generally referred to as a *stochastic regularization* technique. An excellent summary and implementation of the dropout method is provided in [Srivastava et al. (2014)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000345).

A related solution to the dropout method is instead of randomly dropping weights, to instead equate (or in some forms of the method, *encourage* equality) some of the weights as a way of 552regularizing the model and discouraging overfitting. This solution is known as *soft weight sharing* ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032); [Nowlan & Hinton, 1992](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000270)). Details are in Bishop ([2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032), p. 269).

### 15.9.3 Regularized Network
A third approach to prevent, or at least reduce the possibility of overfitting is to introduce a regularization parameter, that is, a penalty function to shrink estimates similar to what is accomplished in ridge and lasso regression, for instance. One form of regularization used in neural networks is that of *weight decay* where a penalty term is added to the error function. As detailed in [Hastie et al. (2009)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), if the error function for the parameter vector `θ` is `R(θ)`, then the error function plus penalty is equal to

`R(θ) + λJ(θ)`

(15.15)

where `J(θ)` is some penalty (e.g., see Hastie et al., [2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)) and `λ` a tuning parameter. It is easy to see that as `λ` increases for `J(θ)` ≠ 0, `R(θ)` will increase which implies the error of the model will be larger than not and weights will be shrunk toward 0. For regularized least-squares specifically, the error function becomes ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)),

$$\\begin{array}{l} {E\\left( w \\right) = R\\left( \\theta \\right) + \\lambda J\\left( \\theta \\right)} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = E\\left( w \\right) + \\frac{\\lambda}{2}w^{T}w} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

where `w` is the weight vector and which is an example of `L[2]` regularization.

So, what could be the benefit to increasing error? As was the case with ridge and lasso regression, imposing a penalty has the effect of shrinking weights toward 0 and hence reduces overfitting. Both `L[1]` and `L[2]` are possible for neural networks just as they were for ridge and lasso regression. For further details, consult [Hastie et al. (2009)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), who also provide a powerful demonstration of regularizing a neural network through weight decay (pp. 402–403). The weights used to initialize a network can be a factor in weight decay as well. Initial weight values should be small, random, and typically in the neighborhood of \[−0.01, 0.01\] ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)) from a uniform distribution, though much larger intervals have also been proposed.

## 15.10 Types of Networks
As we saw when we considered the simpler neural network, the multilayer network is likewise not that far removed from traditional regression. In fact, multilayer networks are analogous to nonlinear regression models for continuous responses and to discriminant analysis models for binary outcomes ([Sarle, 1994](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000322)).

A *fully connected network* is one in which all input nodes are connected to the nodes in the first hidden layer, and all nodes in the first hidden layer are connected to all nodes in the second hidden layer, and so on. If some of the connections are missing, then we have a *partially connected network*. By setting or constraining some of the weights to zero, we can obtain a partially connected network from a wider and more general fully connected network. This idea of constraining parameters is familiar by now; *most models constrain select parameters either implicitly or explicitly*. In general, in any statistical model we entertain, parameters can be fixed or constrained to particular values, or even more generally chosen to not be estimated at all. Usually, substantive theory and prior knowledge of the research situation will help determine which weights to fix. Sometimes 553fixing parameters is a necessity to aid in identifying the model as in structural equation modeling. The important point for now is to appreciate the similarity of fixing weights in a neural network to that of fixing weights in other statistical models. It is an entirely general *concept* which simultaneously frees up degrees of freedom (such as we saw in regression splines when constraining the solution to have no jumps or discontinuities).

The neural networks we have surveyed thus far are all examples of what are known as *feedforward neural networks* such that the “flow” of the network is from one end to the other (typically from left to right). However, there are many other types of networks. In fact, feedforward networks can be viewed as special cases of *recurrent networks*, where recurrent networks are not necessarily always forward-feeding and instead allow output from some nodes to influence nearby or subsequent inputs to those very nodes. Recurrent neural networks are often used in the processing of sequential data ([Goodfellow et al., 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)). *Convolutional networks* are yet another type of network which typically feature the use of kernels and are usually subjected to regularization to mitigate overfitting. Such networks are often used in image and video recognition to help narrow in on specific or *local regions* in the case of an image, for example, and combine local features (e.g., the smaller details of what in totality make up a vehicle called a “truck”) to generate compound features (e.g., identifying the object as a truck instead of a car). Both recurrent and convolutional networks are well beyond the scope of the current text, and can become quite complex. For a good introduction, see [Goodfellow et al. (2016)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131) or [Bishop (2006)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032).
