
What has been described above as the typical course of study for the advanced undergraduate or beginning graduate student is now *out of date*. *It is no longer enough*. *It is grossly incomplete*. *It is archaic*. You get the picture! In the last 20–30 years, certainly post-2000, a machine learning and data science *Kuhnian paradigm shift* has occurred. In the age we find ourselves in, applied statistics courses are desperately in need of a *makeover*. They are in need of a major “updating,” overhaul, and renovation, not necessarily because foundational elements of modern data science and machine learning differ *that much* from the traditional univariate and multivariate essentials presented in the typical science curriculum, but mostly because the very landscape of what we may more generally call *data analysis* has changed. What is called “data analysis” today is much broader than prior to the data science and machine learning revolution, yet at the same time the foundations of these areas are often found in statistics. For the applied scientist or practitioner, 4exposure to traditional courses in statistics that do not incorporate data science and machine learning theory and concepts are now, *incomplete*.

## 0.1.1 A Wide Divide

Consider the current reality – it is entirely possible for a student to graduate from an applied science program having taken a typical statistics sequence yet upon graduating be completely unfamiliar with what is going on in *machine learning* and having little to no idea what *artificial intelligence* is despite the fact that many of the concepts and features learned in their statistics courses are directly applicable to what is occurring in these fields. Indeed, many of the concepts studied in statistics can be considered *foundations* to these modern fields. Hence, the student from a statistics course sitting down to lunch with a student from a machine learning course would soon realize that many of the things they are learning in their respective courses overlap a great deal, and in many cases, may even be very much the *same*. They begin to share notes and compare jargon used from each field and quickly realize the extensive *overlap* of their coursework. They are presumably coming from different disciplines yet may be talking very much about the same things! If they fail to connect and realize this, it may be more to do with the *jargon* used in each field than anything else.

Now, the above is not to suggest that jargon alone is what differentiates these fields. *Far from it*. Machine learning, and its underachieving and overpromising parent *artificial intelligence*, are their own respective fields, and as we will see in this book have already made tremendous advances and contributions for disciplines that are still relatively quite young. However, the overlap, especially at the level of *foundations*, is undeniable. Depending on one’s historical and philosophical perspective, one might even say that in many cases statistics gave *birth* to these fields. When a student or even researcher in machine learning speaks of a “learner” to a student just coming from a statistics course as though it is a “new” concept, the reaction of the statistics or science student should not be one of confusion and ignorance. Instead, it should be one of *recognition* of the similar foundations learned in their respective courses. Of course, the student of statistics, coming from an introductory lecture on the analysis of variance, for example, has just been subjected to a *statistical learner* as well, and it would be a shame if that student were unable to identify with the concept brought to the lunch table by the machine learning student. Indeed, those seemingly simple and “basic” *t*\-tests were kind of “machine learners” themselves in their own right and in the most general sense. Translated to a dummy-coded regression, weights are optimized, and if we feed more data through the *t*\-test or corresponding regression “algorithm,” those scalars get optimized further. *That simple model is learning based on experience*. The statistics student needs to appreciate what the machine learning student is talking about and vice versa, the machine learning student would do well to get in touch with some of his or her own roots in statistical theory.

## 0.2 A Unified Vision: The Bridge

Though machine learning and artificial intelligence are disparate fields, it is high time for a *unified introduction and top-down overview* of these areas for the student of the social and natural sciences. It is well time to construct a *bridge*. For *specialists* in statistics or machine learning the awareness of these bridges is (or should be) old news. But to the newcomer or applied science student, without a roadmap it can be exceedingly difficult to know or even recognize the *landscape*. You may not even know what forest you are in, which makes it terribly difficult to even recognize or navigate the trees.

A unified treatment of these areas should include a strong introduction and overview of topics in *univariate*, *bivariate*, *multivariate*, *machine learning*, *design and analysis of experiments*, along with select topics from *measurement theory*. To this last point, before one analyzes data or designs 5the next fancy algorithm, one should be intimately interested and invested into what *one is actually measuring*. This is true whether one is measuring emotional intelligence or one is measuring product preference via an online retailer. The customer may have “clicked” on product *A* because they *thought* they preferred it over product *B*, until it arrived on delivery day and they realized what they actually preferred was the *image* it falsely represented on the computer screen. In the end, their preference was for the *image*, the *perception*, the *idealization*, the *feeling*, not the *product*, yet the data point is advertised to have recorded *preference for the product*. *The measurement mechanism has failed*. Hence, what was measured in that impressive terabyte SQL database was not what we originally thought. It isn’t communicating what you think it is. Measurement issues abound in science and research, and a good course in statistics and machine learning should address them to some extent regardless of whether that course is for social or natural scientists. *Measurement is the first principle of good science*. Methods to analyze those measurements, of which much of this book is about, are second to that top priority.

What is more, modern texts on statistical and machine learning often tout the discipline as somewhat “novel” even though, of course, it overlaps a great deal with traditional and *classical* statistics in both univariate and multivariate domains. To reiterate, a presentation of machine learning that ignores this foundation or goes “skimpy” on the statistical side of things makes it possible for the graduate student to take a computer science course, for instance, yet fail to realize that many of those same foundational elements can also be found, and often have their *origins* in, *classical statistics*. For example, “unsupervised learning” in the name of techniques such as principal components analysis sounds “new,” but actually had its origins in 1901 with Karl Pearson. It’s a *learning algorithm* now, whereas it used to be simply a *statistical technique*. Oh my, have times changed! Karl Pearson would look befuddled had we told him his minimizing of squared perpendicular distances was a “learning algorithm.” Education needs to emphasize *history*, where these things came from and how they morphed into the verbiage and jargon of today. Very little is truly “new.” Most is usually a mixture of historical elements and ideas.
