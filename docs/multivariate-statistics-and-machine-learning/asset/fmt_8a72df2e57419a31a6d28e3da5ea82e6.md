
The aforementioned measures of model fit, whether absolute or relative, are just a very small sample of model fit measures available in SEM. Specialists in SEM continue to publish new measures it seems almost yearly, all of which offer different ways to assess and evaluate model fit. An understanding of these most common ones, however, opens the door to understanding many variations and gets the proverbial “foot in the door” to the vast area of model fit statistics in SEM.

## 13.7 Structural Equation Modeling with Nonlinear Effects

Most structural equation models, as we have seen thus far with so many models in statistics and machine learning, assume *linearity in the parameters*. They are a complex network of otherwise *linear models*. As we know by now, assuming linearity can provide relatively good generalizability of findings and hence relatively low variance. However, if the true relationship underlying the data is *nonlinear*, then assuming linearity can seriously bias the model as well as bias parameter estimates generated based on such a restrictive assumption. Indeed, entire trends or features in the data can be missed if attempting to fit more complex models is not attempted ([Kelava & Brandt, 2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000184)).

For example, a linear SEM model may fail to detect a quadratic effect entirely, which may otherwise prove to be very important to the investigator on a scientific level. And since structural equation models can increase in complexity very quickly, exploratory visualization of such models is much more difficult or even impossible compared to visualizing much simpler models, for example a scatterplot where one might immediately detect nonlinearity simply by observing the trend in the corresponding plot. Visualization of multivariate data, especially in complex models such as SEM or neural networks as we will see later in the book, can prove daunting to say the least. This implies that detecting nonlinearity in such data can be equally challenging.

When structural relationships are, in fact, nonlinear, but nonetheless fit using a linear model, many issues arise, the most serious of which is that latent endogenous variables can no longer be assumed to be normally distributed as they are assumed so in linear SEM models ([Klein & Muthén, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000188)). This implies that parameter estimates cannot be determined based solely on the covariance structure of the data ([Vegelius & Jin, 2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000374)). A failure to include nonlinear effects in a structural equation model when such effects are present, as mentioned, can result in biased estimators (Rdz-Navarro & Yang-Wallentin, [2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000294)).

There has been much work in the development and estimation of nonlinear structural equation models. [Klein and Muthén (2007)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000188) developed a nonlinear structural equation model using a *quasi-maximum-likelihood* approach for estimation, which when evaluated in simulation studies produced robust standard errors and was relatively computationally inexpensive. [Bollen (1996)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000038) proposed a two-stage least-squares approach (`2SLS`), and more recently Holst and Budtz-Jorgensen ([2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000156)) have also proposed a two-stage least-squares estimation method. An efficient maximum-likelihood *latent moderated structural equations* approach has also recently been advanced ([Klein & Moosbrugger, 2000](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000189); [Umbach et al., 2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000367)).

### 13.7.1 Example of a Nonlinear SEM

The following is an example of a nonlinear SEM model, one without an intercept but featuring an interaction term,492

`η = γ[1]ξ[1] + γ[2]ξ[2] + ω[12]ξ[1]ξ[2] + ζ`

(13.14)

where the interaction term is given by `ω[12]ξ[1]ξ[2]`, and hence `η` is said to be a nonlinear function of `ξ[1]` and `ξ[2]`, which implies `η` has an expectation that will be *non-Gaussian*. One way to deal with this nonlinearity historically has been to treat the product term `ξ[1]ξ[2]` as a separate latent variable and construct so-called “indicators” based on products of original indicators ([Vegelius, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000375)). That is, the new variable becomes `ξ[3] = ξ[1]ξ[2]` (e.g., see [Kenny & Judd, 1984](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000185)). The inclusion of product terms, however, can lead to relatively smaller communalities as well as larger sampling fluctuation and multicollinearity in addition to the aforementioned nonnormal error terms ([Klein & Muthén, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000188)).

An example of a more complex model, one that not only includes an interaction term but also quadratic terms, is the following ([Kelava & Brandt, 2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000184)):

`η = α + γ[1]ξ[1] + γ[2]ξ[2] + γ[3]ξ[1]ξ[2] + γ[4]ξ[1]² + γ[5]ξ[2]² + ζ`

(13.15)

where now the quadratic terms are given by `γ[4]ξ[1]²` and `γ[5]ξ[2]²` in addition to the aforementioned interaction term, `ξ[1]ξ[2]`. Due to the fact that interaction terms and quadratic terms are often correlated, [Klein and Muthén (2007)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000188) proposed including quadratic effects to rule out spurious interaction effects, which gave rise to the more general nonlinear SEM model,

`η = α + Τξ + ξ'Ωξ + ζ`

(13.16)

in which the quadratic term is now indicated by `ξ'Ωξ` and where the corresponding measurement equations for the latent variables are the following:

$$\\begin{array}{l} {x = \\tau\_{x} + \\Lambda\_{x}\\xi + \\delta} \\\\ {y = \\tau\_{y} + \\Lambda\_{y}\\eta + \\varepsilon} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

The model assumes that `x` is multivariate normal, `δ` and `ε` are multivariate normal and mutually independent where `E(δ) = 0` and `E(ε) = 0`. The covariance assumptions include `Cov(δ, ξ') = 0` and `Cov(ε, ξ') = 0`, `Cov(ζ, ξ') = 0`, `Cov(ζ, δ') = 0`, and `Cov(ζ, ε') = 0`. For further details on the specification of this model, see [Klein and Moosbrugger (2000)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000189).

Nonlinear models in SEM can be fit using the package `nlsem` ([Umbach et al.](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000367), 2017). Consult Umbach et al. ([2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000122)) for an introduction and details about the package.

### 13.7.2 Structural Equation Nonparametric and Semiparametric Mixture Models

We have thus far surveyed the general nonlinear SEM model featuring parametric approaches to its estimation. An alternative and promising approach to estimating nonlinear SEM models is that of using a *semiparametric* approach (SEMM), first proposed by [Bauer (2005)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000025) featuring mixture modeling. The central idea behind mixture modeling in this context is to represent what is a nonnormal distribution as a combination of typically normal densities ([Kelava et al., 2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000184)). The density of the nonnormal distribution is then computed as a weighted sum of the (typically) normal densities. We say “typical” because the SEMM allows for flexible approximations of nonnormal distributions ([Umbach et al., 2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000367)). The nonparametric component of these models, as is true of other nonparametric models studied in this book, better allows the data to “speak for themselves” 493as it were rather than have an *a priori* functional form imposed on them such as the classic linearity or similar assumption about parametric form.
