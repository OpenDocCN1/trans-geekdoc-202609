
We see that the measure computed above (0.89) matches our manual computation. If the ground truth is known before the clustering, then cluster purity in this example is simple to conceptualize, it is essentially assessing the total proportion of cases that were clustered correctly into their ground truth classifications. For instance, if *setosa* had 50 cases, and all of those cases were clustered into “*setosa*,” then such would constitute perfect cluster purity for *setosa*. The cluster purity for *versicolor* is not quite as good because out of the 50 cases that *should* have gone into *versicolor*, only 48 ended up classified into that class. *Virginica* had the worst cluster purity at only 36 out of 50. The overall cluster purity is effectively summing up these class-specific purity ratios.

## 14.10 Hierarchical Clustering

As we have seen, in the `k`-family or `k`-representatives of clustering the number of clusters is specified *a priori*. Methods such as `k`-means and their variants rely on distance matrices to perform their clustering. An alternative approach to clustering is *hierarchical clustering*, in which the number of clusters is not requested *a priori*, but instead the algorithm will build a tree, a *dendrogram*, that displays the historical linkages made as the algorithm progresses from single cases to fused conglomerates of cases subject to some minimization criterion. A variety of linkages can be used that define case similarity, which we will survey shortly. Hierarchical clustering can either be agglomerative or divisive. *Agglomerative clustering* begins with single cases and fuses cases together building up the dendrogram. *Divisive clustering* begins with one big cluster and separates into smaller clusters at each step. We only survey the agglomerative case here as it is the most common.

[Figure 14.3](#c14.xhtml#f14.3) is an example of a simple dendrogram where we can easily visualize the history of where cases were joined from the bottom up in the dendrogram. We see that cases 2 and 3 were first fused, followed by the fusing of those cases to case 4. Lastly, this cluster made up of cases 2, 3, and 4 was fused to case 1 to form the largest cluster.

![Figure 14.3 Example of a cluster analysis dendrogram, revealing the history of linkages from the bottom up.Return to text.⏎](images/fig14.3.jpg)

*Figure 14.3* Example of a cluster analysis dendrogram, revealing the history of linkages from the bottom up.[Return to text.⏎](#c14.xhtml#re_f14.3)

As mentioned, there are competing linkages one can use in fusing cases and clusters, including `complete`, `single`, `average`, and `centroid` methods, each having its strengths and weaknesses. In single linkage (also known in the literature as `MIN`), the minimum distance between cases is used as a criterion for fusing, while in complete linkage (also known in the literature as `MAX`) that of the maximal furthest distance between cases is employed. Average linkage, not surprisingly, takes an average of distances between groups. Dendrograms in single and complete linkage are invariant under monotone transformations of pairwise dissimilarities, however this is not true for average linkage ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). These are not the only linkages possible but are the most common ones.

For a good discussion of linkages used in hierarchical clustering, see [Johnson and Wichern (2007)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173). The key point for now is to recognize that employing different linkages could lead to different solutions.

Hierarchical clustering can be expensive computationally because it operates on what are potentially quite large dissimilarity indexes. The slower speed of hierarchical clustering can be considered a drawback of this approach and of agglomerative approaches more generally as they 518can be quite expensive since they require entire distance matrices as inputs. Another drawback to hierarchical approaches is that contrary to `k`-representative clustering, there is usually no clear single optimization criteria or objective that is optimized in the final solution ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). Further, once cases are fused, they cannot be *unfused*. Rather, the user typically has to mostly rely on substantive theory in determining whether a cluster solution is successful.
