
As a result of the covariance matrix incorporating both variances and covariances, we can establish a generalized version of the variance that parallels the univariate case. A measure that is often used to denote a generalized variance in a multivariate setting is the determinant of the covariance matrix, `|S|`. For a visualization of the generalized variance, see Johnson and Wichern ([2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173), p. 125).

It should be emphasized that `|S|` only provides a measure of variance and like `s²` in the univariate setting does not by itself provide any information as to the pattern of the data or direction. That is, without visualization, a report of `|S|` simply gives us a sense of the volume of the space; it does not by itself tell us which directions are most prominent or emphasized in the data. This is not surprising since as mentioned the reporting of a univariate variance `s²` does not either by itself give us information on the particular distances between data objects or points; it only provides an average of those (squared) distances. A generalized variance of `|S|=0` implies the entire space has collapsed onto itself with a corresponding volume equal to 0. A volume greater than zero is indicated by `|S|>0`.

Instead of the determinant of the covariance matrix, we can also conceive of a total variance as the sum of univariate variances along the main diagonal of the covariance matrix,

`s²[11] + s²[22] + ... + s²[pp]`

where `s²[11]` through `s²[pp]` are variances in the corresponding covariance matrix. More concisely, this version of the variance is simply the trace of the covariance matrix. Note that this interpretation does not include covariances and hence is not a true measure of “multivariate” variance in the same spirit as that of the generalized variance, `|S|`.

## 2.42 Matrix Inverse

The inverse of a matrix plays a fundamental role in matrix algebra and parallels the notion of an inverse in ordinary scalar algebra. To understand what an inverse is, consider a simple example from scalar algebra. The inverse of the number 6 is that number which, when multiplied by 6, will equal 1. That number, of course, is ⅙, since ⅙ multiplied by 6 yields 1.

The concept is analogous in matrix algebra. The inverse of matrix `A` is that matrix `A^{-1}` such that `A A^{-1} = I`. For two matrices `B` and `A`, if `B A = I`, then `B` is called the left inverse of `A`. For another matrix `C`, if `A C = I`, then `C` is the right inverse of `A`. An invertible matrix has both a right and a left inverse (which are identical) and hence in this book, whenever we speak of the inverse of a matrix, the distinction between left vs. right inverse is immaterial. In this book, the inverse of a matrix is simply the one and only inverse.

Computing inverses in numerical linear algebra is “big business” and some mathematicians have devoted much of their lives to developing new and better algorithms for approximating inverses. Thankfully, for simple matrices, computing inverses is relatively easy and straightforward. As an example, consider the generic matrix `A`:

$$A = \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} {a\_{11}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, a\_{12}} \\\\ {a\_{21}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, a\_{22}} \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack$$

The inverse of `A` is equal to138

$$A^{- 1} = \\frac{1}{a\_{11}a\_{22} - a\_{12}a\_{21}}\\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} {a\_{22}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, - a\_{12}} \\\\ {- a\_{21}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, a\_{11}} \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack$$

(2.107)

An inverse for a matrix exists only if the determinant of that matrix is unequal to 0. If the determinant is equal to 0, then the inverse does not exist. Hence, for the simple square 2 × 2 matrix `A` above, we require

$$\\begin{array}{l} {A = \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} {a\_{11}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, a\_{12}} \\\\ {a\_{21}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, a\_{22}} \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = a\_{11}a\_{22} - a\_{12}a\_{21} \\neq 0} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

The properties of inverse matrices are many. A few of the more important ones are the following:

`(A^{-1})^{-1} = A`, the inverse of the inverse of a matrix is the original matrix. This parallels what we would expect in simple scalar algebra, since the inverse of 1/`a` is equal to `a` and so the inverse of the inverse of 1/`a` is 1/`a`.

`(A^{T})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{T}`, the inverse of the transpose is equal to the transpose of the inverse.

`(A B)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}`, the inverse of a product is equal to the product of inverses in reverse order.

`|A^{-1}| = 1/|A|`, the determinant of the inverse is equal to the reciprocal of the determinant.

In R, we can get the inverse by the function `solve()`. For example, if `A` is a square matrix, the inverse is computed as `solve(A)`. In Python, we can compute the inverse using `numpy.linalg.inv()`:

```
a = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4]])
```

If `A^{-1}` does not exist, such as with rectangular matrices, a pseudo-inverse may be used known as the Moore-Penrose inverse. For details, see [Rencher and Christensen (2012)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295).

### 2.42.1 Nonexistence of an Inverse and Singularity

To emphasize the point already made, the most important property (or “consequence”) of inverses, for our purposes anyway, is that if the inverse of a matrix does not exist, then it implies that that matrix has a determinant equal to 0 and is thus deemed singular. Hence, it is generally a “big deal” in statistics when a matrix is not invertible. An additional consequence is that a non-invertible matrix also implies a dependency within the matrix; that is, not all rows and columns are linearly independent. We discuss linear independence and dependence shortly. ## 2.43 Quadratic Forms

A quadratic form is nothing more than a polynomial for which all terms (not just some) are of the second degree. An example of a quadratic form is `y = x[]² + z[]²`. The following polynomial `y = x[1] x[2] x[3]` is not a quadratic form since it involves a cubic form ([Hubbard & Hubbard, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000161)). In vectors and matrices, as we will soon elaborate on, an important quadratic form is that of `x' A x`, for which if `x' A x > 0` for all vectors `x ≠ 0`, it indicates that `A` (symmetric) is positive definite. We will come across quadratic forms in one way or another throughout this book mostly implicitly and “behind the scenes.”

## 2.44 Positive Definite Matrices

Referring to the matrix representation of quadratic forms above, `x' A x`, it is easy to see that for `x = 0`, `x' A x = 0`. Now, consider the case where `x' A x ≥ 0`, where `A` is a symmetric nonzero matrix (since zero matrices are, by definition, also symmetric). As already noted when discussing quadratic forms, if the only way for equality to hold for `x' A x ≥ 0` is for `x` to be the zero vector, then `A` is called positive definite, which implies that the quadratic form `x' A x` will be positive, that is, `x' A x > 0`.
