
Is the above question novel in any way? Absolutely not. What we are about to survey with the maximum margin classifier and support vector machine *is* new, but the question these techniques seek to answer is definitely not. That is, the *concept* of hyperplanes that are optimized by some criteria is the same question we asked in least-squares regression, discriminant analysis, and other techniques. In this chapter, we simply survey a novel way of classification based on what we may more generally call *the hyperplane problem*.

The answer to this question is that we will choose the *maximal margin hyperplane*, also known as the *optimal separating hyperplane*, which, in the sense we will define shortly, is the hyperplane *farthest* from the training observations. That is, *it is the hyperplane that maximizes the margin of the data to the hyperplane*. Of course, this definition is vague and we need to define its criteria more precisely. With a single point on either side of a hyperplane, maximizing the margin would simply equate to maximizing the distance between the hyperplane and the points, as shown in [Figure 9.3](#c9.xhtml#f9.3).354

![Figure 9.3 A hyperplane maximizing the distance between two observations.Return to text.⏎](images/fig9.3.jpg)

*Figure 9.3* A hyperplane maximizing the distance between two observations.[Return to text.⏎](#c9.xhtml#re_f9.3)

The difficulty, of course, occurs when our data consist of more than two points, such as when we have many observations as in [Figure 9.4](#c9.xhtml#f9.4).

![Figure 9.4 Hyperplane separating two groups consisting of several data points, introducing the problem of deciding which points should have the most influence in deciding the placement of the hyperplane.Return to text.⏎](images/fig9.4.jpg)

*Figure 9.4* Hyperplane separating two groups consisting of several data points, introducing the problem of deciding which points should have the most influence in deciding the placement of the hyperplane.[Return to text.⏎](#c9.xhtml#re_f9.4)

The question becomes how to best determine a “maximal margin” since there are so many competing points and we cannot simply compute a Euclidean distance in best separating two points. We will operationalize in a moment what is described best by James et al. ([2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000169), p. 341):

> We compute the perpendicular distance from each training observation to a given separating hyperplane; the smallest such distance is the minimal distance from the observations to the hyperplane, and is known as the margin. The maximal margin hyperplane is the separating hyperplane for which the margin is largest, that is, it is the hyperplane that has the farthest minimum distance to the training observations … In a sense, the maximal margin hyperplane represents the mid-line of the widest “slab” that we can insert between the two classes.355

[Figure 9.5](#c9.xhtml#f9.5) is a depiction of the “widest slab” generated by the maximal margin classifier.

![Figure 9.5 Depiction of the “widest slab” concept in determining support vectors.Return to text.⏎](images/fig9.5.jpg)

*Figure 9.5* Depiction of the “widest slab” concept in determining support vectors.[Return to text.⏎](#c9.xhtml#re_f9.5)

Notice that the placement of the hyperplane generates the widest margin. The points, or vectors that determine this widest margin are called *support vectors* since they determine the position of the hyperplane; that is, they “support” the placement of the maximal margin hyperplane. *Movement of the support vectors will have the effect of moving the hyperplane*. Hence, the support vectors themselves define the placement of the hyperplane and are thus extremely important in determining its location. *No other points in the plot are actually determining the placement of the hyperplane*.

The fact that only the support vectors determine the location of the hyperplane, and no other points, is of great consequence. Consider for a moment how this differs from ordinary least squares (`OLS`) regression or discriminant analysis. In both of those methods, *all points in the training data contributed in determining the placement of the hyperplane*. The concept of support vectors, however, relies on only a *few points or vectors*. We can already tentatively conclude then that hyperplanes determined by support vectors are more *resistant* to outlying data points than hyperplanes that “average” the data in some sense. To illustrate the point, consider the plot in [Figure 9.6](#c9.xhtml#f9.6), specifically the circled points.356

![Figure 9.6 Movement of a single data point (represented by the arrow) does not influence the placement of the hyperplane unless that data point is a support vector.Return to text.⏎](images/fig9.6.jpg)

*Figure 9.6* Movement of a single data point (represented by the arrow) does not influence the placement of the hyperplane unless that data point is a support vector.[Return to text.⏎](#c9.xhtml#re_f9.6)

Notice that the movement of the point circled from the lower right to a higher location has had zero effect on the hyperplane. If the hyperplane were defined more in the sense of `OLS` regression or discriminant analysis, the hyperplane should necessarily *shift* due to the shifting point. The maximum margin classifier, however, is insensitive or *robust* to any points not going by the name *support vectors*. This is not to suggest outliers cannot have a significant influence on the *placement* of the maximal margin classifier; they surely can. However, once the support vectors are determined, a change in position of any other points will not move the placement of the maximum margin classifier *unless such has an impact on the placement of the support vectors*.

### 9.1.2 Building the Maximal Margin Classifier

To reiterate, when we built the least-squares regression line or the discriminant function, both respective hyperplanes in their own right, we *maximized* or *minimized* some function of the data in a basic problem of optimization. In building the maximum margin classifier, we must likewise estimate parameters, once more for `β[0]` and `β[1]` through `β[p]` just as we did in regression and discriminant analysis. However, in doing so, we will impose some constraints. We will wish to maximize the margin `M` subject to the following conditions or constraints,

$${\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{p}\\beta\_{j}^{2}} = 1$$  

(9.6)

and,357

`y[i](β[0]+β[1]x[i1]+β[2]x[i2]+⋅⋅⋅+β[p]x[ip]) ≥ M`

(9.7)

where `M=1/||β||`. The solution can be obtained via *convex optimization* ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). As the reader may recall from earlier in the book, convex optimization is simply the process of optimizing a function that has a *bowl-like* shape, which implies that finding a global minimum is achievable without the algorithm getting “stuck” in local minima. It is worth asking, however, what the above constraints guarantee. The two constraints assure us that each observation `y[i]` will be on the correct side of the hyperplane, so long as the margin, `M`, is positive ([James et al., 2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000169)). The maximal margin classifier is a useful and efficient way to perform classification if a separating hyperplane exists, that is, if we can place a hyperplane such that both classes are completely separated. If we cannot do this, such that the best hyperplane we could draw would not separate points completely, then we need to build on the maximum margin idea but allow for some flexibility. We consider this possibility next.

## 9.2 The Case of No Separating Hyperplane

There are two situations to consider here. First, suppose a separating hyperplane does exist. Even if it does, it can be tremendously sensitive to movement of one or more of the support vectors, as depicted in [Figure 9.7](#c9.xhtml#f9.7).
