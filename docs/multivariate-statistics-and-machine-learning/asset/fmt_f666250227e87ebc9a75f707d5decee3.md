
The multivariate density is significant for many reasons, most of which parallel the reasons for the popularity of the univariate density in statistics and science more generally. However, there is an added feature of importance as regards the multivariate density, and that is, if we can assume multivariate normality, we can be assured that *any linear combination of the random vector* `X` *is also normally distributed*. While this may seem like an intuitive result, it has huge implications for statistical inference in multivariate models. The theorem also implies that conditional distributions will be multivariate normal as well.

## 2.15.5 Evaluating Multivariate Normality

Assessing multivariate normality is exponentially more difficult than assessing univariate normality due to it being a higher-dimensional problem. The best advice for such evaluation is probably given by Johnson and Wichern ([2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173), p. 177):

> It will become clear that our investigations of normality will concentrate on the behavior of the observations in one or two dimensions (for example, marginal distributions and scatter plots). As might be expected, it has proved difficult to construct a “good” overall test of joint normality in more than two dimensions because of the large number of things that can go wrong. To some extent, we must pay a price for concentrating on univariate and bivariate examinations of normality: We can never be sure that we have not missed some feature that is revealed only in higher dimensions … for most practical work, one-dimensional and two-dimensional investigations are ordinarily sufficient. Fortunately, pathological data sets that are normal in lower dimensional representations, but nonnormal in higher dimensions, are not frequently encountered in practice.83

Still, there are tests that can be used to evaluate normality, even in the multivariate domain. A generalization of the Shapiro-Wilk test can be used, as well as a more recent test called the *Henze-Zirkler test*. See [Zhou and Shao (2014)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000405) for details. The consequence of not ensuring multivariate normality is that the asymptotic distribution of many statistical tests becomes much more complicated ([Bilodeau & Brenner, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000030)). Recall that the asymptotic distribution is that obtained as `n`→∞.

In practice, researchers often use visualization and graphical techniques to verify univariate (e.g., *Q-Q plots*, *histograms*, etc.) and bivariate normality (e.g., scatterplots), and if such are more or less *approximately* satisfied, they then make the inferential “leap” to assuming that multivariate normality holds as well. However, it needs to be emphasized that while multivariate normality implies bivariate and univariate normality, the reverse is not necessarily true. That is, univariate and bivariate normality does not automatically imply multivariate normality. Hence the aforementioned “leap” required. Univariate and bivariate normality may increase the probability of multivariate normality, but it does not necessarily imply it.

## 2.16 Binomial Distributions

When a variable has a binary outcome, such as pass vs. fail, and whose probability of a success is constant or *stationary* over a series of trials, the binomial distribution is a plausible distribution for modeling such data. A random variable `x` is binomially distributed if `x` can take on one of two binary-valued numbers. For convenience, we denote these numbers 0 and 1; however in theory they could be any two numbers. We assume that 1 occurs with probability `π` and 0 occurs with probability 1 − `π`, where of course `π` is bound by limits 0 and 1 as is true of all probabilities.

The binomial density is given by

$$p\\left( x;n,\\pi \\right) = \\frac{n!}{x!\\left( n - x \\right)!}\\pi^{x}\\left( 1 - \\pi \\right)^{n - x}$$

(2.25)

where `x` is the number of outcomes that we are modeling, `n` is the total number of trials, and `π` is the probability of a success on any given trial. A classic example of the binomial in practice is modeling the probability of getting 2 heads out of 5 flips on a fair coin (i.e., where “fair” implies `π` = 0.5). This probability is easily computed,

$$\\begin{array}{l} {\\,\\, p\\left( x;n,\\pi \\right) = \\frac{n!}{x!\\left( n - x \\right)!}\\pi^{x}\\left( 1 - \\pi \\right){n - x}} \\\\ {p\\left( 2;5,0.5 \\right) = \\frac{5!}{2!\\left( 5 - 2 \\right)!}\\left( 0.5 \\right)2\\left( 1 - 0.5 \\right){5 - 2}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = 0.3125} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

Knowing that the probability of getting 2 heads out of 5 flips of a fair coin is equal to 0.3125, or about 31% of the time, such can help us evaluate the null hypothesis that a given coin is fair. Surely, if the coin is producing 2 heads out of 5 flips, this would give us no reason to suspect the coin is *not fair*. On the other hand, had the coin produced, say, 0 heads out of 5 flips, such would have yielded a probability of 0.03125. That is, the probability of getting zero heads out of five flips on a fair coin is quite low. It is so low that it might suggest we reject the null hypothesis that the coin is fair and infer the alternative that it is not, or at minimum, that the probability of heads is *not* equal to 0.5.84

We can compute binomial probabilities very easily in R. For the case of 2 heads out of 5 flips with a stationary success set at 0.5, the probability is computed as

```
> dbinom(2, 5, 0.5)
[1] 0.3125
```

The mean of a binomial distribution is `nπ`, that is, the number of trials multiplied by the probability of success on any given trial. For the above data, this number is 5(0.5) = 2.5. That is, on 5 flips, we would expect an average of 2.5 heads. Of course, since the variable is a discrete one, this interpretation should be made with caution as it is not possible to obtain “fractions” of heads. Recall that averages do not care about fractions. The mean number of persons per household may very well be 3.4, impossible as this number is for human beings (or any discrete objects, for that matter).

The variance of a binomial random variable is equal to `nπ(1−π)`. For example, the variance of a binomial distribution with probability `π` = 0.5, and as before `n` = 5, is equal to `nπ(1−π)` = 5(0.5)(1−0.5) = 2.5(0.5) = 1.25, with standard deviation (square root) equal to 1.118.

### 2.16.1 Approximation to the Normal Distribution

The binomial distribution approximates the normal distribution for large number of trials, and in the limit, converges to the normal distribution. We can easily demonstrate in R how increasing `n` approximates normality via the plots in [Figure 2.5](#c2.xhtml#f2.5).
