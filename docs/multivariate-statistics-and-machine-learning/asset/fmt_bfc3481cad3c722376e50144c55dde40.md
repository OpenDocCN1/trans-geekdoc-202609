
Other examples of this effect abound (see [Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259), p. 42 for examples). In short, *visualization is a must for good data analysis and exploration, not an option*. Let’s put this another way; if you are not visualizing your data in at least *some* capacity, you are usually conducting a *poor data analysis*. There are special cases where one may be able to circumvent visualization, but the point is that visualization will tell you things about your data that numerical summaries may never. Numerical summaries alone will never provide the widest or most complete picture or understanding of the data, and as we have said already ad nauseam by now, and will continue to repeat throughout the book, all variables exist in a wider *context* of other variables. Visualization helps provide a description of that context, which is why so much effort is put toward developing new visualization techniques in statistics. As we will see later in relation to manifold learning, multivariate visualization can be especially challenging and difficult, and as the dimensionality of the data increases, can be virtually impossible.

## 2.9 Sensitivity and Specificity: More Conditional Probabilities

Another area where conditional probabilities are featured and used is when determining the sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic tests. A diagnostic test is a *sign*. It is a piece of *data*. It is a moment of *experience*. It is not a deterministic indication that the disease or condition is present. A classic and modern example of this is diagnosing COVID-19. A diagnostic test may indicate the presence of the virus, but there is also associated with that a probability that the disease is not present. A test that detects the disease when the disease is present is called the *sensitivity* of the test. A test that fails to detect a disease that is not present is called the *specificity* of the test. Good tests have strong sensitivity and specificity though the two will seldom, if ever, be perfect or equal to one another.

Of course, we desire any test to have maximum sensitivity and maximum specificity. That is the ideal we shoot for. Where things go wrong with diagnostic tests is when a test indicates the virus or condition is present when the virus or condition is not present. This is known as a *false positive*, or more commonly in the statistical literature as a *Type I error* in hypothesis testing. On the other hand, a test that fails to detect a virus when the virus is present has committed a *Type II error*. 72Which is the worst error? Which error has the greater consequence? Such, of course, depends, again, on the *context of the problem*. Context matters! Again! Which is most important depends on which type of error the scientist wishes to minimize. Surely, we do not want to misdiagnose the virus. Yet at the same time, diagnosing the virus when it is not present can lead to many problems, such as emotional distress, financial costs, the inconvenience of further testing, etc. Indeed, this has been one of the controversies surrounding habitual mammogram testing, for example; some argue that due to the error rates involved, yearly mammograms may be doing more harm than good. The risks of diagnosing a disease when none exists are real and can have great consequences. At the same time, missing a condition when the condition is present can likewise have serious consequences. Which error is more consequential largely depends on the situation, context, and the inclination of the scientist or subject.

## 2.10 Bayes’ Theorem and Conditional Probabilities

There is perhaps no better or more important of an example of conditional probabilities as with Bayes’ theorem. Recall that we mentioned at the outset of this chapter that the two primary camps in statistics are those of the frequentist and subjective perspectives. Maximum likelihood estimation, the most common method for estimating parameters, generally stems from and is governed by a frequentist approach in which the probability of the data given a parameter is evaluated, that of `p(y|x)`, where here `y` is the data and `x` is the parameter (in place of the notation `θ` for our discussion here). The conditional probability `p(y|x)` is referred to in frequentist probability as the *likelihood*.

In Bayesian reasoning, the probability of the parameter given the data is instead evaluated, that of `p(x|y)`. Bayes’ theorem inverts conditional probabilities from `p(y|x)` to `p(x|y)`:

$$p\\left( x \\middle| y \\right) = \\frac{p\\left( y \\middle| x \\right)p\\left( x \\right)}{p\\left( y \\right)}$$

(2.16)

where `p(y|x)`, as before, is called the *likelihood*, `p(x)` is the *prior probability*, and `p(x|y)` is the *posterior probability*. The denominator, `p(y)` is the *total probability* of the data, or evidence more generally.

An even more meaningful way to interpret Baye’s theorem is by replacing `x` with `T` (theory) and `y` with `D` (data):

$$\\begin{array}{l} {\\, p\\left( x \\middle| y \\right) = \\frac{p\\left( y \\middle| x \\right)p\\left( x \\right)}{p\\left( y \\right)}} \\\\ {p\\left( T \\middle| D \\right) = \\frac{p\\left( D \\middle| T \\right)p\\left( T \\right)}{p\\left( D \\right)}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

That is, `p(T|D)` is the probability of the theory given the evidence or data. The difficulty with Baye’s theorem is that one must enter a *prior probability*, which can be quite subjective in nature. Note that no matter the strength of the data, if the prior probability `p(T)` is equal to 0, then the posterior probability will also equal 0, since,73

$$\\begin{array}{l} {p\\left( T \\middle| D \\right) = \\frac{p\\left( D \\middle| T \\right)\\left( 0 \\right)}{p\\left( D \\right)}} \\\\ {p\\left( T \\middle| D \\right) = 0} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

The big idea in Bayesian analysis is that contrary to frequentist approaches where parameters are regarded as fixed constants, Bayesian analysis treats parameters as *random variables*.

### 2.10.1 Bayes Factor

In the case where we have two competing models, `M[0]` and `M[1]`, we can form the *Bayes factor*, which is a ratio of likelihoods of the data under these two hypothetical conditions:

$$B = \\frac{p\\left( D \\middle| M\_{1} \\right)}{p\\left( D \\middle| M\_{0} \\right)}$$

(2.17)

where `p(D|M[1])` is the probability of the data under the condition of the alternative hypothesis, `M[1]`, and `p(D|M[0])` is the probability of the data under the condition of the null hypothesis, `M[0]`. If the two likelihoods are the same, then we would, obviously, expect `B` to equal 1, whereas if `M[1]` is preferred over `M[0]` then we would expect `B` > 1. The Bayes factor provides a competing alternative to classical frequentist statistics that focuses on the `p-value` as a means of making a decision between the two states. According to Jeffreys, a Bayes factor greater than 10 indicates strong evidence for `M[1]` while a Bayes factor greater than 100 indicates exceptionally strong evidence for `M[1]` ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). However, as always, the size of the ratio should be evaluated, at least in part, relative to the context of the investigation. A Bayes factor of 2 in some research settings may be considered relatively large, whereas a Bayes factor of 10 in another research setting may be considered trivially small. Have we mentioned before that context matters?

Suppose we run a host of competing models and wish to select the “best” one in some sense. The qualifier “in some sense” is necessary here, as selecting a “best” model is in no way a straightforward task and will usually depend on much more than a single criterion for selection.
