Up to now in our discussion of modeling, we have assumed the response variable is more or less *continuous*, or at least “pseudo-continuous,” since no empirical variables are ever truly continuous in the idealistic mathematical sense. Continuity itself is an *abstraction*, a philosophical idea that is expressed mathematically using such things as epsilon-delta definitions in real analysis. More pragmatically, we have assumed the response exhibits a rather wide range of “dense” values and the goal has been to predict a value of the response along this continuum based on one or more predictors. While many of these models have been linear, we have also surveyed a number of nonlinear models. However, in all of these models, response variables have all been assumed to be continuous and treated as such in our analyses. There are many instances in modeling where the response variable is not continuous but is instead *categorical*. Such responses contain mutually exclusive categories instead of a continuum of numerical possibilities. Some responses having this property are naturally occurring (e.g., pass vs. fail), while others can be operationalized by the user into specific categories (e.g., numerical score on a test classified into the binary outcome of pass vs. fail).

How can statistical models deal with binary or categorical responses? Thanks to the theory of the *generalized linear model* ([McCullagh & Nelder, 1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000243)), binary and polytomous responses can be situated in the same general framework that we can find classic linear models. The generalized linear model contains the entire family of these models and is the topic of this chapter.

Before surveying the generalized linear model, we should ask an important question from the outset: *Is “binning” a response a good idea*? What are the advantages and disadvantages of doing so? We begin this chapter with a brief survey of the benefits and drawbacks of operationalizing the response as a binary or polytomous outcome.

## 8.1 How to Operationalize the Response

As mentioned, some response variables are naturally categorical. For instance, a salmon fish is either going “upstream” or “downstream,” and cannot be going both (though as we have all seen, they can just seemingly wade in the water without direction at times). When responses take on this quality, leaving the variable “as is” and treating it as binary is an easy decision since leaving it *as is* maintains it in its natural *measured* state. In other cases, however, the researcher may actually choose to bin the variable into mutually exclusive categories. In the machine learning literature, the pseudo-continuity of a variable before “binning” it is sometimes referred to as a *pre-classifier* (Kroese et al., [2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000202)) because it is relatively continuous before forcing those values into categories.309

### 8.1.1 Pros and Cons of Binning

Whether or not to bin a variable is an important decision. Indeed, different operationalizations of a single variable can lead the resulting statistical model to “discover” different effects. As a simple example, the variable *grade* on a class test operationalized as *pass* vs. *fail* may be predictive of final grade, but operationalized as a numerical score may alter the predictive ability. The variable is the same, but how we have operationalized it is now different. What is more, the choice of how to operationalize a variable in analysis of variance (ANOVA) can influence the resulting mean squares and ultimately the rejection of the null hypothesis. There is a whole literature on the influence of operationalizing effects in ANOVA (for example, see [Prentice & Miller, 1992](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000292)).

### 8.1.2 Detecting New Classes or Categories

While we have discussed the pros and cons of binning, it is worth mentioning as well that in machine learning, there are also techniques that actually allow the algorithm to detect new classes or categories based on the algorithm’s examination of the data. Such is referred to as *novelty detection* in machine learning circles, so that instead of classifying cases in one of `C[t]` classes, a new class label `C[t + 1]` is derived not by the researcher’s judgment or decision of how to bin, but from the actual algorithm discovering the new category. This process sometimes goes by the general concept of *open set recognition* ([Murray, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). In this book, we do not survey methods that detect new categories. We assume the researcher is actively deciding how to operationalize their variables.
