## 5.11 Further Directions and a Final Word of Warning on Mediation and Moderation

Mediation and moderation in statistics is an enormous topic with books devoted to these methods (e.g., see [Hayes, 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000147)). Recent work on mediation includes that of [Ge et al. (2023)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000124) who develop a reinforcement learning framework for *dynamic mediation analysis*. In many cases, treatments are assigned over time and this work seeks to evaluate mediators along a longitudinal trajectory as well as sequential decision-making over an infinite number of decision points. [Pearl (2012)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000283) critically assesses the habitual way of performing mediation, which assumes linearity and independence of errors, and proposes an alternative approach for modeling nonlinear relationships, which includes that with categorical variables. Judd et al. ([2001](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000178)) give details on estimating mediation and moderation models in within-subjects designs.

As mentioned at the outset of our discussion, however, mediation and moderation, if used and more importantly, *interpreted* incorrectly, can do more harm than good. Far too often in the social sciences are mediational and moderation processes concluded simply because statistical evidence supports it.

To reiterate, and as any good methodologist or philosopher of science will tell you, *no statistical method, whatever the kind, can alone indicate a physical process* (Fiedler et al., [2011](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000098)). The problem with using these “power” and “action” words to describe statistical processes is *dangerous*. “Causal modeling” as mediational analysis has sometimes come to be known, is not causal at all unless there is a substantive *context* to induce such causality. There is absolutely nothing wrong with conducting a mediation or moderation analysis, but if more researchers actually understood how *little* it truly tells them about their data instead of recklessly imposing *powers* on their variables that they do not 247*necessarily* possess, the methods would probably be used less than they currently are. *It behooves all researchers to understand the limitations of the statistical procedures they use before using and interpreting them on empirical data. Researchers regularly “empower” statistics to say much more from their data than they are able. Mathematical and statistical models are first and foremost abstractions; they have no knowledge of your empirical observations, they are simply using those numbers as inputs in their computations. Whether the statistical model says anything substantive about those empirical observations is a much more complex scientific and philosophical issue than simply fitting the statistical model. Narratives rarely correspond to “reality” one-to-one.*

## 5.12 Principal Components Regression

Recall that in the typical multiple regression problem, a linear combination of predictors `x[1]` through `x[p]` is sought such that it maximizes the correlation with the response variable, `y`. This maximum correlation is expressed via fit statistics such as `R²`. As we know already, multicollinearity can have detrimental effects on a regression model. Having several predictors that have high correlations with one another can, for one, *inflate standard errors*. Inflated standard errors reduce statistical power for detecting effects and hence make it more difficult to reject null hypotheses for predictors.

Statistical issues are not the only reason for disliking collinearity. From a scientific point of view, having correlated predictors is a waste of resources. For example, recording your birth date in months and then again in days is a waste of time, as one variable essentially does the job of two. They are essentially duplicates of one another. More precisely, one variable is a linear combination of the other. In such a case, the most likely solution is to simply drop one of the variables from the regression and simplify the model, both to eliminate or reduce collinearity but also to substantively reduce the scientific complexity of the model.

There are alternatives to simply eliminating variables from the set of predictors, however. One relatively popular alternative approach to this problem is to reduce the dimensionality of `x[1]` through `x[p]` *before* making a prediction on `y`. Especially if `p` (the number of predictors) is very large, it may make more sense to transform `p` into a smaller number of *components* and then regress `y` onto this smaller number of components. *Principal components regression* attempts to do just this. To understand principal components regression, however, we must first get an idea of what *principal components analysis* is all about.
