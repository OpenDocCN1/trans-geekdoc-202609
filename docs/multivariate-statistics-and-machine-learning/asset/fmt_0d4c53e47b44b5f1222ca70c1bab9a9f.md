
There can be said to be two ways of learning the material of multivariate statistics and machine learning (Deisenroth et al., [2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000080)). There are risks inherent in both approaches. The first approach is what we will call a *top-down approach*, where students are instructed from the highest and most general “overview” global perspective on what these methods accomplish and how they can be used. For example, when teaching principal components analysis, one could instruct students that `PCA` is designed to assess the dimensionality of the variables subjected to analysis. The description, though partially correct, is at the same time grossly incomplete. A *bottom-up approach*, on the other hand, would be that of beginning with fundamental mathematics such as essential ideas of vectors, matrices, and most importantly, linear combinations, which quite naturally leads into the definition of what a component actually *is*, that of the aforesaid *linear combination*. But again, quite incomplete without a blending of the aforementioned top-down perspective regarding the “big picture” from the top of the mountain, which is *dimension reduction*.

As an instructor, given that we only have a limited amount of time with students (or readers), we want them to take away the most essential *lessons* that they will carry with them for a *lifetime* and provide for them a *foundation* on which they can generalize and extend their abilities and understanding. We do not want them to get lost in the trees and not recognize the forest. The top-down approach with regard to `PCA` (as just one example of a technique) is incomplete of course because it neglects to *contextualize* `PCA` as *one* such technique for assessing underlying dimensionality, and of those techniques, only assesses *linear dimensions*. `PCA`, put into proper context, is but a special case of a wide variety of dimension-reduction techniques. The danger with so-called “cookbook” approaches to statistics and data analysis is that a student could come away from a course in multivariate statistics believing that `PCA` is *the one and only* technique for dimension reduction, which would be blatantly false. Not only does it deprive the student of learning, it is embarrassing for them to discuss or present the method in a public forum if they are asked about the method to any degree and hold fast and true that `PCA` is *equivalent* to dimension reduction rather than simply one *option* for it.

Hence, even if one begins with a top-down approach, either in teaching or learning, one should quickly get to the “bottom” to make these important distinctions, and the further the “bottom” we go, the more we get into the technical details, differentiations and distinctions, right up to necessary qualifiers such as *the assumption that we are discussing only applies to real numbers*, as an example. That’s probably too far, but the point is that students should be taught proper *context* in learning statistics and machine learning.

> *Much of our thinking is grounded in metaphor and the art of substituting symbols, whether those symbols be words, pictures, or even musical tones.*

Equally, the problem with a *bottom-up approach* is that there can be so much focus spent on distinctions that the student may never grasp the bigger picture that `PCA` (to use the same example) is a dimension-reduction technique in the first place and how it can be applied to address scientific questions. After a semester of vectors, matrices, and other mathematics which may or may not include proofs, the student could feasibly come away from the course not truly understanding in any global way *what PCA is for*, *when it can (and cannot) be used*, and *what the* *big picture* *is* behind it. I have had first-hand experience in meeting many students who have had calculus, linear algebra, some statistics under their belts, yet who are unable to have a discussion about what these subjects are truly *about* because they were too busy intaking technical computational detail and 10solving problems blindly or by rote computational heuristics via planned and patterned “problem sets” that they never quite grasped the bigger picture of what they were even “doing.” *Zero insight into the bigger picture*.

The pedagogical approach adopted in this book is to be mindful of the bottom-up, top-down distinction and not emphasize one *too much* at the expense of the other.

### 0.5.2 Ways of Writing a Book: Making It Pedagogical Instead of Cryptic

Perhaps the best way to become an overnight genius is to write a book nobody understands then claim authority over the knowledge! That is one approach to writing a book, albeit a poor one and very egoistic. Alternatively, one can write a book that many if not most understand and become known as a great communicator of challenging material. We have all come across mathematics texts that were “unreadable,” and the only audience for the book was between the ears of the author, while at the same time we have come across books that are far too general and appealing to too wide of an audience as to entirely misrepresent the subject.

Mathematics (or at minimum, mathematical *symbolism*) should not be avoided in a book like this, yet at the same time making such mathematics purposely *cryptic* benefits nobody. Rigor should not be avoided in teaching, but students should be instructed on *why* such rigor exists, the importance of differentiating concepts instead of blanketing them in imperfect understanding. The student should be given the opportunity to appreciate why rigor is prized by the mathematician or analytical philosopher in the first place.

### 0.5.3 Standing on the Shoulders of Giants: A Companion to Advanced Texts

Aside from this book being a stand-alone introductory textbook and reference to multivariate statistics and machine learning, it can also be thought of as a *companion* to understanding other texts based on the same or similar topics. The author’s aim was to make this text stand alone in its own right, but also be a “gateway” to understanding what the “next level” books discuss, so that the reader may follow up from this book and get into those more advanced texts with relative (or at least *more*) ease.

For example, if you are a newcomer to statistical learning, understanding existing standards in the field such as *Elements of Statistical Learning* (Hastie et al., [2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)), *Probabilistic Machine Learning* ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)) or *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning* ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)) can be at minimum difficult and daunting and at most impossible without a good teacher at your side. One of the aims of this book is to be that *companion*, that *teacher* to provide an interpretation of the concepts discussed in those higher-level books. With our emphasis on foundations, our hope is that the reader can then excursion into more advanced sources as well as begin perusing the research literature themselves having a foundational base from which to grow.
