
This chapter has been about dimension-reduction techniques, that is, to find a lower-dimensional embedding from higher-dimensional data. Dimension reduction can be seen as a special case of the wider `blind source separation` or `manifold learning` paradigm. The focus of the chapter was first on principal components analysis since it is the simplest of the dimension-reduction techniques, the most used, and understanding it is the gateway to understanding extensions of the PCA approach as well as more modern techniques for dimension reduction.

In the following chapter, we will discuss yet another approach to dimension reduction, that of the `exploratory factor analysis model`. Though we have mentioned exploratory factor analysis in the current chapter, it is such an extensive and popular technique that it merits its own chapter.

PCA has its origins with Pearson in 1901 and Hotelling in 1933. As discussed in the chapter, a limitation of PCA is that it can only extract `linear components` and also can be sensitive to multivariate outliers. `Robust PCA` ([Locantore et al., 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000228)) and `Expectation Robust PCA` ([Serneels & Verdonck, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000332)) have been proposed as a means for dealing with outlying data. Other methods of PCA include `Local PCA`, `Generalized PCA`, and `Multi-linear PCA`. `Principal Components Analysis` by [Jolliffe (2002)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000174) is perhaps misnamed, as it provides an extensive survey of not only PCA but also many other techniques related to PCA. These include `discriminant analysis`, `canonical correlation`, and even `neural networks`.

In our survey of dimension reduction and manifold learning, it is important to realize that we have merely skimmed the surface. As is the case with most statistical and machine learning topics, entire books and monographs have been written on the topic, many of which we have not had time or space to treat in this book. For instance, `latent semantic analysis` (LSA) is yet another dimension-reduction technique, this time for finding an embedding of words to decipher semantic meaning and thus specifically designed for text data. It is essentially an SVD on text data. As an example, suppose an author uses a group of words that have a “dark” tone to them. Do these words have an underlying dimension? LSA might be useful in uncovering such dimensionality. Many of these techniques are in one way or another related to SVD, and much statistical research has been targeted toward demonstrating parallels and equivalencies.

`Non-negative matrix factorization` is another dimension-reduction technique that once more creates a new basis for data that is originally in high dimensions. Such a basis is also guaranteed to be non-negative, which is where NMF gets its name. That is, the components and coefficients are non-negative ([Müller](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000258) & Guido, 2017). However, in NMF, the basis will not necessarily be orthonormal as is the case in PCA, however orthogonal versions of NMF have also been proposed ([Yang & Xu, 2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000397)). However, like PCA, “vanilla” NMF is restricted to uncovering only linear dimensions. Essentially, NMF reduces the data down to a non-negative weighted sum and may lead to more interpretable components than PCA. The procedure is effective at providing semantic interpretability for text analysis and dimension reduction (see [Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005), p. 191).

At a more advanced level, sometimes manifolds contain loops (e.g., torus or circle) in them which makes traditional dimensionality much more difficult, if not prohibitively challenging. Techniques for reducing the dimensionality of such complex manifolds have been suggested ([Lee & Verleysen, 2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000214)). For an excellent overview of various dimension-reduction techniques and where current developments stand, see [Nanga](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000263) et al. (2021).459

In the previous chapter, we surveyed the method of principal components analysis, or PCA, as well as many extensions, derivatives, and special cases of the PCA method, including nonlinear approaches. The theme of all of these techniques was that of `dimension reduction` and could be considered special cases of an even more general problem, that of `manifold learning`. Whether it was principal components, multidimensional scaling, or other reduction technique, the overarching goal was to learn something about the hypothesized underlying lower-dimensional manifold that might subsume the observed data. That is, the goal was to seek out a lower-dimensional `embedding`. The purpose of PCA, in particular, was to uncover dimensionality in the variables by transforming from one `basis` to another such that the new variables (called `components`) have desirable properties, the most significant of which was to maximize variance. The first principal component was that which maximized the variance in the variables, and each component thereafter maximized a decreasing proportion of the variance. Components were constrained to be `orthogonal` to one another and the hope was to explain most of the variance in the variables by the first few components. Other techniques such as `independent components analysis` (ICA) held the stronger property that components be independent and not only uncorrelated. ICA was useful for such things as extracting the independent sources of variation from signals, speech, or images and proved especially useful for so-called `blind source separation` problems. Both classical PCA and ICA are examples of dimension-reduction techniques such that we believe the true (or at least our working hypothesis) dimensionality of the data lies in a lower `subspace` than in the original empirical representation. We noted that if the true dimensionality, or true manifold underlying the data was not linear, then a classical PCA could actually mislead more than it could enlighten. In this respect, PCA does not uncover *the* dimensionality underlying a set of data; it uncovers the `linear` dimensionality.
