## 4.3 Random-Effects and Mixed Models

As we have said, the ANOVA we have surveyed thus far in this chapter has assumed that levels on the independent variable are *fixed*. To reiterate, in theoretical repetitions of an experiment, it was assumed we would be using the same levels each time. This is why it was called a *fixed-effects analysis of variance*.

When we do not assume levels are fixed but instead *random samples* of a population of possible levels, the factor is no longer fixed but instead *random*, which gives rise to the *random-effects analysis of variance model*. For example, if one were interested in the effects of dosage on headache symptoms, in a fixed-effects model, one could purposely and strategically select levels 1 vs. 2 vs. 3 mg of drug. The advantage of this approach is that once we obtain means, we can compare mean differences across the drug groups. The disadvantage of this approach is that we have no knowledge of differences between other possible levels of drug (e.g., 1.5 vs. 2.3, for instance). Is variance in headaches a function of these other levels as well? Under the fixed model comparing 1 vs. 2 vs. 3 mg, we simply do not know. The comparison of means does not allow us to infer such things. However, if instead we designate drug to be a *random effect*, and selected the levels 1, 2, 3 or others *randomly*, then if the factor is found to be statistically significant, we can conclude differences not only between doses 1, 2, and 3, but instead on *drug doses in general*, which includes levels not observed in the sample. This is the benefit of random effects; they allow us to conclude that levels not seen in the sample are also associated with *variation* in the response variable. *Random effects allow for a more general statement*.

When a model contains a blending of fixed and random factors, it is referred to as a *mixed model*. The classic general form of the linear mixed model is given as follows:

*Y* = *X**β* + *Z**U* + *ε*  

(4.14)

where `β` are the fixed parameters and `U` the random, and where `U` and `ε` are independent zero mean random vectors. Both the random effects in `U` and `ε` are assumed to be normally distributed. The assumption that `U` is normally distributed may be unrealistic, however, and hence an alternative nonparametric random-effects model may be more appropriate. We survey that possibility a bit later in this section. For an excellent overview of both parametric and nonparametric random-effects and mixed models, see [Nguéma et al. (2021)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000268).

In the model in (4.3), for fixed factors, mean comparisons are of interest. For the random factor, variance on the response variable is what is being modeled. Simple as this brief summary may sound, mixed models become theoretically complex very fast. In fact, books (and careers) are devoted to the subject. See [Demidenko (2004)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000084) for a book-length treatment on the complexity of mixed models.201

### 4.3.1 The Meaning of a Fixed vs. Random Effect

A precise definition of fixed vs. random effects is critical. As we will discuss shortly, there is sometimes a misunderstanding in the literature that random effects are defined by simply having numerous statistics such as intercepts and slopes of which we would like to take an average. For example, consider Figure 4.2, where there are numerous intercepts which might represent starting points of trajectories for given individuals in a longitudinal study, as well as numerous slopes that emphasize a difference in trajectories over time. We see that there is quite a bit of variation in both intercepts and slopes in the sample.

![Figure 4.2 Plot with several intercepts and slopes, potentially representing different starting points and trajectories if the data were longitudinal.Return to text.⏎](images/fig4.2.jpg)

*Figure 4.2* Plot with several intercepts and slopes, potentially representing different starting points and trajectories if the data were longitudinal.Return to text.⏎

What distinguishes an effect to be random or fixed in this case is *not the fact that we have many intercepts or slopes in our sample data*. Indeed, one could have a thousand different trajectories for different individuals and still identify the slope as a fixed effect. It may make more sense however to consider it random, but *that identification is not determined by the fact that we have numerous slopes*. *It is determined by the fact that we consider the slopes as randomly sampled from a wider population of slopes and thus having their own probability distribution*. This is the key component to considering an effect to be random, not the *volume* of variation in the sample. Whether to regard an effect as fixed or random is entirely a *modeling decision* and is embedded in the assumptions we make for the model. It is never “implicit” in the data, though that isn’t to say the data cannot “help” us make decisions on how to model the effect.

### 4.3.2 Is the Fixed-Effects Model Actually Fixed? A Look at the Error Term

The reader may recall that the standard linear model assumes an error term that is normally distributed with finite variance. That is, in the standard linear model, `ε[i]` ∼ `N`(0,`σ[ε]`²) and `ε[i]`, `ε[i′]` are independent for `i` ≠ `i′`. But what is the error term, `ε[i]`, really? It is a random effect! Hence, when we are performing a fixed-effects ANOVA, we are, in reality, performing a *mixed model*. The random effect in the standard linear model is `ε[i]`, the variance component is `σ[ε]`², that is, `V(ε[i])`. As put so 202well by Fox ([2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106), p. 701), “Unlike the standard linear model, linear mixed-effect models include more than one source of random variation – that is, more than one random effect.” And while this, logically, may be true, the custom is *not* to refer to fixed models as mixed models simply as a result of including an error term. By tradition, a mixed model is defined by having a blending of fixed and random factors beyond that of the error term. Our point is merely that if we look at these models in a bit of a deeper way, we come to realize that purely fixed-effects models actually do not exist unless we either fix the error term or leave it out entirely, which would be the same thing as setting it to zero.
