
> The standard task in nonlinear dimensionality reduction consists of taking a set of data points sampled from a smooth, low-dimensional submanifold of a high-dimensional Euclidean space and to re-embed those points in a low-dimensional Euclidean space while preserving the local structure of the submanifold. The purpose is to find useful new coordinates for the data.

Of course, very little of the data the scientist will encounter will lie exactly on a linear or nonlinear manifold and hence *approximation of the manifold* will be a key concept in all of this.

### 11.12.2 Example of a Simple Manifold

It should be emphasized that while manifold learning generally refers to higher-dimensional spaces, manifolds themselves can be extremely simple in lower dimensions. For example, the following simple function, simple as it is, is nonetheless a manifold:

`f(x) = (x/10)³`

The above will generate a one-dimensional plot (curve in this case) that is nonlinear (try plotting it). What dimension-reduction techniques will do is find a lower-dimensional representation. For example, we saw that `PCA` found the major axes underlying the variables in a very *global* fashion (i.e., based on the entire data). As we will see, `Isomap` will accomplish something similar, but will also preserve linearity *locally*, not only *globally* as is the case with `PCA`. That is, `Isomap` finds a nonlinear manifold but not directly through a global function as in `PCA`, but instead through connecting smaller local linear functions. `Locally linear embedding` does something similar to `Isomap` but goes about the task by finding weights that perform local interpolations between data points that closely approximate the data. We survey these approaches shortly.

### 11.12.3 Nonparametric Manifolds

When we perform dimension reduction of any kind, we can be said to be trying to find the intrinsic “true” dimensionality that underlays the data. `Nonparametric manifolds` are generally those that learn a low-dimensional embedding often at each point in the training data, but in general do not learn a mapping function that can be applied universally to out-of-sample data. That is, they typically do not impose an overall *functional form*, but instead focus heavily on *what the current data have to say*. Recall this has been one of our definitions of nonparametric, such that they utilize the current data maximally and do not, in general, impose implicit parametric forms on the data that come laden with prior assumptions.

> *A nonparametric manifold learns a low-dimensional embedding often at each point in the training data but in general does not learn a concise and universal mapping function that can be applied to out-of-sample data. That is, nonparametric manifolds are, generally, “built” around the training data.*

### 11.12.4 Geodesic Distances

The shortest distance between any two points on a manifold is called the *geodesic distance*. Key to a geodesic distance is that it is the *shortest* distance or “path” connecting two points on a surface. 444 If that surface happens to be on a plane, then the geodesics of the plane are straight lines ([Tanton, 2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000352)). If the surface happens to be more complex, then the geodesic will likewise be more complex and may not even be unique (e.g., for a sphere the distance between poles is not unique). The use of geodesics assumes there are no gaps in the manifold on which they are measured, and also assumes the data do not lay outside of the data manifold ([Ozertem & Erdogmus, 2011](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000278)). Geodesics often have no closed-form formula such as Euclidean distances and hence are usually difficult to calculate, requiring numerical methods. In R, `geodist` ([Padgham](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000279) & Sumner, 2024) can compute such distances.

## 11.13 Locally Linear Embedding

Local linear embedding (`LLE`) recovers global structure by using linear “patches” in the wider structure. Each “patch” of the manifold is approximately linear. That is, `LLE` does what many other local methods do, whether in dimension-reduction or even nonparametric regression, and that is to “zoom in” on narrow *slices* or *patches* where things can be regarded as linear because the slice or patch is so small (think of a magnifying glass “zooming in”). As the slices become larger, the local linearity concept begins to vanish and so the slices cannot be too large. `LLE` uses this local linearity idea to essentially find a weight-based representation of each point of the manifold using its neighbors so that there are locally linear “neighborhoods” in the manifold. As the name suggests, the “embedding” is locally linear, even if the global manifold is not (i.e., the global manifold is typically highly *nonlinear*). The hope, however, is to capture the nonlinearity through smaller linear *areas* (i.e., *patches*) of the manifold.

Recall at the outset that one overarching theme we gave for statistical learning methods; they either impose a *global* structure onto data, or, they focus in more *locally* to observe segments of the data. [Hastie et al. (2009)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145) brilliantly introduce the whole edifice of statistical learning emphasizing this one key distinction. Nearest-neighbor methods essentially use local information in accounting for the data. These are generally nonparametric techniques because they heavily use the data that surrounds the given observation and rely exclusively on training data. This is in contrast to more global parametric methods that impose parametric structure on the data, such as linear or polynomial regression models. These methods are less concerned about the training data and are more interested in generalizing to future observations. The local parameterization of `LLE` is such that one can “embed” a new set of points in a lower-dimensional manifold. Such preserves, to the extent that is possible, the same relative positioning information ([Ihler, 2003](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000163)) via a nearest-neighbor approach.

More specifically, locally linear embedding hypothesizes that the best linear approximation around a given data point `x[i]` can be found by predicting the data point `x[i]` as a linear combination of its `k`-nearest neighbors using reconstruction weights. The weight vector is found by minimizing the following cost function,

$$\\hat{W} = \\min\_{W}{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{N}\\left| \\middle| x\_{i} - {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{N}\\left. w\_{ij}x\_{j} \\middle| \\right|^{2}} \\right.}$$

(11.20)

where the sum of weights `w[i j]` equals 1, and where `w[i j]` denotes the “contribution” of `x[j]` to the eventual reconstruction (to be discussed shortly). We must choose a suitable neighborhood of points from `x[i]` when computing $\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{N}{w\_{ij}x\_{j}}$. That is, $\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{N}{w\_{ij}x\_{j}}$ essentially constitutes the *neighborhood* of the focal point, `x[i]`. We wish to weight the neighbors via `w[i j]` such that `Ŵ` is minimized. 445 If the neighborhood is too large we lose the *local linearity* of local embedding. The neighborhood can be chosen by criteria such as `k`-nearest neighbors or by imposing a sphere around `x[i]` with a minimal radius `ε` ([Ihler, 2003](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000163)). The possibility also exists to use different values for `ε` for different segments of the input space to help assure that `ε` is never too large to significantly weaken the embedding ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009); [Saul & Roweis, 2003](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000323)). Standard algorithms for `k`-nearest neighbors can be used as a more efficient method for determining neighbors. A randomized approximate nearest-neighbor algorithm can also be used ([Jones et al., 2011](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000175)).
