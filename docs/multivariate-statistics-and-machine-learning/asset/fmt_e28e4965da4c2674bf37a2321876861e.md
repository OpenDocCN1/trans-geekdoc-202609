
The Vapnik-Chervonenkis or “V-C” dimension is a measure of a classifier to perform. It characterizes its *learnability*. It is the maximum number of points that can be “shattered” or less formally, “separated” by a hypothesis class. As a simple example of the theory, consider the following plots (adapted from Hastie et al., [2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 238), where each point takes its turn as `class = 1` (bolded square) vs. `class = 2`:

As shown above, we can see that a linear function is able to “shatter” the two-group structure in each case. Hence, we would say that the class of linear functions for the above is able to shatter three points.

The above is but a trivial example of V-C dimension, as its theory gets much more complex. The details of the theorem can be found in [Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000373). The theorem effectively can help provide an upper bound on *optimism* of a set of algorithms ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)) and tells us the capability or capacity of a concept class, that is, what the class is *able* to do, such as in the above example of lines shattering three points. A learning machine with a high number of parameters in general has a high V-C dimension; however, this is not necessarily always the case. One could have a very wiggly line that shatters many points yet only has a single parameter (see [Hastie et al., 2009,](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145) for an example).

## 1.18 Parametric vs. Nonparametric Learning Methods

Virtually all statistical and machine learning approaches can be distinguished between parametric vs. nonparametric, though in reality many approaches will fall in between these two extremes. What distinguishes a parametric vs. a nonparametric approach? As with most things in statistics, the distinction quickly becomes *fuzzy*, but we will do our best to delineate the primary features.50

*Parametric* methods make assumptions about the functional form *thought* or *assumed* to underlie the observed sample data. *Nonparametric* methods do not assume such a form, but instead try to estimate the functional form more directly from the observed data. In a sense then, we can generally say that parametric models *arrive on the scene* with a Bayesian prior of sorts about the kind of distribution and function that may have given rise to the observed data, whereas nonparametric methods generally simply want to “get to know the data” without prior beliefs or functional form assumptions getting in the way. This is an oversimplification of the distinction, but still useful as an approximation.

Why make parametric assumptions at all? Why not just let the data “speak?” Making parametric assumptions often facilitates the analysis so that one does not have to start all the time at the very “beginning” as though one knows absolutely nothing at all about what functional form might have given rise to the data. As a result, *parametric approaches typically require less data than do their nonparametric counterparts*. For example, if you already have a good idea that the parametric form of the relationship will be linear, then you will need less data to fit your model than if you allowed the functional form to be entirely determined by the data, which would entail a more nonparametric approach. *There are usually benefits to being able to assume a prior functional form to data. However, if the assumption is incorrect, accuracy and validity of the analysis will be compromised.*

Another way of appreciating this difference is with respect to the number of parameters required in each type of modeling. Using the linear model as an example once more, once we fit the intercept and slope, the job is done, regardless of how much data we are fitting the line to. Two parameters get the job done regardless of whether we fit the line to ten observations or 10,000. The parametric line for a smaller sample size keeps the data from “speaking too loudly,” which is reasonable, since there isn’t much data to begin with. In other words, we are, through our imposing of a parametric form, *helping the data determine its underlying form*. However, as sample size increases and we get more data, we begin to “trust” the data more with determining its own underlying structure, and hence are more likely to allow it to “speak for itself” than restrict what it can say via a smaller *allowance* of a parametric form. After millions of observations, if the data suggest the underlying form is a very complex polynomial, we will have more trust than if the data were quite small in number. When the data were smaller, we imposed a line (or some other parametric form) so that the conclusions do not get too unrealistic by perhaps “connecting the dots” in the smaller sample.

### 1.18.1 Flexibility and Number of Parameters

Another and very useful way to think about the distinction between parametric vs. nonparametric approaches is via *flexibility* and the number of parameters required to carry out the modeling. As we have said, parametric models (e.g., a linear model) have a fixed number of parameters coming to the modeling scene already packaged and ready to be fit to the data. In the nonparametric situation, however, the number of parameters needed for the modeling is theoretically and pragmatically unbounded. As we will see when we fit such things as regression splines, we can pretty much fit as many knots as we wish to best approximate the curve. We have greater *flexibility* in how we model the data when compared to a parametric approach. The effective number of parameters is essentially unbounded ([James et al., 2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)).51

#### 1.18.1.1 Concept of Degrees of Freedom

Degrees of freedom can be conceptualized in a variety of ways. All ways pretty much point to the same concept. For example, in a system of equations, degrees of freedom refer to the number of variables needed to complete the solution set, that is, if we know values for `x`, `y`, but not `z`, then the degrees of freedom for the system are equal to one. In a physical system, degrees of freedom refer to the minimum number of coordinates required to describe the state of the system ([Tanton, 2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000352)).

The basic sample variance as an estimator of the population variance is probably the simplest *statistical* illustration of this concept, where `n`–1 is used as the denominator to express the loss of a degree of freedom in

$$s^{2} = \\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right.} \\right)^{2}}{n - 1}$$  

(1.6)

The numerator $\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right.} \\right)^{2}$ features the estimation of `μ` through `y`, hence the loss of the degree of freedom.

More universally in terms of fitting models, the concept of degrees of freedom is related to the *complexity* of a model. In general, *the more complex a model fit to training data, the more degrees of freedom that model possesses* (e.g., spline with several knots or higher-order polynomial fit). A model with more degrees of freedom will generally fit better than a model with less, and hence overfitting becomes very probable as degrees of freedom increase ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). An example is that of an exaggerated higher-order polynomial fit essentially at some point accounting for mostly noise in the empirical observations.
