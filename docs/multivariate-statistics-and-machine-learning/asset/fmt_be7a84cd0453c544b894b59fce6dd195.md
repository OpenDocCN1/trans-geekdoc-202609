
`Z`-scores form the basis for the standard normal distribution which has a mean equal to 0 and a standard deviation equal to 1. Pay notice to the word “standard” here. Most empirical distributions that are normal (or approximately so) do not have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1.

## 2.32.1 `Z`-Tests and `T`-Tests for the Mean

Recall the one-sample `z`-test for the mean,

$$z\_{M} = \\frac{\\overline{y} - E\\left( \\overline{y} \\right)}{\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}} = \\frac{\\overline{y} - \\mu\_{0}}{\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}}$$

(2.76)

The reader will recall from introductory courses that we typically do not know the standard error of the mean, $\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}$, and instead require an estimate of it,

$${\\hat{\\sigma}}\_{M} = \\frac{s}{\\sqrt{n}}$$

(2.77)

When we use `s` as an estimate of `σ`, the resulting statistic is no longer a `z` statistic; it is instead distributed as `t`, which gives rise to the `t`-test for one sample,

$$t = \\frac{\\overline{y} - E\\left( \\overline{y} \\right)}{\\frac{s}{\\sqrt{n}}}$$

(2.78)

It is a fact of mathematical statistics that the `t-`distribution converges to the `z`-distribution as degrees of freedom increase without bound. This is the case because the variance of `t` is defined as

`σ[t]²` = `v`/`(v−2)`

(2.79)

and so as `v`→∞, the above ratio goes to one, that is,

$$\\lim\\limits\_{v\\rightarrow\\infty}\\left( \\frac{v}{v - 2} \\right) = 1$$

For two samples, the `z`-test for means is given by117

$$z\_{M} = \\frac{E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{1} \\right) - E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{2} \\right)}{\\sqrt{\\frac{\\sigma\_{1}^{2}}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{\\sigma\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{2}}}} = \\frac{\\mu\_{1} - \\mu\_{2}}{\\sqrt{\\frac{\\sigma\_{1}^{2}}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{\\sigma\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{2}}}}$$

(2.80)

The justification for summing the variances in the standard error is due to the variance-sum law that holds that if `y[1]` and `y[2]` are normally distributed and independent random variables with means `μ[1]` and `μ[2]` and variances `σ[1]²` and `σ[2]²`, the difference between the random variables has a mean equal to `μ[1]` − `μ[2]` with variance `σ[1]²` + `σ[2]²`. That is, the standard error of the difference between means is equal to,

$$\\sigma\_{{\\overline{y}}\_{1} - {\\overline{y}}\_{2}} = \\sqrt{\\frac{\\sigma\_{1}^{2}}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{\\sigma\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{2}}}$$

(2.81)

What is more, due to the central limit theorem, the sampling distribution of ${\\overline{y}}\_{1} - {\\overline{y}}\_{2}$ is approximately normal for increasingly large samples and is theoretically normal for `n`→∞.

As for the one-sample situation, we rarely, if ever, know the values of `σ[1]²` and `σ[2]²` and hence estimate them with `s[1]²` and `s[2]²`,

$$t = \\frac{E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{1} \\right) - E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{2} \\right)}{\\sqrt{\\frac{s\_{1}^{2}}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{s\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{2}}}} = \\frac{\\mu\_{1} - \\mu\_{2}}{\\sqrt{\\frac{s\_{1}^{2}}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{s\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{2}}}}$$

(2.82)

For unequal sample sizes (not unequal variances), we pool variances, which means we will require an estimated standard error not of the mean, but of the difference between means,

$$\\begin{array}{l} {{\\hat{\\sigma}}\_{diff} = \\sqrt{{\\hat{\\sigma}}\_{pooled}^{2}\\left( {\\frac{1}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{1}{n\_{2}}} \\right)}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\sqrt{\\frac{\\left( n\_{1} - 1 \\right)s\_{1}^{2} + \\left( n\_{2} - 1 \\right)s\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{1} + n\_{2} - 2}\\left( \\frac{n\_{1} + n\_{2}}{n\_{1}n\_{2}} \\right)}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

(2.83)

The classic two-sided null and alternative hypotheses for the `t`-test is

`H[0]`: `μ[1]` = `μ[2]` `H[1]`: `μ[1]` ≠ `μ[2]`

With the pooled variance estimator, the `t`-test becomes

$$t = \\frac{E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{1} \\right) - E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{2} \\right)}{\\sqrt{\\frac{\\left( n\_{1} - 1 \\right)s\_{1}^{2} + \\left( n\_{2} - 1 \\right)s\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{1} + n\_{2} - 2}\\left( \\frac{n\_{1} + n\_{2}}{n\_{1}n\_{2}} \\right)}}$$

(2.84)

evaluated on (`n[1]`−1) + (`n[2]`−1) degrees of freedom. It should be noted that sometimes the numerator of these difference tests are stated with an assumed value `D[0]` under `H[0]` for the difference, $E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{1} \\right) - E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{2} \\right) - D\_{0}$. In theory at least, this allows the researcher to specify a value for `D[0]` other 118than zero; however, zero will typically be taken as the default value unless there is good substantive reason to set it unequal to zero.

## 2.33 Unequal Variances: Welch-Satterthwaite Approximation

Recall that the two-sample `t`-test assumes equal population variances, that is, `σ[1]²` = `σ[2]²`. If there is evidence to suggest that variances are unequal, then an approximation can be used. The approximation adjusts for the degrees of freedom of the test where instead of degrees of freedom equaling (`n[1]`−1) + (`n[2]`−1), they are computed as

$$df = \\frac{\\left( n\_{1} - 1 \\right)\\left( n\_{2} - 1 \\right)}{\\left( 1 - c \\right)^{2}\\left( n\_{1} - 1 \\right) + c^{2}\\left( n\_{2} - 1 \\right)}$$

(2.85)

where,

$$c = \\frac{s\_{1}^{2}/n\_{1}}{\\frac{s\_{1}^{2}}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{s\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{2}}}$$

Likewise, approximate confidence intervals may be computed via

$$\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{1} - {\\overline{y}}\_{2} \\right) \\pm t\_{\\alpha/2}\\sqrt{\\frac{s\_{1}^{2}}{n\_{1}} + \\frac{s\_{2}^{2}}{n\_{2}}}$$

(2.86)

## 2.34 Paired Data

When working with paired data, we require making inferences about `μ[1]` − `μ[2]` just as in the independent-samples `t`-test, only that now, observations are not independent; they are matched or naturally paired. What is interesting about paired data on relatively simple data sets is that the concept of paired data generalizes to much more complex models (e.g., elaborate longitudinal models, blocking designs, and multilevel models with repeated measures). The numerator of the `t`-test is the same, since $E\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{1} - {\\overline{y}}\_{1} \\right)$ is still `μ[1]` − `μ[2]`. However, the standard error of the difference in means incorporates the fact that the samples are paired, and is equal to

$$\\sigma\_{{\\overline{y}}\_{1} - {\\overline{y}}\_{2}} = \\sqrt{\\frac{\\sigma\_{1}^{2} + \\sigma\_{2}^{2} - 2\\sigma\_{1}\\sigma\_{2}\\rho}{n}}$$

(2.87)

where `ρ` is the correlation between variables `y[1]` and `y[2]`. Note that for `ρ` = 0, the numerator under the radical sign is simply `σ[1]²` + `σ[2]²` as it was in the unpaired situation, that is, `σ[1]²` + `σ[2]²` − 2`σ[1]` `σ[2]`(0) = `σ[1]²` + `σ[2]²`. However, for `ρ` ≠ 0, the standard error for paired data has an expectation less than that of independent data, since 2`σ[1]` `σ[2]` `ρ` is being subtracted from the sum, `σ[1]²` + `σ[2]²`. In practice, the `t`-test usually takes the form of computing mean difference $\\overline{d}$ between columns, and like in the independent-samples `t`-test, subtracting a null value of `D[0]`, which yields,119

$$t = \\frac{\\overline{d} - D\_{0}}{s\_{d}/\\sqrt{n}}$$

(2.88)

The resulting `t` is evaluated on `n`−1 degrees of freedom since there is now only a single column of differences, in effect, it is now a one-sample test. A confidence interval for the mean difference can be computed,

$$\\overline{d} \\pm t\_{\\alpha/2}\\frac{s\_{d}}{\\sqrt{n}}$$

(2.89)

where `s[d]` is the standard deviation of the column of difference scores.
