
## 13.2

Since the time of Hume, philosophical work on causality has filled volumes, books, and academic careers. The study of causality is a full-time job and truth be told, nobody is still quite sure what causality even means. There are several different versions and interpretations of the meaning of causality (e.g., see [Bollen, 1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000037)). Exploring the nature of causality alone in this book would take up at least a chapter if not more, and hence, we do not go there. It will be enough, however, to reject the idea of structural equation models having anything *intrinsically* and *necessarily* to do with causality.

### 13.2.1 Misguided History

If modern causality goes back to Hume, the study of causality itself can be said to go back to the ancient Greeks. The development of statistical regression took place primarily in the late 1880s and early 1890s with the original work of Francis Galton, followed by Karl Pearson and his student, George Udny Yule. Did these pioneers refer to regression models as being causal? Not explicitly, but they did at times use the word *causal* when describing the relationships among the response and predictor variables. However, nowhere did these pioneers ever give regression the power or “license,” *unequivocally*, to make causal claims or draw causal conclusions from non-experimental designs.

Fast-forward to 1917 with the work of Sewall Wright on path analysis, well documented elsewhere (e.g., see [Denis & Legerski, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000085)). Wright was interested in the generational transmission of color in the guinea pig. From the very inception of Wright’s path models, for which he named the coefficients from such models *causal coefficients*, opposition to such claims of causation surfaced almost immediately. As argued in [Denis and Legerksi (2006)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000085), path models, and their eventual offspring, structural equation models, have no more philosophical authority to claims of causation than the regression models invented in the 1890s. *Causality has nothing to do with the model; it has everything to do with the objects and variables that are being modeled*.481

To illustrate the above point, consider a figure ([Figure 13.1](#c13.xhtml#f13.1)) from Wright’s original guinea pig diagram (left) where he traced what he called the *causal* paths from parent to offspring.

Obviously, in the context of parents giving birth to guinea pigs, color transmission was a heritable, biological reality. That is, the fact that offspring had a similar color as their parents could be explained by what was known at the time of heritable influences. Naming such paths “causal” did no harm, as the genetic transmission from parent to offspring agrees more or less with the idea of causality. However, suppose we now replaced the guinea pigs in the picture with simply abstract variables (right): `X`, `Y`, `W`, and `Z`. Could we now make an analogous causal claim? Does it make any sense to claim that `W` causes `Z`, for instance? Surely not! After all, we do not even know what these variables even *are*. They are entirely *abstract*.

From this simple example and comparison, it is clear to see that the model alone could never infer causality. Ascribing causal “power” to the statistical *model* instead of to the *variables* on which the model is applied is nothing less than faulty reasoning. *There is nothing inherently causal about a path model or a structural equation model or any other statistical model*. The only conceivable possible exception to this is if all variables in the entire *universe* and all residuals (and their relationships) were able to be included in the model such that the model was fully (and unreasonably on a pragmatic level) specified. Then, it *may* make more sense to speak of the model as causal regardless of the substantive natures of variables used in the model. But of course, such is a fanciful reality that will never be achieved. It is simply unattainable and unrealistic. Hence, identifying or associating causality with the path or structural equation model, or with *any* model for that matter, is simply illogical. Causality is a *scientific* issue, not a *statistical* one.

### 13.2.2 Baron and Kenny (1986)

We fast-forward for a moment to Baron and Kenny’s seminal and highly cited 1986 publication, *The Moderator-Mediator Variable Distinction in Social Psychological Research: Conceptual, Strategic, and Statistical Considerations*. In it, the authors wished to emphasize the difference between moderators and mediators, which is a noteworthy contribution. They offer the following definition of a moderator:482

> In general terms, a moderator is a qualitative (e.g., sex, race, class) or quantitative (e.g., level of reward) variable that affects the direction and/or strength of the relation between an independent or predictor variable and a dependent or criterion variable (p. 1174).

Of course, the above definition, taken *literally*, is somewhat problematic. Statistical moderators have no license whatsoever to *affect* the direction or strength of anything. The word “affect” usually implies “to have an influence on” or similar. As discussed when we surveyed moderation earlier in the book, statistical moderators are simply unable to do such a thing; *substantive moderators*, perhaps, but not *statistical ones*. Calling a variable a *statistical moderator* does not by itself imply that it moderates anything.

Why bring up Baron and Kenny at all here in a discussion of path analysis and structural equation models? We do so for the reason that mediation and moderation models can easily be cast as structural equation models, and hence addressing the causality issue here in relation to SEM models is mandatory. To be fair to Baron and Kenny, they do later in their seminal paper discuss such things as experimental controls and manipulation where causality may possibly be inferred, but the point we wish to make here is that defining moderation or mediation with any kind of *action words* encourages the student (and even researcher!) to falsely and illogically ascribe powers to statistical techniques that they simply do not possess. Some sciences, such as psychology, have a long history of inferring and concluding far “too much” from what a statistical paradigm is philosophically able to provide. We believe that this is in part because in some subfields, carrying out *experimental designs*, where claims of causality may be more reasonable, can be tremendously challenging or impossible due to ethical and other constraints. One cannot randomly assign age, for instance, and hence, many designs in these fields and areas are necessarily *correlational* in nature. Even given these interpretational constraints, however, this may not be enough to stop researchers’ “Freudian id” from stepping in and concluding more from the statistical analysis than what may be warranted.

## 13.3 Is the Variable Measurable? The Observed vs. Unobserved Distinction

Structural equation modeling distinguishes between *observed* or *manifest* vs. *unobserved* variables. With a little thought, the distinction here, like many terms in statistics, gets fuzzy and blurry very quickly, and once more we end up studying the philosophy of these distinctions rather than simply defining them. Still, we must do our best to attempt some definitions here so we can move on and not get stuck in philosophical quicksand.
