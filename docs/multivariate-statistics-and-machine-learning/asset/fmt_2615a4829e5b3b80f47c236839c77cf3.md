
> *PCA, under most circumstances, will generate as many components as there are variables. The actual “data reduction” occurs by selecting the first few components to account for as much of the variance in the variables as possible, or equivalently, minimizes the reconstruction error. Choosing the number of components to keep or maintain is largely dependent on the research or scientific context and often based on user judgment.*

### 11.2.5 Constraints on Components

Recall that we mentioned that the eigenvector weights `e[1]` through `e[p]` were applied to observed variables in order to generate components. In deriving these components, we shall impose the constraint on these eigenvector weights so that the *sum of their squares is equal to unity*. That is, the sum of squared eigenvector weights we will set equal to one,

$${\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}e\_{i}^{2}} = 1$$

(11.5)

When we impose such a condition on each component, the component is said to have been *normalized*. There is no absolute requirement that the sum needs to be 1; it could in theory be any other constant positive value. Setting it equal to 1 implies that the length of the component is equal to unity. But why impose such a constraint at all? The purpose of this constraint is such that if we did not impose at least *some* constraint on the loadings, then in theory we could allow the component’s variance to increase without bound. By imposing this constraint, or other positive constant for the variance, we are assured that the variance of the component will not simply be a reflection of increasingly large eigenvector weights.

As we have seen throughout this book, constraints such as the above are not atypical in statistical or machine learning methods. It is a regular occurrence in mathematical modeling more generally that some quantities are fixed or constrained to particular values so that other variable quantities can be more freely estimated under these constraints. Constraints such as these, in the end, *simplify problems*.

### 11.2.6 Centering Observed Variables

Observed variables are also often (though need not *necessarly* be) centered before being subjected to the components analysis, so that each variable has a mean equal to 0. By *centering* here we simply mean subtracting the mean of each observed raw variable. For example, if the observed variable is `x[i]` with mean $\\overline{x}$, then centering the variable means generating $x\_{i} - \\overline{x}$ for each observation. The reader will recognize centering here as a precursor to computing `z-scores` where the centered deviation is then further divided by the standard deviation, `s`. As a simple example of centering, consider the following vector of data in R having original raw mean equal to 6.2:

```
> v <- c(0, 4, 8, 9, 10)
> mean(v)
[1] 6.2
```

To center this data, we simply subtract the mean from each observation:

```
> v.center <- v - mean(v)
> v.center
[1] -6.2 -2.2 1.8 2.8 3.8
```

We can then easily confirm that, within rounding error, the mean of the centered data is equal to 0:

```
> mean(v.center)
[1] -1.775489e-16
```

### 11.2.7 Orthogonality of Components

As mentioned already, one of the attractive features of components analysis is that derived components are guaranteed to be orthogonal (i.e., eigenvectors) and uncorrelated to one another. Recall what orthogonality means, it means derived components are at *right angles*, or *perpendicular* to one another (that of 90 degrees). From a substantive point of view, orthogonality ensures that each component accounts for, or *explains* a differing proportion of variance in the variables and that there is no overlap. It is important to emphasize that orthogonality is not the same as *statistical independence*. *Statistical independence is, in a sense, a stronger property than orthogonality in this case and principal components will only be both orthogonal and independent if the data are multivariate normal*. For components to be both orthogonal and independent beyond the assumption of multivariate normality one must go beyond traditional PCA and instead perform an *independent components analysis*, to be discussed later. Assuming all components are nonzero, however, orthogonality of components will imply *linear independence* (as opposed to *statistical* independence).

> *Classic principal components analysis ensures orthogonality of components but does not guarantee their statistical independence. Statistical independence is the stronger property in this case, reserved for the technique of independent components analysis.*

### 11.2.8 Proportion of Variance Explained by Each Component: Covariance vs. Correlation Matrices

Having computed components in decreasing order of variance explained, a natural question worth asking now is: how much variance is actually accounted for by each component? Recall that components analysis does not *change* the amount of variance in the original variables. It merely reconstructs that variance along new dimensions (called the *components*, which are in effect, *eigenvectors* of the transformation). Hence, it stands that *the total variance in the totality of components should be equal to the total variance in the original variables.* That is, when we sum up the variance accounted for by all of the components, that sum should equal the total variance in the original variables. In a covariance matrix, that sum of variance will equal the sum of variances across all observed variables. In a *correlation matrix*, that sum will equal to the total number of *variables* because in a correlation matrix, the variance contributed by each variable is equal to one. Whether one analyzes a covariance or correlation matrix can result in quite vastly different outcomes. Principal components analysis is not invariant to type of matrix analyzed. This fact may at first seem to go against intuition, such that one might have assumed or expected that both the covariance and correlation matrices would yield similar results, or at minimum, one result would be a trivial transformation of the other. However, this is not the case. A PCA performed on a correlation matrix will usually yield *different* eigenvalues and eigenvectors than a PCA performed on a covariance matrix.

Given this distinction, the question becomes: which matrix to analyze? While some hold the covariance matrix is more in spirit with PCA (e.g., see [Rencher & Christensen, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295)), others recommend that PCA should virtually always be performed on the *standardized* covariance matrix, otherwise known of course as the *correlation matrix* ([Bilodeau & Brenner, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000030); Murray, 2022). Of course, there is no harm in trying both solutions and comparing them. The important point, however, is to recognize that performing PCA on one versus the other will, in general, not produce the same result. Principal components analysis also requires at least *some* covariance (or correlation) among pairs of variables. In the absence of such covariance, that is, if all covariances or correlations are equal to 0 and only variances remain along the main diagonal, PCA will be of little use. At most, PCA will simply generate components that are a reflection of the original variables but in descending order of variance ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057)). For now, we will assume we are working with the covariance matrix. Given that the eigenvalues are variances of each component, then the following relation should then hold:

∑`s²` = ∑`λ[i]`

(11.6)

That is, the sum of variances of the original variables should equal the sum of eigenvalues from the components analysis. We can easily demonstrate this on empirical data. Consider some data on `x`, `y`:

```
> x <- c(2.5, 0.5, 2.2, 1.9, 3.1, 2.3, 2.0, 1.0, 1.5, 1.1)
> y <- c(2.4, 0.7, 2.9, 2.2, 3.0, 2.7, 1.6, 1.1, 1.6, 0.9)
```
