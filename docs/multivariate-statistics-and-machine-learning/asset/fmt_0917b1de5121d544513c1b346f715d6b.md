
## Transformation to z

The transformation to `z` is an example of a `linear transformation` (the meaning of which we review shortly). It being an example of a linear transformation means that conversion to `z` does not change the shape of the distribution of the original data. This is very important! At first glance it can seem that conversion to `z` should `normalize` a distribution and hence give it a normal shape. But this intuition is incorrect and is a perfect example where what one feels `should` be correct is, in actuality, not. Intuition in mathematics and statistics can be a boon but at times a dangerous thing which is why proofs are usually required before a statement is considered true.80

## 2.15.2 Mixtures of Gaussians

Mixture models are those that combine different distributions, typically `linearly`, at arriving at a probability estimate. Oftentimes those individual distributions are Gaussian, and hence `Gaussian mixture models` are quite common. We write a mixture of Gaussians as

$$p\\left( x \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}\\pi\_{k}}N\\left( x \\middle| \\mu\_{k},\\Sigma\_{k} \\right)$$

(2.22)

The components `k` = 1 through `K` of a mixture model are the different `k` distributions that are “mixed” into a single distribution, each having its own mean `μ` and covariance matrix ∑ and where `π` is the mixing parameter or “weight” applied for each distribution. Combined, we wish to model the probability of `x`, `p`(`x`). The sum of mixing proportions must equal 1, that is, ${\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}\\pi\_{k}} = 1$ and where 0 ≤ `π`[`k`] ≤ 1. We can also impose equality constraints in a mixture distribution such that certain parameters are set to be equal across distributions, such as that of equal covariance matrices in a multivariate setting ([Goodfellow et al., 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)).

A Gaussian mixture model is a `universal approximator`, which, as we will see when we survey neural networks, implies that any smooth function (or density) can be approximated to any small degree of error given enough components ([Goodfellow et al., 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)). Hence, given enough mixtures of distributions, function approximation becomes a certainty just as with neural networks; if we are given enough hidden layers and nodes, they too become universal approximators. Mixture models, as we will see, are also useful in methods ranging from random effects models to cluster analysis to neural networks and beyond and are much more general than simply mixing normal densities. Our example here is of normal densities only for the reason that we are currently surveying the normal distribution. Mixtures for discrete variables, for instance, might include mixing Bernoulli distributions ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)).

## 2.15.3 Evaluating Univariate Normality

Many methods and techniques have been proposed for evaluating the normality of distributions. Perhaps the most useful are simple graphical and visualization techniques; however, inferential methods exist as well to estimate how likely it is the current sample distribution was selected from a population distribution that is normal. Tests for univariate normality include the `Kolmogorov-Smirnov test`, the `Shapiro-Wilk test`, the `Anderson-Darling test`, and many others. According to [Bilodeau and Brenner (1999)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000030), the Shapiro-Wilk test has been found to be the `best omnibus test`. We assume the reader has some experience with interpreting these tests, especially the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Further details can be found in [Denis (2021a)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000087).

We can demonstrate the Shapiro-Wilk test very easily in R:

```
> shapiro.test(rnorm(100, mean = 5, sd = 3))
```

```
  Shapiro-Wilk normality test
```

```
data: rnorm(100, mean = 5, sd = 3)
W = 0.99085, p-value = 0.7331
```

The null hypothesis is that the population from which the sample data were drawn is normally distributed. Since the `p`-value is not even close to a traditional cut-off, such as `p` < 0.05 (`p` = 0.7331), we do not have evidence to reject the null. Note that this in no way confirms the population to be normal, but it does not give us evidence to suggest it is `not` normal.81

There are many ways and packages available for plotting all kinds of densities in R. For example, we can easily obtain a plot of the normal distribution in R by the following (Figure 2.4):

```
> x <- seq(-3, 3, length = 100)
> y <- dnorm(x)
>plot(y)
```

## 2.15.4 Multivariate Gaussian

As we have just seen, in the univariate density,

$$f\\left( x \\right) = \\frac{1}{\\sigma\\sqrt{2\\pi}}e^{- {(x - \\mu)}^{2}/2\\sigma^{2}}$$

the exponent featured  − (`x`-`μ`)²/2`σ`². When we generalize the univariate Gaussian to the multivariate setting, we replace observations `x` with vectors `x`, `μ` with `μ`, and individual variances, `σ`² with covariance matrices `Σ`:

$$f\\left( x \\right) = \\frac{1}{\\left. \\left( \\sqrt{2\\pi} \\right)^{p} \\middle| \\sum \\right|^{1/2}}e^{- (x - \\mu)'\\Sigma^{- 1}(x - \\mu)/2}$$

(2.23)

For `μ` = 0 and `Σ` = `σ`²`I`, where `σ`² = 1, this is the parallel setting for the standard normal distribution with `μ` = 0 and `σ`² = 1, where now `I` is the identity matrix. But how exactly is `Σ` = `σ`²`I` the analogous condition to `σ`² = 1? Recall that when `Σ` = `I`, this means that all values along the main diagonal are equal to 1 and all elsewhere is equal to 0 indicating that there is zero pairwise covariance between all variables. This implies that all variances in the multivariate density for `Σ` = `I` are equal to `σ`², where `σ`² = 1 in this case, just as `σ`² = 1 is true for the univariate density under a standard normal distribution.

We can easily compare respective univariate and multivariate densities. In the univariate density, note that the density begins with82

$$\\frac{1}{\\sigma\\sqrt{2\\pi}}$$

In the multivariate setting, this is generalized to

$$\\frac{1}{\\left. \\left( \\sqrt{2\\pi} \\right)^{p} \\middle| \\sum \\right|^{1/2}}$$

and now reflects `volume` as opposed to `area` as was the case in the univariate setting due to the now multidimensional space occupied by the multivariate density. The reason why it reflects volume instead of area is because we are now working in greater than two dimensions (i.e., think of a cube or cuboid, for example, instead of a square). The univariate distribution is often referred to as a `one-dimensional` distribution `statistically`, but such refers to the `number of variables` being studied, which is one. On paper, `geometrically`, the distribution occurs in `two` dimensions.

But what of the bivariate density? The bivariate density is simply a special case of the multivariate density for the case of two variables and hence, we find it more convenient to simply regard it as a multivariate density. However, it can also be written in the following form:

$$f\\left( x\_{1},x\_{2} \\right) = \\frac{1}{2\\pi}\\frac{1}{\\sigma\_{1}\\sigma\_{2}\\left( 1 - \\rho^{2} \\right)^{\\frac{1}{2}}}\\exp\\left\\{ {- \\frac{1}{2\\left( 1 - \\rho^{2} \\right)}\\left\\lbrack {\\left\\lbrack \\frac{x\_{1} - \\mu\_{1}}{\\sigma\_{1}} \\right\\rbrack^{2} - 2\\rho\\left\\lbrack \\frac{x\_{1} - \\mu\_{1}}{\\sigma\_{1}} \\right\\rbrack\\left\\lbrack \\frac{x\_{2} - \\mu\_{2}}{\\sigma\_{2}} \\right\\rbrack + \\left\\lbrack \\frac{x\_{2} - \\mu\_{2}}{\\sigma\_{2}} \\right\\rbrack^{2}} \\right\\rbrack} \\right\\}$$

(2.24)

where `ρ`² is the squared correlation between variables `x`[1] and `x`[2]. For further details on the bivariate density, see Johnson and Wichern ([2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173), p. 151).
