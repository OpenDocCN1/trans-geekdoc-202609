(14.10)

such that the quantity being minimized is that of the average within-case or within-point scatter variation discussed earlier:508

$$Within\\left( C \\right)\\, = {\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}N\_{k}}{\\sum\\limits\_{C(i) = k}\\left| \\middle| x\_{i} - {\\overline{x}}\_{k} \\middle| \\right|^{2}}$$

We follow James et al. ([2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000169)) quite closely in detailing precisely how the `k`-means algorithm goes about clustering:

1. Each observation in the data receives a random number 1 to `K`. These are the *initial* cluster assignments.
2. For each of the `K` clusters, the cluster *centroid* is computed, where “centroid” is another word for *arithmetic mean* in this case. More generally, a centroid in mathematics is simply a *center point*, and thus it is, at least in theory, open and flexible regarding how we wish to define it. For example, the *median* can also be regarded as a centroid in cluster analysis. In classical `k`-means, the arithmetic mean is featured as the centroid.
3. Assign each observation to the cluster whose centroid is closest. The measure of “closeness” is defined by the squared Euclidean metric or some other distance function we have chosen *a priori*. There is nothing preventing the user from trying out different distance metrics on the same clustering problem and study the patterns that result.
4. Iterate on steps 2 and 3 until the minimization criterion is achieved, which as mentioned earlier is typically `Within(C)`, the within-cluster sums of squares.

The algorithm is guaranteed to decrease the value of the objective function at each step and optimizes a *non-convex* function ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)), which recall implies that achieving a global minimum is more difficult in `k`-means since numerous local minima exist in its place, and hence the algorithm will typically converge to local minima. As a result of the non-convexity, how the algorithm is initialized can make a big difference in the final cluster solution. For this reason, choosing several *random starts* is often advised to learn if the various cluster solutions more or less converge on a single solution ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)) or whether any of the single cluster solutions obtained are favorable to the user. Another approach is successively selecting points that are more distant from cluster centroids thus ensuring that initial seeds are well separated. This should be done with caution, however, as to not exclusively sample outliers.

### 14.8.4 Setting `k` in `K`-Means

A key decision in `k`-means clustering is that of deciding how to set `k`. One could set it by trial and error to learn what kinds of clusters emerge and decide whether they are substantively meaningful, but it would be advantageous to have some statistical “objective” criteria to help us determine an optimal value for `k`. We could at first attempt to set it such that it minimizes the reconstruction error. The problem with this approach is that reconstruction error will naturally decrease with increasing `k`, and hence setting this as a criterion to minimize will be meaningless as we could always minimize the error further by increasing the number of clusters desired, somewhat akin to forever increasing `R²` due to increasingly adding more predictors to the model. Since we know that `R²` must virtually always increase with increasing numbers of predictors, an inflating `R²` is a poor measure of anything, which recall is why `R[adj]²` or some other penalized statistic (e.g., `AIC`) is typically recommended over, or at least in conjunction with, `R²`. This situation occurs also in probabilistic mixture models that we will survey later in which we can compute the log marginal likelihood of the solution as an aid to determining an “ideal” number of clusters such that whichever cluster solution yields the largest likelihood may be considered “best.” In this context, however, it will be 509entirely possible for a solution with fewer clusters to generate a more favorable substantive finding than a solution with a greater number of clusters.

### 14.8.5 Weakness of `K`-Means

`K`-means comes with it a host of weaknesses. As noted by [Müller and Guido (2017)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000258):

> Even if you know the “right” number of clusters for a given dataset, `k-means` might not always be able to recover them. Each cluster is defined solely by its center, which means that each cluster is a convex shape. As a result of this, `k-means` can only capture relatively simple shapes. `K-means` also assumes that all clusters have the same “diameter” in some sense; it always draws the boundary between clusters to be exactly in the middle between the cluster centers.
>
> (p. 175)

This implies that `k`-means clustering essentially comes with it a prior idea of what the clusters *should* look like to some extent, and if the empirical realities do not conform to that idea, then recovering them can be quite difficult or even impossible. `k`-means implicitly also assumes a *spherical shape* for each cluster, and as we will see when it comes to mixture modeling, the EM algorithm is capable of discovering a broader class of *elliptical clusters*. Once again, this is a general problem when it comes to clustering algorithms. To reiterate, *what you find often depends on what tool you use to do the searching*. As warned by Meila and Zhang ([2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000247), p. 26) with regard to manifold learning more generally, “Are the features observed really in the data or artifacts of the algorithm?” Add to this difficulty that in most applications of cluster analysis there is typically no *ground truth*, as it is sometimes called, to the data. That is, we do not usually know the labels before we do the clustering. Only in toy problems do we typically have this benefit (e.g., species membership on the iris data).

### 14.8.6 `K`-Means vs. ANOVA vs. Discriminant Analysis

We said above that the goal of `k`-means was to minimize within-cluster difference. Does this criterion sound familiar? This minimization of such an objective function may have caught the eye of the reader and reminded them of what occurs in the analysis of variance or discriminant analysis. Consider the following conceptual comparison of approaches:

> `ANOVA` – goal is to maximize between-group differences and minimize within-group differences.
>
> `Discriminant analysis` – goal is essentially the same as in ANOVA, except in reverse roles of the independent and dependent variables such as to maximize group separation.
>
> `K-means` cluster analysis – goal is to minimize within-group differences and maximize separation between clusters.
