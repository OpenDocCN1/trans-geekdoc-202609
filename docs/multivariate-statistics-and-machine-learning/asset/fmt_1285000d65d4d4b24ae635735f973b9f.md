
## 14.1 Cluster Classification Paradigms

There are a variety of cluster classification paradigms that attempt to literally “cluster” the types of clustering algorithms. One rudimentary distinction often made is that between *top-down* vs. *bottom-up* approaches. In the top-down approach, one might designate the number of clusters one wishes to “extract” from the data or allow an algorithm to find smaller clusters from a much larger initial one. In the bottom-up approach, the algorithm begins with individual cases and builds up clusters by fusing case memberships, usually in a hierarchical manner. As we will see, the classic `k-means` clustering algorithm is one such example of a top-down approach ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 503) while agglomerative clustering is an example of a bottom-up method to clustering.

Following Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)), we can also more generally classify clustering algorithms into one of the following three types:499

### `Combinatorial algorithms`
These are applied directly to the data without reference to an underlying probability model. These are sometimes known as *hard* clustering algorithms, because cases are deterministically allocated to cluster membership. That is, there is no probability associated with a case being allocated to a given cluster, or, if we prefer to be all-probability-inclusive about it, cases are allocated with probability equal to 1.0; that is, the case either belongs in the cluster or it does not. This is what we generally mean by *hard clustering*. Combinatorial algorithms can also be interpreted as *encoders* in which the encoder is the `k`th cluster in an analysis, to which any given observation in the data is assigned ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)).

### `Mixture modeling`
Assumes an underlying probability model and is thus part of the family of *probability-based clustering*. Mixture models have a similar goal as combinatorial algorithms but use association rules to construct simple descriptions (also known as *conjunctive rules*) that describe regions of high density. Mixture modeling is an example of *soft clustering*, where a case is not deterministically guaranteed to belong to a given cluster. Rather, it belongs to a given cluster with an associated *probability*. Mixture models are especially useful when working with binary data. More specifically, in mixture models we assume observed data arose or was originated from a mixture of distributions, that is, the data were “generated” based on those distributions. For this reason, not surprisingly, mixture models are often regarded as *generative*. The choice of distribution is often Gaussian, but is essentially variable, and allows the user to incorporate prior knowledge of the cluster distributions. In this sense, a *prior probability* idea is utilized in clustering. In such probabilistic mixture models, as opposed to non-probabilistic models, the `log-likelihood` can be used as a metric to determine the success of clustering ([Murray, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)), though this approach will not necessarily be effective in entirely evaluating the clustering that is discovered by the model.

### `Mode seekers`
Or “bump hunters” are nonparametric approaches to clustering and seek to estimate distinct modes of a probability density function. Classification of cases to respective clusters is made by assignment of cases to the cluster for which the observation is the closest. Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (`DBSCAN`) is a popular method of density-based clustering, one which we will survey in this chapter.

The above are not the sole categorizations possible for clustering algorithms. For instance, [Xu and Tian (2015)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000396) provide *nine* different categories by which clustering algorithms can be differentiated and distinguished. For instance, traditional `k-means` and `k-medoids` are generally referred to as algorithms based on *partition* due to their way of segregating data. This is in contrast to algorithms such as `DBSCAN` which is in its own clustering category of those based on *densities* (i.e., the category of *density-based clustering algorithms*). For our purposes in this chapter, we stop short of classifying algorithms too finely and are generally content with the above aforementioned classification.

This chapter features a survey and discussion of the more popular methods of clustering, which include `k-means`, `hierarchical clustering`, `density-based clustering` (`DBSCAN`), and `cluster mixture models`.

## 14.2 Are Clusters Meaningful?

Cluster analysis, whatever the algorithm, will typically always “find” clusters since they are designed to do just that. However, whether those clusters are meaningful in any sense other than *mathematically* is often left to the user to ultimately determine. Unlike supervised methods that typically feature a loss function to quantify the extent to which predictions are in error, unsupervised methods, of which cluster analysis is one, do not generally feature such “hard” measures to assess how “far off” one is from an ideal solution. This is especially so if natural groupings are 500unknown before carrying out the cluster analysis, which is in most cases why we are employing cluster analysis in the first place. In evaluating cluster solutions, as Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 487) remark,

> One must resort to heuristic arguments not only for motivating the algorithms, as is often the case in supervised learning as well, but also for judgments as to the quality of the results. This uncomfortable situation has led to heavy proliferation of proposed results since *effectiveness* \[emphasis added\] is a matter of opinion and cannot be verified directly.

Indeed, Venables and Ripley ([2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000376), p. 316) issue a rather dire warning on relying on cluster analysis exclusively in discovering groupings:

> Do not assume that “clustering” methods are the best way to discover interesting groupings in the data; in our experience the visualization methods are often far more effective. There are many different clustering methods, often giving different answers, and so the danger of over-interpretation is high.

Perhaps the take-away message from the above warnings in that, as is the case with so many statistical models, to be aware that *what you find is often a function of what tool you use to do the finding*. There is no such thing as the definitive “existence” of clusters. Rather, as was the case with previously studied dimension reduction techniques in this book, the “existence” of a dimension or cluster solution will be intimately tied to the algorithm that “discovered” the solution. Naturally, this reality has huge philosophical implications and raises many questions. For instance, that one cannot make immediate sense of a cluster solution should not necessarily imply the solution is not sensible. All we can really conclude is that it currently is not sensible to the user *at this point in history, given the context and current state of the science in which he or she practices*. Indeed, this dilemma makes for an interesting philosophical debate regarding whether the *existence* of cluster solutions is primarily *mathematical* or whether it is primarily *substantive*. It is not a debate we will have here but is an important one in the *philosophy of statistics and mathematics* and is something the user should keep in mind as we survey a variety of cluster algorithms.
