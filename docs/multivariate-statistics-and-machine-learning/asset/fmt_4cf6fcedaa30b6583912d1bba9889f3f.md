
The law can be formalized as follows for the case of the sample mean $\overline{y}$ when used as an estimator of the population mean, `μ`: for any `ε` > 0, the probability of any deviation of the kind $\overline{y} - \mu$ goes to 0 as `n` increases, eventually without bound, that is, `n`→∞. Alternatively, we could express the same thing by saying for any |`ε`|>0, the probability that $\overline{y} - \mu$ is between  − `ε` and `ε` goes to 0 as `n`→∞. Incidentally, it is worth noting that infinity, `∞`, is not regarded as a number in mathematics, but is simply a *concept*. The symbol for positive infinity in `R` is `Inf`.

The law stems from a relative-frequency interpretation of probability and more pragmatically means that *if you want to know the true probability of an event, the more data you collect, in general, the better your estimate of that probability*. Note that this does not necessarily imply that collecting more data is always advisable. A million data points can do no worse than a thousand, unless of course the cost of collecting such extra observations is prohibitive and one can conclude about just as much knowledge from the initial sample of a thousand than the million. While the law of large numbers wants us to collect more data, it is a theoretical argument only. It may not be practical in reality. That thing we call the “real world” and is the unpleasant distraction from the theoretical mathematician often interferes with our capacity to collect more data and if the identical conclusion can be drawn with lesser data points, then collecting more data may simply be a waste of time and resources. Limiting theorems such as the above are attractive, but we must not get lost in their beauty at the expense of practical, real-world considerations.

An implication of the above is that statistical *power curves* tend to asymptote (horizontally) as sample size increases, and hence the cost of collecting more data may simply not be worth it. In the age of big data and the facility by which large data sets can be collected, these truths still hold, but since collecting data can be done rather cheaply these days in many contexts, such costs are unlikely to deter businesses such as *Amazon* from collecting terabytes of data and hence ignore power curves altogether. When “Big Data” is available, power curves are left in the wind. In such contexts, data is far too easy to come by to be concerned with power curves.

However, in other settings, such as smaller experimental ones where collecting data may be very difficult and vastly expensive, establishing just how much information is needed to draw 76conclusions is a very worthwhile activity. For example, if your units of observation consist of children with a particular rare disorder, each single data point may be extremely difficult to collect and the difference between 20 and 25 observations may be a huge undertaking. Collecting data may be prohibitively expensive. The development of small-sample statistics in the early 1900s made it possible to study populations without having to sample huge numbers. This was the gift of inferential statistics. We seem to be coming full circle in the current climate where collecting bigger data sets is the norm in some areas (definitely not all), mostly due to the facility by which such data can be collected (i.e., electronically) and easily stored. Early inferential statistical procedures were originally designed to circumvent this activity and make the most out of small data. Will the age of “small data” ever return? Let’s imagine what that might look like in the following fictional academic news report headline:

> An amazing new quantitative technology is being popularized for which one only requires very small samples to make generalizations to much wider populations. We call this new age of data analysis, `SDA`, or “Small Data Analysis.”

Increasing sample size can also have an influence on Bayesian posteriors. For example, in choosing a model, for small sample size, a posterior may indicate one model, whereas as sample size grows, a competing model may have a higher posterior due to the increase in information. `Weak` vs. `strong` laws of large numbers can also be distinguished though not discussed here. For details, see [Shao](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000334) (2023) for a clear but relatively theoretical discussion. [Casella and Berger (2002)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000055) also feature a discussion (p. 235).

### 2.11.1 Law of Large Numbers and the Idea of Committee Machines

One indirect application of the law of large numbers that we will revisit later in the book is contained in the idea that *average prediction functions converge to expected prediction functions*. For instance, ten prediction functions, when averaged, get us closer to the true expected (i.e., in terms of long-run probability) prediction function than any of the single prediction functions. That is, *when we combine many weak learners, we often achieve better predictions than the capacity of any of the weak learners that make up the combination*.

An analogy to sports would be that a team of weak players produces a better outcome than any of the individual teammates. Indirectly, the law of averages is present in this statement, since it implies that the greater the `n`, where “`n`” in this case is the number of prediction functions (players), the better we will converge to the true function. This idea of combining so-called “weak learners” to produce a stronger learner generally falls under the general category of *committee machines*.

## 2.12 Random Variables and Probability Density Functions

A *random variable* is a mathematical variable that is associated with a probability distribution. More precisely, a random variable is a function from a sample space into the real numbers ([Casella & Berger, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000055)) such that for any “slice” or interval, that is, for any subset of the sample space is called an *event*. The function must as well be *measurable* ([Mohri et al., 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000253)) though measurability of functions is well beyond the scope of the current text. We simply make mention of the word “measurability” here to denote the fact that not all functions are measurable, and hence saying that random variables are a function onto the real numbers is incomplete. Deeper and more sophisticated definitions of a random variable will first distinguish between *measurable* versus *nonmeasurable* functions (e.g., see [Shao](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000334), 2023).77

As an example of a random variable, consider the variable `x[i]` having values 0, 1, 2, 3. The variable `x[i]`, as it currently stands, is often called a *mathematical variable*. The descriptor “mathematical” is usually implicit, but such a variable may often be called such in a statistics text simply to distinguish it from a random variable. As the reader will recall, it is a variable because `x[i]` can take on different values in the set. If `x[i]` could only take on a single value (e.g., 0 or any other value), it would not be a variable but instead would be a *constant*. It becomes a *random* variable when we associate probabilities with those values in the set such that they sum to unity. For instance, the aforementioned `x[i]` is now a random variable in the table below, where each value of `x[i]` is now associated with a probability of 0.25:

| `x[i]` || `p(x[i])` |
| --- | --- |
| 0 || 0.25 |
| --- | --- |
| 1 || 0.25 |
| 2 || 0.25 |
| 3 || 0.25 |
|  || `∑` = 1.0 |

Note that there is no requirement that probabilities be equal across values of `x[i]` for `x[i]` to be a random variable. The only requirement is that the sum of probabilities is equal to 1.0. That is, we could have just as easily distributed the probabilities as 0.20, 0.40, 0.30, and 0.10 to illustrate the concept.
