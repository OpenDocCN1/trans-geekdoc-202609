
Theoretical explanations can become quite complex in science and industry. Pragmatic and simple explanations are often better. Why does a stock go up in the stock market? Yes, we can consider the multidimensional and multifactorial economic influences and market forces, yet on a more descriptive, pragmatic level, stocks go up for primarily one reason and one reason only: *more people than not are buying it*. Of course, such isn’t much of a theory as to *why* more investors are buying the stock, but it is the simplest and most practical explanation regarding why the stock is increasing.

### 1.13.1 Parsimony vs. Complexity Trade-Off

Choosing the simplest theory or model is not the only priority of the scientist or quantitative modeler. Even if a very complex model explains the data better than a simpler one, it does not necessarily imply the more complex model should be chosen. For example, in a multiple regression model one can make the model as complex as one desires by adding more and more predictors. As is well known, `R²` for the model will necessarily increase given the inclusion of an increasing number of predictors even if the addition in variance explained (greater than zero) by those predictors is negligible. Hence, a model with 1,000 predictors will have a higher `R²` than a model 39with 100, but variance explained should not be our only criterion for model selection, especially if the purpose is scientific explanation. If the goal is simply raw prediction and the availability of such data on multiple variables is easy to come by, then choosing the model with 1,000 predictors is the correct choice. One example of this might be *Amazon* collecting numerous predictors from online purchases that add to its ability to predict your next purchase. Choosing the simpler, more parsimonious model is not priority given the ease by which such data are collected by Amazon’s virtually unlimited storage and upper-limit computing power.

However, if we are trying to explain the behavior of migrating birds, for instance, building a theory on 1,000 predictors may be unwieldy if not impossible. The complexity of the model is too rich, and hence simplifying the model, even at the risk of lowering predictive power, may be called for. From a scientific perspective, *the goal of modeling is rarely to generate models that fit perfectly, but instead to explain as much as possible using the least amount of information as possible*.

These ideas motivate the use of *penalized* model fit statistics that attempt to strike a balance between providing a good fit while minimizing the number of predictors providing that fit. In this book we will survey a number of penalized versions of statistics and unpack them to see exactly how they work.

## 1.14 Overfitting vs. Underfitting

In continuing our discussion of theory and model-fitting, we note there to be a delicate balance between *overfitting* versus *underfitting* a theory to data. The general idea behind overfitting is when a model is tailored too specifically to the training data and will likely not generalize well to the test data. At the opposite extreme, *underfitting* is when it is not specific enough to the training data. The goal of statistical inference and machine learning is that of *generalization* and hence both overfitting and underfitting have consequences. Not surprisingly, the magnitude of these consequences is related to the sample sizes used in such generalization or lack thereof. If the sample size is too small and the complexity of the hypothesis class too rich, overfitting is almost guaranteed to occur. On the opposite end, for increasingly larger sample sizes, a lack of complexity in a model will be more prone to underfitting.

Consider a very simple conceptual example of over vs. underfitting. Suppose you are a therapist attempting to identify the issue with your client’s daily anxiety and elevated heart rate. After referrals to medical practitioners, you have successfully ruled out any biological issues. You suspect however that the “jitters” have to do with the amount of caffeine intake by the client. After asking the client to change their caffeine intake over the next while, you theorize that their anxiety is a function of having exactly 4.357 cups of coffee per day. Perhaps you have good reason to theorize that exact amount for this *particular client* after experimenting with various caffeine intake amounts. That is, your theory “fits” the client extremely well and seems to explain his behavior. However, as a general theory, how well will it do? Suppose you take your results and seek to generalize to future clients, telling them, “You must drink less than 4.357 cups of coffee per day.” Of course, common sense tells us that the theory built on the specific individual will not generalize to future clients, at least not very well.

What has happened here from a theoretical point of view is that you have *overfit the data*. You paid too much attention to *particulars* of the given individual (sample data) and likely capitalized on random noise in the individual’s specific caffeine sensitivity, so that when you attempted to generalize to new clients, the theory failed miserably as essentially a *point prediction*. How might you change the theory so that it generalizes better? You might instead fit a theory such as “Moderate intake of caffeine is predictive of anxiety level.” This theory will likely generalize 40better to new individuals (i.e., to new data) because it no longer relies as heavily on the specifics of the given individual you are dealing with.

On the opposite end of things, consider what *underfitting* would look like in this case. Suppose you tested the theory on your client as to simply *not drink hot beverages*. That is, you theorize that if your client avoids hot beverages altogether, such will have the effect of decreasing anxiety. Of course, that’s far too *general* of a theory; you must make it more specific. You have *underfit* the data. [Murphy (2022)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259) gives an even simpler example using a coin-flipping paradigm. Suppose you flip a coin 20 times and obtain 15 heads on the 20 flips. The maximum likelihood estimator resulting from this exercise would be heads for future flips. But clearly, this *overfits* on the training data as most likely all future flips will not be all heads. That is, we overfit the theory on too small a sample of data.
