
As we have discussed, the focus of machine learning is a bit less on the data and a bit more on the actual algorithms that carry out the data analysis. How stable are they? How stable is a procedure such as regression across similar or different samples? Does it have such high variance that even slight perturbations of the data would result in wildly different outcomes? Questions such as these fall under the general rubric of *algorithm stability*. A stable algorithm is generally one for which if the training data are altered or perturbed slightly, the output of the algorithm more or less matches the degree of perturbation in the data and that as `n` increases these changes eventually disappear altogether (Liu et al., [2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000226)).

*Uniform stability* specifies a bound by which two samples cannot exceed in order to be considered stable. More narrowly, a learning algorithm is uniformly `β-stable` if when a hypothesis trained on two different samples, `h[s]` and `h[s′]` that differ by only a single point, the difference in loss functions `L` is equal to or smaller than a constant `β` with probability equal to 1. That is,

`p[(|L(h[S])−L(h[S′])|) ≤ β] = 1`

(1.1)

where `S` is the original sample, `S′` the sample with perturbation, and `β` is the *stability coefficient* that sets the upper bound (Liu et al., [2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000226); [Mohri et al., 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000253)).

A related concern is how fast or efficient is the algorithm or computation more generally. A computation is said to run in *polynomial time* if the number of elementary steps can be expressed as a polynomial function of the size of the input. Running in polynomial time is generally considered a favorable characteristic of an algorithm. A computation not running in polynomial time is often running in *exponential time*.

## 1.4 The Importance of Design, Experimental or Otherwise

Statistics and machine learning without consideration of design issues, from a scientific point of view, is vacuous. Indeed, one of many features that makes this book unique from other books on multivariate analysis and machine learning is the constant reminder throughout that *design issues are most important from a scientific point of view*, not the actual method or algorithm applied to 26the data. That is, if you are doing *science*, then design matters most. Statistics, machine learning, and computing come in second.

For example, you can perform a cluster analysis with your fancy machine learning software and algorithm, but it will never tell you “why” people who drive Hondas form a cluster from those who drive Nissans. It will tell you what is *associated* with each cluster possibly but it will likely not tell you *why*. Only experimental designs can even come close to approximating such conclusions. Hence, throughout the book, we remind the reader that this or that statistical or machine learning tool often cannot answer the deeper scientific questions of most interest. Only good design can provide approximate answers to those questions. A statistical or machine learning model cannot tell us whether vaccine A is better than vaccine B when it comes to treating COVID-19, but a good experimental design might.

## 1.5 Inductive, Deductive, and Other Logics

In philosophy there are generally two broad ways of reasoning, of “getting to the truth” as it were, *inductively* and *deductively*. Crudely, inductive logic is that of drawing conclusions from the *specific to the general*, whereas deductive logic implies drawing conclusions from the *general to the specific*. As an example, if I have never seen dogs before and witness ten dogs all with four legs, I may wager that *all dogs have four legs* based on the observations I have witnessed. This is an example of inductive logic, where the particular is the set of dogs I have witnessed and the general is that of all dogs, most of which I have not witnessed and may never witness. The larger set of observations could even be infinite in size. On the other hand, if I somehow already knew that all dogs have four legs, then it is an easy matter to *deduce* that the next dog I meet will have four legs. In this case, we are going from the general to the particular and is an example of deductive logic. *Boolean logic* is an example of a deductive process; for instance, statements such as if `a` > `b` and `b` > `c`, then `a` > `c`, the law of *transitivity*, is an example of a deductive “implication” statement in Boolean logic ([Lucci & Kopec, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000229)). Empirical science is necessarily inductive as in most cases we simply cannot have access or observe all events and must draw conclusions based on only a select few. Science can be said to have *deduction envy* in that it would love to be deductive, but such is generally not philosophically possible.

Generalizing from the specific to the general is usually imperfect and comes with error. *Statistical inference* is a way of generalizing from the specific to the general and relies heavily on probability theory. When we see ten dogs with four legs and induce that all dogs have four legs, we are taking a “leap” in such a conclusion. That is, we do not *know* if all dogs have four legs; we are “gambling” or “betting” that they do based on our current observations. This is how science generally proceeds. *Science is a gamble*. Indeed, *inductive learning* is about finding functions that best approximate an unknown function, which is essentially what *statistical learning* is about.

There also exists *fuzzy logic*, which implies that a proposition can be “sort of true.” This is related to the more broad idea of *fuzzy sets*. In fuzzy logic, one is not either dead or alive, but instead one is dead to a *matter of degree*. Likewise, fuzzy set theory assigns membership to sets by matter of degree, not absolutely. It must be emphasized that inductive and deductive logic are well a part of our lives and science; they form the basis of much of our thinking about what is “true” or “false.” The Greeks are largely responsible for providing us with the rigors of deductive logic, where mathematical statements, for instance, are only regarded as *true* if they can be rigorously deduced.

More recently, theoretical logicians such as Boole and Frege elevated logic into its own mathematical discipline. Despite the long and historical study of logic, it should be emphasized that the “goodness” of logic relies on the assumption that *logic itself is correct*. If our assumptions about 27the power of these logics ends up being found false one day (presumably by way of even more logical thinking!), much of mathematics if not all would fall to the ground as would other modes of thinking as well. That is, if essential *arguments* such as the following could somehow be refuted one day (e.g., through a truth table shown to be false), or at minimum no longer generally assumed to be true, it would pose a revolution (to put it *mildly*) in science and thinking in general:

$$\\begin{array}{l} \\left. p\\rightarrow q \\right. \\\\ p \\\\ {\\therefore q} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

where the above reads that if `p` then `q`, `p`, therefore `q`. Of course, at first glance, the proposition that the above *modus ponens* could actually be incorrect as a way of concluding `q` sounds entirely irrational if not idiotic. More than likely, these essential capstones of inference will continue to hold the test of time. We merely point out the nature of *constructing knowledge* of this kind, that so much of it depends on prior assumptions, or provable assumptions based on even deeper assumptions no matter how “convincing” or “obvious” those assumptions may be. History can sometimes surprise us, as we will soon discuss in reference to Gödel’s theorems.
