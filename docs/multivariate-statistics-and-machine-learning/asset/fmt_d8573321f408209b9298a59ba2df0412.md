It turns out that taking squared deviations is mathematically more tractable and facilitates differentiation easier than would absolute values. However, it should be emphasized that taking absolute-valued deviations is permissible and is done in many contexts, especially those in which outliers in the distribution may make the squared sum especially large. That is, squaring a deviation magnifies its deviation much more than taking an absolute value. For example, a deviation of 4 has a squared value of 16, whereas it only has a value of 4 in absolute value. Hence, if you wish to minimize the influence of outlying data, then taking absolute deviations may be preferable in some cases to taking squared deviations. There is a whole literature on taking absolute-valued deviations in a variety of statistical and machine learning methods. Such a procedure is usually called *least-absolute-value (deviation) regression* in a linear model context.

## 2.23 Skewness and Kurtosis

The *skewness* of a distribution is a measure of the general shape of that distribution away from the typical bell-shaped normal distribution and is given by93

$$\\gamma = \\frac{E\\left\\lbrack \\left( y\_{i} - \\mu \\right)^{3} \\right\\rbrack}{\\left( {E\\left\\lbrack \\left( y\_{i} - \\mu \\right)^{2} \\right\\rbrack} \\right)^{3/2}}$$

(2.47)

A distribution that is positively skewed has a tail that stretches out toward the right (i.e., higher scores), while a distribution that is negatively skewed has a tail that stretches out toward the left (i.e., lower scores). A distribution with an absence of skew may have a single mode (i.e., the classic normal distribution) or may have several modes (e.g., a bimodal distribution). *A lack of skew in a distribution should not automatically be associated with or imply a normal shape*. For example, the distribution in [Figure 2.7](#c2.xhtml#f2.7) exhibits virtually no skew.

To reiterate, it can be easy to assume that upon hearing a distribution has no skew to visualize a normal distribution with a single mode. However, this could be in error, as the plot in [Figure 2.7](#c2.xhtml#f2.7) reveals. *An absence of skewness does not necessarily imply a single mode*. This is one more reason to always plot data. Numerical summaries are usually never enough to draw strong conclusions about distributional characteristics.

The *kurtosis* of a distribution measures the general “tailness” of that distribution ([Upton & Cook, 2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000368)) and more succinctly the behavior of the distribution’s tails with regard to how “heavy” they are, meaning how much mass exists in the tails ([DeCarlo, 1997](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000078)). It is given by

$$k = \\frac{E\\left\\lbrack \\left( y\_{i} - \\mu \\right)^{4} \\right\\rbrack}{\\left( {E\\left\\lbrack \\left( y\_{i} - \\mu \\right)^{2} \\right\\rbrack} \\right)^{2}}$$

(2.48)

A distribution is said to be *mesokurtic* if the distribution exhibits kurtosis typical of a bell-shaped curve, *platykurtic* if the distribution has lighter tails and is often flatter toward the center of the distribution when compared to the normal, and *leptokurtic* if the distribution exhibits heavier tails and is often more narrow in the middle of the distribution, typically making it more “peaked” than what would be expected in the normal density. While degree of “peakness” of a distribution is not the formal definition of kurtosis, the heaviness of tails often to some extent (though definitely not *always*) corresponds to whether the distribution is wider or more narrow toward the center of the distribution. However, associating kurtosis entirely with the peakness of a distribution has largely been jettisoned.94

## 2.24 Coefficient of Variation

The *coefficient of variation* is yet another measure of dispersion in a data set, and essentially “scales” the standard deviation `σ` by the value of the mean, `μ`. It can be useful in this regard to know how the standard deviation for a set of data (sample or population) relates to the size of that data’s mean. Hence, if we report the standard deviation equal to 2, knowing what the mean is can help us assess the extent to which a standard deviation of 2 is considered “big” or “small.”

The coefficient of variation is given by

$$CV = \\frac{\\sigma}{\\left| \\mu \\right|}$$

(2.49)

where `σ` is the population standard deviation and `|μ|` is the absolute value of the population mean. As an example, for `σ = 5`, the coefficient of variation when `μ = 5` is much smaller (i.e., 1) than if `μ = 1` (i.e., 5). Hence, comparing standard deviations of distributions without incorporating respective means may, in some cases, be misleading, and a useful practice when comparing variability in distributions is to also incorporate the coefficient of variation into the discussion.

## 2.25 Statistical Estimation

Classical (and Bayesian, for that matter) statistics is all about *estimation*. Unless we have entire populations, we cannot know, for certain, the parameters of those populations and instead must estimate them using sample data. Recall that if you have the population data at your disposal, there is nothing to estimate, as one can simply compute descriptive statistics on the parameters of interest. This is sometimes misunderstood by newcomers to statistics, many of whom, upon learning about significance tests and `p`-values become in a hurry to conduct such tests on every data situation they encounter, all too proud to report `p`-values! However, a significance test on population parameters provides no additional insight beyond observing the parameters themselves. Significance tests are only meaningful when performed on sample data and when we wish to know something about the population parameters from which the sample data was presumably collected.

We say that `θ̂` is an estimator of the population parameter `θ`. More precisely, it is a *point estimator* because it seeks to estimate one particular and specific value of the parameter, `θ`. In general, an *estimator is any function of the sample data that is used to estimate a population parameter*. When we apply the estimator to some data and obtain a value for it, for instance, applying the mean estimator and obtaining a numerical mean, we say that the *estimator* has produced an *estimate*. Point estimators are almost always *statistics* (since they are computed on samples), but statistics are not always point estimators. As an example, if we compute the mean salary of a university department, such a mean is a descriptive statistic on the sample; however, it is not a point estimator because we are not using it to estimate any parameter.

An estimator is *unbiased* if the expectation of `θ̂` is equal to `θ`, that is,

`E(θ̂) = θ`

(2.50)

In contrast, an estimator is biased if the above equality does not hold,95

`E(θ̂) ≠ θ`

(2.51)

with the amount of bias being equal to the difference between the expectation and the actual parameter value:

Bias = `E(θ̂) − θ`(2.52)
