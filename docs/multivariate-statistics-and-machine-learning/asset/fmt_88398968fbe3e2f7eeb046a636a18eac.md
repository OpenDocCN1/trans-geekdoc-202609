## Introduction, Motivation, Pedagogy, and Ideas About Learning
DOI: [10.4324/9781003376941-2](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003376941-2)
Everything has changed, yet at the same time so much has remained the same. In the past, aspiring scientists, whether in the natural or social sciences, would specialize in their discipline while at the same time taking a number of undergraduate and graduate courses in statistics and research methods ranging from theoretical to applied depending on the flavor of the course and the preference of the instructor. In addition to the typical introductory and usually relatively superficial statistics course that virtually every undergraduate must enroll regardless of specialization (e.g., biology, psychology, business, etc.), advanced undergraduate and graduate students are typically required to take a one-half semester of studying foundations (e.g., probability, hypothesis-testing, etc.) leading up to the analysis of variance (ANOVA) while the second half of the year would be typically spent studying more advanced ANOVA models and simple linear and multiple regression as well as possibly including a survey of the generalized linear model (e.g., logistic and Poisson regression). Such exposure would provide the student with a basic understanding of *what’s going on* in applied statistics sufficient to get their foot in the door to conducting and interpreting their own analyses for theses and dissertations, or at minimum and probably more realistically, be able to converse with someone (e.g., supervisor) able to *guide* them in conducting and interpreting their own analyses. After such a year-long sequence, a multivariate analysis course might also be offered that would feature topics such as *multivariate analysis of variance* (*MANOVA*), *discriminant analysis*, *principal components analysis* (*PCA*), *factor analysis*, among others, including possibly *path analysis* and *structural equation modeling* for students in the social and allied sciences. Much of the above coursework would feature, to varying extents, the use of software, such as Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Statistical Analysis System (SAS), and more modernly, R, and possibly even Python.
