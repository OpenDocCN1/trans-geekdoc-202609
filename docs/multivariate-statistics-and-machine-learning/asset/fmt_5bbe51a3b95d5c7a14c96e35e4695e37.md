
## 5.9 Forward, Backward, and Stepwise Regression

In typical forward regression, predictors are entered based on some entry criteria, such as statistical significance (e.g., attaining *p* < 0.05). The procedure halts when no more predictors reach that criterion. In backward regression, all predictors begin in the model and are removed once they no longer reach *p* < 0.05 (or whatever level one chooses). Stepwise regression is typically a mix of both forward and backward approaches; however there are many stepwise programs and packages and it behooves the reader to study the details of the package they are using to clearly understand what the stepwise program does or does not do. The algorithms in various packages can become quite complex, which if not understood, provides ample opportunity for drawing false substantive conclusions from the stepwise results.

### 5.9.1 Forward, Backward, and Stepwise in R

There are several ways (and packages) for conducting forward, backward, or stepwise regressions in R. Many packages and routines will often combine routines, so as already emphasized, it is essential that one thoroughly familiarize oneself with the given package to know exactly what is being computed.

For our example here, we feature the `mtcars` data in R once more, on which we conduct both a `forward` and `backward` stepwise regression. Recall that the `mtcars` data contains numerous vehicle specs such as gas mileage (`mpg`), number of cylinders (`cyl`), and displacement (`disp`), among other characteristics of vehicles. We will use the `StepReg` package (Li et al., [2024](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000219)) to carry out stepwise regression:240

In specifying `mpg ~ .`, we are implicitly requesting modeling `mpg` as a function of all other variables. We will now specify that we wish to conduct a stepwise, specifically:

```
> fit <- stepwise(formula = formula,
+ data = mtcars,
+ type = “linear”,
+ strategy = c(“forward”, “bidirection”, “backward”),
+ metric = c(“AIC”, “BIC”, “SL”))
> fit
```

We have requested above that forward, bidirection, and backward elimination be performed, and hence our output will be quite long. We have also requested `AIC`, `BIC`, and `SL` as the metrics for evaluating the regressions, where “SL” corresponds to “significance level.” Indeed, if we choose to look at the results from the stepwise (`fit`), we will get a listing of the model selection variables in the data set that is quite long and provides much detail. ## 5.10 Mediated and Moderated Regression Models

Mediation has a relatively long history in statistics and has been motivated primarily by the substantive interests of social scientists, primarily psychologists. Despite its popularity and hype in 242some fields, it is important to understand what statistical mediation *can* vs. *cannot* do. Moderation, on the other hand, is most generally simply another word for *interaction*. In ANOVA-type models, we usually refer to such as interactions, whereas in regression models it has come to be known as moderation. It is vitally important to realize at the outset that *mediation and moderation do not, by themselves, imply a physical process*. That is, conducting a test of statistical mediation and concluding *Z mediates the relationship between X and Y* is only necessarily true *statistically*; it is not true *substantively* unless one has a substantive rationale or argument outside of the statistical model for inferring such a mediational process. There are countless published articles, even in “high impact factor” journals, where researchers conclude a mediational process simply based on *statistical* mediation, which exemplifies one of many philosophical and methodological abuses of statistics. We revisit and discuss this issue more extensively later.

### 5.10.1 Statistical Mediation

We purposely title this section by the name of *statistical* mediation for the reason that, as mentioned, far too often researchers tend to believe that if they apply a mediation model to data, it can thereby “extract” a mediational *process*. This is simply not the case. In addressing this important issue, let’s first very briefly survey how mediation (and moderation) is defined in the literature. For example, [Little et al. (2007, p. 207)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000225) offer the following definition and distinction:

> Generally speaking, a mediator can be thought of as the carrier or transporter of information along the causal chain of effects. A moderator, on the other hand, is the changer of a relationship in a system.

Pearl ([2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000284), pp. 1–2) claims, “Scientifically, mediation tells us “how nature works” and, practically, it enables us to predict behavior under a rich variety of conditions and interventions.”
