
An alternative approach to these clustering approaches is to assume the data arise from a `mixture` of (typically) Gaussian distributions or other parametric distributions (e.g., Poisson for discrete data, etc.). These distributions, both kind and number, are often chosen by the user based on prior substantive experience in the area and prior knowledge or hypotheses about the mixture components, though in most cases where specific knowledge is not known, Gaussian distributions are usually the default. The number of components, however, can be regarded as a hyperparameter 526that the user must specify ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)). If the number of clusters is known `a priori` and hence the “ground truth” of the number of clusters exists, specifying the number of components is easy. However, and as usual, cluster analysis is performed when knowledge of the number of clusters is not known `a priori`, in which case prior knowledge of what the user might `expect` to see may be useful in designating the number of components.

A `mixture model`, as the name suggests and as we have seen already in other contexts, is a mix of probability density distributions which for cluster analysis has the general form,

$$f\\left( x;\\theta \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{g = 1}^{G}{\\pi\_{g}f\_{g}\\left( x;\\theta\_{g} \\right)}}$$  
(14.12)

where there are `g` = 1 to `G` clusters, `π`[`g`] is the probability of an observation arising from the `g`th cluster (`π`[`g`] represents the `mixing proportions`), `f`[`g`](`x`;`θ`[`g`]) is the probability density function in the `g`th cluster, and `θ`[`g`] is a vector of parameters that requires estimation. The usual form to assume for `f`[`g`](`x`;`θ`[`g`]) is that of multivariate normality, implying that `θ`[`g`] will therefore consist of a mean vector and covariance matrix ([Jolliffe, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000174)).

Once the mixture model is fit, posterior probabilities `p`(*y*/*θ*) are obtained. The most popular mixture model, as mentioned, is that of a `Gaussian mixture model` (Murray, 2022) with density `N`(*y*|`μ`[`g`],`Σ`[`g`]) where now `N`(*y*|`μ`[`g`],`Σ`[`g`]) denotes a multivariate normal distribution with mean vector `μ`[`g`] and covariance matrix `Σ`[`g`]. A more robust version here would utilize the `t`-distribution instead of the normal ([McLachlan & Chang, 2004](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000245)).

The statistical objective in a `mixture model` is to estimate the parameters of the model by maximizing the `likelihood function` of the data to generate posterior probabilities of cluster membership with respect to the data, given knowledge of the model parameters. A problem can occur when maximizing the likelihood in a mixture of Gaussians, however, which is the problem of potential `singularities`. A singularity can occur if one of the components of the mixture model has a mean equal to that of a single data point. As noted by [Bishop (2006)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032), under this condition, a single data point contributes a single term to the likelihood function,

$$N\\left( x\_{i} \\middle| x\_{i},\\sigma\_{j}^{2}I \\right) = \\frac{1}{\\left( 2\\pi \\right)^{1/2}}\\frac{1}{\\sigma\_{j}}$$  
(14.13)

where `σ`[`j`]² is the variance of the `j`th component and `x`[`i`] is a given data point, which, as a consequence of the singularity, is equal to the mean of the given component, that of `μ`[`j`]. The problem occurs as `σ`[`j`] gets closer and closer to 0, and in the limit,

$$\\lim\\limits\_{\\sigma\_{j}\\rightarrow 0}\\left( {\\frac{1}{\\left( 2\\pi \\right)^{1/2}}\\frac{1}{\\sigma\_{j}}} \\right) = \\infty$$  

and hence the log-likelihood will go to infinity due to 1/`σ`[`j`] = 1/0. In cases where this does occur, a random value for the mean can be “reset” to help avoid the “collapsing” of a point on the likelihood in this way. For a more thorough description of this problem, see [Bishop (2006)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032).

As mentioned, the user can select from different choices of model distributions and parameters which have the effect of inducing `flexibility` with regard to the probability distribution for each component. The advantage of a mixture modeling approach is that resulting clusters will tend to 527have high peaks resulting from relatively tightly and distinct cluster centers and hence the mixture model can account for cluster data relatively well in many cases.

Mixture models are part of the wider family of probability-based clustering algorithms which feature the idea of `soft clustering`, where a case is assigned to a cluster not `deterministically` but instead, `probabilistically`. These models are often referred to as `generative models` such that they assume observations were `generated` from one or more probability distributions. The reader may recognize “Bayesian” tones in this idea. More technically, the underlying idea behind a generative model is that one can generate synthetic data by drawing values from a marginal distribution ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)).

A popular method for the estimation of these probability-based models is that of maximum likelihood (ML) using the well-known EM algorithm. Recall that the EM algorithm is a general optimization routine typically employed for the treatment of `incomplete data`, but has been found to be especially valuable for fitting mixture models in cluster analysis, consisting of the `E-step` and `M-step` in its iteration. The `E`-`step` (`expectation`) and `M-step` (`maximization`) are iteratively executed to convergence to determine optimal parameters, where in the end posterior probabilities are estimated and a probabilistic model is obtained with `soft assignments` in place of `hard assignments` of cases to clusters. It turns out that special cases of the EM algorithm are equivalent to a soft version of the `k`-means algorithm, where the representative distances are used to define soft EM assignment probabilities ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)).

### 14.12.1 Model Selection for Clustering Solutions

At first glance, model selection in a cluster analysis mixture model environment might be performed by simply maximizing the marginal likelihood, or more specifically, its logarithm, and choosing the cluster solution for which the log-likelihood is the greatest for `g`[1,]`g`[2], ...`G` solutions. However, formal tests for this are difficult to come by ([McLachlan & Chang, 2004](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000245)). A formal test of `H`[0] : `g` = `g`[0] vs. `H`[1] : `g` > `g`[0] (where `g`[0] is the number of clusters under a null and alternative, respectively) is possible via the bootstrap, where bootstrap samples are drawn from a given fitted mixture model for a specific `H`[0] of `g` = `g`[0] components and the value of  − 2log `λ` is computed both for `H`[0] and for `H`[1] : `g` > `g`[0]. A `p`-value can then be obtained to learn whether adding another component is, in a statistical sense, worthwhile.
