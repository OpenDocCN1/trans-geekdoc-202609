
where “`p`” here is the number of parameters in the model, including the intercept term. From (5.24) we can see that as `p` increases relative to a stable `R²`, `R_adj²` decreases instead of automatically increasing as was the case for `R²`. Hence, `R_adj²` essentially “penalizes” the user for including predictors that are not “worth their salt” in increasing `R²`.

The `C_p` statistic (“Mallow’s `C_p`”) is another approach to penalizing model fit,

$$C\_{p} = \\frac{1}{n}\\left( RSS + 2d{\\hat{\\sigma}}^{2} \\right)$$  
(5.25)

where, as before, `n` is the number of cases or observations, `RSS` is the residual sums of squares on the training data, `d` now is the number of predictors (recall that `p` was the number of parameters in `R_adj²`), and `σ̂²` is an estimate of error variance using all predictors, usually MS residual. The nature of the penalty in `C_p` is by way of `2*d*σ̂²`. Smaller values of `C_p` are preferred over larger values since smaller `RSS` implies a better-fitting model. However, this is offset by `2*d*σ̂²`, where increasing `d` has the effect of increasing `2*d*σ̂²`. Hence, `C_p`, if it could speak, would say, “Congrats on getting `RSS` down, but that decrease will be penalized by how many `d` it required to do so. If any of your `d` are not sufficiently contributing to the observed decrease in `RSS`, you may want to consider removing them instead of being penalized for including them.”

A similar statistic to the `C_p`, and one that is more popular, is that of the AIC (`Akaike’s Information Criterion`) statistic,

$$AIC = \\frac{1}{n{\\hat{\\sigma}}^{2}}\\left( RSS + 2d{\\hat{\\sigma}}^{2} \\right)$$  
(5.26)

which is very similar in logic to that of the `C_p`: the only difference is the multiplier $\\frac{1}{n{\\hat{\\sigma}}^{2}}$ instead of $\\frac{1}{n}$ in `C_p`. The penalty is still equal to `2*d*σ̂²`, however. In this formulation, increasingly smaller values are preferred over larger values. The following is another version of AIC, the original version given by Akaike ([1974](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000409)),236

− 2 ln `p(D|θ)` + 2 `m`  
(5.27)

which is the familiar log-likelihood, ln `p(D|θ)`, however pre-multiplied by −2. For this version of AIC, smaller is also better than larger, as the penalty `m` (number of parameters in the model) acts to increase AIC, and if the decrease in − 2 ln `p(D|θ)` isn’t “worth” the increase by `m`, it makes adding the extra parameter(s) less worthwhile in terms of maintaining model parsimony. However, “smaller is always better” does not always pertain to the report of an AIC statistic; it depends on how it is formulated. AIC could, in theory, appear as simply ln `p(D|θ)` − 2 `m` where now a larger than not value is preferred. Notice that now the log-likelihood is positive without pre-multiplying it by −2 and as before we are subtracting the number of parameters in the way of `2m`. Be sure you are clear on how AIC (or BIC) is specifically defined for your particular application (e.g., software, package, etc.) before you interpret it.

A derivative of both the `C_p` and AIC is the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC),

$$BIC = \\frac{1}{n{\\hat{\\sigma}}^{2}}\\left( RSS + \\log\\left( n \\right)d{\\hat{\\sigma}}^{2} \\right)$$  
(5.28)

The BIC often penalizes to a greater extent than the AIC. The key difference in the two statistics is in what follows `RSS` in their respective formulations. In the AIC, that term is `2*d*σ̂²`, where in the BIC, it is `log(n)*d*σ̂²`. All else equal, as sample size `n` increases, so does log(`n`) and BIC will typically “punish” more than AIC for the same `RSS` and `σ̂²`. As with AIC, be sure you know how the author is defining BIC before concluding that “smaller is better” (e.g., see [Schwarz, 1978](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000328); [Fraley & Raftery, 1998](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000109), where BIC is translated into a “larger is better” statistic).

As the name suggests, BIC originates from Bayesian decision theory and hence is derived from Bayesian principles. It can be shown that choosing a model with the minimum BIC is equivalent to choosing the model with the highest approximate posterior probability ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). What is more, BIC is asymptotically consistent as a selection criterion, which implies that the probability that BIC will select the correct model increases toward unity (i.e., 1) as the amount of information (sample size) on which it is based increases. This is not true for AIC, which will too often select a model that is too rich or complex as information on which it is based increases (i.e., `n`→∞). For smaller sample sizes, BIC typically penalizes much more severely than AIC and hence may choose a model that is too simple ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)).

An additional measure of effect size that is sometimes recommended in regression is that of the `semi-partial correlation` coefficient ([Hays, 1994](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000149)). The semi-partial correlation partials out variance due to other predictors in the model before relating a predictor to the response variable. That is, it is the bivariate correlation between an adjusted variable (predictor) and an unadjusted variable (response). For details, see [Hays (1994)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000149).

### 5.7.2 Comparing `R_adj²`, `C_p`, `AIC`, and `BIC` to Cross-Validation

We have surveyed above a few ways of penalizing training data to assess model fit and model selection. Which measure is best? Should one use any of the `R_adj²`, `C_p`, AIC, or BIC options, or should one use cross-validation? Cross-validation is usually first choice, since it is a direct estimate of the test error, whereas only `C_p` and AIC rather efficiently, though indirectly, estimate test error. Cross-validation also makes fewer assumptions about the underlying model ([James et al., 2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)). Historically, before high-speed computing power and very large data sets (at least in some fields), performing cross-validation directly was computationally quite difficult or borderline impossible and hence computing measures such as `C_p`, `R_adj²`, etc. was more feasible and convenient. Today, however, with high-speed computing, cross-validation can be performed with relative ease. Hence, given the availability of relatively cheap computing speed, cross-validation is often the preferred option for model validation. Of course, nothing stops the investigator from simultaneously computing many competing effect-size measures and comparing them to see if they triangulate on the same data set. There is no “type I” error rate when computing effect sizes; the researcher can compute as many of them as she would like.

## 5.8 Assumptions for Regression

Virtually all statistical models are founded on a set of assumptions. Verifying assumptions for regression is important because least-squares estimates and inference are only as good as the assumptions on which they are based. Further, techniques have been developed that allow for users to evaluate the impact that data points have on the estimated least-squares solution. Thus, in addition to verifying assumptions such as `linearity` and `homoscedasticity of variance`, residual analyses that evaluate such things as leverage, raw, standardized, and studentized residuals are generally recommended.
