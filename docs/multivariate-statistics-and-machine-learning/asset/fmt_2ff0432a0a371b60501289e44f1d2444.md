
which the reader will recognize as the familiar *residual sums of squares criterion* (`RSS`) where `y[i]` are observed values and `ŷ[s[j]]` are predicted values within a given subsection of the decision surface for a combination of predictors. The predictor which results in the greatest reduction in `RSS` is chosen at each step. This process continues for each predictor and space until some criterion to stop the process is attained. The stopping criteria may include a consideration of sample size limitations. That is, tree splitting may continue until there is minimal `n` at each split, enough to halt the algorithm.372

### 10.2.3 Why Not Multiway Splits?

In our construction of decision trees, note that we are performing *binary* splits at each node. But why not perform splits containing more than two possibilities? While it is also possible to split a tree node into more than two groups, generally referred to as *multiway* or *multivariate* splits, it generally produces inferior results when compared to the simpler binary split. As well, multiway nodes can often be difficult to interpret ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005); [Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)). The major reason for this is that multiway splits can lead to too many nodes near the tree root that have only a few data points, thus leaving insufficient data for later splits. Therefore, instead of attempting multiway splits, the generally preferred strategy is for numerous binary splits.

At times, it can be beneficial to preprocess the data before fitting a tree. This includes standardization of the data. Trying out both solutions, raw and standardized, may be advantageous to learn of any effects due to standardizing scores prior to building a tree.

> *Minimizing residual sums of squares is a common criterion for choosing a predictor at each step of building a decision tree. The algorithm is “greedy” in that by minimizing `RSS` at a given split, it does not consider longer-term consequences for branches to follow.*

### 10.2.4 Overfitting, Saturation, and Tree Pruning

A single decision tree fit to data is likely to overfit the data because it is built too specific to the particular data set. This would be akin to fitting a regression curve (not limited to a line) a bit too closely to a scatterplot of points. Of course, the consequence of this is that an overfit tree is likely to not perform that well on test data for reasons we are already familiar with.

If the tree is grown to the point where none of the nodes can be split any further, the tree is said to be *saturated*. That is, saturated trees have exhausted their potential for performing splits. Especially in high-dimensional space, it is very easy to allow the tree to grow extremely large, and if we allow it to grow to the point of saturation, it can actually become unwieldily large. So, how does one prevent overfitting? In the earlier days of decision trees, tree growers attempted to restrict the growth of the tree by implementing so-called “stopping rules” to prevent overfitting. However, as shown by [Breiman et al. (1984)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000045), such stopping rules are not generally advised and perhaps counter-intuitively, the preferred solution is often to build a very large tree and then perform what is known as *pruning* to obtain a *subtree* ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 308). That is, it can be shown that building a saturated tree and then *scaling back* the tree is often preferable to early stopping or trying to, more generally, *regularize* the tree too early.

How to prune then becomes a crucial process. Indeed, much of the research in the evolution of decision trees has not been so much about how to grow a tree, but rather *how to prune it back*. The general problem to overcome in pruning is that *pruning generates bias*. Why is this the case? Consider that when we prune a tree, we are now imperfectly estimating the data, the same way a regression line imperfectly estimates a swarm of data in a scatterplot. Another approach might be to forego tree pruning entirely and simply grow many trees quite large (via resampling) and then take an average of the trees to reduce variance. As we will see later, this technique of averaging is referred to as *bagging*, which is a short name for *bootstrap aggregating*. Not surprisingly, the “best” pruned tree would be the one that performs best on *cross-validation*. Doing so, however, 373would involve entertaining many different trees. Hence, we need an alternative strategy for pruning and selecting a smaller set of trees for consideration.

> *Decision trees are easily subject to overfitting. Implementing “stopping rules” to prevent the tree from growing too large is usually not the ideal solution. The preferred solution is often to allow the tree to grow to saturation and then prune it back.*

### 10.2.5 Cost Complexity or Weakest Link Pruning

The procedure of *cost complexity pruning*, which also goes by the name of *weakest link pruning*, is one way of providing us with the subset of trees we require when pruning. Instead of considering every possible tree, we consider only a sequence of trees that will be indexed by a nonnegative tuning parameter `α`. Tuning parameters, of course, are not new by now, as we have seen them repeatedly in other procedures such as ridge and lasso regression. For each value of the tuning parameter `α`, we will obtain a *subtree* such that the following criterion is minimized:

$$\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{J}\\left( y\_{i} - {\\hat{y}}\_{s\_{j}} \\right)^{2}} + \\alpha \\middle| T \\right|$$  

(10.1)

The number of terminal nodes is indicated by `|T|` of the given tree `T`. The tuning parameter `α` controls the trade-off between the subtree’s complexity and its fit to the training data ([James et al., 2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)). We can see that as `α` increases, it results in a relatively large increase in the overall error *especially when there are several terminal nodes to the tree* (`|T|`) since `|T|` multiplied by a larger number than not in `α` will result in a larger product `α|T|` than not. Informally, what this means is that if you want a lot of nodes to your tree, *then there is a cost to doing so*, since we would expect `RSS` to decrease with increasing number of nodes unless we invoked a penalty. This idea parallels that of multiple regression where the addition of more predictors, without a penalty, generally decreases residual sums of squares. Recall the goal is to minimize $\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{J}\\left( y\_{i} - {\\hat{y}}\_{s\_{j}} \\right)^{2}$ which means smaller values are preferred to larger values. The choice of `α` is generally made through cross-validation or an independent sample test set where an independent test set may be preferable to cross-validation if the data set is large enough and can be split accordingly. It should be emphasized as well that for any value of the penalty, there may be more than a single minimizing subtree which makes selection of the “best” tree in this situation a bit more challenging. As values for `α` increase away from zero, the decision tree gets “pruned” and so obtaining an ideal value for `α` is of paramount importance ([James et al., 2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168), p. 309).

> *For increasing values of `α`, the product `α|T|` increases, where `T` is the number of terminal nodes. That is, as the complexity of the tree increases, `α|T|` serves as a penalty and increases `RSS`. Hence, if the nodes included in T are not “worthwhile” in reducing `RSS` sufficiently, then penalized `RSS` will end up increasing rather than decreasing.*374
