# 6 Regularization Methods in RegressionRidge, Lasso, and Elastic Net

DOI: [10.4324/9781003376941-9](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003376941-9)

In the previous chapter we provided an overview of simple and multiple linear regression methods. We saw that the estimator of choice was an unbiased one, that is, a “BLUE” estimator. The quality of being *unbiased* seemed like a desirable feature to possess. Indeed, from elementary statistics courses onward, students are regularly taught that an unbiased estimator is one of the most favorable qualities of an estimator, in addition to other characteristics such as consistency, sufficiency, etc.

In some cases, however, especially where the matrix of predictors is ill-conditioned (e.g., collinearity), it may be worthwhile to drop the requirement of an unbiased estimator and instead *adopt an estimator that is biased*. At first glance, this may seem statistically unintuitive and borderline reckless. To actually *prefer* a biased estimator seems to go against all essential statistical teaching for favoring unbiased estimators. However, as we will see, choosing a biased estimator in regression has the effect of driving coefficients of predictors toward or to 0, thus potentially de-emphasizing predictors that are not essential to the regression solution. By driving coefficients to or toward 0, using a biased estimator can facilitate variable selection in multiple regression. In fact, we will demonstrate in this chapter that the residual sums of squares of ordinary least-squares is nothing more than a special case of regularized regression for a specific choice of hyperparameter.

Three popular estimators that are known to regularize a model are the *ridge*, *lasso*, and *elastic net*. Before discussing each of these, a general and broad introduction and overview of the *principle of regularization* is in order. Regularization as a general concept is not exclusively associated with any particular estimator per se, and shows up in many different statistical and machine learning methods. That is, regularization is an *idea*, not any specific particular methodology. This point is perhaps the most important to understand as it concerns regularization.

## 6.1 The Concept of Regularization

The main idea of regularization is getting a “handle” on a model to simplify its complexity so that it potentially generalizes better to new test data and hence minimizes overfitting. Equivalently, we can say that the goal of regularization is to improve performance on the test set by adding a penalty to the training loss (Kroese et al., [2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000202)) so that the learner we do select is one that does not overfit on the training data and that generalizes relatively well on the test data. *Regularization is all about preventing overfitting and increasing generalizability*. It is often associated with so-called *shrinkage* and techniques such as ridge regression and the lasso, but it is important to understand that it is much more general and broader than this. One can regularize a model without implementing any specific algorithm. Regularization, the *concept* at least, is very simple and quite general.
