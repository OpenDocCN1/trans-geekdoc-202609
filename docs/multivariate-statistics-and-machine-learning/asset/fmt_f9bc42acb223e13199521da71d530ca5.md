
[Stevens (1946)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000347) proposed four scales of measurement: *nominal*, *ordinal*, *interval*, and *ratio*. We let `o[i]` represent the object we wish to measure. The measured object is denoted by `m(o[i])` while the true object is denoted by `t(o[i])`. Nominal scales are not true measurement at all, since they simply assign a different label to each object as merely an identifier. That is, `m(o[i])≠m(o[j])⇒t(o[i])≠t(o[j])`. The classic example of nominal scales is that of football jersey numbers on the backs of football players. That one football player has a different number than another simply implies that it is a different football player and nothing more. The numerical assignment certainly does not imply that one football player is “better” than the other.

Ordinal measurement also carries the implication of nominal measurement, `m(o[i])≠m(o[j])⇒t(o[i])≠t(o[j])`, but now with an added *magnitude* property. That is, if `m(o[i]) > m(o[j])`, such implies that `t(o[i]) > t(o[j])`. Ordinal measurement is the first instance of what we regard as measurement instead of simply assigning numbers to different objects as in the nominal case. Be sure to note, however, that `m(o[i]) > m(o[j])` does not imply *how much* `t(o[i])` > `t(o[j])`, it only implies that `o[i]` has *more* of the quantity than `o[j]`. Hence, that you finished first in the race, me second, and our mutual friend third tells us nothing about the magnitude or distance between first and second vs. second and third place. It only tells us the order, hence the name *ordinal*. To assume distances between objects to be equal, we require interval measurement, which we discuss next.

The interval scale is characterized by being an ordinal scale but with the added quality that for all distances `i`, `j`, `m(o[i]) > m(o[j]) = c`, where `c` is some amount or constant, implies `t(o[i+1]) > t(o[j]+1) = c`. That is, exact distance between objects is a characteristic of the interval scale. As an example, the distance in temperature on the Fahrenheit scale between 0 and 10 is the same *amount* of temperature change as the temperature change from 10 to 20, at least measurably.

The ratio scale has all the properties of the interval scale but with an important addition. Zero on the ratio scale implies *zero* of the quantity we are working with. That is, `m(o[i]) / m(o[j]) = c` implies `t(o[i]) / t(o[j]) = c`. The only adjustment we need to make to express the scale as ratio distinct from an interval one is to denote the origin as a *true zero point*. On the interval scale, zero is simply an arbitrary point; that is, it is just another point on the scale. That it is zero carries no special meaning. On the ratio scale, however, *zero means zero*.

If a scale is ordinal, then it necessarily possesses characteristics of a nominal scale. If a scale is ratio, it necessarily possesses characteristics of an interval, ordinal, and nominal scale, and so on for all scales in between. For example, temperature on an interval scale simultaneously implies a scale weaker than interval such that a temperature of 10 degrees is more than a temperature of 5, which is characteristic of an ordinal scale. We leave it to the reader to think up more examples. The idea that more primitive scales (e.g., nominal) can be considered special cases of more advanced scales (e.g., ratio) is referred to generally as *collapsing scales*.

## 1.15.6 Likert Scales

Likert scales were invented by [Likert (1932)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000222) and are designed to elicit subjective measurement from individuals on a scale arbitrarily constructed by the researcher. In sciences such as psychology and sociology, measuring attributes of interest can be extremely difficult; some would even 46say impossible in many cases. For example, how can we measure how much you like pizza? We could observe how much you like pizza by following you around to see how much you eat of it every day, but such would be too timely and obtrusive. A more efficient way, if less valid, may simply be to *ask you* how much you like pizza and numerically scale your response. This is an example of a Likert scale.

Likert scales are a useful attempt to measure what we want to measure, but to be precise, they are not actually measuring the question in the scale. Instead, they are measuring the individual’s *response* to the question. For example, when we measure the person’s opinion on the overall quality of healthcare, their actual opinion might differ from the response they are providing. Their *response* and their *actual opinion* are not a one-to-one guarantee. This is the first problem with Likert scale data.

Second, the type of Likert scale can have a great influence on the accuracy of measurement. It is not difficult to come up with examples where participants will either be too *optimistic* or *pessimistic* on a particular item. It is equally easy to come up with examples where a participant may purposely mislead on the particular item. Human beings are not always honest, and if they are, they likely are not aware of their biases. A wealth of psychological research has been done in support of this idea. Asking a participant their level of anxiety is a *subjective representation* of their level of anxiety. *There is no guarantee that it is their actual anxiety.* This is especially true since nobody really knows precisely what anxiety is in the first place! We think it *exists*, but we can’t “point to it” as we can something like height or weight.

Hence, so long as the weaknesses and drawbacks of Likert scale data are recognized, they can be useful, but they need to be used and interpreted with care. Research has also been performed to help determine whether numerical distances on Likert scale data actually correspond to psychological measurement. See Wakita et al. ([2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000377)) for details.

### 1.15.6.1 Statistical Models for Likert Data

How should Likert data be analyzed? A first thing to note regarding any data is that the numbers do not know where they came from nor how they should be treated. *Scales of measurement are ideas we impose on data*. Hence, a data set can be analyzed in any way we wish, though the analysis will only be reasonable if it conforms to properties we impose on or assume of the data.

*Categorical data methods* are usually most appropriate for data at the nominal level since numbers in the nominal scale most often represent categories. Chi-squared analyses and log-linear models are well-suited for these types of data. Data that are at the ordinal level of measurement have more options for analysis. For one, we could simply treat the data as interval or ratio, drawing conclusions quite cautiously. Or, we could adopt methods specifically targeted for ordinal data, which we now briefly discuss.
