
We also usually assume the errors have a mean equal to 0, that is, `E(ε)=0`, and we assume the errors do not covary. We also typically assume that the covariance between the error term and the factors is equal to 0, that is, `E(ε f')=0`. When we consider the confirmatory factor analytic model in the following chapter, we will see how these assumptions are not set in stone and can be relaxed such as to generate a factor model that is much more flexible.

### 12.2.3 Implied Covariance Matrix
The factor analysis model we have described implies the following structure to the covariance matrix,463

`Σ = ΛΛ' + ψ`

(12.2)

That is, the covariance of observed variables is equal to `ΛΛ'` loadings plus a matrix of errors. The above equality is *implied* by defining the factor analysis model as we did earlier such that we can reproduce the covariance matrix `Σ` based on `ΛΛ' + ψ`. Recall that we said errors were uncorrelated, which implies that `ψ` will be an identity matrix, with values of one along the main diagonal and zeroes everywhere else. Given that `ψ` is set this way, this further implies that the covariances in `Σ` must be accounted for by the contents of `Λ`. Contrast this for a moment to PCA in which there is no `ψ` term to “explain” the covariance matrix, which implies that PCA will attempt to not only explain covariances, but also variances ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)). Another way of looking at this is to allow `ψ` to be in the PCA set-up, but set it to zero, that is, `ψ = 0` for the PCA model but `ψ ≠ 0` for the factor analysis model. As we will discuss later, if all error variances are assumed to be equal, that is, `ψ = ψ I` (i.e., *isotropic error*), PCA becomes the aforementioned *probabilistic PCA* of the previous chapter, which, in the sense of the error structure, is more similar to factor analysis than it is to components analysis.

To understand and appreciate further why the above covariance identity holds, see [Denis (2021a)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000087) or [Johnson and Wichern (2007)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173). The important point for our immediate purposes here is to appreciate that the covariance matrix can be “deconstructed” into the above as a result that follows from the very definition of the factor model. The equality in (12.2) implies that if we can estimate the contents of `Λ`, we can “account for” the covariance of observed variables, that is, the factor analytic model attempts to reconstruct the covariance matrix through its estimation of factors.

Is this idea new to factor analysis? Not at all! In virtually all modeling, we are trying to *reconstruct* something in terms of something else, some *translation* or *reorganization* of data or variables. This is what is behind most function statements (i.e., the left side and right side of equality signs in model equations) in statistical models as we are simply trying to account for something that is already observed, which in this case, is the covariance matrix. In factor analysis, we are trying to reproduce the observed covariance matrix based on the specification of the factor analytic model. In the case of linear regression, it was the observed vector of response values that we were trying to reproduce via our model equation. In the case of discriminant analysis, it was the binary or multi-coded response that we were, again, attempting to reproduce. In the case of neural networks, as we will see later, it is the continuous or categorical outcome, and so on with most other kinds of modeling. *Many models, whether in multivariate statistics or machine learning, attempt to reconstruct, in some sense, an outcome*. *How well they reconstruct that outcome is measured by how well the model “fits.” In many applications, the model serves as a “narrative” for how the data were generated just as physicists, for example, try to find a narrative for how the universe began or psychologists a narrative for how neurotic behavior arises. Those narratives are typically (though not necessarily always ideally) expressed via mathematical frameworks.*

## 12.3 The Problem with Factor Analysis
What would a model be if it did not come with some baggage? Perhaps more than any other statistical method, factor analysis comes with it a legacy of criticism and perhaps even more, a legacy of *misunderstanding*. The major criticism of factor analysis is that *factor loadings are not unique*. What this means is that we can change the factor loadings (through a *rotation* of the factor solution, as we will see later), and still reproduce the same covariance matrix. Both original factor loadings and transformed loadings (by an orthogonal matrix) can reproduce the same covariance matrix ([Rencher & Christensen, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295)). Hence, the factor analyst is often left with trying to decide which are the “correct” loadings. The correct loadings will often be determined by which solution 464makes the most *substantive* sense to the researcher, which can make factor analysis seem a bit too subjective for some and historically is often a reason for some of the disdain for factor analysis by critical audiences.

Full details about why this is the case can be found in [Denis (2021a)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000087), but for now we cut right to the chase to the end result. The bottom line is that we can introduce an orthogonal matrix `T` such that the following holds:

$$
\begin{array}{l} {x - \mu = \Lambda f + \varepsilon} \\ {\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, = \Lambda{TT}'f + \varepsilon} \\ \end{array}
$$

(12.3)

That is, by simply including `T T'` into the factor model, we obtain an equivalent representation of `x - μ`. The substantive implication of this is that the loadings we obtain in factor analysis are not *unique* and are subject to an orthogonal matrix. Substantively, such implies that if a researcher does not “like” the factor solution they obtained, they can modify it (via *rotation*) until they find a solution they do like! Had the solution been unique, then the factor analyst would be “stuck” with the loadings produced.

### 12.3.1 The Problem Is the Users, Not the Method
To conclude this thought on the chief difficulty with factor analysis, we should emphasize that first and foremost, there is nothing “wrong” with factor analysis. The lack of uniqueness of its loadings may be a limitation of the technique, but all statistical methods, when considered carefully and in much detail, can be said to have limitations. The key is to *recognize* those limitations, be acutely aware of them, and not conclude from the methodology more than is warranted. This is the real danger of not understanding a statistical method, that one will draw more conclusions from it than are warranted by the model. Hence, the chief problem as it concerns factor analysis is more to do with how it is *applied* and *interpreted*. That is, the problem is usually with the *users* of the method not interpreting factor analysis *ethically*.

The author has time and time again seen the factor analytic model *misapplied*, *misused*, *misinterpreted*, and substantive conclusions drawn from applications of the model that violate the model’s assumptions and are simply not epistemologically supported. But again, this can be said to be true of the use of most statistical methods at times. *Too often researchers believe statistics tell them something about data that it simply cannot*. With factor analysis, especially when applied to social data, the problem of misunderstanding is usually compounded. *It is often more important to understand and appreciate what a statistical model will not tell you than it is to learn what it can tell you about reality.*
