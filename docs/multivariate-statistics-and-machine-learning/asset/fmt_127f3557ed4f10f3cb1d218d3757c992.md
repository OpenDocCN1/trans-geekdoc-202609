
For the above reasons, for most comparisons of effect sizes, it may be best to simply report general *ranges* of effect sizes and focus less on *specific* effect sizes, especially if the material under study is also expected to exhibit much measurement error. For example, if we were working with pristine measures that are reliable and repeatable to multiple decimal places (e.g., speed of an engine or angle of descent of an airplane), then a difference of 0.6 to 0.5 would be more meaningful than with say, a social measure that may be more prone to measurement error and error due simply to the way the data were sampled. Of course, if you suspect that the multiple issues that could affect the size of effect are more or less *constant* across studies, then perhaps you could conclude *0.6 is greater than 0.5 and all the factors which may influence this difference we assume are randomly distributed or more or less equivalent across the studies*, which allows for a more blanket and general statement. However, barring such equivalency, there may be good reasons why one study reported the 0.6 vs. 0.5 even if in reality both effect sizes are in fact, and for all purposes, virtually *equivalent*.

Cohen’s `d`, or any other effect size, is a function of the specific data that went into it, so in that sense it is *unique* to the data set. If a second data set exhibits the same (or very similar) numerical properties as the first data set, then `d` values are much more comparable. Differences in scaling can also mean tremendously different effect sizes to the point where we may be not even sure of what is being compared when we compare `d` values. However, if a first study had an effect size of 3 and another 0.5, one might be, in general, fairly confident that the first study exhibits more of an effect than the second *if both studies were similar in many characteristics, including scaling*. As that difference gets smaller and smaller, the confidence in making such a conclusion decreases. If a researcher reports 0.51 vs. 0.52 in Cohen’s `d` values and concludes, “Therefore, the second study (0.52) found a larger effect,” yes, numerically it did, but each effect size computation is but an estimate of the true underlying effect and is riddled with all the measurement errors and random influences that went into the computation, not the least of which is the way the scale is set for the variables under study. A Likert scale with a range of 1 to 7 for responses is likely to elicit a much different distribution than a Likert scale of 1 to 3, even though in both cases the same variable may be being measured. This issue relates to the *operationalization* of the independent or predictor variables, which we now briefly discuss.

### 2.29.7 Operationalizing Predictors

How one chooses to operationalize the independent or predictor variable(s) can have a significant influence on whether an effect is found in statistical modeling. For example, consider `η²` from ANOVA. Suppose that `η² = 0.80` in one study vs. `η² = 0.70` in another. Does such imply that the first study has a truly larger effect size? Without knowing any more details of each study, it is entirely possible that `η² = 0.80` has resulted more from the way the independent variable was operationalized than anything else. The following principle should be heeded:

> Where each experiment or study “comes from” is just as important as the numerical measure one is choosing to contrast and compare studies because that numerical measure (e.g., Cohen’s `d`, `η²`, etc.) is a function of all those underlying influences.109

The above is key. When one traces the roots of the equation for `d` or `η²`, etc. one gets into the very numbers and characteristics of the data. Effect sizes are not simply *summary statistics*; they are general *reports* of the *data*. Just as comparing the sample mean from one study to another depends on what went into each sample mean, so it is also the case with effect sizes. To emphasize the point, consider the following statement: “The mean grade in one class is a bit higher than another.” What does that tell us? It tells us that *the mean grade in one class is a bit higher than in the other*! To go further into analysis would require us to examine what went into each mean, and so on, which is a much deeper investigation such that in some cases could even reverse the direction of the effect. The takeaway point here is that summary statistics never tell the full story. The deeper story is told in the actual data points, and *the even deeper story is told in how the data were obtained and measured*. “The student’s GPA is 4.0” means nothing unless we know more about what courses were taken, how courses were graded, difficulty of each course, and so on. Data is *experience*, and unless you know the experience intimately, summary statistics can only provide crude approximations.

## 2.30 Central Limit Theorem

There are many situations where theoretical results are facilitated by assuming a normal sampling distribution. For example, when we obtain a sample mean and wish to compare it to the sampling distribution of the mean, we need to assume the sampling distribution takes on a normal (or at minimum, *approximately* normal) shape. If the original population distribution from which we have sampled is known to be normal, then it stands that the sampling distribution will likewise be normal. However, in most cases we do not know the shape of the original population distribution and hence we need a way nonetheless to assure the normality of the sampling distribution. The *central limit theorem*, or CLT, allows us to know the shape of the sampling distribution, whatever the shape of the original parent population.

The *univariate* central limit theorem of the mean holds that if we let `x[1]`, `x[2]`, ..., `x[n]` be independent observations from any population with mean `μ` and finite variance `σ²`, then $\\sqrt{n}\\left( \\overline{x} - \\mu \\right)/\\sigma$ has an approximate normal distribution, `N(μ,σ²)` for large sample sizes. In the limit, as sample size increases, the sampling distribution of the mean takes on an increasingly normal shape.

Likewise, we can state the *multivariate* central limit theorem more formally as if we let `X[1]`, `X[2]`, ..., `X[n]` be independent observations from any population with mean `μ` and finite covariance matrix `Σ`, then $\\sqrt{n}\\left( \\overline{X} - \\mu \\right)$ has an approximate normal distribution, `N[p](μ,Σ)` for large sample sizes ([Johnson & Wichern, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173)). As in the univariate case, if the parent population is normal, the multivariate distribution of the sample will be normal. The extent to which the parent population is normal speeds up the process for normality of the sampling distribution as sample size increases. For a more formal definition and characterization of the central limit theorem, see Casella and Berger ([2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000055), p. 236). Note that in general, the central limit theorem is about the behavior of *random variables*, not specifically the mean `μ` or mean vector `μ`. In this sense, there are many different “versions” of the central limit theorem. Most students come across the CLT in the context of the behavior of the *mean*, but such should not imply the CLT is *uniquely* about the mean.
