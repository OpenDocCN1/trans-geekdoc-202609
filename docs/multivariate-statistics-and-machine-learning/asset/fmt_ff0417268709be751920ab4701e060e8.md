
To be clear, ANOVA, discriminant analysis, and `k-means` clustering are *not* the same procedures and have their own individual respective roles in data science. However, they often seek to minimize a similar objective function and can be, to some extent, likened to one another. Indeed, one way to think about `k-means` clustering, where there is no *a priori* ground truth, is such that we seek to *discover* groups. In classic ANOVA or discriminant analysis, the investigator comes to the analysis already with a theory in mind in the form of either a factor with various levels in ANOVA or a response variable in discriminant analysis featuring a binary or categorical grouping. That is, in ANOVA and discriminant analysis the user advances the hypothesized grouping structure. Is it the *best* grouping structure in some sense? Not necessarily, but it is the one hypothesized by the 510researcher. In cluster analysis, we typically do not yet have a hypothesized grouping structure in mind and seek to “find one” via the algorithm. This is a powerful conceptual way to relate these approaches, even if their technical details will differ substantially.

### 14.8.7 Making `K-Means` Probabilistic via `K-Means++`

We have seen that `k-means` assigns cases that are closest to cluster centers but have noted that initial cluster seeds can have a significant impact and influence on the determination of clusters. Given this impact, we have said that it is usually recommended to try a variety of initial seeds and not simply rely on a single “run” of seeding. But how exactly should this be done? We could simply rerun the `k-means` algorithm manually a few times and settle with that approach to determining initial seeds. While this is not a terrible way of doing things, such an approach is not very efficient and could take a long time to execute.

A much more efficient approach to the above problem is to extend the traditional `k-means` algorithm and adopt a method in which the initial seeds are selected *at random* as in classical `k-means`, but then subsequent points are selected subject to being probabilistically *proportional* to the square distance to the point’s nearest cluster center (Murray, 2022, p. 719). The approach here is to try to, essentially, “cover” the data as much as possible. This approach is called *farthest point clustering*, or `k-means++`.

### 14.8.8 Using the Data: `K-Medoids` Clustering

We have seen that `k-means` clustering assigns cases to the cluster for which it is closest to the cluster centroid. In `k-means`, that “representative” centroid is that of the *arithmetic mean* while in `k-medians` clustering it is the *median*. Note that both of these approaches use a representative that is a *function* of the data and not the data itself. That is, the mean is a summary statistic of the data; it is not an *actual* data point in the data. In this sense, the mean does not “exist” in the data, it is simply an abstract measure we apply to the data set. Even if the mean happens to equal a particular data point, it is still not being drawn from the data itself, and likewise with the median; the median may or may not exist in the data. The bottom line is that whether we are using the mean or median, we are nonetheless using a *function of the data* and not the data itself.

A variant of such approaches is that of `k-medoids`. In `k-medoids` clustering each cluster center is estimated not through computing a *function* of the data, but instead by choosing an *actual* empirical data value from the actual data set that is “optimal” in some sense. How should we choose this value? One strategy is to select it such that *its average dissimilarity to all other points in the data set is a minimum*. The empirical value, or *representative object*, that is, the *medoid*, may also be chosen randomly. The key advantage of `k-medoids`, in addition to being less sensitive to outliers than `k-means`, is that it can be applied to spaces that are non-Euclidean which do not necessarily permit the computation of an average ([Leskovec et al., 2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000218)). In such cases, a representative can be calculated called a *clustroid*, of which there are many ways of choosing the relevant clustroid for a given problem (see Leskovec et al., [2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000218) for details).

The algorithm typically used in `k-medoids` is called the *partitioning around medoids* (or *PAM*) algorithm. Essentially, the algorithm exchanges medoids for non-medoids in an iterative fashion recomputing the cost each time to learn whether the exchange was advantageous in minimizing the cost. If the exchange was worthwhile, it is kept. An allied method to PAM is the *Voronoi iteration*. See [Murphy (2022)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259) for details.511
