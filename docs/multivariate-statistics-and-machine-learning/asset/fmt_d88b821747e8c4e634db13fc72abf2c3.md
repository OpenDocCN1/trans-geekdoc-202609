
We can demonstrate simple logical arguments in software. In `R`, for instance, logical arguments can be carried out quite easily. For example, we can ask `R` to evaluate certain expressions as true or false:

```
> 1==5
[1] FALSE
```

where in the above, `==` is called a *relational operator* ([Davies, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000077)). Another example is the following:

```
> 5 == 4+2
[1] FALSE
```

Since logic contains “1” and “0” values corresponding to true and false, we can treat logical values as numbers as in the following where we add up “true” values:

```
> T + T + T
[1] 3
```

### 1.5.1 Consistency and Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems

Euclid’s *Elements* is regarded as one of the most famous of all mathematical texts ever written, and more specifically in geometry. The fifth postulate of the text was for a couple thousand years assumed to be an axiom, but in the 1800s it was shown that Euclidean geometry was not the *only* geometry possible. One could construct an edifice of *non-Euclidean geometry*. This event helped to introduce a crisis into mathematics.

Up to late 19th century (1800s), much of mathematics was thought to be based on logic, and given this, one could reasonably expect that the entire edifice of mathematics would be essentially a *closed system* that is entirely logically consistent. Consistency, in this regard, refers to the idea that if a statement is true, it cannot simultaneously be false. More succinctly, a consistent system is one that is free from *contradictions*. For instance, if `p→q`, a contradiction here, for example, 28might conclude `q` and `¬q` (i.e., not `q`) *simultaneously*. A consistent system does not contain such contradictions.

After the invention of the calculus in the 1600s, it was discovered that calculus was not based on a theoretically solid foundation. A group of mathematicians attempted to rigorize calculus and were largely successful. The new discipline became known as *real analysis*, or simply *analysis*, and purported to be a rigorized and deeper version of calculus. For example, the notion of continuity at the time of Newton and Leibnitz was quite informal. Real analysis rigorized continuity and gave it a very precise definition. There would be no more informal talk regarding what was meant by notions such as continuity. Mathematicians delivered a rigorous definition for these and other key mathematical ideas. Mathematics prided itself on rigor, precision, and well-defined statements and definitions.

While the attempt to put mathematics on a rigorous logical foundation was relatively successful, the idea that it could be *entirely* put on such a coherent and consistent foundation was challenged by Gödel. Gödel showed that there are some statements in mathematics that could not be proved from other statements *within the system*. The theorems advanced by Gödel in showing this were known as his *incompleteness theorems*. The second of his theorems essentially held that it is impossible to program a computer to answer all mathematical questions. As Tantom ([2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000352), p. 229) notes, “Gödel’s work dashed centuries of hope of finding a small set of basic axioms on which to base all of mathematics.”

The above surprise was just the beginning of reconceiving mathematics not as a bullet-proof edifice of irreversible truths, but rather as a *construction* which like many other things requires something to initiate the construction. Not everything within the constructed system can be proved or justified. This was exactly what was found by Gödel who propositioned that mathematics, more precisely *Peano’s axiomatization of arithmetic*, contains within it statements that cannot be proved with reference to the system. That is, it must require something *external* to the system for the rest of the system to be proved. In other words, any first-order theory containing Peano’s axiomatization “is either self-contradictory or incomplete for the purposes of deriving *certain* \[emphasis added\] arithmetic statements” ([Muggleton, 1991](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000256)). As Kline ([1972](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000191), p. 1026) remarked:

> The rigorization of mathematics may have filled a nineteenth-century need, but it also teaches us something about the development of the subject. The newly founded logical structure presumably guaranteed the soundness of mathematics; but the guarantee was somewhat of a sham. Not a theorem of arithmetic, algebra, or Euclidean geometry was changed as a consequence, and the theorems of analysis had only to be more carefully formulated. In fact, all that the new axiomatic structures and rigor did was substantiate what mathematicians knew had to be the case. Indeed, the axioms had to yield the existing theorems rather than determine them. All of which means that mathematics rests not on logic but on sound intuitions.

Kline hits the nail on the head. *Dead center*. While analysis provided a rigorous “foundation” for calculus, it was essentially yet still a *human construction* for ideas already assumed to be true. Again, the classic case here is that of continuity. Continuity was assumed to already exist; all that mathematicians did was provide a more rigorous description or account of it. The contributions were extraordinary in nature, no doubt, but many of such contributions simply provided justification for concepts already assumed to exist without justification. The assumptions (and intuition, as Kline put it) still came *first*.29

#### 1.5.1.1 What Is the Relevance of Gödel?

The reader may reasonably ask why any of this Gödel talk is relevant to this book. In truth, it isn’t, other than to augment awareness of the mathematical system on which statistics and machine learning are based. In all of the statistical and machine learning principles and methods we will survey, it should be recognized that these are essentially *human constructions*; they are not written in stone and not *entirely* deductions from a smaller set of fundamental truths. Too often students believe that mathematics is rigid, dry, and outside of the realm of creativity. Nothing could be further from the truth. Mathematics, and by extension statistics and machine learning, are *creative enterprises*. Rigorous ones, but creative just the same. Rigor does not imply the absence of creativity; it simply requires stronger justification for new ideas and principles born out of such creativity. The rules are much “stiffer” than creativity in music or art. Sure, in jazz there are “rules,” but in the end, you can play what sounds “good.” When reflected on even more deeply, it may be realized that *we make up the rules in all of these*, including in mathematics, statistics, and machine learning, yet more accountability for such rules exists in these latter three since most rules are a consequence of *logic*.
