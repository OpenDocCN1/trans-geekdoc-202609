
## 1.9 Base Rate Thinking
Suppose you perform an elective ultrasound on an 11-year-old male German Shepherd. The ultrasound reveals a splenic mass. As a dog owner, you are of course concerned that the mass could rupture but have no idea how long the mass has actually been there. The mass, of course, is an abnormality on the spleen, meaning that the dog was unlikely to have been born with the mass. It appeared at some point during the dog’s life, though at what point, you do not know. You lack an ultrasound *baseline* with which to compare the current data. It is an abnormality on the spleen, but is it an abnormality for an 11-year-old German Shepherd? Likely not. Consider data reported recently on the prevalence of splenic masses on a random sample of Golden Retrievers aged 6 through 13 that found that a whopping 52.8% of Golden Retrievers in this sample were found to have splenic masses! Further, since it is well known that splenic masses are much more common in elderly German Shepherds than young ones, the percentage of elderly dogs (i.e., a *conditional distribution*) carrying splenic masses is probably closer to 70–80%. Hence, the detection of a splenic mass on the ultrasound of the 11-year-old is most likely an *expected* finding, not an *anomaly*. The point here is that there are likely many dogs that carry splenic masses, many of which are likely benign and for which those masses never pose a problem or the dogs die of other causes. Indeed, the next 11-year-old (or older) German Shepherd that you observe on a walk down the street with their owner *likely has a splenic mass*. That a mass was found on the ultrasound does not make it an anomaly; *the detection of the mass and doing an ultrasound on the dog may be the actual anomaly*. This is what we mean by *base rates.
