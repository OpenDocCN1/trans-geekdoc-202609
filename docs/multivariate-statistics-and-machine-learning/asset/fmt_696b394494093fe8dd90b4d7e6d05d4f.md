
2. *The first* *principal component, as mentioned, is the one with the largest variance*. However, this does not necessarily imply that it is the component of *most interest*. There is no guarantee that the first principal component will be more theoretically interesting than the second, or the third, etc. It is the “best” component only in the sense of maximizing variance. Other components may be preferred based on their correlation with other variables or how they perform in ensuing regression analysis for instance, or even how they cluster in a cluster analysis ([Marden, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000239)). Variance explained is only one criterion that should be used in addressing the “importance” or “relevance” of a component.

3. *Principal components will under most circumstances generate as many components as there are original variables*. PCA, under most circumstances, will necessarily generate components. However, there is absolutely no requirement that the components obtained are meaningful or useful. The fact that you ran a PCA on variables and obtained components is not a “finding.” It is a mathematical *certainty* that existed long before you performed the components analysis. Whether you believe the transformation is in some sense “interesting” will likely depend on your substantive science and even philosophy of mathematics. That is, if the components look “meaningless” now substantively, does the mathematical transformation still imply they are nonetheless “real?” This isn’t a statistical question, it is one of the *philosophy of mathematics*. If you believe mathematics corresponds to “reality,” then regardless of whether you can “make sense” of the components or not you may still believe them to nonetheless “exist.” This is an exceptionally interesting topic, but far beyond the scope of this book.

4. *Related to #3, components are only as good or interesting as the input variables subjected to the components analysis*. Hence, the first and primary concern of interest is subjecting PCA to a set of variables that is inclusive of all the variables you wish to analyze. If the data set is not interesting or you leave out important variables from the ensemble of variables, conducting a PCA on such data will likely be limiting, or your components analysis will be misspecified.410

5. *It is possible, even if unlikely, that a PCA generates eigenvalues that are equal or near equal in size*. Further, population eigenvalues are usually not exactly equal to eigenvalues generated by the sample. Inferential tests for eigenvalues exist (e.g., [Camargo, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000052)), but are rarely used. Tests have been developed and usually require the normality assumption ([Collins & Chatfield, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057); Peres-Neto et al., [2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000285)).

6. Components derived in PCA depend more on the *ratios* of correlations in a correlation matrix than the correlations themselves. As noted by Collins and Chatfield ([1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 67), dividing all off-diagonal correlations by a constant greater than 1 reveals that the eigenvalues change but that the eigenvectors, which implies the components as well, do not.

7. PCA does not assess the dimensionality of the variables; it assesses the *linear dimensionality* of the variables. If the data are nonlinear, which amounts to the underlying manifold being nonlinear, then classical PCA will fail to capture this nonlinearity. As discussed later, techniques such as *kernel PCA* will be more effective at uncovering a nonlinear underlying manifold.

8. Related to the previous point (#7), PCA is, in its most general sense, a type of *manifold learning* where the manifold is assumed to be linear. Nonlinear techniques may be more effective at uncovering the true manifold. *A failure to uncover meaningful components using PCA should never be taken as conclusive evidence that deeper dimensionality is nonexistent underlying the variables*. Such interesting dimensionality may indeed exist, but it may be *nonlinear* in form, a structure that PCA will be limited in uncovering.

## 11.3 Independent Components Analysis

In the classical PCA just discussed, we required components to be linear functions and orthogonal to one another. We sought to reduce the rank of the matrix of observed variables into one of smaller rank, but that accounted for most of the variance in the original variables, preferably in the first few components. The fact that the components were orthogonal to one another was a key feature of principal components analysis.

In *independent component analysis* (ICA), we require components (i.e., the *latent variables*) to be mutually independent and typically non-Gaussian ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). That is, the goal in ICA is to find a linear representation of non-Gaussian data so that the components are statistically independent, and if not perfectly so, then *as independent as possible*. *This is a different requirement than simply requiring orthogonality*. As noted by [Jolliffe (2002)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000174), the links between classical PCA and independent components analysis are, perhaps surprisingly, not that strong, and ICA is closer in relation to factor analysis than it is to components analysis.

The ICA model ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)) can be stated as follows:

`X = f(S) + e`

(11.7)

where `X` is a vector of random observed “manifest” variables, `S` is an unobservable random vector variate of sources whose components are independent latent variables, each having zero mean, and `e` is a random vector representing noise, for instance, in the measurements taken on `X`. The key point is that the independence of the sources implies that each individual source signal is hypothesized to be generated by a process unrelated to any other source signal ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). Without `e`, the model is referred to as *noiseless ICA*. When `e` is included, we refer to the technique as *noisy ICA*.411

In ICA, projected points `P` are obtained as a product of the original data `D` and an unmixing matrix `M^(u)`, where the mixing matrix is equal to `M`. Hence, `P = D M^(u)`. ICA essentially looks for rotations of data that have (approximately) independent coordinates. This will necessarily be true for all rotations from samples from multivariate normal distributions ([Venables & Ripley, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000376), p. 313), and hence ICA will not be of most interest for such a “trivial” situation. Instead, ICA is most valuable and useful for data arising from distributions that are not normal, that is, *non-Gaussian*.

### 11.3.1 Principal Components vs. Independent Components Analysis

How does independent component analysis relate to PCA? It stands that there should be some relation between them, and indeed there is. PCA can be considered *a special case of ICA*, where PCA yields orthogonal components while ICA generates independent components.

Jolliffe ([2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000174), p. 396) best summarizes the difference between PCA and ICA:

> PCA has as its main objective the successive maximization of variance, and the orthogonality and uncorrelatedness constraints are extras, which are included to ensure that the different components are measuring separate things. By contrast, independent components analysis (ICA) takes the “separation” of components as its main aim. ICA starts from the view that uncorrelatedness is rather limited as it only considers a lack of linear relationship, and that ideally components should be statistically independent. This is a stronger requirement than uncorrelatedness, with *the two only equivalent for normal (Gaussian) random variables* \[emphasis added\]. … This is much closer to the set-up for factor analysis, and it is this “model” that ICA generalizes.
