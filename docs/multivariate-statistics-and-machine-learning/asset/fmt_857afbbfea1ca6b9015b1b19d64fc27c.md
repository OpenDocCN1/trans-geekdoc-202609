### 4.1.1 Mean Squares
The analysis of variance gets its name because it analyzes `variances`, and not simply sums of squares. The sums of squares we have surveyed are not variances. Recall that, as was the case for the simple sample variance, to generate a variance we need to divide by degrees of freedom. Dividing each source of variation, that of `between` and `within`, by corresponding degrees of freedom will produce the aforementioned `mean squares`, which is simply another name for variances. The degrees of freedom for between are equal to `J - 1`; that is, one less than there are number of groups on the independent variable. The degrees of freedom for within are equal to `N - J`, the total number of observations minus the number of groups.
Dividing sums of squares by degrees of freedom generates the `mean squares` for between (`MS between`) and within (`MS within`), of which we can then make a comparison of how large `MS between` is relative to `MS within`. This comparison via a ratio is the aforementioned `F-ratio`. Recall that we said the expectation of `F` under the null hypothesis is equal to approximately 1. If there are treatment effects, then we would expect `MS between` to be larger than `MS within`, and hence `F > 1`. However, small deviations from 1.0 can be easily attributed to chance or random fluctuation, and hence we will conduct a statistical significance test on `F` to determine the probability of observing an `F` as we have obtained under the hypothetical (and somewhat unrealistic) situation that the null hypothesis was “true.” If the probability of the `F` we have observed is less than some conventional level such as 0.05, then we reject the null hypothesis and infer the statistical alternative (not necessarily the substantive alternative; we will discuss the difference later) that somewhere among the population means there is at least one pairwise difference.189

### 4.1.2 Expected Mean Squares of ANOVA
In our overview of how ANOVA works, though we walked through the logic of the summary table, we did not in any way defend or justify that the `F-ratio` is actually estimating quantities of interest to us. In other words, we did not justify that computing the `F-ratio` as we have done actually makes good sense, even if it did seem intuitive arithmetically to compute it that way. `MS between` and `MS within` are simply computations made on sample data, but how do we know what they are actually estimating in the population?
Recall that when we computed a simple sample variance on our data, we justified its computation by taking its expectation, and found that with `n - 1` in the denominator it produced an unbiased estimate of the population variance,  σ 2 . We need to ask an analogous question of the variances we have generated from ANOVA. That is, what is the expectation of `MS between` and `MS within`? Only then will we know that we are in fact estimating quantities that justify the ensuing `F-ratio`.
For full details on taking expectations, see [Denis (2021a)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000087). Due to space limitations, we here only summarize the end result. In a `fixed-effects model`, the expectation of `MS between` turns out to be
$$E\left( \text{MS\ Between)} = \frac{\sum\limits_{j}\left( {n_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}} \right)}{J - 1} + \sigma_{e}^{2} \right.$$
(4.3)
and for within, `E(MS Within)=sigma_e^2`. If there are no treatment effects, then `alpha_j^2 = 0` and hence
$$\begin{array}{l} {E\left( \text{MS\ Between)\ =}\frac{\sum\limits_{j}\left( {n_{j}\left( 0 \right)_{j}^{2}} \right)}{J - 1} + \sigma_{e}^{2} \right.} \\ {\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, = \sigma_{e}^{2}} \\ \end{array}$$
and so an `F-ratio` computed on these variances would produce a ratio approximately equal to 1.0, since `sigma_e^2/sigma_e^2 = 1`. The extent to which `alpha_j^2 > 0` is the extent to which treatment effects exist and thus we would expect $\sum\limits_{j}\left( {n_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}} \right)$ to be greater than 0, resulting in `E(MS Between)` being greater than simply `sigma_e^2`. Hence, we would expect under these conditions an `F` statistic greater than 1.0 such that if large enough when evaluated as `F` on `J - 1` and `N - J` degrees of freedom, we would deem `statistically significant` and reject the null hypothesis.
We mentioned that this was for a `fixed-effects model`, for which the reader may already be familiar. In a fixed-effects model, we assume that in theoretical repetitions of the experiment, the same levels of the independent variable would be used. For instance, a fixed-effects ANOVA with drug levels 0, 1, and 2 mg implies that these specific levels are of interest to the researcher and would be used again if we performed the experiment repeatedly. This is in contrast to a `random-effects model` in which each experiment would feature a random sampling of levels from a theoretical probability distribution of potential levels that could have been selected based on some probability model (usually random sampling). Understanding fixed-effects models benefits greatly from understanding random-effects models, which we survey shortly.
To reiterate, the purpose of the above is simply to assert that the computation of the `F-ratio` for the fixed-effects analysis of variance by taking `MS between` to `MS within` is justified by taking expected values. In practice, the user of ANOVA is not computing expected mean squares each time she conducts the procedure. Rather, she is simply computing `F` (or interpreting output from software) and making a decision on `H[0]`. The purpose of the above expected mean squares 190is simply to provide theoretical evidence that the `F-ratio` is theoretically justified by expected mean squares theory. It may seem at first trivial to require such justification in the classic one-way ANOVA. However, more complicated models in random-effects and mixed-models theory reveal that `MS within` (or “MS error”) is not always the most suitable denominator for evaluating null hypotheses for various terms in the ANOVA. We will survey these models a bit later in the chapter.
