
A mixture density of American adults (males vs. females) is a classic example of a mixture density where each distribution contributes to the mix. Essentially, and most generally, a mixture density is a type of combination, or crudely, an *average* of other normal or nonnormal densities. In traditional non-mixture maximum-likelihood SEM, multivariate normality is typically assumed for the indicators `x`, `y` of the model, where each of `x`, `y` are vectors corresponding to a number of observed or “manifest” indicator variables. That is,
`f(x,y) = N(μ,∑)`
where `μ` and `∑` are the implied mean and covariance structures. The mixture model can be considered as a simple extension of the above,
$$f\\left( {x,y} \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{g = 1}^{G}w\_{g}}N\\left( \\mu\_{g},\\sum\_{g} \\right)$$
(13.17)
where `g` = 1 through `G` are now the number of mixture components, `w[g]` are mixture probabilities and where each component is distributed multivariate normal, `N(μ[g],∑[g])`. The sum of `w[g]` must equal one to ensure it is a proper density. Each of the `g` = 1 through `G` mixture components will then have their own individual SEM model ([Kelava et al., 2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000184), p. 471),
`η[g] = B[g]η[g] + α[g] + Γ[g]ξ[g] + ζ[g]`
where we now note the subscript `g` to denote the given model under consideration (e.g., `g` = 1 would be the first model, and so on). Most often, at least some of the parameters across the mixture models are constrained to be equal so that identification of the model is possible (Kelava et al., [2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000184)). However, the model is still yet linear in the parameters. To make it a *semiparametric nonlinear model*, which of course is our ultimate goal here, the model becomes
`η[g] = B[g]η[g] + α[g] + Γ[1g]ξ[g] + Γ[2g]h(ξ[g]) + ζ[g]`
(13.18)
where the nonlinear terms are included in `Γ[2g]h(ξ[g])` and for generality, their exact nature is not specified specifically in this formulation. That is, `h(ξ[g])` could include product terms, quadratic terms, and so on, which implies the model has become nonlinear in `h(ξ[g])`. For further details on this model, see [Kelava et al. (2014)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000184), which is an exceptionally clear and well-written summary of mixture models and nonlinearity in SEM.
Other mixture models are seen operating where finite mixtures of linear SEMs are used to approximate unknown nonlinear relationships of the latent variables ([Umbach et al., 2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000367)). Since the semiparametric approach does not *a priori* designate the *type* of functional relationship between latent variables, curvilinear relationships can be modeled between latent variables ([Umbach et al., 2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000367)). Song et al. ([2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000343)) propose nonparametric estimation of multiview latent variable models as special cases of Bayesian networks. In `R`, the aforementioned package `nlsem` can also be used to fit semiparametric SEM models. Once more, consult Umbach et al. ([2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000367)) for details.
SEM models can also accommodate longitudinal data. Such models usually go by the name of *latent curve modeling*, *latent growth curves*, and *latent trajectory modeling*, among others. See [Bollen and Curran (2006)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000039) for details. [Liu (2023)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000227) provides a comprehensive review of longitudinal 494models in SEM as well as `R` packages for fitting them. [Liao et al. (2020)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000220) propose a *generalized structural equation model* that incorporates neural networks in their estimation. [Sol and Baras (2022)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000342) combine a neural network approach with structural equation modeling in modeling activity trackers.

## 13.8 Caveats Regarding SEM Models
In concluding our discussion of structural equation models, we feel it relevant to clarify a few items of confusion that often arise for those trying to understand these models for the first time.
The first is that SEM models are often marketed as accounting for *measurement error*, presumably because they include unique *error terms* for variables as well as more than a single indicator of a construct, such as is typically the case in simpler models. Including error terms and more indicators than not usually lowers the error with which a construct is measured (or *indicated*). However, if one uses poor indicators, minimizing measurement error is not the chief concern; *the priority instead will be to choose better indicators*. That is, accounting for and minimizing measurement error is only as relevant as the quality of indicators used in reducing said measurement error. For example, measuring heart rate with a stethoscope has relatively little measurement error. Including a second indicator of heart rate or level of cholesterol would not reduce measurement error, but would instead simply make for a relatively poorly defined or indicated latent variable. That is, measurement error can be reduced in SEM but only if indicators are chosen carefully and wisely. *Structural equation models do not unconditionally reduce measurement error. They simply account for it better than many classical statistical models.*
A second caveat is regarding the aforementioned issue of causality. *SEM models have nothing, by themselves, to do with causality*! If the *context* of the model is reasonable, then one *may* be able to infer causality, but by themselves, SEM models are not any more causal than any other statistical or mathematical model or abstract mathematical system. The fact that SEM models often go by the name of *causal models* is not due to their ability to impart or impose causality via their structural relations or parameter estimates. They are named causal models largely for historical reasons and also because researchers often use them on data networks they *believe*, *a priori*, represent *causal processes*. However, the causality assumption arises from the nature of the *science*, not the *statistical method*. For Wright’s pioneering work on path analysis, assuming a causal process in the generational transmission of color in the guinea pig made good sense because it had a scientific biological “organic” basis. *Causality is primarily a design and scientific issue, not a statistical one*.
A third caveat is regarding *context*. As we have discussed throughout this book, all models are *context-dependent*, which means that any relations we observe in a model depend on other variables included or not included in the model. Since perfect specification of a model is rarely if ever possible, virtually all SEM models constructed will be incomplete and hence all parameter estimates will be to some extent, *biased*. All modeling (not just SEM) is but an *approximation* to reality. It could very well be that including an extra few variables in the model results in a vastly different model, so it is essential that when planning an SEM model, or any other model for that matter, the researcher ensures that all instrumental variables are included in the model as to minimize the extent to which it is misspecified.495
