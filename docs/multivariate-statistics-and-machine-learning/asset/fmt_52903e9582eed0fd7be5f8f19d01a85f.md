
`Σ = ΛΛ′ + ψ`

(12.15)

This is why PCA is sometimes cast as the limiting case of a linear-Gaussian model, equivalent to the classical factor analysis model as the error or “noise” becomes extremely small and in the limit goes to zero.

So, what is the difference between probabilistic PCA and factor analysis then? It would seem they are the same since they both contain error terms. They are slightly different, however. The key difference lies in the restrictions placed on the error term. In the factor analysis model, recall that `ψ` was constrained to have `σ²I` along its main diagonal. That is, `σ²I` translates to values of noise `ε` along the main diagonal of `R`. However, `σ²` *did not need to be constant (i.e., the same number) along the main diagonal*. In probabilistic PCA, however, `ψ` is constrained to have `σ²I` along its main diagonal and it is required for `ψ` to be *isotropic* or *spherical*, which means it must be *proportional* to the identity matrix ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032), p. 584). Since the identity matrix has values of one along the main diagonal, this implies that `ψ` *must have errors that are equal to one another*. This is why probabilistic PCA is considered a special case of factor analysis, where factor analysis allows, as Lawrence ([2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000211), p. 1784) puts it, a “richer error term” which simply means in this context that the error term need not be *constant*. That is, the covariance matrix need not be spherical in classic factor analysis. In probabilistic PCA, it is required to be so.

## 12.10 Mixtures of Factor Analysis: Modeling Local Linearity

Instead of assuming a linear mapping to unobservable latent variables, as is true of other methods, we can relax this assumption and model things more *locally*. That is, we can assume that locally the model is linear, and then combine all these local model segments to approximate a curved 476manifold. Of course, this concept is not new, we have come across this idea of *local approximation* many times throughout this book (such as in locally linear embedding). In the current context, when one sums together local linear approximations, one gets a better picture of the overall curved manifold, somewhat analogous in concept to the idea of fitting many tangent lines to get the “fit” of a curve in calculus. In essence, a mixture of factor analyzers devotes a factor analysis to each of individual groups or clusters. It is perhaps best described by Ghahramani and Hinton ([1997](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000128), p. 1):

> we present an EM learning algorithm for a method which combines one of the basic forms of dimensionality reduction – factor analysis – with a basic method for clustering – the Gaussian mixture model. What results is a statistical method which concurrently performs clustering and, within each cluster, local dimensionality reduction.

First, we recall the factor analysis model, omitting the mean, `μ`,

`x = Λf + ε`

For the mixture model, where now `f = z`, the generative model is the following,

$$P\\left( x \\right)\\left. = {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{m}{\\int{P\\left( x \\right.}}} \\middle| z,w\_{j} \\right)P\\left( z \\middle| w\_{j} \\right)P\\left( w\_{j} \\right)dz$$

(12.16)

and where `w[j]` are the different mixing distributions such that

`p(z|w[j]) = p(z) = N(0,I)`

which reads that the conditional probability of `z` given `w[j]` is equal to the unconditional probability of `z`, of which both are normally distributed. Following Ghahramani and Hinton ([1997](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000128), p. 4), each factor for each mixing distribution will also have its own mean,

`p(x|z,w[j]) = N(μ[j]+Λ[j]z,ψ)`

(12.17)

which is unlike in traditional factor analysis, where the mean was constant across all factors (i.e., `x = μ + Λf + ε`).

The mixture of factor analyzers is estimated using the EM algorithm. For details, see [Ghahramani and Hinton (1997)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000128). If the error structure happened to follow that of the probabilistic PCA model just surveyed, then the model becomes a *mixture of probabilistic PCA models*. Indeed, probabilistic PCA is a special case of the above model when, as we saw, the error matrix is spherical ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). The covariance matrix for the mixture model will have `mkp + p` parameters whereas the classic nonmixture factor analysis had `mp(p + 1)/2`, where `m` is the number of factor analysis models to use in the mixture model, `k` is the number of factors to extract in each of those factor analyses, and `p` is the number of variables in `x`.

## 12.11 Item Factor Analysis

Item factor analysis, or IFA, is the factor analysis model adapted to analyzing multivariate *categorical* data, which proves especially useful for modeling item-level response data on surveys and questionnaires. These data are often *binary* or *polytomous* or otherwise *categorical* in nature and thus the classic maximum-likelihood factor analysis model ,which assumes continuous variables 477can cause issues ([Everitt & Dunn, 2001](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000096)). Indeed, performing such a traditional factor analysis on data that is not continuous can result in very disparate findings when compared to performing it with an alternative estimation method. See Everitt and Dunn ([2001](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000096), p. 286) for a comparison of output.

[Muthén and Kaplan (1985)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000260) compared estimators for factor analysis of non-normal skewed variables, which included binary and categorical variables, and which included *maximum likelihood*, *generalized least-squares* (GLS), and *asymptotically distribution-free* (ADF) *generalized least-squares*, as well as a categorical variable estimator. They found that ML and GLS standard errors were typically biased downward, but that for ADF no bias was found and the CVM (*categorical variable estimator*) worked well for skewed variables that had been dichotomized. For further details, see [Muthén and Kaplan (1985)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000260).

[Urban and Bauer (2021)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000369) proposed a deep artificial neural network for exploratory item factor analysis, who also showed that other factor analytic models such as nonlinear factor analysis may be subsumed under their novel algorithm. Software-wise, `Mplus` ([Muthén & Muthén, 2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000261)) has especially good capabilities for fitting factor analysis models featuring binary or categorical indicators. Item factor analysis can also be fit using the `OpenMx` package in `R` (Boker et al., [2024](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000035)).

## 12.12 Sparse Factor Analysis

Recall that a *sparse* model is one in which there is a purposeful effort to simplify the model by driving coefficients *to* or *toward* values of `0` in the same spirit as estimators such as ridge and lasso regression surveyed earlier in the book. In one sense, we have already surveyed a sparse-like factor analysis model when we introduced the *varimax rotation*, whose objective, recall, was to drive large coefficients to be larger and small coefficients to be smaller as to approximate *simple structure* for each factor. The sparse factor analysis likewise aims to increase the sparsity of the factor model by introducing penalization or regularization methods that drive coefficients to or toward `0`, as to simplify and clarify the factor structure.
