## 6.7 Additional Variable Selection Algorithms

Other selection algorithms similar in spirit to the ridge and lasso include the *Dantzig selector* as well as the *Garotte* ([Breiman, 1995](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000046)). But why use different penalties in the first place? That is, why not use the same selection algorithm in all situations regardless of the data? The answer to this question once more lies in the *No-Free-Lunch Theorem*, discussed earlier in the book, which, recall, holds that *no universal algorithm universally dominates all data sets*. That is, one data set may have specific “peculiarities” for which one algorithm capitalizes, while another data set may not ([James et al., 2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000169)). Hence, different regularization techniques may generate slightly different results depending on the peculiarities of the given data set and it is virtually impossible to be certain that one algorithm or method will dominate others on any particular data set. Just as no single treatment is guaranteed to “work” on any given individual, no single statistical or machine learning method is guaranteed to dominate all others on any single data set.289

Still, the above admonition does not mean we cannot narrow things down to at least a “preferred” approach in “most” circumstances. According to Izenman ([2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167), p. 154), when comparing penalized regression approaches:

> Extensive simulations comparing prediction accuracy under a wide variety of conditions and models … show that the Lasso, which tends to choose too many variables, almost always contains the correct model; the nonnegative garotte tends to select smaller models than the Lasso, and all-possible subsets, while more likely to choose the correct model than the Lasso, selects models that contain the correct model far less often than the Lasso.

# 7 Nonlinear and Nonparametric Regression

DOI: [10.4324/9781003376941-10](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003376941-10)

The regression methods surveyed thus far in this book have all featured one common assumption: that of the relationship between response variables and explanatory variables being modeled is *linearly related*. Why is linearity the focus of so many statistical methods? Huge books, some well over a thousand pages, are written primarily on linear methods alone. Why the obsession with linearity? We see two primary reasons for this focus on linearity:

1. *Many phenomena in the sciences can be well approximated by linear methods*. The true function underlying the data may not be (and in fact, is *usually* not) linear, but linear models allow us to *approximate* those unknown functions rather well. In short, linearity “works” in many sciences because, though specifying the exact functional form underlying data is difficult to impossible, linearity is a useful *assumption* to make and makes for a nice *approximation* of scientific reality. The key words here are *assumption* and *approximation*. Very few data, if any, actually fall on a straight line.

2. A second reason why linearity is popular is because it is *simple* and *convenient*. In the absence of knowing the true functional form, linearity provides reasonable prediction and generality analogous in some sense to why we use means and averages to describe many phenomena, even though such can be grossly misleading at times. Even in its sister discipline, *linear algebra*, linearity is, not surprisingly given its name, assumed in most applications. The convenience of fitting simple, functional forms comes at a price, however. Rarely, if ever, do those functional forms represent empirical *reality*. Reality is usually much more complex, or at least in many cases, we *assume* it is more complex. However, assuming a linear relationship generally guards against overfitting and hence can be more generalizable than if we try too much to model the exact form of the relationship that may underlie the data.
