### 7.1.3 Polynomial Regression as a Global Strategy

What is the problem with polynomial regression? There is no problem, really, if one wishes to model the entire range of `x[i]` by assuming a common functional form. That is, both the classic linear model and polynomial regression fit the data in a very *global* manner, similar in some sense to how the arithmetic mean summarizes a set of data from one end of the distribution to the other. For example, consider the small data set

2, 5, 8, 10, 15, 16, 170, 171, 172

The mean of this data is 63.2. But how well does the mean represent the data? Not that well, since, for example, the data points 170, 171, and 172 are very much greater than the rest of the data and the mean will thus be pulled *upward* because of these more extreme scores:

> 2, 5, 8, 10, 15, 16, **170, 171, 172**

Computing the mean on the above set of data provides a very “global” summary of the data. We may not wish to model the data in this way, whether it is in computing the mean or conducting a regression, and may prefer a more *local* approach.

### 7.1.4 A More Local Alternative

For the above data, we may be better off calculating two averages, one for the data 2, 5, 8, 10, 15, 16, and one for the data 170, 171, 172. In doing so, we are essentially modeling the data at a more *local* level. Though this example is very simple, it demonstrates a strong but obvious point, and that is, *fitting global measures to data provides a global summary of that data*. If the data does not conform to a global representation, then fitting *subsets* of the data more locally may be preferable. Indeed, at its extreme, the most local we could go is to model each data point *individually*, but such would be foolish and detract from the purpose of modeling in the first place, except for extremely small data sets. Exact “curve-fitting” is essentially “connecting the dots,” which, for most purposes, defeats the goal of statistical modeling. In this case, we are not averaging anything; we are simply connecting observations on a descriptive level. The general goal of modeling is to estimate a general function over the data, even if local. The goal is not simply “connecting the dots” on the training data and achieving perfect fit. *Anything less than “connecting the dots,” in most cases, can be considered a very general universal form of the aforementioned and discussed regularization concept as it concerns generalizability and minimizing test error.*

### 7.1.5 Least-Squares Regression Line as a “Floating Mean”: Toward a Localized Approach

To further motivate localized regression approaches, recall that the classic least-squares regression line can be interpreted as a “floating mean” across the range of values of the predictor, `x[i]`. That is, the slope `β[1]` in `y[i] = β[0] + β[1]x[i] + ε[i]`, analogous to the mean, is a kind of “summary statistic” because the model assumes it is constant across the range of `x[i]`. Even if the data suggested a different slope or functional form within a given range of `x[i]`, the classic least-squares line would not account for this. Likewise for polynomial regression, in which the polynomial is fit across the range of `x[i]`, essentially ignoring more “local behavior” within intervals of `x[i]`. Both linear and polynomial regression *generalize* over `x[i]` to provide a summary of the data in terms of model fit.

What if we wished, however, to consider *local variation* more carefully? Perhaps the easiest example is that of a scatterplot that you may have come across in introductory statistics, one featuring the presence of two “groups” within the plot, as in [Figure 7.2](#c7.xhtml#f7.2).

![Figure 7.2 Basic scatterplot on two groups (males vs. females) suggesting a more localized approach to modeling, possibly by fitting an individual slope to each group.Return to text.⏎](images/fig7.2.jpg)

*Figure 7.2* Basic scatterplot on two groups (males vs. females) suggesting a more localized approach to modeling, possibly by fitting an individual slope to each group.[Return to text.⏎](#c7.xhtml#re_f7.2)

Of course, for the data in [Figure 7.2](#c7.xhtml#f7.2), a more localized approach to the regression could be as simple as modeling each group separately, with one slope for males and one slope for females. The locality of the data, in this case, is the *group*. But consider this example carefully and you will see that what we are actually proposing here, at least implicitly, is to recognize the potential for an *interaction* in the plot and not simply a main effect. That is, *spotting the potential of an interaction in this sense is an effort to provide a more localized representation of the data*.

> *A localized representation of data can be as simple as computing different averages by identifying different sets in data, or by modeling interaction terms in a regression (or ANOVA) context.*

#### 7.1.5.1 Zooming in on Locality

What if we take locality to the limit, to the extreme? As mentioned, to do so would be to narrow in on each individual data point in our plot, or, for the example of averages, to simply consider each data point by itself. It doesn’t get much more “local” than considering the case study without producing an average at all!

In the regression context, we could produce interaction terms with increasingly greater degrees of “split” or factor levels in ANOVA in one or more variables. Hence, instead of dividing the data into two groups, we could divide them into four or five, for instance. The general principle then in a localized approach to regression (or ANOVA) is that instead of assuming a general functional form across the range of `x[i]`, we wish instead to “zoom in” and inspect *local variations* within given intervals of `x[i]`. This is where localized methods of regression come into play. However, as we survey a few approaches to accomplish this, the reader should take away what we have discussed here: that zooming in locally on data can be as simple as splitting data in different ways or modeling interaction terms in regression or ANOVA. That is, local examination and modeling of data need not necessarily take the form of a sophisticated or advanced modeling strategy.
