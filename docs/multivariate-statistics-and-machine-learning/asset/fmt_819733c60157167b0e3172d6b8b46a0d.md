
Essentially, an `observed variable` is any variable for which we can, at least in theory, obtain a direct `measurement`. For instance, `height` would be regarded as an `observed variable`. We can easily measure it and have well-defined units to describe it (e.g., inches, feet, etc.). If we are unable to obtain such a `direct` measurement of a variable and need or desire to infer its existence based on other measurable variables, then that variable is usually regarded as `unobserved`. The most classic example of a `latent variable` is that of intelligence (`IQ`). Intelligence is a `construct` for which it is difficult or even impossible to measure without inferring it based on other criteria, such as `IQ` tests, clinical interviews, etc., and hence, to measure something called “`IQ`,” one must `indicate` it via other more observable variables. Consequently, these observed variables are often referred to as `indicators` for the reason that they `indicate` the unobserved variable.

With this distinction in mind, and again with a little critical thought, it does not take long to realize that all variables are, at least philosophically, `unobservable`, though some variables are less unobservable than others. As an example, consider `height` once more. Is `height` observable or unobservable? If we measure your `height` to be 70 inches, we have, psychometrically, not measured your `true` `height`. We have merely a `measurement` of your `height` as an `indicator` of what your 483true height might be. `Height` is thus an unobservable variable but one might argue it is much less unobservable than a variable such as `IQ`. Likewise, though one’s `weight` is regarded as observable, in the end, `weight` is also an unobservable `construct` for which the measurement of `weight` is but an attempt to “tap into” the construct. This is how, when we push these ideas and distinctions to their philosophical limits, we come to realize that all variables are essentially `unobservable`, but some less so than others. However, for the purpose of building and analyzing path and structural equation models, we will consider the less unobservable variables to be observable and have the capacity of being directly measured. Variables such as `height`, `weight`, `distance`, `temperature`, `biometric pressure`, and so on, we will consider to be observable and easily measured variables. Variables such as `IQ`, `self-esteem`, `happiness`, `depression`, `self-actualization`, and so on, we will consider to be unobservable or `latent` variables that must be indicated by more observable ones.

## 13.4 Path Analysis: Extending Regression and Previewing SEM

Thus far, we have discussed path analysis informally. We now present the model a bit more formally. Path analysis is essentially a structural equation model that features only observed variables without the `direct` inclusion of unobservable or latent variables. The path model does include residual or `error` variables, however, and these are, by their nature, `latent`. They are considered unobservable because we cannot literally `see` or easily measure what might be “inside” such residual variables, or know precisely what it is composed of, implying it must be indicated by more observable variables. This meets the definition of a `latent variable`. An equivalent interpretation of a path model is as an extension of the classic regression models surveyed earlier in the book.

### 13.4.1 Exogenous vs. Endogenous Variables

An `exogenous` variable is one emanating from outside of the system and has no arrows pointed toward it. In [Figure 13.2](#c13.xhtml#f13.2), parental `IQ` is one such exogenous variable. An `endogenous` variable has at least one arrow pointing toward it, such as offspring `IQ` in the path diagram. The only endogenous variable in this model is that of child’s `IQ`, as both parental `intelligence` and `environment` are exogenous.
