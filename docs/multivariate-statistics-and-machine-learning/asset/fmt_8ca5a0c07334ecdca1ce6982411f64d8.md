
$$O\_{1} = \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} {\\cos\\phi\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\sin\\phi} \\\\ {- \\sin\\phi\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\cos\\phi} \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack$$

whereas a *counterclockwise* rotation of `Λ` features multiplication by the matrix `O`[2],

$$O\_{2} = \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} {\\cos\\phi\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, - \\sin\\phi} \\\\ {\\sin\\phi\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\cos\\phi} \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack$$

The new rotated loadings we now denote by `Λ[R]` and it can be shown that these new rotated loadings *provide the same fit to the covariance matrix as the unrotated loadings*. Recall our original deconstruction of the covariance matrix `Σ` presented earlier:

`Σ` = `ΛΛ′` + `ψ`

It can be shown that with the new loadings `Λ[R]`, the same covariance matrix can be reproduced:

`Σ` = `Λ[R]Λ[R]′` + `ψ`

(12.10)

It is for this reason that loadings in the orthogonal factor model are not unique. We can rotate the loadings quite freely and still generate the same covariance (or correlation) matrix. There are numerous ways of rotating factors in factor analysis. In what follows we survey a couple of the more popular methods in use.

### 12.5.1 Varimax Rotation

The varimax rotation is given by468

$$\\left. V = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{p}\\left( l\_{ij}^{2} - \\overline{l\_{j}^{2}} \\right.} \\right)^{2}$$

(12.11)

where `l[i j]²` are the squared loadings `i` to `p` for a given factor, and $\\overline{l\_{j}^{2}}$ is the average squared loading across the `p` variables (i.e., across the *observed variables*, not factors). The name *varimax* derives from the fact that it maximizes these sum of squared loadings for each factor. How is such a maximum achieved? It attains a maximum by driving larger weights to be even larger and smaller weights to be even smaller, creating *heterogeneity* in the estimated weights in a manner that maximizes their squared sum. Varimax rotation has the benefit of clarifying factor structure since the larger weights will, hopefully, be more indicative of what the factor is “made up of” (in some sense) compared to the smaller weights that poorly indicate the factor structure. In his original use of factor analysis, Thurstone called this idea that of establishing *simple structure* ([Thurstone, 1954](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000358)).

### 12.5.2 Quartimax

The quartimax rotation is given by

$$\\left. Q = {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{m}\\left( l\_{ij}^{2} - \\overline{l\_{j}^{2}} \\right.} \\right)^{2}$$

(12.12)

where now the sum of squared loadings, `l[i j]²`, is maximized *across* factors `j` = 1 through `m`, having the effect of maximizing the variance due to observed variables rather than the factor variance as was the case for varimax. That is, maximizing *across* factors instead of *within* factors as varimax does has the effect of maximizing *variable* variance in place of the *factor* variance maximized by varimax.

Regardless of which of the above rotations we perform, the same correlation (or covariance) matrix is reproduced. To reiterate, this point is key to understanding the indeterminacy problem of factor analysis. One could, in theory, rotate the solution until one finds the solution they “prefer” without any consequence on the structure or makeup of the covariance matrix. Clearly, this will lead to the analyst’s subjective preference for a given solution, one that naturally accords with what the analyst was “hoping” to see.

## 12.6 Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity

A factor analysis, not surprisingly, requires there to be correlations greater than 0 in `R`, the correlation matrix. Prior to conducting a factor analysis then, one might wonder whether performing the EFA on the correlation matrix is worthwhile. That is, we may question whether there is *sufficient* correlation in the matrix on which to conduct the factor analysis. Of course, nothing prevents us from trying an EFA on a matrix with even minimal correlation. However, tests exist to evaluate the feasibility of doing so prior to doing the factor analysis and might also help explain why the ensuing factor analysis did not quite work out as the investigator had originally hoped.

*Bartlett’s test of sphericity* is one such test that seeks to evaluate the “appropriateness” of the data as it concerns factor analysis and is given by469

$$\\left. \\chi^{2} = - \\left\\lbrack {\\left( n - 1 \\right) - \\frac{\\left( 2p + 5 \\right)}{6}} \\right\\rbrack\\ln \\middle| R \\right|$$

(12.13)

where `p` is the number of variables, `n` is the number of observations, and ln|`R`| is the natural logarithm of the determinant of the correlation matrix. For any given problem,

$$- \\left\\lbrack {\\left( n - 1 \\right) - \\frac{\\left( 2p + 5 \\right)}{6}} \\right\\rbrack$$

will be a constant, and hence what determines the size of `χ²` will be a function of `R`, more specifically the determinant of `R`, |`R`|, where the latter can be considered a measure of *generalized variance* ([Johnson & Wichern, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173)). Rejecting the null in this case suggests the matrix under analysis is not an identity matrix, which implies there exists sufficient correlation in `R` to carry on with the analysis. A failure to reject the null here does not “prove” the matrix to be an identity matrix, but it may cast doubt on whether performing the factor analysis is worthwhile. As with all significance tests, however, since Bartlett’s will be influenced by sample size, researchers are sometimes encouraged to perform the factor analysis regardless of what is reported by Bartlett’s, especially if sample size is fairly large. On a practical level, performing the factor analysis can’t hurt either way, and at worst, the investigator may simply not find any meaningful factor structure from the analysis.
