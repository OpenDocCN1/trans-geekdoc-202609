
The origins of the bootstrap are usually credited with [Efron (1979)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000093) and is actually a special case of what is generally known as the `jackknife` which came before it, but is far more applicable and generalizable than the `jackknife`. The `jackknife` itself can be thought of as a `linear expansion method` (called the `delta method`) for approximating the bootstrap ([Efron, 1979](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000093)). As noted by [Efron and Hastie (2016)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000094), “Before computers, applied statisticians needed to be Taylor series experts in 100laboriously pursuing the accuracy of even moderately complicated statistics” (p. 155). The `jackknife`, originating in 1957, was a first step toward a computational approach to standard errors.

The basic idea of the bootstrap is to capitalize on computing power to draw repeated samples from a sample that, for the given bootstrap scenario, serves as a `pseudo-population`. We include the bootstrap here in our discussion of estimation as it is yet another way to estimate parameters of a distribution; while the methods we have just surveyed do so `parametrically` through time-tested analytical formulae, the bootstrap is a `nonparametric` way of estimation through simulations of resampling using the computer.

Even more generally, bootstrap methods are a special case of the wider family of `Monte Carlo methods`, which is a general name given for `resampling`. To be clear, the bootstrap is not `equivalent` to Monte Carlo methods by any stretch, as many other techniques that utilize Monte Carlo sampling exist. However, in applied statistics and machine learning circles the bootstrap is undoubtedly one of the more popular `applications` of the general Monte Carlo technique. As a result of the bootstrap typically being used for estimation, we have included a discussion of it here instead of under Monte Carlo techniques, but including it under a general discussion of Monte Carlo techniques would not have been wrong.

### 2.28.1 Simple Examples of the Bootstrap

As discussed, `the bootstrapped sample is to the original sample as the original sample is to the population`. Why would we ever want to sample from a sample? There are a few reasons, but one primary reason is that obtaining things like standard errors and such `analytically` is sometimes not feasible and in many cases, actually impossible. True, we know what the standard error is for the mean, for instance, equal to

$$\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}$$

but we are less sure, for instance, what the standard error is for the `median` (i.e., the standard error for the median is less `exact`). That is, the standard error of the median is less `analytically available`. However, thanks to the bootstrap, we can assess “empirically” the standard deviation of medians (i.e., the standard error of the median). This is a chief benefit to being able to bootstrap, that of being able to “simulate” statistics empirically via resampling that have no analytical sampling distribution or that deriving such sampling distributions proves very difficult. In theory, we could “invent” a new statistic tomorrow that has no known sampling distribution and simply bootstrap the statistic via resampling to determine its “behavior” over the long run. This is perhaps the biggest reason why the bootstrap is such a “big deal” in statistics.

More formally, assume we have a `training set`, denoted by `Z = (z[1],z[2],...,z[N])`, where any given `z[i]` corresponds to data on `x`, `y`, that is, `z[i] = (x[i],y[i])`. Imagine now we resample from `Z` a total of `B = 100` samples. We will refer to these samples as the `bootstrapped samples`. To use our example of the standard error, we would compute standard deviations on each of the bootstrapped samples and observe standard deviation behavior over the 100 samples. This would denote the standard error we want to compute. However, we need not necessarily be computing standard errors; we could be computing virtually `anything` on these bootstrapped samples. As mentioned, bootstrapping is widely applicable to virtually any statistic, even those for which deriving sampling distributions is impossible.

As an example, we could compute linear regressions on the bootstrapped samples and observe the behavior of the linear equations across the samples, or any other models or statistics. This is the 101power of the bootstrap and Monte Carlo simulation in general; `it allows us to resample repeatedly and build a distribution of functions of the data, whatever those functions may be`. The bootstrap in this regard is very flexible and has wide application. It is `nonparametric`, meaning it assumes nothing about the population, making it extremely flexible and well-suited for creative ventures pertaining to whatever you wish to calculate on the repeated samples. Bootstrapping `allows you to create an actual empirical distribution of computed statistics instead of deriving a theoretical one analytically`.

More formally, consider once more `Z = (z[1],z[2],...,z[N])`. Suppose we compute some quantity on `Z`, that is, some function of `Z` which we will appropriately denote `f(Z)`. Suppose that function `f(Z)` is the variance of `Z`, that is, `Var(Z)` defined as

$$f\\left( Z \\right) = Var\\left( Z \\right) = \\frac{\\sum\\limits\_{b = 1}^{B}\\left( Z\_{}^{{}^{\*b}} - {\\overline{Z}}^{\*b} \\right)^{2}}{B}$$

where `Z^{*b}` is a particular computed bootstrapped sample and `${\\overline{Z}}^{\*b}$` is the `mean` of all bootstrapped samples. Notice carefully that `Var(Z)` here is just an ordinary variance but computed on the `B` bootstrapped samples. All of this looks familiar based on our earlier study of the variance. For instance, if we wished for the more typical estimator, we could use `B - 1` instead of `B` in the denominator,

$$f\\left( Z \\right) = Var\\left( Z \\right) = \\frac{\\sum\\limits\_{b = 1}^{B}\\left( Z^{\*b} - {\\overline{Z}}^{\*b} \\right)^{2}}{B - 1}$$

It is easy to see then that `Var(Z)` is yielding a measure of variance around bootstrapped samples.

### 2.28.2 Why Not Bootstrap Everything?

As we have seen, the bootstrap is quite universal and general and can be applied in many different contexts. Indeed, we can literally bootstrap virtually any function on a sample if we have sufficient reason for doing so. It does not need to be something familiar, such as a mean, median, or other well-known statistic. This is precisely the advantage of the bootstrap, in that it allows us to estimate statistical quantities for which we may have literally no theory to guide us and for which there may be no chance of ever knowing the sampling distribution without resampling.

So, why isn’t the bootstrap used more often? Why don’t we bootstrap virtually `everything`? The reason is that except when assumptions are violated, analytically-derived solutions may produce more favorable results than resampling. Computer simulations can be inexact when compared to timeless analytical derivations. Hence, when statistical theory is available and analytical solutions possible, `classic formulas may dominate bootstrapped approaches`. As a general guideline as to when or not to bootstrap, first check to see if an analytical solution is available and use it if it is a valid one that is strongly supported theoretically. Second, try the bootstrap. Of course, there is never any harm in trying out both approaches and comparing results.
