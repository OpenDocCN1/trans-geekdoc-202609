
Rejecting a null hypothesis in `ANOVA` only suggests that the observed statistic is relatively implausible or unlikely under the null hypothesis and as such there is evidence to suggest population means are unequal. It does not, by itself, suggest that differences observed are in any sense *sizable*, *meaningful*, or otherwise *scientifically important*. Indeed, inspection of the `ANOVA` summary table reveals that with sufficiently large sample size, `N`, statistical significance and thus rejection of the null hypothesis is assured even for small deviations of group means from the grand mean. As an example of this, consider what happens to `SS within` when divided by a sizable `N` − `J` term, due to `N` being extremely large. The numerator $\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{J}{\\sum\\limits\_{i}^{n}\\left( y\_{ij} - {\\overline{y}}\_{j} \\right)^{2}}$ is divided by an increasingly large number, `N` − `J`, and the ratio becomes smaller and smaller and in the limit as `N` grows without bound, `MS within` eventually goes to 0:

$$\\lim\\limits\_{N\\rightarrow\\infty}\\text{MS}\_{Within} = 0$$

A numerator divided by an increasingly larger denominator generates a ratio that gets smaller and smaller. As `MS[Within]` gets smaller, `MS[Between]` begins to look large in comparison, and hence a statistically significant `F`-ratio is possible even if the actual distance between means is extremely small. *Hence, “`p` < `0.05`” can occur even under conditions where mean differences are negligible and not substantively meaningful or important*. If one naively associates the statement “`p` < 0.05” with the experiment necessarily revealing an “important” effect in any way, such a conclusion could prove tremendously misleading to a reader who may be otherwise ignorant of what a `p`-value is a function of. *Not understanding what a p-value tells you vs. what it does not tell you can result in serious miscommunication of results and this occurs daily in published research results across many professional and even “high impact factor” journals*. *Conflating statistical significance with effect size is still very common across the sciences despite almost 100 years of attempts to correct these misconceptions.*

As a result of the `p`-value not indicating the strength of effect in an experiment or study, a measure that should always be reported alongside the `p`-value is that of *effect size*. A good effect size does not allow its magnitude to be influenced by simple increases or decreases in sample size. For `ANOVA`, a classic effect size is obtained by taking a ratio of `SS between` to `SS total`:

$$\\eta^{2} = \\frac{SS\\,\\text{Between}}{SS\\,\\text{Total}} = \\frac{\\sum\\limits\_{j}^{J}{n\_{j}\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{j} - \\overline{y}. \\right)^{2}}}{\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{J}{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{ij} - \\overline{y}. \\right)^{2}}}$$

(4.4)

The extent to which `SS between` is large relative to `SS total` is the extent to which total variation is “accounted for” by treatment effects. It is easy to show that `η²` has a maximum of 1.0 and a minimum of 0. A value of 1.0 would indicate that 100% of variation in the data is accounted for by 191treatment effects, whereas a value of 0 would indicate that 0% of variation in the data is accounted for by treatment effects and would also imply that `SS within/SS total` is equal to 1.0 since `SS between` + `SS within` must equate to `SS total`.

*Eta-squared* is a popular statistic reported as a first step to effect size; however, it is biased upward, meaning that it typically *overestimates* the true effect size in the population. A less biased effect size is that of *omega-squared*, `ω²`, for which the following is the corresponding estimator:

$${\\hat{\\omega}}^{2} = \\frac{SS\\, between - \\left( J - 1 \\right)MS\\, within}{SS\\, total + MS\\, within}$$

(4.5)

We note that `ω²` features degrees of freedom for between (`J` – 1) in its formulation, making it more “inferential” than Eta-squared which only includes sums of squares. The extent to which the numerator is large relative to the denominator is the extent to which `ω²` will be larger than not. Unlike for `η²`, `ω²` can be negative ([Okada, 2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000271)), where a value of 1 indicates 100% of the variance in the response is accounted for by mean differences on the independent variable. An alternative but equivalent computation for `ω²` is

$$\\omega^{2} = \\frac{\\left( J - 1 \\right)\\left( F - 1 \\right)}{\\left( J - 1 \\right)\\left( F - 1 \\right) + nJ}$$

(4.6)

where `F` is that obtained from the `ANOVA` and `n` is sample size per group, assuming in this case equal `n` per level of the independent variable. This alternative formula for `ω²` can be of use in cases where a researcher or journal article may not present the full summary table for the `ANOVA` but instead only make mention of `F`. More details on `ω²` can be found in [Kirk (1995)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000187). Ideally, researchers should report *at least* both `η²` and `ω²` in summarizing `ANOVA` findings. Generalized versions of both of these statistics that incorporate design features into their computation can also be computed. See [Olejnik and Algina (2003)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000274) for details.

### 4.1.4 Contrasts and *Post-Hoc* Tests for ANOVA

Typically, researchers are not so much interested in simply a statistically significant `F`-ratio from the `ANOVA` as much as they are in specific group *contrasts* between levels of the independent variable. In a two-group `ANOVA`, which is simply a generalization of univariate `t`, there is only one contrast to make, one group vs. the other, and the `ANOVA` or `t`-test has already made it. However, in cases where there are three groups or more, researchers often wish to specify specific pairwise group comparisons. For example, in a three-group problem, the researcher may wish to compare group 1 to a combination of groups 2 and 3. Perhaps group 1 is the control group and groups 2 and 3 are experimental groups. To do this, a *population comparison* `C[i]` can be computed,

$$C\_{i} = c\_{1}\\mu\_{1} + c\_{2}\\mu\_{2} + \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot + c\_{J}\\mu\_{J} = {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{J}{c\_{j}\\mu\_{j}}}$$

(4.7)

where `c[1]` through `c[J]` are weights, usually integers, and `μ[1]` through `μ[J]` are population means. A population comparison then is a *sum of weighted population means*. If we choose weights `c[1]` through `c[J]` such that these weights sum to 0, the population comparison becomes a population 192*linear contrast*. Should the sum $\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{J}{c\_{j}\\mu\_{j}}$ deviate from 0 at all under this situation, it is clearly not due to the linear sum of weights `c[1]` through `c[J]` but instead must be a function of an inequality of population means `μ[1]` through `μ[J]`. This is the precise reason for choosing weights `c[1]` through `c[J]` so that they sum to 0, so that a deviation from 0 for `C[i]` is reflective of a pairwise group comparison of interest to the researcher (in the form of the defined contrast).

As an example of a comparison featuring three groups (populations), we have the following:

`C[i]` = `c[1]` `μ[1]` + `c[2]` `μ[2]` + `c[3]` `μ[3]`

Suppose the researcher hypothesizes that `μ[1]` is different from `μ[2]` and `μ[3]` as a set. This hypothesis could be reflected in the following population contrast:

$$\\begin{array}{l} {C\_{i} = c\_{1}\\mu\_{1} + c\_{2}\\mu\_{2} + c\_{3}\\mu\_{3}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\left( 2 \\right)\\mu\_{1} + \\left( - 1 \\right)\\mu\_{2} + \\left( - 1 \\right)\\mu\_{3}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

By choosing weights “2” and “−1” and “−1” that sum to 0, we see that the above successfully compares `μ[1]` to `μ[2]` and `μ[3]` as a set. Any deviation of `C[i]` from 0 can only be due to a difference between `μ[1]` vs. `μ[2]` and `μ[3]`.
