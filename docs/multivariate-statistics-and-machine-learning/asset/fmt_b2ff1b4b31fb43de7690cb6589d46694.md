Observe the movement of the single circled point and notice how it dramatically shifts the maximum margin classifier. Can we tolerate such a shift of the hyperplane? Yes, we can, but do we really want a single point or vector to determine the location of the hyperplane to this extent? Probably not. We may be better off with a hyperplane that is not so much influenced by a point or two in determining its location. But to effectuate this compromise, we will need to require our separating hyperplane to not classify *perfectly*. That is, we may be better off with a hyperplane that classifies *most* of the observations correctly instead of *all* the observations, while still allowing some error for points or vectors that are not on the correct side of the hyperplane. As we will see, this is one motivating factor for developing the *support vector classifier* and preferring it over the maximum margin classifier.

There is a second reason for wanting to do better than the maximum margin classifier. Earlier we noted that even if a vector or two dramatically shifted the hyperplane, these did not by themselves *negate* the separating hyperplane. The hyperplane was affected by the movement of the 358vectors, but it could still be *defined* on the data. However, *what if no separating hyperplane exists?* The solution for a problem like this is to extend the concept of a separating hyperplane to one that *almost* separates the classes. The *support vector classifier* generalizes the maximal margin classifier to the non-separable case. Hence, note carefully that we started with the maximal margin classifier for the linearly separable boundary; in the linearly non-separable case, the support vector classifier is what we wish to construct for data that look similar to that in [Figure 9.8](#c9.xhtml#f9.8), as one possibility.

It is easy to see in [Figure 9.8](#c9.xhtml#f9.8) that a simple hyperplane will not be sufficient in separating the classes since there is clearly no separating hyperplane that can exist with this data. The solution to this problem will be to construct the *support vector classifier*. The support vector classifier will seek to classify most of the observations correctly but will allow *imperfect classification* such that it is not as *rigid* as the maximum margin classifier. As a comparison, recall that linear discriminant analysis (LDA) featured a relatively “lax” hyperplane as well, but in a different sense. In LDA, we sought to maximize between-group variance relative to within-group variance, which, unless the data were “perfect,” implied that some of our classifications would also be in error, which we could easily observe by computing a confusion matrix, for instance.

## 9.3 Support Vector Classifier for the Non-Separable Case

The non-separable case operates by *almost* separating the two classes via what is known as a *soft margin*. This classifier will attempt to classify *most* of the training data. That is, it allows for some observations to be on the wrong side under the condition that it is still classifying most of the observations correctly. This is indeed why it is called a *soft margin* since, though it establishes a margin, it is not “too” strict about it, analogous to a parent who establishes ground rules for the house but allows a bit of deviation away from those rules from time to time. Soft margins have benefits over hard margins, however, as most of the time they will still get things right; that is, they will still, in general, classify correctly.359

To appreciate how the support vector classifier is operationalized, we return once more to the maximum margin classifier discussed earlier. Recall its optimization criterion,

`y[i](β[0]+β[1]x[i1]+β[2]x[i2]+⋅⋅⋅+β[p]x[i p]) ≥ M`

The optimization criterion of the support vector classifier is similar,

`y[i](β[0]+β[1]x[i1]+β[2]x[i2]+⋅⋅⋅+β[p]x[i p]) ≥ M(1−ε[i])`

(9.8)

What has changed? We note the addition of `(1−ε[i])` on the right-hand side, so that instead of the criterion being `≥ M` it is now `M(1−ε[i])`, where `ε[i]` is referred to as a *slack variable*. The slack variable is what gives the soft margin its flexibility and non-rigidness when compared to the hard margin. For `ε[i] = 0`, there is no slack, and the `i`th observation is on the correct side of the margin, and the support vector classifier reduces to the maximum margin classifier featured earlier as can easily be seen by substituting `ε[i] = 0`:

$$
\begin{array}{l}
{y_{i}\left( \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i1} + \beta_{2}x_{i2} + \cdot \cdot \cdot + \beta_{p}x_{ip} \right) \geq M\left( 1 - \varepsilon_{i} \right)} \\
{y_{i}\left( \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i1} + \beta_{2}x_{i2} + \cdot \cdot \cdot + \beta_{p}x_{ip} \right) \geq M\left( 1 - 0 \right)} \\
{y_{i}\left( \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i1} + \beta_{2}x_{i2} + \cdot \cdot \cdot + \beta_{p}x_{ip} \right) \geq M} \\
\end{array}
$$

Of course, `ε[i] = 0` is not the reason why the support vector classifier was established. It was motivated for cases in which `ε[i] > 0`, since such implies the observation is on the wrong side of the margin, and if `ε[i] > 1`, then on the wrong side of the hyperplane ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032); [Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). The value of `ε[i]` here is thus very important, since it alone determines the degree of *flexibility* we would like to establish in our classifier both for the case in which `ε[i] = 0` (in which the case is simplified to the maximum margin classifier) and `ε[i] ≠ 0` for which we need to “regulate” the degree of slack that we will allow. Hence, this becomes a *regularization* problem, and we introduce the tuning parameter `C` to regulate the extent to which we will allow `ε[i]` to deviate from 0. We will require `ε[i] ≥ 0` such that the sum of all `ε[i]` is less than some constant, `C`,

$$
\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}{\varepsilon_{i} \leq C}
$$

(9.9)

Similar to how we conceptualized the tuning parameter in methods such as ridge and lasso regression, the number `C` above provides a kind of “budget” for how much of a violation we will tolerate as it relates to our soft margin ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). It is thus a *tunable hyperparameter*. We could have also required a different constraint, that instead of `M(1−ε[i])` on the right-hand side, we could, at least in theory, choose `(M−ε[i])` such that our criterion now becomes

`y[i](β[0]+β[1]x[i1]+β[2]x[i2]+⋅⋅⋅+β[p]x[i p]) ≥ M − ε[i]`

(9.10)
