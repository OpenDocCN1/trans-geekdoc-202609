
## 8.16 Naïve Bayes
$$p\\left( y = k \\middle| x = x\_{i} \\right) = \\frac{\\pi\_{k}f\_{k1}\\left( x\_{1} \\right) \\cdot f\_{k2}\\left( x\_{2} \\right) \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot f\_{kp}\\left( x\_{p} \\right)}{\\sum\\limits\_{l = 1}^{K}{\\pi\_{1}f\_{l1}\\left( x\_{1} \\right) \\cdot f\_{l2}\\left( x\_{2} \\right) \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot f\_{lp}\\left( x\_{p} \\right)}}$$  
(8.42)
where the product `f[k1](x[1])` ⋅ `f[k2](x[2])` ⋅ ... ⋅ `f[kp](x[p])` denotes the independence (i.e., product of densities). Hence, we see that Naïve Bayes is simply a generalization of the classic Bayes formula, where within each class, `k` predictors are *assumed* independent and can, depending on the given research context, also in many cases conveniently be assumed to be drawn from a normal distribution with mean `μ[jk]` and variance `σ[jk]²`. As James et al. ([2021, p. 155](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000169)) remark,
> While this may sound a bit like QDA, there is one key difference, in that here we are assuming that the predictors are *conditionally independent* [emphasis added]; this amounts to QDA with an additional assumption that the class-specific covariance matrix is diagonal.
A diagonal matrix is one with zeroes everywhere except the main diagonal, which in this case, implies the predictors will have a variance (along the main diagonal), but that covariances along the off-diagonal will equal 0. Recalling our covariance from our statistical review earlier for a 3 × 3 matrix, the diagonal matrix would look like this:
$$\\sum{= \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} {\\,\\sigma^{2}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, 0\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, 0} \\\\ {\\, 0\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\sigma^{2}\\mspace{6mu}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, 0} \\\\ {\\, 0\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, 0\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\sigma^{2}\\,} \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack\\,\\,}$$  
Hence, this is a key difference between QDA and Naïve Bayes. In QDA, nowhere did we assume predictors were uncorrelated or conditionally independent. In Naïve Bayes, we assume they are. Again, it’s an *unrealistic assumption*, but one that, if made, allows us to process and fit this type of model.343

## 8.17 LDA, QDA, or Naïve Bayes: Which Is Best?
We conclude our discussion of classification methods surveyed in this chapter with a very brief comment comparing approaches. Recall that a major purpose of this book is to encourage the reader to situate statistical and machine learning models relative to one another, to “see” the *grand structure* so that one does not think that different methods necessarily imply wholly different technical structures. To this end, we wish the reader to be able to appreciate that many of the methods featured in this book are *special cases* of others, or at minimum, *relatable* to other techniques. We refer the reader to James et al. ([2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168), pp. 151–152) who provide a much more thorough discussion.
First, as we have already discussed, `LDA` can be considered a restricted version, or “special case” of `QDA`, one for which the equality of covariance matrices condition is imposed,
`Σ[1]` = ... = `Σ[K]` = `Σ`
Second, we can conclude that many classifiers with a linear boundary can be considered, in a sense, as a special case of `Naïve Bayes`, which means that `LDA` can be considered a special case of `Naïve Bayes`. However, because `QDA` does not impose linearity, this correspondence does not hold for `QDA`. `Naïve Bayes` is additive only, where `QDA` and `LDA` can also include interaction terms, which is an important deciphering characteristic between these models.
So, which of these models is better than the other? Not surprisingly by now, it turns out that *none are universally better than the others*. As summarized by James et al. ([2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000169), p. 160):
> None of these methods uniformly dominate the others; in any setting, the choice of method will depend on the true distribution of the predictors in each of the K classes, as well as other considerations, such as the values of n and p. The latter ties into the bias-variance trade-off … we expect `LDA` to outperform logistic regression when the normality assumption (approximately) holds, and we expect logistic regression to perform better when it does not.

## 8.18 Nonparametric K-Nearest Neighbors
`K-nearest neighbors` (`KNN`) takes a completely different approach from the parametric classifiers in this chapter (such as `LDA`) and is a completely nonparametric approach. That is, virtually no assumptions are made about the shape of the decision boundary whatsoever. `KNN` will likely dominate `LDA` and logistic in most circumstances when the decision boundary is relatively nonlinear. Unlike logistic regression, `KNN` will not tell us which predictors are *important* in any sense as we will not get a table of coefficients as part of the output in `KNN`. We discuss `KNN` in more detail now.

### 8.18.1 K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN): Only Looking at Nearby Points
The `k`-nearest-neighbor approach is often used for classification into one of two categories, though multiclass `KNN` is a possibility as well for cases in which there are more than two classes. What makes K-nearest neighbors nonparametric? `KNN` relies exclusively on the data to make its classification. It derives its name from the fact that it looks at *neighboring data* to determine which group the current observation should be classified to. `KNN` stores the training data and simply makes a 345decision based on points “close” (in terms of distance) to the observation under consideration. For this reason, `KNN` is sometimes referred to as a *lazy learner*; “lazy,” because it does not bother to incorporate that much information in making its classification decision; it only looks at the nearby points and that is enough.
