
## Hopkins Statistic
The *Hopkins statistic* is one popular measure that can be used to assess the clusterability of data. It can be interpreted as a measure of *clustering tendency* of a data set ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005); [Wright, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000393)). In this approach, `p[r]` values are randomly generated as well as `p[d]` points randomly sampled from the data. For both sets of points, distances to their nearest neighbors in the data are computed. That is, if we let `x[i]` be a nearest neighbor, then what we wish to compare are the distances `p[r]` to `x[i]`, which we will call `D_random` vs. `p[d]` to `x[i']`, which we will call `D_data`. That is, we are interested in knowing how the distances to nearest neighbors compare based on a *random data set* vs. *actual data points* in the data set.

The Hopkins statistic is thus defined as

$$H = \\frac{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{r}D\_{i(random)}}{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{r}\\left( D\_{i(data)} + D\_{i(random)} \\right)}$$

(14.9)

For uniform randomly generated data, the expectation for Hopkins is in the neighborhood of 0.5. This is as a result of `D_i(data)` and `D_i(random)` being similar. If the data are clusterable, `D_i(data)` is typically less than `D_i(random)` and hence `H` will increase. Values of `H` greater than 0.75 have been found to equate to clustering “confidence” at 90% ([Lawson & Jurs, 1990](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000212); [Wright, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000393)). [Adolfsson et al. (2019)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000004) provide an extensive review of so-called clusterability methods (many other techniques exist over and above Hopkins), and have found that the Hopkins statistic may be a somewhat limited measure when clusters touch or overlap.

The package `hopkins` ([Wright, 2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000394)) is available in R for computing the Hopkins statistic. As an example from the package, we compute a Hopkins statistic on the iris data (sepal length through petal width, excluding species), where we compute Hopkins on `m = 15` observations (or sampled *rows* of the data, the default is 1/10th of the rows if not set specifically):
```
> library(hopkins)
> hopkins(iris[, 1:4], m=15)
[1] 0.9986529
```
We can also obtain a `p-value` for the computed Hopkins statistic where `x` is the value of the computed Hopkins and `n` is the number of observations sampled:506
```
> hopkins.pval(x = 0.9986529, n = 15)
[1] 0
```
As a result of the value for Hopkins being so large, the `p-value` is extremely small (it is rounded to 0, `p-values` *never* equal 0 *exactly*). Suppose instead we obtained a Hopkins equal to 0.5, the `p-value` would thus be
```
> hopkins.pval(x = 0.5, n = 15)
[1] 1
```
The value is equal to 1 since recall the expectation of Hopkins for a uniform distribution is equal to 0.5. The further away from 0.5, the lower the `p-value`, as demonstrated once more,
```
> hopkins.pval(0.1, 15)
[1] 3.925226e-08
> hopkins.pval(0.9, 15)
[1] 3.925226e-08
```
We see that for both values of 0.1 and 0.9, identical `p-values` are obtained. As usual with a significance test, as `n` increases, all else equal, `p-values` drop. Using our previous example but increasing `n` to 100 not surprisingly yields
```
> hopkins.pval(0.9, 100)
[1] 0
```

## 14.8 Algorithms for Cluster Analysis
In this section we survey some of the more popular clustering algorithms, which include `k-means` and associated `k-representatives`, hierarchical or agglomerative clustering, density-based clustering (specifically DBSCAN), and clustering via mixture models.

### 14.8.1 `K-Means` Clustering and `K-Representatives`
The `k-means` clustering algorithm is probably the simplest and well-known, most used form of cluster analysis. `k-means` is a special case of the wider group of *representative-based* algorithms for clustering that rely on the concept of *distance* between observations as the basis for clustering. These approaches are also sometimes called *partitional* ([Madhulatha, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000233)) or *unnested* methods since they seek to partition the data into groups or clusters by assigning cases nearest cluster centroids whereas hierarchical clustering is considered a form of *nested* clustering that builds clusters by essentially fusing smaller subordinate clusters. The centroids in this case are the so-called “representatives” of the given clusters. Other representative-based algorithms include `k-medians`, `k-medoids`, and `kernel-based k-means`. Briefly, the `k-medians` approach uses the median instead of the mean and is thus more robust to extreme and outlying cases than is the `k-means` approach. In the `k-medoids` approach, initial starting points to initiate the algorithm are drawn from the original database, and in kernel-based `k-means`, a kernel function via the familiar *kernel trick* is applied in generating clusters.

As mentioned, representative-based algorithms are usually contrasted to *hierarchical approaches* which take an either *bottom-up* or *top-down* approach to building clusters and generally require the storage of a distance matrix. Nonhierarchical clustering methods are generally 507more efficient than hierarchical methods since they do not require the storing of a distance matrix. The jargon can differ slightly depending on the source one reads, so it behooves the reader investigating clustering algorithms to be sure of how terms are being used by the author.

### 14.8.2 How `K-Means` Works
`k-means` clustering attempts to partition cases into distinct clusters where the number of clusters is requested by the user beforehand. In `k-means`, the sum of squared distances to their closest representatives is optimized and an observation belonging to one cluster cannot simultaneously belong to another. That is, cluster membership is *mutually exclusive*. This will typically not be the case in some other clustering methods such as density-based or mixture modeling clustering. So-called *fuzzy clustering* algorithms also allow for a case to be clustered into more than a single cluster.

The `k-means` algorithm also clusters every object, implying that there will not be any observations left out of the clustering. In this sense, `k-means` is regarded as a *complete clustering* algorithm such that it seeks to cluster *every* case. This is in contrast to *partial clustering* algorithms that allow some cases to go unclustered. For example, DBSCAN, a density-based clustering algorithm which we will survey shortly, typically does not produce complete clustering since it defines some cases as simply *noise* points rather than classifying them to clusters. These “noise” points are those that do not seem to “fit” in any of the clusters being generated. And while `k-means` is often regarded as an “algorithm” today, there is nothing special about the word “algorithm” as it applies to the procedure. Indeed, in the past, `k-means` was often called a data-analytic *procedure* or *method*. Like many statistical and machine learning approaches today, it is often referred to as an “algorithm” largely, though not *exclusively*, due to the zeitgeist that we currently find ourselves in. Otherwise excellent works on cluster analysis may not once even mention the word “algorithm” and may simply describe the process by which clusters are derived.

> `K-means` is a special case of the wider group of representative-based clustering algorithms. Hierarchical algorithms on the other hand can be divided into bottom-up and top-down approaches.
