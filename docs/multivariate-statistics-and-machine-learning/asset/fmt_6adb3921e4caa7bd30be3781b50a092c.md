
Hence, when we think of bagging, it is convenient to simply think of the word *averaging*. *Averaging reduces variance*. As an analogy, imagine taking betting predictions for a sporting event from one forecaster vs. ten forecasters. If you averaged the results from ten forecasters, the prediction would have less variance. On average, the forecast might be more “stable” than if you relied on the prediction of a single, stronger forecaster. This is the idea of bagging, except that we will apply it to bootstrapped samples instead of to actual empirical divisions of the data. In theory, if we had enough data, we could feasibly take segments of the data and average results, but usually, 381we do not have enough data to do this. This is why bootstrapping is utilized in bagging – *because of an insufficient amount of real data*.

### 10.5.3 A Deeper Look at Bagging

Let us look more closely into how bagging works. First, we begin with the most ideal and fanciful but unrealistic of situations. Suppose we apply a statistical learning method to some data and compute a function on many different empirical subsets of that data. Let’s suppose for a moment that we were able to partition the data perfectly and have all subsets of the data represented. Then, the average of all those subsets would be the following:

$${\\hat{f}}\_{avg}\\left( x \\right) = \\frac{1}{B}{\\sum\\limits\_{b = 1}^{B}{{\\hat{f}}^{b}\\left( x \\right)}}$$

where `B` is the number of subsets that we are drawing and computing on each a function on `x`. The estimated function is `f̂^b(x)`. Thus, `f̂^b(x)`/`B` is the average of all those computed estimated functions. But why is this situation unrealistic? It is so because we are likely to not have access to all partitions of the data, or, the number of partitions of the data would likely simply be too large to be practical. Data is usually expensive (at least in some fields), and so the idea of having large partitions of data available may not be pragmatic. However, it should be emphasized that if this were possible, it would be the ideal situation to shoot for. *Real data is always better than simulated data*.

The way around the above problem will be to bootstrap samples from the only sample we do have and compute the average of all such bootstrapped samples:

$${\\hat{f}}\_{bag}\\left( x \\right) = \\frac{1}{B}{\\sum\\limits\_{b = 1}^{B}{{\\hat{f}}^{\*b}\\left( x \\right)}}$$

Notice that the only thing that has changed is where before `f_avg(x)` was the average of all partitions of the training data, now `f̂^*b(x)` is the average of all the *bootstrapped* samples. Hence, we are averaging a subset of all samples via the bootstrap procedure. That is, we are *aggregating the bootstrapped samples*. This is precisely what bagging means. *It simply means taking an average of functions via bootstrapping*. For a good theoretical discussion of just why bagging works on a technical level superseding our intuitive conceptual explanation, see Breiman ([1996](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000047), p. 129). Breiman justifies that the degree of instability in the method is paramount to determining whether bagging will or will not be effective.

> *Bagging is simply taking an average of bootstrapped samples. The reason for taking bootstrapped samples is that dividing up the data into many divisions is impractical. Dividing the actual empirical data into subset samples would, theoretically, be our first choice, however, if practical.*

When working with decision trees, bagging can combine hundreds or even thousands of bootstrapped trees. Think of what this means for a moment – we have a single sample, we bootstrap many samples from this sample, and on each, we compute a separate decision tree. We then average the results of all the decision trees we compute. Each tree alone suffers from high variance, 382but when we bag (i.e., average) all of the bootstrapped trees, *we reduce variance*. As already discussed, bagging is especially useful when dealing with predictors that tend to overfit the data, such as in decision trees, where the unpruned tree structure is very sensitive to small changes or perturbations in the training set.

### 10.5.4 Out-of-Bag Error

Once we bag, we would like to know how well the bagging procedure performed. We need a way of evaluating the results of the bagging. To properly evaluate, it would make little sense to validate on the bagged decision trees (or whatever statistical learning method we used). This would be akin to evaluating how well a boxing fighter performs on practice opponents; it is not a true test of the boxer’s skill.

To evaluate the goodness of bagging, *we require observations that were not part of the bagging procedure*. That is, as with other cross-validation, we require a kind of *test set* on which to evaluate bagging. Observations that were not part of the bagging procedure are referred to as *out-of-bag (OOB) observations.* These are the observations that are part of the training set, but not used to generate the given bagged tree. In spirit, they serve the same purpose as in typical cross-validation; however, the out-of-bag observations are simply the set of observations (roughly 1/3; James et al., [2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)) in the training data not used in generating the given bootstrapped tree.

### 10.5.5 Interpreting Results from Bagging

When we bag, there is no easy way to interpret the result since now we have a number of trees rather than a single tree. Hence, while bagging does tend to improve prediction accuracy, as is true of so many machine learning algorithms, it does so generally at the expense of *interpretability*. There is simply no easy way to combine trees and read off the “meaningfulness,” in some sense, of the individual predictors.

The situation is not entirely hopeless, however. There are some measures of importance that can be used. For bagging regression trees, as already discussed, a decrease in `RSS` can be used to evaluate the importance of a predictor due to its split inclusion. A large decrease in `RSS` suggests the given predictor is valuable to the decision tree. In a classification context, decreases in the `Gini index` can be used in a similar way to evaluate the importance of a given variable. Large decreases in the `Gini index` suggest the variable under consideration may be important.

It should be emphasized as well that bagging does not always improve performance and relies very much on the base models being *unstable*. There are some situations where bagging will not improve performance or prediction. For instance, [Buja and Stuetzle (2000)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000050) found bagging to, at times, increase bias; however, the increase or decrease in mean squared error was contingent upon the given statistic and its distribution. There is no free lunch, and hence bagging on one data set may perform better or worse than bagging on another.
