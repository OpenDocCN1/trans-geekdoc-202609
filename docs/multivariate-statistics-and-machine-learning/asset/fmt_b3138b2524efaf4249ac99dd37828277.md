
In computing `p[j|i]`, we need a value on which the normal distribution is centered so we will choose `x[i]`. Hence, we see in the numerator of `p[j|i]` that the squared distance between points `x[i]` and `x[j]` is reflected by `||x[i]−x[j]||²`. Ignoring the constant `2σ[i]²` for a moment, we see that `p[j|i]` is nothing more than a ratio of terms. The first term, the numerator, consists of (squared) distance measured from `x[i]` to `x[j]`. We compare this distance to the sum of (squared) distances across all points `x[k]` in the denominator. It is easy to see the fraction must be equal to or less than 1, making it a legitimate probability. Hence, when we compute `p[j|i]`, we are computing the probability of `x[j]` being a “neighbor” given (i.e., conditional) that it is `x[i] − x[j]` deviation from `x[i]`. As the deviation `x[i] − x[j]` 451decreases relative to the overall sum of deviations in the denominator, it becomes more likely (i.e., `p[j|i]` increases) that it is regarded as a neighbor. Note that `k ≠ i` so that a point cannot choose itself as its neighbor.

Having performed the dimension reduction, we now define a new measure of similarity based on the new lower-dimensional scores `y`:

$$q\_{j/i} = \\frac{\\exp\\left( - \\middle| \\middle| y\_{i} - y\_{j} \\middle| \\middle| {}\_{2} \\right)}{\\sum\\limits\_{k \\neq i}{\\exp\\left( - \\middle| \\middle| y\_{i} - y\_{k} \\middle| \\middle| {}\_{2} \\right)}}$$  
(11.27)

where `q[j/i]` is now the probability of point `i` selecting point `j` as its neighbor in the `lower-dimensional space`. Recall that in computing `p[j|i]` earlier, we were able to freely choose values for `σ[i]²`. In the embedding of `q[j/i]`, `σ[i]²` is set to $1/\\sqrt{2}$ ([van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000372), p. 2581) though other values can also be used.

But how is the new, now `embedded` point, `y[i]` obtained? That is, how do we best determine the low-dimensional images `y[i]`? By what criterion or cost function `C`? It is obtained by minimizing what is called the `Kullback Leibler` (`KL`) `divergence`. That is, SNE seeks to minimize the sum of Kullback-Leibler divergences over all new embedded data points using a gradient descent method, with cost function equal to:

$$\\left. C = {\\sum\\limits\_{i}{KL\\left( P\_{i} \\right.}} \\middle| \\middle| Q\_{i} \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{i}{\\sum\\limits\_{j}p\_{j|i}}}\\log\\frac{p\_{j|i}}{q\_{j|i}}$$  
(11.28)

where in (11.28) `P[i]` is the conditional probability over all other data points conditioned on `x[i]` and `Q[i]` is the conditional probability distribution over all other map or “constructed” points given the mapped point `y[i]` ([van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000372)). Remember, we have two conditional probabilities now; `P[i]` is based on the original high-dimensional data, whereas `Q[i]` is based on the lower-dimensional embedding after performing the dimension reduction. The idea of SNE is for `P[i]` and `Q[i]` to be `as close as possible`. That is, the probability of `j` being selected given `i` should be as similar as possible as we go from the original distribution of data to the new, embedded distribution.

More specifically, the key to understanding `KL` is in understanding `p[j|i]/q[j|i]` where recall `p[j|i]` is the conditional probability computed earlier based on the high-dimensional data, and `q[j|i]` is a distance function based on the transformed induced lower-dimensional projections. Since log (`p[j|i]/q[j|i]`) = 0 only when `p[j|i]` = `q[j|i]`, this implies that `KL` is minimized when the embedding is successful at maintaining the distance between points `x[i]` and `x[j]` in the reconstruction `y[i]` and `y[j]` based on the dimension reduction. `KL(P[i]||Q[i])` is always equal or greater than zero and cannot be negative.

To minimize `KL`, we need to take the partial derivative of the cost function, `C`, $\\frac{\\partial C}{\\partial y\_{i}}$ ([Hinton & Roweis, 1998](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000153), p. 2582). The result is

$$\\frac{\\partial C}{\\partial y\_{i}} = 2{\\sum\\limits\_{j}{\\left( y\_{i} - y\_{j} \\right)\\left( p\_{i/j} - q\_{i/j} + p\_{j/i} - q\_{j/i} \\right)}}$$  
(11.29)

The goal of SNE then, is to find points that minimize `KL(P, Q)`, where `KL` essentially minimizes the divergence between probability distributions of the input space (i.e., the original high-dimensional 452data) and that of the output space (i.e., the reduced low-dimensional data). Defining a stochastic neighborhood this way, however, suffers from the `crowding problem`, which we discuss shortly.

> *Stochastic Neighborhood Embedding, or “SNE,” imposes a stochastic or random element into what constitutes a neighbor rather than a deterministic one as many other nearest-neighbor methods do. In SNE, for data points that are close, they have a higher probability of being neighbors than if they are distant. That is, SNE establishes stochastic neighborhood distributions, where closeness is defined by reference to Kullback-Leibler (`KL`) divergence. The goal of SNE is to find points that minimize `KL` divergence.*
