
where `D[G]` is a matrix of geodesic distances, `D[Y]` is a matrix of Euclidean distances resulting from the embedding and preserving the manifold’s essential geometry, and `τ` is an operator that 448converts distances into inner products. See Tenenbaum et al. ([2000](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000354), p. 2322) for the precise details regarding the computation of `τ`.

We can see then how `Isomap` is related to `MDS`. But what about `LLE`? Where does `LLE` fit into all this? While `LLE` and `Isomap` are similar, *LLE makes no effort to estimate the true geodesic distances, whereas Isomap does*. `LLE` instead attempts only to measure local linear “patches.” Furthermore, `Isomap` makes additional assumptions over `LLE`, such as the data being located in a convex region of `ℝ^(q)` and that the data was originally embedded in `ℝ^(p)` by some isometric transformation, both assumptions which may not hold for any particular data set. The assumption of convexity, however, has been challenged recently (see [Trosset](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000364) & Buyukbas, 2020 for details).

For all of its strengths, the problem with `Isomap` is that it first relies on finding “neighboring” distances on the manifold, which could be faulty. In seeking out the nearest neighbors, if `k` is too small, then the graph may not even be connected. If `k` is too large, then the graph may be too dense giving us wrong distances on the manifold. That is, finding the “right” `k`, if it exists at all, is quite hard. *Stochastic Neighborhood Embedding* (`SNE`), as we will soon see, attempts to address and solve some of these problems. Another issue with `Isomap`, as was true of `MDS` as well, is that neither approach learns a general mapping `function` that can be applied to new points. Hence, if a new data point is added to the original data point, the entire algorithm needs to generally be repeated to incorporate that point ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)).

> *A key difference between local linear embedding and Isomap is that LLE makes no effort to estimate the true geodesic distances, whereas Isomap does.*

## 11.15 Stochastic Neighborhood Embedding

Up to now, we have dealt with techniques that have a deterministic idea of what a “neighbor” is. That is, whether with nearest neighbor (`NN`) or k-nearest neighbor (`KNN`), or `Isomap`, when we designate `k`, what constitutes a “neighbor” is thus determined. As an example, the neighbor to your house or apartment is determined. If you use `k` = 1, then your *neighbor is your neighbor*, there is no doubt about that. *It is what it is*. This is what we mean by your neighbor being deterministically determined. You don’t look at the house next to yours and ask yourself the probability that that house is your neighbor. As we will see, however, this is precisely what stochastic neighborhood embedding does.

*Stochastic Neighborhood Embedding*, as the name suggests, imposes a *stochastic* element to what constitutes a neighbor. Recall that “stochastic” essentially means “random.” That is, if we specify `k` = 1 in `SNE`, then *your neighbor is not necessarily your neighbor*. Instead, the house a few feet away from you is *probably your neighbor*. That is, for every pair of points, a probability is assigned that they are neighbors such that close points are more likely (i.e., *probably*) to be neighbors than points that are more distant. That is, instead of *deterministically* determining neighbors, we instead seek a *stochastic neighborhood distribution*.

Stochastic neighbor embedding operates by translating high-dimensional Euclidean distances between data points into conditional probabilities that correspond to similarities ([van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000372)). How is similarity between data points evaluated? Similarity is transformed into a conditional probability that a given data point, `x[i]`, would choose `x[j]` as its neighbor if neighbors were selected in proportion to their probability density under a Gaussian centered at `x[i]`. If a data point is nearby, then `p[j|i]` will be relatively high, whereas for data points that are separated a great deal, `p[j|i]` will be increasingly smaller. The precise conditional probability we are modeling is given by the following:

$$p\_{j|i} = \\frac{\\exp\\left( - \\middle| \\middle| x\_{i} - x\_{j} \\middle| \\middle| {}\_{2}/2\\sigma\_{i}^{2} \\right)}{\\sum\\limits\_{k \\neq i}{\\exp\\left( - \\middle| \\middle| x\_{i} - x\_{k} \\middle| \\middle| {}\_{2}/2\\sigma\_{i}^{2} \\right)}}$$

(11.24)

where `x[i]` and `x[j]` are two distinct data points and `σ[i]²` is the variance from the familiar Gaussian distribution, which, as a tuning parameter, effectively sets the size of the given neighborhood and can be different for each point `x[i]`. Smaller values of `σ[i]²` will increase the probability that `x[i]` will choose nearby `x[j]` while larger values for `σ[i]²` will make that probability increasingly smaller since a higher `σ[i]²` implies more dispersion of points. The value for `σ[i]²` is determined by `SNE` conditional upon how the user sets another parameter known as the `perplexity`,

`perplexity(P[i])` = 2^( `H(P[i])` )

(11.25)

where `P[i]` is the probability distribution of a sample such that $P\_{i} = {\\sum\\limits\_{j}p\_{ij}}$ and where

$$H\\left( P\_{i} \\right) = - {\\sum\\limits\_{j}{p\_{j/i}\\log\_{2}}}p\_{j/i}$$

(11.26)

is the *Shannon entropy* of `P[i]` (Rodosthenous, Shahrezaei and Evangelou, [2024](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000305)). `H(P[i])` is a measure of *information gain* based on knowing `x[i]` − `x[j]`. The perplexity parameter `P[i]` can be considered the *effective number of neighbors*, which is usually chosen by the user in the range of 5 to 50 ([van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000372)).
