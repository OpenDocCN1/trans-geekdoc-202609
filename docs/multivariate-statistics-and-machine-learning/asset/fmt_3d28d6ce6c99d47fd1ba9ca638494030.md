
[Drton et al.](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000091) (2024) detail algebraically sparse factor analysis models as models that set or hypothesize some or many of the coefficients (i.e., `factor loadings`) equal to 0. Other approaches do not necessarily set weights equal to zero, but instead “encourage” ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)) them to equal 0 through regularization, such as an `l1` penalty (Choi et al., [2010](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000062)). Penalties of course have a Bayesian flavor in a sense because we are influencing the size of weights `a priori` and thus having a corresponding influence on prior probabilities. [Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. (2024)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000119) detail `sparse Bayesian factor analysis` for the case where the number of factors is unknown with what are known as `spike-and-slab priors` on the factor loading matrix. The use of such priors drives some factor loadings to 0, which allows the model to be identified. Setting weights to 0 to help identify a model is also a common analytical choice in the structural equation models of the following chapter.

# 13 Confirmatory Factor Analysis, Path Analysis, and Structural Equation Modeling

DOI: [10.4324/9781003376941-16](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003376941-16)

In the previous chapter, we surveyed the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) model and learned that in the classic factor analysis model, observed variables were being hypothesized as linear functions of unobserved latent variables. We also surveyed some of the very many derivatives and extensions of the classic EFA model, which included nonlinear factor analysis and other possibilities, which ranged from models emanating from statistics and social science to more modern advances in machine learning.

In the current chapter, we develop the full structural equation model, which can be said to consist of elements common to both factor analysis and regression. First, we consider the confirmatory version of factor analysis, appropriately called `confirmatory factor analysis` in which the researcher has a specific factor pattern or hypothesis they would like to test instead of conducting a purely exploratory search as in EFA. What a confirmatory test reduces to, on a technical level, is a more deliberate `fixing and freeing of model parameters` than in its exploratory counterpart. However, as we will see, distinctions between what makes a model exploratory vs. confirmatory can be quite blurry. We then introduce the structural equation model, which can be conceptualized as a series of (typically) linear equations, however nonlinear structural equation modeling is also a possibility and we briefly survey those as well.

The origins of structural equation modeling are twofold. The measurement model of SEM can be said to have begun with Charles [Spearman (1904)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000344), while the structural portion of SEM has its roots primarily with Sewall [Wright (1918)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000392) who developed the method of `path analysis`. Path analysis features networks among observed variables and hence is largely seen as a precursor, historically, to the arrival of structural equation models. Combining the measurement model with that of the structural components of path analysis gives rise to the full structural equation model. Jöreskog ([1970](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000176), [1973](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000177)) is usually credited with the first true SEM model ([Tarka, 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000353)) although researchers, especially in sociometrics, were running structural equation modeling in the 1950s and 60s. It was Jöreskog, however, who is primarily responsible for pioneering cutting-edge estimation methods for SEM.

## 13.1 What Makes a Model “Exploratory” vs. “Confirmatory”?

Exploratory factor analysis models are named as such because the user does not typically have any idea in advance regarding how many factors may result from the analysis. This is the `exploratory` nature of the model; the user is diving into the data without any preconceived `theory` as to the number of factors that may result. At first thought, this may seem like an unrealistic proposition, since researchers usually have at least `some` idea of what may come out of their factor analyses before conducting them. That is, the Bayesian prior is typically not `uniform`, even in exploratory analyses.480

The way to improve on the above exploratory idea of a factor analysis model is to have a `pre-conceived structure` of what to expect from the factor analysis. In this case, the researcher has a pre-established theory in mind and wishes to evaluate that specific hypothesized structure. `Confirmatory factor analysis` in this case is a viable option, where the model allows the researcher to virtually control and specify almost every parameter of the model. That is, the user can `fix`, `free`, or even `equate` estimated parameters with other model parameters and obtain measures of model fit assessing how well or how poorly her model stood up to the data.

A step above the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model is the full-blown structural equation model, which in addition to hypothesizing specific latent structures, also hypothesizes relationships among latent structures. For example, the SEM model allows a researcher to hypothesize one latent structure as a predictor of another, and many more possibilities. Many discussions of CFA in the literature are “housed” within the wider SEM framework, and hence it is for this reason that we focus primarily on SEM in this chapter. Confirmatory factor analysis is best understood as a special case of the wider SEM framework.
