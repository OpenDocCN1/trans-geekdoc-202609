
The algorithm essentially connects core points that are within `ε` (or “`eps`”) of one another, which is a flexible parameter that can be freely adjusted by the user. A choice for `ε` too large and what will result is a cluster with density equal to the number of cases. On the other hand, for `ε` set too small will have virtually all points essentially being their own cluster. Border points are then assigned to the cluster for which they have the highest `connectivity`, meaning that it is not sufficient on its own to be a core point (due to not meeting the `MinPts` criterion), so is classified to the cluster for which it is closest to a core point. The groups that result are the clusters and the remainder of the points are noise points. The precise steps of the algorithm are detailed in Schubert et al. ([2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000329), p. 3).

The `DBSCAN` algorithm can actually be viewed as a more robust generalization of the single-linkage method where instead of only detecting spherical clusters as in `k-means`, it can find arbitrarily shaped clusters ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)). `DBSCAN` can also be considered a modification of *single-linkage agglomerative clustering* by treating border and noise points with special consideration relative to other methods where they are not given any special treatment. That is, in classic 523single-linkage clustering, for instance, border and noise points have no meaning in such an algorithm, and hence are just more points around which to compute linkages. As noted by [Aggarwal (2015)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005), the benefit of `DBSCAN` over single linkage is that with the consideration of border and noise points, the sensitivity of outliers is diminished in addition to the algorithm being able to detect clusters of arbitrary shape rather than only spherical ones such as in `k-means` approaches. Recall that we said from the outset that one of the chief difficulties with clustering algorithms is that they often make prior assumptions about the shape of clusters (e.g., spherical in the case of `k-means`). `DBSCAN` is useful because it is not limited to detecting spherical or other pre-defined shapes. `DBSCAN` has also been found to have better cluster classification when used on smaller data sets.

## 14.12 Clustering via Mixture Models

The clustering algorithms we have surveyed thus far, those of `k-means` (and `k-representatives`), hierarchical clustering as well as density-based approaches such as `DBSCAN`, have made no assumptions about where the data have been sampled. That is, they have not made any *parametric assumptions* about the data or *prior assumptions* about the nature of the clusters (aside from assuming they must be spherical in shape in `k-means` or in dense regions as in `DBSCAN`), nor have they invoked anything to do with probability. They have simply relied on the empirical data to essentially tell the story of whether or not clusters might exist and have proceeded as such through hard-rule algorithmic processes of assigning cases to clusters.
