Contrary to what most believe about McCulloch and Pitts’ early intentions, they themselves did not hold too strongly to this *all-or-none* binary principle and acknowledged that the process could be continuous as well rather than only binary ([Abraham, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000003)). It is also worth noting that McCulloch and Pitts recognized from the outset that their neuronal model was likely *not* an exact representation of neuronal function. As noted by Abraham:

> Their goal in the paper was not to present a factual description of neurons, but rather to design “fictitious nets” composed of neurons whose connections and thresholds are unaltered … They noted emphatically that they were not arguing that any “formal” equivalence between their nets can be part of a “factual explanation” … in no way did McCulloch and Pitts claim their model was a true description of known networks.

> (pp. 20–21)

In this early depiction, or shall we say “idealization” of the nature of neuronal transmission, there are no adjustable parameters or weights ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167), p. 321). That is, each of `x[1]`, `x[2]`, ..., `x[p]` are not weighted by scalars but instead are simply summed, and if that sum exceeds a given threshold, then the signal is activated. To forecast further developments of the neuron and to be even more general, we could say that `x[1]`, `x[2]`, ..., `x[p]` are implicitly weighted, but by scalars of *one* for each input. However, these weights are not *adjustable*, and hence as noted by [Izenman (2008)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167), they were not a sufficient model for biological systems to “learn.” Forecasting to later developments up to the modern day neural network, it is the *adjustable* nature of these scalars that will constitute *learning* in modern neural networks. Of course, this idea of “adjustable scalars” is nothing new since virtually all statistical models beginning with regression in the 1890s featured adjustable weights and scalars. Adjusting the weights based on learning from the data is what statistical modeling, or statistical *learning*, has always been about since its inception. Optimizing weights is what learning actually *is*, from a statistical point of view.534

Hence, though the McCulloch and Pitts neuron was not thought to “mimic” brain activity *exactly*, it was a pretty good start and first stab at mathematically representing the communication between neurons. Computational advances would have to take place before one could seriously think of modeling neuronal activity. In the brain, there are approximately 86 billion neurons, many of which are interconnected and can stimulate other neurons ([Cheng & Titterington, 1994](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000059)). To mimic the parallel processing of neurons, *parallel processing* in computing power would have to arrive in full, which would have to wait until the 1980s. It is the computer that provides the capacity for neural networks and many other statistical and machine learning methods to actually become pragmatic for use. However, the mathematics underlying these and other models is distinct from computation. Mathematical studies of the perceptron continued with [Rosenblatt (1959)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000309) but it was [Novikoff (1963)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000269) who became more widely known for providing the conditions for convergence of the perceptron.

> *In the early McCulloch-Pitts neuron, though input values were summed and then subjected to a threshold “activation” function, none of the inputs were weights nor were they adjustable. Hence, there was no way, from a modern point of view, for the neuron to actually “learn.” At the time, regression models had long been on the scene since the 1890s and featured adjustable weight parameters.*

## 15.2 Rosenblatt’s Perceptron

Rosenblatt aimed to improve the MC neuron which meant applying weights to the inputs. His ideas were influenced by Donald Hebb’s book in 1949 called *The Organization of Behavior* ([Hebb, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000150)). The book conceptualized memory traces via sets of connected neurons which help determine future behavior. Such connections were referred to as *Hebb synapses*. As [Izenman (2008)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167) notes, Hebb’s book was a significant development in behavioral neuroscience even if its findings have been largely overturned since its writing. Hebb’s ideas motivated a large body of work.

Rosenblatt was not convinced that most neural connections were random and that cell assemblies could self-generate within a purely homogeneous mass of neurons. He believed instead that he could improve upon Hebb’s work and constructed a “minimally constrained” system he called the *perceptron* ([Rosenblatt, 1958](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000308), [1962](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000310)). A perceptron is nothing more than a McCulloch-Pitts neuron but *now with adjustable connection weights*. The magnitude of the weights communicates the strength of the connection. The perceptron is hence more flexible than the aforementioned MP neuron.

Recall that for the MP neuron, the neuron either fired or did not fire subject to the threshold or `heavyside` function (e.g., +1, −1). Rosenblatt’s perceptron is similar to the MP neuron, but with one crucial difference; the inputs `x[i]` are now associated with “connection weights,” `β[1] ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ β[p]`, where positive weights `β[p] > 0` are *excitatory* synapses, and negative weights `β[p] < 0` are *inhibitory* synapses. These weights could then be summed to obtain the overall *action* of the neuron or the strength of the connection ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167), p. 322).

Does all this sound familiar? Of course it does. It sounds very much like regression analysis where `β[1] ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ β[p]` are simply partial regression weights. As with the MP neuron, the sum of values in Rosenblatt’s perceptron is computed and then subjected to the threshold binary logic. The difference, of course, is that the sum this time consists of weighted values of `x[i]`, that is, $S = {\sum\limits_{j}\beta_{j}}x_{j}$. Thus, it is easy to see that the MP neuron is a special case of the perceptron where, as previously mentioned, `β[1] ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ β[p]` are all understood to be unity such that

$$S = {\sum\limits_{j}\beta_{j}}x_{j} = {\sum\limits_{j}\left( 1 \right)}x_{j} = {\sum\limits_{j}x_{j}}$$

or other fixed constant. A final component to the perceptron is the inclusion of what is known in neural networks as a *bias element*, `β[0]`, which is nothing more than the familiar *intercept* in regression.
