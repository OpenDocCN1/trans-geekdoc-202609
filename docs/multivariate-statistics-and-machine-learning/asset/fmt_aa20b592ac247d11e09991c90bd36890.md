
## 2.4 Probability Fundamentals

Regardless of the interpretation of probability adopted, virtually all probability theorists agree on the fundamental mathematics of probability. That is, while the nature of probability (i.e., frequentist vs. subjectivist) is and has been up for debate for literally centuries, the essential mathematics underlying fundamental probability are usually not contested, except perhaps by true rebels and nonconformists! Hence, in what follows in our brief presentation of mathematical probability, there are no true “debates.” Most are “on board” with these basic notions and definitions. Much of modern probability is based on the *theory of sets*, which we do not review here, except as it applies directly to the concepts we do present.

The probability of the event `A` is denoted `P(A)`, and is a number between 0 and 1:

`0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1`

(2.2)

The *complement* of `A` denoted $\\overline{A}$ is the probability that `A` does not occur. Since the probability of the entire sample space, by definition (and convenience) must equal 1, this implies that

$$P\\left( \\overline{A} \\right) = 1 - P\\left( A \\right)$$

(2.3)

The *joint probability*, that is, of `A` and `B` occurring at the same time is denoted

`P(A∩B)`

(2.4)

If `A` and `B` cannot occur simultaneously, then

`P(A∩B) = 0`

(2.5)

and we say the two events `A` and `B` are *disjoint* or *mutually exclusive*. Examples of mutually exclusive events include the events “head” and “tail” on the flipping of a single coin since one cannot obtain a head and a tail on the same flip.

To know if two events are mutually exclusive, one must know about the *context* or *physical model* of the problem. For example, the events “heads” and “tails” are not *intrinsically* and *organically* mutually exclusive events; they are only so under the model of a *single flip*. For successive flips, they are not typically mutually exclusive. The only absolutely necessary criterion for mutually exclusive events is as stated in (2.5), that of `P(A∩B) = 0`, and hence so long as this condition 65is satisfied, even in the abstract, the two events are mutually exclusive. Knowledge of temporal conditions of the physical model imposed serves to help us know whether the condition is met.

Why is this point so important? Why are we going on about it? The reason is that physical models (which include *temporal sequences* as a special case) are special cases or circumstances of a more general “real-world” context. Abstract mathematics (or philosophy, for that matter) does not require such a basis in reality. To emphasize the point, if the aliens come one day and have no conception of time, the concept of mutual exclusiveness would still be “communicable” by simply drawing the idea in [Figure 2.1](#c2.xhtml#f2.1) with Venn diagrams on a sheet of paper.

Knowing whether mutual exclusiveness applies to the objects under study must come from the *context*. If there is only one flip of the coin, head and tail are mutually exclusive events. If there are two flips, they are not. *Context matters*. Everything happens in a given context; this includes scientific, mathematical, statistical, and machine learning matters. This “theme” will return time and time again throughout this book and it is worth emphasizing and repeating here:

> Context Matters!

### 2.4.1 Unions and Intersections of Events

The probability of `A` or `B` occurring on a given trial is denoted by their union,

`P(A∪B)`

(2.6)

and is equal to the sum of probabilities of `A` and `B` minus their intersection,

`P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A∩B)`

(2.7)

where `P(A∩B)` defines the probability of the intersection.

It is easy to see that for mutually exclusive events, where `P(A∩B) = 0`, the union of `A` and `B` is equal to66

$$\\begin{array}{l} {P\\left( A \\cup B \\right) = P\\left( A \\right) + P\\left( B \\right) - P\\left( A \\cap B \\right)} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = P\\left( A \\right) + P\\left( B \\right) - 0} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = P\\left( A \\right) + P\\left( B \\right)} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

This is sometimes referred to as the *special rule for the addition of probabilities*, that is, where the intersection is equal to 0 and we can simply compute the union by adding probabilities. To be on the safe side, it is good advice to always plan on subtracting an intersection from the addition to ensure for yourself that there is no overlap. It is obvious and easily provable that the intersection of a number of sets must be a subset of their union as the overlap, no matter how large, cannot be larger than the sum of all sets. The reader may recall that a *subset* can consist of the entire set, whereas a *proper subset* must be smaller than the full set.

## 2.5 Conditional Probability

Most phenomena in science are not about probabilities; they are instead about *conditional probabilities*. We rarely care about the probability of an event occurring; we care more about the probability of an event occurring, *given something else has already occurred*. This “conditional” statement translates to a reduction in the original sample space from what it originally was for the unconditional probability estimate. The probability of cancer is interesting, but the probability of cancer *given that you do or do not smoke two packs of cigarettes per day* is much more interesting. Virtually every problem and finding in science, no matter how simple or complex the model or lack thereof, can be translated into a statement of conditional probabilities.

Conditional probabilities add the aforementioned “context” to the problem since unconditional probabilities, while often accurate in and of themselves, can be grossly misleading. In passing, this is precisely why some detest statistics, because depending on what other factors or conditions are imposed on a problem, one can draw one of many conclusions. For instance, while the probability of perishing from COVID-19 may be 0.05, the probability of one dying given that one is an otherwise healthy 30-year-old is likely significantly less. Unconditional probabilities are, in a sense, “averages” across objects and conditions, and as we will discuss later, averages can be grossly misleading. Conditional probabilities are specialized *focused slices* within that spectrum. They help us narrow in on the probability estimate of most interest and provide that necessary context to the problem. The difficulty is that in many or most problems, there is no theoretical limit to how much context can be included which makes arriving at the “true” conditional probability in many situations very difficult or even impossible. At worst, a lack of context will simply make the probability estimate unmeaningful and very misleading. The probability of a human being currently eating baby formula is very low. It’s a silly probability estimate because we have not conditioned it on age and thus have not provided proper context to the problem. The probability estimate is accurate. The lack of context to the problem is foolish and makes the unconditional probability estimate *uninteresting*.
