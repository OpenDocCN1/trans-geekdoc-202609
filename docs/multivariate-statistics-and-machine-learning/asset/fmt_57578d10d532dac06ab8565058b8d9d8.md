
By a Bayesian approach, that model should be the one that maximizes `p(m|D)`, where `m` is now the model and `D` is, as before, the data:

$$p\\left( m \\middle| D \\right) = \\frac{p\\left( D \\middle| m \\right)p\\left( m \\right)}{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}{p\\left( D \\middle| m \\right)p\\left( m \\right)}}$$

(2.18)

We recognize in the above the quantity `p(D|m)`, which, when unpacked, is equal to

`p(D|m) = ∫p(D|θ,m)p(θ|m)dθ`

and is called the *marginal likelihood* (Murphy, 2022). If `p(D|m)` holds strong under different settings of the parameter `θ`, then `m` is likely to be considered a reasonable model. The key to the marginal likelihood is the summing (via integration) across a variety of parameter values (`θ`). This is why it is called “marginal,” as a result of the summation over all values of `θ`. If it were for a particular value of `θ = θ[i]`, then it would be “conditional” instead of marginal. Under the condition of two competing models `m[1]` and `m[2]`, should `p(D|θ̂[1],m[1])` and `p(D|θ̂[2],m[2])` both be large, Occam’s razor requires us to select the model that is simplest. Marginal likelihoods then help us select a suitably complex model, which has analogies to cross-validation and other model selection guidelines in a frequentist context (see [Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259), pp. 179–180 for details). However, 74marginal likelihoods can be difficult to compute in practice because they require integrating over the entire parameter space ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). The *Bayesian Information Criterion* (`BIC`) can be considered a kind of approximation to the log marginal likelihood, which, as we will see later in the book, is used in many contexts, including regression and cluster density estimation, among others.

### 2.10.3 Bayes’ Theorem as Rational Belief or Theorizing

Bayes’ theorem is not a trivial idea. Far from it. By itself, Bayes’ theorem tells us how, as *rational agents*, we should update our belief about `T` in light of new `D`, where `T` is nothing more than a belief or theory and `D` is nothing more than experience in its fullest generality. Bayes’ theorem is not a statistical idea; first and foremost it is a *philosophical* one. Bayesian inference essentially tells us how a rational agent *should* update their belief in light of new experience. “Should” equates to being *rational*. It is not equivalent to how human beings *actually* update their beliefs. Indeed, much psychological research has been conducted on human subjects to demonstrate that human beings are far from being Bayesian.

As one might imagine, this idea has made Bayes central to not only statistics, but also other areas where the study of rational behavior is of primary interest. The application of Bayes’ theorem, whether formal or informal, is a way of being “intelligent” about updating one’s belief. *Game theory*, *psychology of beliefs*, *economic theory*, etc., are all areas that, in the end, study human behavior. Sub-specialties of these domains have interests in learning whether human beings update their beliefs in correspondence with what Bayesian theory would prescribe. The *Bayes estimator* tells us which action to take, if acting *rationally*, to minimize the risk of making a wrong decision. Maximizing expectation, however, is not always equivalent to maximizing *utility*, and hence Bayesian decision theory will often have us maximize the latter instead of the former, except if the problem is relatively simple and both expectation and utility coincide. Utility, simply put, is a measure of how beneficial this or that action is to the agent, not simply evaluated based on probability or mathematical expectation. Not buying that overpriced post-pandemic McDonald’s hamburger may maximize budgeting constraints, but buying it because it tastes *so good* maximizes utility. A fiscally poor and irrational choice, but has much utility for the buyer and thus becomes rational from that perspective. Utility theory is a fascinating area of investigation. For an excellent discussion, consult [Winkler (2003)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000388).

While Bayes’ theorem is generally useful, it should be remarked that it could introduce bias and discrimination into the process of updating belief. For example, the high probability of a black person committing a crime may be justified by reference to Bayes’ theorem because for a given crime, past crimes may have featured a disproportionate number of blacks, causing the prior probability to skyrocket. Bayes’ theorem is rational updating of belief, but it may be considered discrimination to use it in this particular *context*. Likewise, the probability of a mass in a dog being cancerous may be high upon application of Bayes’ theorem, given the age and breed of the dog. While Bayes’ theorem is doing its work, it can also be terrifying that a dog is put down because of their breed and without the result of a biopsy. The prior probability makes a cancer diagnosis more likely, perhaps even highly probable, but if it’s a decision of *life vs. death* for the dog, putting the dog down on the “gamble” that it is cancer is an unwise action. *The cost of making an error in this regard may be too high, even if the posterior probability is exceedingly high*. *Bayes theorem can only estimate the probability of the theory; it cannot, by itself, tell you how to act*, *nor is a posterior probability ever equal to unity in most real-world applications*. Further, it must always be remembered that we do not know anything about the current event; we only know about *past events*, and the prior probability in Bayes’ theorem, even if estimated correctly, is not about the current event under consideration. *Calculate Bayes’ theorem with reckless abandon, but use, and most importantly* 75***interpret Bayes’ theorem with caution*** . As a result of the prior probability potentially having an overwhelming influence on the posterior, work has been done on what are called *noninformative priors* ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)), which, as the name suggests, attempts to reduce the influence of the prior.

We will revisit Bayes’ theorem often in this book, such as when determining the optimal decision boundary when discussing *discriminant analysis*, or when considering *Naïve Bayes* as a nonparametric alternative to classification. The idea of including prior information in a model is not new and can be witnessed throughout statistics and science, as well as more modernly with machine learning. For instance, a new paradigm in machine learning called *physics-informed machine learning* has the goal of informing machine learning models using physically relevant prior information (Pateras et al., [2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000282)). That is, the model or algorithm is “informed” by prior knowledge of the object or system that is being modeled.

## 2.11 Law of Large Numbers

The law of large numbers is a fundamental law in statistics that in words essentially says *the larger the sample size, the closer one gets to the true probability of an event*. As a simple example, getting 9 heads out of 10 trials yields an estimate of the probability of heads equal to 9/10 = 0.90. The estimate of the probability of heads is not that great in this instance because it is based on only a few observations, that is, a relatively small sample. As sample size `n` increases, the approximation gets better, until in the limit as `n`→∞, the true probability is known. The limit, in this case, infinity ∞, is never achieved. The true probability, at least in this frequentist perspective, will never be known. All we really have are estimates. The bigger the sample size, in general, the better the estimate.
