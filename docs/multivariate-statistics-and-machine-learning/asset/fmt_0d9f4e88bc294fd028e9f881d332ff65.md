Throughout much of this book, we have surveyed and previewed neural networks while in the midst of discussing other techniques. This was done intentionally to illustrate and emphasize the extent to which other models could be understood as *related* or even as *special cases* of the more general neural network design. Indeed, this was one of the major objectives of this book, to “demystify” neural networks and other models by relating them to similar methods. As with any new statistical or machine learning model the reader may come across, it is helpful, in fact virtually *essential* in understanding them to relate them to other techniques. This is one reason why introductory books and courses on statistics and machine learning should emphasize *foundations* at the expense of surveying every method or algorithm under the sun. If students properly learn foundations, they are then in a good position to generalize and extend on that knowledge as needed later in their careers. Without a grounding in foundations, however, one risks not only misunderstanding additional methods, one also risks misusing and misinterpreting those same methods. *Foundations are always the key to a good education*.

In this chapter, we finally arrive at the full neural network and unpack it in its full generality. In our treatment, our hope is that the reader will appreciate more fully how neural networks are related to other statistical procedures but also why they have essentially revolutionized data analysis in the last 20–30 years. We begin the chapter with a brief survey of the motivation behind the rise of neural networks. We then introduce what a neural network actually *is*, emphasizing that even a simple linear regression can be regarded as a simple single-layer feedforward neural network. Based on this foundation, we then inspect more closely the nature of `activation functions`, a concept that is central to neural networks, surveying different types of activation functions and differentiating between `saturating` vs. `non-saturating` functions. We then generalize the feedforward network to the multilayer perceptron, which is the most common neural network in use today. Incidentally, in our discussion of neural networks we do not strongly distinguish between what is a `neural network` vs. what is a `perceptron`, and at times use the terms interchangeably. However, due to their history, the term `perceptron` is usually reserved for neural networks having discontinuous step-function nonlinearities as hidden units ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)) while classic neural networks are typically understood to have continuous nonlinearities in the hidden units. So long as this distinction is appreciated, there is no harm in using the terms interchangeably as authors sometimes do.

## 15.1 The Rise of Neural Networks: Original Motivation

Artificial neural networks are often what first comes to mind when we think of modern machine learning. The origins of machine learning as it pertains to artificial neural networks dates back to at least psychologist Rosenblatt and his work on the human nervous system, developing a machine for recognizing the letters of the alphabet. This “machine,” he called a `perceptron`. The 532McCulloch-Pitts neuron was featured in the seminal publication “*A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity*” in 1943, which is often considered to be one of the initial “seeds” of the artificial intelligence movement that is in full bloom today. [Abraham (2002)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000003) details the historical origins of this important publication as well as the contributors to it. Beginning in the 1930s, the mathematical description of the brain was becoming an important topic and these pioneers were of the earliest to apply mathematics in developing such networks.

Drawing on Turing’s work at the time, which was itself groundbreaking, McCulloch and Pitts reasoned that the task of computation could be described *logically* in a finite number of steps, and that Turing’s “machine” could be perceived as a “logic machine” ([Abraham, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000003)). Of course, from today’s perspective, these developments are obvious as key contributions to the modern algorithm and discipline of machine learning more widely, but at the time these realizations were quite novel. As well summarized by [Abraham (2002)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000003):

> What distinguished the McCulloch-Pitts neural network from previous concepts of networks was its basis in axioms and definitions, and their use of such axioms to construct a logical calculus of the relations between neurons … McCulloch’s and Pitts’s goal was to represent the functional relationships between neurons in terms of Boolean logic: to embody the mental function of reasoning in the actual physiology of the brain.

> (pp. 19–20)
