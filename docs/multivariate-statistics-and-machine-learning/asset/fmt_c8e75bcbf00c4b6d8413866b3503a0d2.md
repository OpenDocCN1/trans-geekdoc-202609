## 10.7 Boosting

Boosting is another committee machine that again essentially operates on combining weak learners, analogous to how bagging averages many bootstrapped samples. However, boosting does this *systematically* and *deterministically*, not randomly. As explained by James et al. ([2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168), p. 321) in reference to boosting with regression trees:

> Each tree \[or single predictive tool, more generally\] is grown using information from previously grown trees. Boosting does not involve bootstrap sampling; instead, each tree is fit on a modified version of the original data set.

Boosting, in general, is what is known as a *slow learner*, which in general, performs better than fast or greedy learners. An analogy to boosting would be you and your friend drawing a picture. Suppose you get the picture started and your strengths are on full display, but also your weaknesses. For instance, suppose you are good at drawing the general shapes of objects, but not so good at filling in some of their more specific characteristics. After you are finished doing what you can, your friend steps in and focuses specifically on drawing the elements that you were poor at drawing, including giving specific attention to errors that you made in your contribution. The combination of both of you generates a rather strong painting even though you were both weak at drawing particular individual elements of the painting.

Another prototype example for understanding how boosting works is the spam e-mail paradigm described by Izenman ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167), p. 511). Suppose we wish to know whether an e-mail is spam or not before opening it. If the e-mail contains the word “money,” this by itself is a weak identifier because identifying it as spam might appear as random and misclassify the result. In other words, this is a *weak classifier*. Indeed, another classifier that is weak is one that classifies as spam if the e-mail contains the word “credit,” for instance. Taken on their own, weak classifiers are not very good and are bound to misclassify many cases. However, as was the case for bagging, *if we combine these weak classifiers, we may be able to substantially improve classification*. This is what is behind the idea of boosting. In this example, classification based on the word “money” will be imperfect and rather weak, but the second classifier trained will try to make up for the error generated by the first classifier. When combined, they make a pretty decent classifier. Boosting relies on the assumption that each of the weak hypotheses has accuracy that is just a bit better than chance levels. This assumption is generally referred to as the *weak learning condition* ([Schapire, 2001](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000325)).

We draw on James et al. ([2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168), p. 323) quite heavily as well as Bishop ([2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032), p. 658) in giving a formal, if not brief, summary treatment of boosting. Suppose in a regression tree problem *f̂*(*x*) is the estimated function. At the initial step of the algorithm, we will set *f̂*(*x*) = 0 and residuals *r*[*i*] = *y*[*i*], that is, residuals equal to the values of the response variable in the training data. We then fit a regression tree with *d* splits to the training data. We call this tree *f̂*^(*b*). We then update the original function *f̂*(*x*) = *f̂*(*x*) + *λ**f̂*(*x*) with a shrunken (weighted) version of the new tree:

*f̂*(*x*) = *f̂*(*x*) + *λ**f̂*^(*b*)(*x*)  

(10.5)

where in (10.5) *λ* is a shrinkage parameter regulating the rate of learning, which notice is being applied to the new tree, *f̂*^(*b*)that has *d* splits. Next, we update residuals by389

*r*[*i*′] = *r*[*i*] − *λ**f̂*^(*b*)(*x*[*i*])  

(10.6)

This process is repeated for all *b* models and the final boosted model is produced, which is the sum of *all* weighted estimated functions, *f̂*^(*b*),

$$\\hat{f}\\left( x \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{b = 1}^{B}{\\lambda{\\hat{f}}^{b}\\left( x \\right)}}$$  

(10.7)

Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 341) show that boosting is a simple example of an additive model and yet another instance of a *basis expansion*. There are many boosting algorithms in existence. However, one particular algorithm has historically dominated boosting, that of `AdaBoost`, short for *adaptive boosting* and generally used to classify into one of two binary categories ([Schapire, 2001](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000325)). The algorithm works by modifying probabilities of drawing instances as a function of error similar to the generic boosting algorithm described earlier ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)). AdaBoost has been found to be equivalent to forward stagewise additive modeling where an *exponential loss* function is minimized ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). Gradient boosting, and extensions, are more general than AdaBoost where additional loss functions can be minimized. See [Izenman (2008)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167) for details.
