
A great teacher once said, *geniuses chunk information, that’s what makes them so smart*. Contrary to how undergraduates (and beginning graduate students) often perceive their professors, no person, even those running around with PhDs, can know *everything* (some even know very little!). But the smart ones *know where to look*. They know which “mental folder” to open and pursue. What is even more vitally important is that they will have a sense of when they are right or wrong. They will also get a sense of when the answer is unachievable or uncertain. The maturity to achieve these milestones is no small feat and is gained through experience. The undergraduate knows a lot, but the seasoned scholar is much more tentative with her own knowledge. *Knowing when you don’t know is probably the most important of all knowledge.*

The philosophy adopted in this book is that it is far better to learn and understand a *few things* really well than to try to learn *everything*, which as mentioned, is always impossible for anyone. With the explosion of statistical learning and such in the current climate, it can be tempting for a book writer to present a “catalogue” of methods, a wealth of “facts” the reader can access anytime they wish. And though, as mentioned, this approach has its place and there are a number of excellent books that already attempt this, for an introductory text or university course a better approach would be to emphasize the more common and *foundational principles*, the rest of which become derivatives and extensions or special cases of these majestic pillar areas. Such is the approach taken in this book. Though our approach is generally “top-down” in that we seek to give the reader a bird’s eye view of what is going on in statistics and machine learning, we prioritize communicating the *foundations* upon which most other topics one could come across are then “learnable.” Without the foundations in place, one’s understanding of new things has no legs to stand on. *Good education starts with strong foundations*.

## 0.6.1 Essential Philosophy of Science and History

Understanding the mathematics of multivariate statistics and machine learning is insufficient for the scientist. One must also have a grounding in the scientific *context* in which these tools are used. Ask the average student in the sciences (or surprisingly, even in mathematics) what philosophy is, and you will probably get some version of pop culture’s interpretation similar to the response you would get if you asked the same question regarding the nature of psychology (“oh yeah, I’ve heard of Freud”). However, closer to truth and the position advanced in this book (and my other books) is that *philosophical thinking is the basis upon which all other knowledge is derived or created*. All of the concepts, statistical and mathematical formulas that we will see in this book have *foundations*. Those foundations are conceptual and very deep. Concepts, even “rigorous” ones do not arise or evolve as concrete objects. Instead, they are first “mushy” and imperfect. There is perhaps no better example of this than the evolution of calculus and the idea of *limits*. The limit concept was not “invented” by Newton or Leibnitz, but instead evolved over a couple thousand years. The great inventors helped “finished the job” as it were (along with several mathematicians afterward), but it should always be understood that they completed a project that was in the works for a very long time. So it is with even advanced statistics and machine learning. The foundations to these “modern” tools often go back centuries. Knowledge is a *progression*, it usually does not simply arrive “on the scene” without prior development.

Why is understanding and appreciating philosophy of science so important and relevant for the scientist? Consider the issue of causation. Statistical analysis could never reveal causation. Causation, whatever it may mean in the first place (for centuries, philosophers have debated the issue), concerns itself mostly with *research design*, at least in experimental science. Further, methods such as neural networks, at least historically, have been more focused on simply getting predictions and classifications “right” without feeling the need to know “why” those predictions are accurate. Analysts using neural networks have more or less been satisfied with the unknown function remaining a “black box” as it were. In many areas, we’re not trying to necessarily create a mathematical “causal” model as one would, in say, biology, but instead are simply trying to create an algorithm that will predict or classify successfully and minimize error and misclassifications in doing so. One is more tolerant of the prediction equation being a “black box” than in sciences where explaining the predictors is of paramount importance. For instance, understanding what factors predict the orbit of a satellite is not simply a black box problem. We have to know “why” so that we understand variations in trajectories.

Consider how a student might learn statistics or machine learning in a typical course. They perhaps take a course on the subject, study the materials used in the course, and then most likely prove results associated with those objects or apply those objects to empirical problems often using software. Presumably, one can learn what correlation *is* through its formulaic definition just as one can learn what a *group* is in abstract algebra and then go on to utilize that concept in solving problems. All this is fine and good, but a more *thorough* and *deeper* understanding of these topics can be achieved by studying their *history*. Pearson’s correlation is not merely a formula in a textbook. Understanding correlation means appreciating and understanding where it came from, how it evolved, and ultimately how it revolutionized both statistics and science. This can be said to be true of virtually all statistical and mathematical objects.

Indeed, the seeming infinite regress that occurs when one begins questioning, as though one is a child, eventually brings one to philosophical and historical inquiries. What you typically see in textbooks, including the current one you are reading, is the current “state of the art,” of a discipline. But beneath that state of the art is a long history of *ideas*, *concepts*, *controversies* that culminated in the “polished” object you currently see in your textbook. Pragmatically, there is a need to cut that search off at some point, but if you question further about the objects you are learning you will eventually become a philosopher and historian, because, quite simply, there is *nowhere else to go to learn of the true foundations*. As we will emphasize throughout this book, *true understanding is found in the foundations*, though we stop well short of examining historical and philosophical underpinnings. The point we make is merely for the student to recognize the importance of understanding statistics and machine learning in proper historical and epistemological *context* and to be aware that these directions can be pursued, should one choose well beyond this book.
