
$$\\lim\\limits\_{n\\rightarrow\\infty}\\left( {E\\left( \\hat{\\theta} \\right) - \\theta} \\right) = 0$$

where in the above recall that `E(θ̂) - θ` is the degree of bias present. As `n` increases, bias increasingly disappears, and is nonexistent in the limit. That is, `E(θ̂) = θ` in the limit as `n`→∞.

The *variance* of an estimator is a measure of how much the estimate would change if we changed or perturbed the data. That is, in repeated sampling where we experience random fluctuations in sample data, the variance of an estimator captures how much the estimate changes as a result of this fluctuation. We define the variance of an estimator as:

`Var[θ̂] = E(θ̂²) - (E(θ̂))²`

(2.53)

It seems intuitive to conclude that favorable estimators are those that have minimal to no bias while simultaneously having minimal variance. In most cases, this is the ideal to shoot for. However, as we will see later in the book, such estimators are not always most favorable for all situations. There are times where purposely allowing bias into an estimator is actually a “good thing” if it helps reduce variance. Of course, the most ideal situation would be to both reduce variance and reduce bias, but this is usually an unattainable goal. As bias decreases, we usually experience an upshoot in variance, and as variance decreases, an upshoot in bias. This *trade-off* is well known in statistical circles and not surprisingly goes by the name of the *bias-variance trade-off*. Biased estimators include those of the *ridge* and *lasso*, which are detailed later in the book. For now, we unpack in more detail the bias-variance trade-off.

## 2.26 Bias-Variance Trade-Off

We just mentioned that two things we need to be concerned about regarding an estimator are its bias and variance. We can unpack this idea into the following fundamental identity:

`E(y[0] - f̂(x[0]))² = Var(f̂(x[0])) + [Bias(f̂(x[0]))]² + Var(ε)`

(2.54)

where `E(y[0] - f̂(x[0]))²` is the expected test mean squared error (`MSE`) at the value of the predictor, `x[0]`. It is important to realize here that the variance and bias in this context refer to the *statistical learning method or model*, not the data. That is, we are not interested in the variance of the sample data in this context; we are interested instead in the variance generated by using such and such statistical *methodology* or *model estimate*. Variance is how much the estimated function, `f̂`, would 96change if a different training data set was used, or if the current one was “perturbed,” and bias is how “off” the learning method is in actually capturing the true function, `f(x[0])`. The more flexible the method, the higher the variance, while the lower the bias. The relative change of these two quantities will influence test `MSE`. As flexibility increases, bias initially decreases faster than the variance increases ([James et al., 2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)). However, beyond this initial state, an increase in flexibility impacts the bias much less but instead increases the variance, at which point test `MSE` likewise increases. The objective is to have both low variance and low bias as much as possible; however, a reduction in bias often results in an increase in variance, and vice versa.

### 2.26.1 Is Irreducible Error Really Irreducible?

It is typically held that variance of a statistical method, `Var(f̂(x))`, can be mitigated or reduced; however, `Var(ε)` is considered to be *irreducible variance*, which implies it cannot be mitigated and we are essentially “stuck” with it. That is, `Var(ε)` is sometimes regarded as a *constant* that cannot be decreased. But is `Var(ε)` truly irreducible? No! Yes, we can find a better function to fit, that is, `f̂(x)` to better approximate the “truth” in the form of `f(x)`, which will get us closer to `E(y - f̂(x[0]))² = 0`, but we can also reduce `ε` via *measurement*, or at minimum, make `ε` less *variable*. It is only “irreducible” in the sense that it may be independent of the function one is fitting, but it is not irreducible in the sense of simply reducing the overall squared error. That is, `ε` exists not solely as a result of not fitting the “correct” function or using a better statistical learning technique, but can decrease simply by an improvement in *measurement*. Reducing `Var(ε)` becomes a realistic possibility.

Are we theoretically able to get `ε` completely to zero? Probably not, but such will depend on what we consider `ε` to actually “be” at a deeper philosophical level. If one believes in *true chance* or *true error*, then it may be impossible to get error down to zero and we will always have at least *some* fluctuation. As noted in James et al. ([2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168), p. 19), “the irreducible error will always provide an upper bound on the accuracy of our prediction for `Y`. This bound is almost always unknown in practice.” While this is true, our point is merely that this does not take into consideration whether error can *theoretically* ever go to zero. More formally, we may write,

`lim[Var(ε) → 0] = E(y - ŷ)² = [f(x) - f̂(x)]²`

That is, as `Var(ε)`→0 in the limit, we simply obtain the squared difference between the observed `f(x)` and estimated `f̂(x)` functions (note that we dropped `Var(ε)`). This “ideal” is theoretically possible as we find ways to decrease `ε` and its `Var(ε)`. While this is a hugely interesting topic on a psychometric and philosophical level, we will not explore the issue further here.

## 2.27 Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Maximum likelihood is, historically, the most common method of estimating parameters in a variety of statistical models and contexts. It is due primarily to [Fisher (1922)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000099) though in concept at least, its origins and germination span several hundred years before Fisher ([Stigler, 1986](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000348)). The idea of maximum likelihood is very simple. Suppose you have observed data in the form of a sample. Maximum likelihood asks the question:97

> *What values of parameters should we choose, that would maximize the likelihood function of such data?*

Note carefully that the data have already been observed. The question we are asking is what value for parameters of interest *would have made such data most likely*?

As a simple example, consider flipping a coin ten times and observing five heads. The five heads constitute the observed data. What parameter value for the probability of heads would have given this observed result a “maximum likelihood” of occurring? The answer, of course, is the probability of heads equal to 0.5, or, more succinctly, `p(H) = 0.5`. An alternative parameter value such as `p(H) = 0.4` would have made the data *less likely* than `p(H) = 0.5`, and hence `θ̂ = 0.5` is the maximum likelihood estimator in this case. The estimates for the parameter values (e.g., `p(H) = 0.5`) are referred to as *maximum likelihood estimates*.

More formally, the maximum likelihood estimator, or `MLE`, is defined by

$$\\left. {\\hat{\\theta}}\_{mle} \\triangleq \\underset{\\theta}{\\arg\\max}p\\text{(}D \\middle| \\theta\\text{)} \\right.$$

(2.55)

which simply expresses the argument value (i.e., `argmax`) that makes the data `D` most likely given the parameter value, `θ`. That is, maximum likelihood (`ML`) provides which value for `θ` makes `p(D|θ)` (the *likelihood function*) most probable. The logarithm of a function is a monotonically increasing function, which means that whether we take the likelihood or the log of the likelihood essentially amounts to the same thing in terms of assessing the size of likelihoods. For technical reasons, and for convenience, taking the log is useful, and hence the log-likelihood is given by

`log L(θ) = log p(D/θ)`

(2.56)
