To extend on the above discussion, *statistical regularities* often make extremely risky engagement seem less so. For example, consider once more airline travel. Is airline travel risky? Is it dangerous? Of course it is! Traveling at greater than 30,000 feet in what is essentially still an experimental capsule is a risky endeavor. Yet, from a statistical point of view driving to the supermarket in your car is riskier. How we qualify risk changes depending on the perspective we adopt. A car traveling on a highway at 65 mph is less risky than an airplane traveling at 30,000 feet going 400 mph, but not *statistically*.

Statistical regularities are well known for reducing our *perception of risk*, but this is only so in the sense of past behavior. That is, if an airline flight is similar to most other airplane flights before it, then statistically it comes with little risk. However, despite the modeling of your flight today as having come from a distribution of past flights, we simply have no knowledge of the current event. *It is entirely new*. It is entirely possible (though highly improbable) that most flights from now on go on to crash; we simply do not know as we have not yet observed these new instances. All we have for certain are probabilities which we apply to new data under the *assumption* that such data are being drawn from similar probability distributions as past events.

Hence, one might say that the flight at 30,000 feet is risky in *absolute* terms, but statistically, it is much less so. In statistical parlance, the flight is *relatively* risk-free because the Bayesian prior of it being successful is quite high (we will discuss Bayesian statistics shortly). However, philosophically, the prior tells us nothing about the actual new physical event. It only models its probability under the *assumption* that the new event was drawn from a distribution made up of similar prior events.

### 1.10.1 Beck Depression Inventory: How Depressed Are You?
Risk is of course also prevalent in the diagnosing of clinical disorders. Suppose as a clinician you administer the *Beck Depression Inventory* (*BDI*) to a new client and that client scores in the range of mild depression. The BDI is a well-known screener for depression. A good clinician knows that no sole diagnostic criteria should be used to diagnose a client. However, what does a high BDI score signify? *It does not signify the disorder*. It instead can only indicate a “sign” of the disorder and classic Bayes reasoning needs to be applied to obtain the actual probability of depression. If the client’s prior probability of the disorder is low, then a high BDI score may simply not be enough to indicate or diagnose depression. This is a basic principle of revising probabilities, yet it has been shown time and time again that such basics are not understood or appreciated by many who diagnose medical and clinical disorders. Patients’ lives have been turned upside down due to inaccurate reporting of HIV test results, for instance. This is more than a triviality; it is of vital importance. *A failure to communicate statistical results properly to a clientele is equivalent to malpractice. Patients and customers have a right to be told accurate probability estimates and given guidance on how to interpret them.*

## 1.11 Decision, Risk Analysis, and Optimization
The above discussion of risk and decision-making quite naturally ties into ideas of formal optimization in mathematics and statistics. In many methods featured in this book, the objective, from an optimization point of view is to *minimize* or *maximize* some function, which in the end is essentially translated into minimizing the risk of making a wrong decision with as little error as possible. The area of study called *risk analysis* or the related area of *decision analysis* are fascinating disciplines of study and permeate many applied areas of practice. In the end, many statistical analyses are performed with the end goal of making a *good decision*. However, few if any decisions come without risk. Though one can argue there are, in theory, perfect decisions, they are only “perfect” in the sense of being rigorously thought out and rationally conceived. They all contain consequences and costs of being wrong, at least in the short term.

Every day we are confronted with decisions and we make them with imperfect knowledge. Whether we formally conduct a decision analysis on such decisions or intuitively try to balance the risk of making a wrong decision with the benefit of making a right decision, these decision tasks permeate our lives. There is a whole literature on the psychology of risk and how human beings manage risk and the interested reader is encouraged to consult such literature should these topics be of interest to her. For now, it is simply enough to survey a few features of this wide and exciting area of research.

### 1.11.1 The Risk of Making a Wrong Decision
Consider the following hypothetical scenario – you have just paid off your home, valued at approximately $400,000. While legally you were required to have fire homeowners insurance when assuming a mortgage with the bank, now that you own it outright you are no longer required to hold any insurance. You ponder the question carefully as to whether you should still purchase insurance or pocket the premium you would pay and devote those dollars to something else. The only way to truly address this decision problem is to assign probabilities to competing events and evaluate *expectations* or *utility*. If the price of insurance is $1,000 per year, it may seem a no-brainer to pay the premium since the cost of losing the home to a fire would be devastating financially at a total loss of $400,000, the market value of the home. However, if you are one to seldom leave your home and the risk of fire is remote, perhaps the $1,000 spent on an insurance premium is best spent elsewhere. Of course, most homeowners would pay the premium as the risk of losing the home, even if small, is too large as to not guard against such an event. Yet, at the same time of not being able to presumably tolerate such risk, the same individual regularly and voluntarily goes joy-riding in their car on a very dangerous stretch of highway where the risk of losing one’s life is much greater than the risk of losing one’s home to a fire. The point we are making is that risk tolerances can vary widely depending on the given problem and the perception of risk, rational or irrational, can be variable depending on the context and situation.
