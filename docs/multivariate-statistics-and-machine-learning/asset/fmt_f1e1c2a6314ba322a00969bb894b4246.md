## 4.2 Factorial ANOVA and Higher-Order Models

We have surveyed the one-way classic ANOVA in which there is only a single independent variable. Factorial ANOVA features more than a single independent variable, usually with the goal of seeking out *interactions*, that of the effect of one variable being contingent upon one or more other variables.198

For example, it may be true that income (response variable) is found to be a function of education, but that relationship may depend, statistically, on what level of age we consider. Hence, there is said to be a *statistical interaction* between education and age. Note carefully that this conclusion alone does not imply that age “affects” education in any way, or that age or education “affect” income. Based on the statistical interaction alone, we do not have evidence to make any kind of “impact” statement. To even *approximate* such a claim would require a strong *research design* or feature variables that have such a naturally “organic” connection to one another (e.g., increasingly colder temperatures “affect” the rate at which water freezes).

Visualizing interactions is the best way to get a feel for them. For instance, in Figure 4.1 there is an interaction between gender and drug dose:

![Figure 4.1 Interaction between gender and drug dose in the sample.Return to text.⏎](images/fig4.1.jpg)

*Figure 4.1* Interaction between gender and drug dose in the sample.Return to text.⏎

We can see from the plot that for females, scores increase from low dose to high dose. However, for males, the opposite effect holds. That is, scores begin high at low dose and then decrease to high dose. This interaction is rather pronounced; all that is technically required for an interaction is that lines be *nonparallel*. *A bit of thought reveals that interactions in sample data are almost always present. The real question, however, is whether we can infer such a sample interaction in the population from which the data were sampled*.
