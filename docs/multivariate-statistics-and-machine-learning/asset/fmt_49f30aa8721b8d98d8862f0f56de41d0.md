
The rise of computers and computing power has revolutionized statistics and data analysis since at least the early 1980s, if not before. What used to take statisticians and scientists *days* to compute now takes, quite literally, *milliseconds*. For instance, computing a factor analysis in the 1930s took several *hours*, not seconds. Today, one can press a few button clicks (e.g., using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), for instance) and obtain factor-analytic output in less than a second. While this obviously has advantages, it also comes with it the potential peril of possibly overlooking important details prior to conducting data analysis, such as the integrity of data, good theorizing, etc., at least as it pertains to scientific fields. Computing can be done so quickly now that it can be all too easy to pay insufficient attention to *what is being computed and what is being measured*.

In business and healthcare analytics, for instance, the power of software has made it possible to analyze humongous databases and see patterns in data that were not possible before advanced computation and visualization. A business analyst can analyze terabytes of data relatively quickly to see emerging trends and advise management where to allocate marketing resources to maximize profits. As emphasized at the outset of this book, however, none of this awesome ability precludes the importance of ensuring the proper measurement and quality of data. Analyzing and concluding patterns from data only makes good sense if the data itself is of good quality and is tapping into the sought-after constructs. Poor measurement, or otherwise sketchy data can only mislead. Do people approve of your product? A rating scale of 1 through 5 suggests most folks may choose 4 or 5. However, that limited data does not tell you whether they approve of other products a lot more than yours. For instance, perhaps your product is at the lowest preference point of competing products, but you would never know it by simply asking a single question of how much people approve of your product. Hence, knowing what you are measuring and putting it into proper context is just as, if not more, important than before “big data” arrived on the scene. Big data equals big conclusions, which leads to big decisions, which may be faulty if not carefully considered because of lightning-fast computing speed and facility.

## 3.1. The Dominance of R and Python

While there are many software packages (e.g., SAS, Matlab, etc.) useful for data analysis, data science, statistics, and machine learning, two of the more popular programs are R and Python. R is probably the more popular in *statistics-based* coursework and applications, whereas Python is more frequently used in computer science departments as a general computing language. Both have their strengths and weaknesses. Other programs that have been popular in analyzing data include SAS and SPSS. Both are excellent for what they do but are limited since they do not have as much flexibility as R and Python (however, SAS is generally far more capable than SPSS). In R 166and Python you can be more creative and program new things as needed than you can in programs such as SPSS and SAS. What is great about SPSS (and Excel), however, is the spreadsheet interface which makes it exceedingly convenient for quick or common data tasks. One should not fall prey to the “cool factor” that one uses complex software to do simple data analytic tasks that can be accomplished even faster with simpler software! The benefit, however, of using complex software such as R or Python even for simple tasks is that when jobs do get more complex (and they always do), you already will have your foot in the door. The universe of software is vast. For instance, packages such as *Theano* (Bergstra et al., [2010](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000029)) in Python, *Caffe* (Jia et al., [2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000171)) and *Torch* (Collobert et al., [2010](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000066)) are other libraries and software useful for deep learning neural networks.

In this chapter, we provide a very (*very*) brief “sketchy” and trivial overview of R and Python software, some of their capabilities and options, along with a few simple demonstrations of what they can do. We move rather quickly in our introduction as we assume the reader has had at least *some* exposure to computing and can “pick things up” rather quickly in this regard. The capabilities of these software packages are immense and we could easily fill up a few volumes with examples of what software can do, but such would detract from the primary focus of the book, which is about multivariate statistics and machine learning, not *primarily* about computing. While computing and programming can of course get very deep and require immense talent, training, and experience, we do not see computing as something “to learn” in this book. That is, we do not see memorizing functions in R or Python as time well spent. We encourage the reader to focus more on *understanding* what functions accomplish.

All of the functions we discuss in this book and offered by R and Python can be looked up in references and otherwise searched in online manuals as needed. Much of our code is adapted in one way or another from existing packages in R or from Python’s site. Often, when working on code in a research setting, even sample code must be adjusted and customized to fit the situation at hand, and hence the most the student of these methods can hope for is some familiarity and practice with what these packages can do to prepare themselves for situations where they will have to work with code, not simply retrieve something from memory. Then, when the time comes to implement a given model or compute something, working through code, tweaking, and hacking will not be something “new” to them. A good course in computing teaches students the *capacity* to compute so that they gain experience, not necessarily *what* to compute in every conceivable instance of what *might* need to be computed over one’s career. Experience and practice in problem-solving are what are most important to prepare for novel situations. Need to do a *recursive neural net* in Python? Finding the code is not hard. The code however will not tell you what a neural net *is*; only a good book on theory will do that. Always be mindful of the following distinction:

> *As a computer scientist, you wish to truly understand the underworkings of the code. As a scientist, you need to understand what code works, and generally, why it works and possibly how to adapt it, but you do not necessarily require understanding such code at a deep “language” level.*

Of course, the more one understands about the specific language, the better a programmer he or she will be and the greater power and flexibility one will have with regard to applications. But we do not venture into that territory in this book. At the conclusion of the chapter we provide a couple recommendations and resources for those who wish to pursue their computing knowledge of either R or Python, or both, and perhaps specialize in one software or the other (or both).
