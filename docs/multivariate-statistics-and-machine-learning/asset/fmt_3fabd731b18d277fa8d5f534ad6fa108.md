
The above result has important and pragmatic implications. Most pragmatically for statistics and machine learning in an applied sense, positive definite symmetric matrices are those for which *all eigenvalues are strictly positive*. *Semi-positive definite* matrices, on the other hand, have some (at least one) eigenvalues equal to 0 and all others positive, while indefinite matrices have at least one eigenvalue that is also negative. A further implication of positive definite matrices is that such matrices have all row and column vectors linearly independent, which implies that the matrix is of full rank and hence nonsingular, and this equates to the determinant of the matrix being nonzero.

## 2.45 Inner Products

One of the most significant foundational aspects of linear algebra is that of *inner products*, also generally known as *dot products*. Though the two terms are often used interchangeably, as we do as well in this book, they are not exactly the same. The dot product can be considered a *special case* of the more general Euclidean inner product, where dot products are usually restricted to finite Euclidean spaces of ℝ² or ℝ³ but where *Euclidean inner products* are generalizable to higher dimensions, including to infinite-dimensional vector spaces ([Anton & Rorres, 2000](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000013), p. 163). Inner products are at the heart of many computations in linear algebra and hence are also heavily used in statistics and machine learning. For example, covariance and correlation are based entirely on the notion of a dot product. The *kernel trick* featured in support vector machines and other algorithms also features the inner product, where, as we will see, computation of the kernel in the original dimensionality of the data points generalizes to an inner product space in higher dimensions.

An inner product is defined as

`x′y = y′x = x[1]y[1] + x[2]y[2] + ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ + x[m]y[m]`

(2.108)

where `x′y = y′x` indicates commutativity. The inner product is also distributive where for three vectors, `x ⋅ (y+z) = x y + x z`. A vector space together with an inner product is called an *inner product space* ([Roman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000306)).

Recall that the *length* of a vector `x` can be expressed as the square root of its inner product,140

$$L_{x} = \sqrt{x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2} + \cdot \cdot \cdot + x_{n}^{2}} = \sqrt{x'x}$$

For two random vectors, `x`, `y`, the *Cauchy-Schwarz inequality* holds that

`|⟨x′y⟩| ≤ ||x|| ||y||`

(2.109)

which in words says that the absolute value of the inner product, `x y`, is equal to or smaller than the product of individual lengths ([Bilodeau & Brenner, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000030)). Equality exists in the case that `x` and `y` are linearly dependent (i.e., scalar multiples since there are only two vectors). A proof of the inequality can be found in Garcia and Horn ([2017](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000122), p. 93).

In Python, we can easily compute the inner product:

```
a = np.array([1, 2, 3])
b = np.array([0, 1, 0])
np.inner(a, b)
Out[342]: 2
```

A *Hilbert space* is an abstract vector space with an inner product that is complete under the metric that is induced by the inner product ([Roman](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000306), 2008, p. 327). Formality matters here, but we can make this definition much more practical. Pragmatically, as it regards statistics and machine learning, a Hilbert space is essentially a kind of feature space ([Mohri et al., 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000253)). The concept of inner products here is key because it forms the basis of the aforementioned kernel function or *kernel trick*. The idea of the kernel trick is to compute a given kernel using raw data in the original space (called the input or “data space”) so as to tell us something about the inner product of vectors in the higher “feature” space. That is, *the kernel function in the data space essentially reproduces kernel Hilbert spaces*. In this way, the elements of the kernel function “live” in the more abstract higher-dimensional Hilbert space, which allows us to compute kernels in low dimensions to tell us something about inner products in higher dimensions, that is, in the feature space. We extend this discussion of the kernel trick shortly.

## 2.46 Linear Independence

The idea of linear independence is of fundamental importance and has wide implications in statistics and machine learning. Consider two vectors, `x` and `y`, weighted by scalars `c[1]` and `c[2]`,

`c[1]x + c[2]y = 0`

If both `c[1]` and `c[2]` are both equal to 0, then the equality is easily shown to be true. This is the *trivial* case, and hence not very interesting. If, on the other hand, we can find scalars `c[1]` and `c[2]` both not equal to 0 such that the equality still holds, then the vectors `x` and `y` are said to be *linearly dependent*. It essentially means, *for the case of only two vectors*, that we can write one vector as a *scalar multiple* of the other. In the case of more than two vectors, linear dependence implies we can write one vector as a linear combination of others in the set. If we cannot find such a set of scalars (other than zero), then the set of vectors is *linearly independent*.

As mentioned, the above example features only two vectors. We can generalize this principle of linear independence and dependence to a much broader set of vectors, indeed, `k` vectors,141

`c[1]x[1] + c[2]x[2] + ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ + c[k]x[k] = 0`

If there exist scalars `c[1]` through `c[k]` not all zero such that the above equality holds, then the vectors `x[1]` through `x[k]` are said to be *linearly dependent*. Otherwise, the set of vectors is linearly independent. Linear independence basically implies that we cannot write one vector as a linear combination of any others in the set, for which in the special case of two vectors in a set, the linear combination necessarily implies a scalar multiple. It is important to note that linear dependence or independence is relative to the *set of vectors* only. For example, two vectors are not themselves *intrinsically* linearly dependent or independent without considering the set in which they are featured. That is, the vector (0, 2, 4) is not by itself linearly independent or dependent; its status must be concluded *relative to the set in which it is referenced*. If that vector is the only (nonzero) vector in the set, then it is still regarded as linearly independent, but it is only so *relative to the set*, which in this case, consists of no other vectors.

## 2.47 Rank of a Matrix

The maximum number of linearly independent rows or columns of a matrix is the matrix’s *rank*. A fundamental, if not surprising result of linear algebra is that *row rank* will always equal *column rank*, which also implies that the matrices `A` and `A^(-1)` (if it exists) have the same rank. Throughout our study of statistics, we will typically be interested in matrices of *full rank*, which implies that the total number of columns or rows is inclusive in the rank of the matrix.

As an example, if we have a 3 × 3 matrix and say it is of “full rank,” then it means the number of linearly independent rows (and columns) is equal to 3. If we say a matrix is less than full rank, or “rank deficient,” then it suggests the rank of the matrix is less than the number of columns or rows. Rank-deficient matrices have one or more columns (or rows) that are linear combinations of other columns (or rows). For instance, the following matrix is less than full rank:
