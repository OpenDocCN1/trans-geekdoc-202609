
However, as Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 419) note, in addition to the two choices leading to disparate conclusions, requiring `M(1−ε[i])` results in a *convex* optimization problem, whereas `M−ε[i]` *nonconvex*, and so `M(1−ε[i])` is chosen over `M−ε[i]` primarily based on this criterion. Recall that optimization of a convex loss function is unaffected by local minima, which is what in general makes a convex solution desirable over a nonconvex one. This is distinct, for instance, from neural 360networks in which loss functions are typically nonconvex and hence renown for getting stuck in local minima. This gives support vector machines a strong competitive advantage over neural network methods as well as decision trees ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)).

How should we choose `C`? In practice we could take a reasonable guess at it, but it would probably be wiser to choose it via *cross-validation*. Note as well that as `C` increases, the margin becomes increasingly wider which translates to many observations violating the margin. Such a classifier would have relatively low variance but likely high bias. On the opposite extreme, if `C` is small, this would imply the classifier would have low bias but relatively high variance. The selection of `C` should be a compromise between these two extremes. Further details on computations involved in the support vector classifier can be found in Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 420) which involves re-expression of the above identities and constraints for computational efficiency and convenience.

## 9.4 Support Vector Machines: Introducing the Kernel for Nonlinearity

Up to now, we have discussed the maximum margin classifier and the support vector classifier. The maximum margin classifier was useful when there was a clear separating hyperplane that clearly divided two groups. The support vector classifier was a bit more flexible in that it allowed observations to be on the other side of the hyperplane; in essence, it built in a bit of “slack” (via *slack variables*) so that even if perfect separation did not exist, the support vector classifier attempted to fit a hyperplane anyway that would separate *most* observations, even if it missed a few. The support vector classifier also featured a tuning parameter `C` that could be chosen possibly via cross-validation to determine the amount of “slack” one wished to incorporate into the classifier. *The commonality between the maximum margin classifier and the support vector classifier is that the decision boundary is linear*. This point is essential, because nowhere in our discussion thus far have we entertained the idea of a *nonlinear* decision boundary. Even if our data up to now suggested the boundary may be nonlinear, we still, at minimum, imposed a linear boundary, possibly with allowing some slack in the form of `ε[i]`.

Now we consider the case where the decision boundary is definitely *not linear*, which will require us to “evolve” our previous classifiers to something new, the full *support vector machine*, of which both the maximum margin classifier and support vector classifiers can be regarded as special cases. Hence, to summarize, our progression has been as follows, from left to right:

> *maximum margin classifier* → *support vector classifier* → *support vector machine*

> 

>    (*linear*)        (*linear*)        (*nonlinear*)

The solution to creating a nonlinear boundary, as we discuss now, is what really put support vectors on the map historically as a machine learning method and that is as a general methodology for dealing with nonlinear boundaries. The solution is to enlarge the feature space by *kernelizing* the problem.

### 9.4.1 Enlarging the Feature Space with Kernels

[Cortes and Vapnik (1995, p. 271)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000068) describe their contribution of the support vector machine as follows:

> The support-vector network implements the following idea: it maps the input vectors into some high dimensional feature space Z through some non-linear mapping chosen a priori. In this space a linear decision surface is constructed with special properties that ensure high generalization ability of the network.

The key to the above is that of *enlarging the feature space*. But what does that mean? Essentially, it means introducing more complex *basis functions*. As a reasonable attempt, we might try to take higher powers and terms of polynomials, such as the following,

`x[1]`, `x[1]²`, `x[2]`, `x[2]²`..., `x[p]`, `x[p]²`

or even more complex interaction terms, etc. *When we enlarge the feature space, in this case taking new basis functions such as powers and polynomials, the decision boundary often becomes separable by a hyperplane.*
