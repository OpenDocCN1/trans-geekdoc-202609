
### 9. `Regression Splines`
In this approach, we choose splines as a `basis expansion` for our predictors. Regression splines are not `equivalent` to a basis expansion, but are merely a special case. Splines, as was the case for polynomial regression, are meant to capture nonlinearities in the data, but do so at a more `local level`. The locality of splines is what makes them so attractive and useful. Splines feature the use of “knots” that are employed to capture the local and nonlinear segments of the data trend. It is generally regarded as a nonparametric technique as it uses the data (not the model) to determine the fit; however, elements of regression splines can also make them partially parametric.

### 10. `Kernel Regression`
This approach also seeks to model the data more locally using a kind of weighted regression and a kernel to draw focus to focal values in the regression while at the same time incorporating values of nearby or “neighboring” points. Kernel regression is a kind of weighted-average regression that extends notions of naïve nonparametric regression.

### 11. `Generalized Linear Models`
These employ a link function to “linearize” an otherwise nonlinear response such as one that is binary or polytomous. The response may also be categorical or count data, for which a Poisson link is an option. Many other link functions are available. The general linear model can be considered a special case of the generalized linear model where the link is the identity function.

### 12. `Generalized Additive Models`
These models feature nonparametric “smoothers” that generalize many other models, including the generalized linear model and polynomial models. The choice of what nonparametric smoother to use is left up to the researcher; however, common ones are smoothing splines, kernels, or many others. Pedagogically, the generalized additive model is very useful for conceptualizing a host of other models as special cases and in turn can also be considered a simplified version of neural networks.

## 8.9 Discriminant Analysis
Recall that we briefly introduced discriminant analysis toward the end of our discussion of the multivariate analysis of variance (`MANOVA`) model earlier in the book. There, we said that we would recap and extend on our discussion of the model later in the book when we introduced the generalized linear model. We discuss discriminant analysis here in this chapter on generalized linear models because it belongs to the types of models used for classification such as binary logistic regression and can be conceptualized as a special case of the generalized linear model with a binary response. Recall that the reason we originally introduced discriminant analysis in the context of `MANOVA` is that it shares many technical similarities with the `MANOVA` model and is often used as a follow-up analysis to the `MANOVA` procedure. Whereas in `MANOVA` we sought mean vector differences on a linear combination of response variables, in discriminant analysis we turned the problem around and sought to classify observations into groups based on a linear combination of predictors.

In the machine learning and statistical learning literatures, the presentations of discriminant analysis are rarely, if ever, related to multivariate analysis of variance. At least authors usually make no mention of the connection. Discriminant analysis is seen purely as a classification technique, one of the many `classifiers` featured in a wider discussion of multivariate and machine learning algorithms. We find the omission of multivariate analysis of variance in these contexts 328unfortunate and a clear missed pedagogical opportunity. Because this is a book on both multivariate statistics *and* machine learning, we have chosen to take both approaches, where earlier in the book we discussed discriminant analysis in the context of `MANOVA` and where here we focus on the machine learning paradigm of discriminant analysis as purely a classifier without any mention of `MANOVA`.

We begin by discussing the most “optimal” strategy for classification, which is that of using `Bayes`. As we will see, `Bayes is best` when it comes to classification. For reasons that we will discuss, using `Bayes` for classification is often not possible, and hence our second choice will be to implement the discriminant analysis model to `approximate` the `Bayes` boundary.

As we will also see, linear discriminant analysis (`LDA`) is yet another example of a statistical methodology in which `generative assumptions` (such as linearity) greatly simplify the learning process and can afford greater predictability. This is the reason why developing the discriminant analysis model is worthwhile; otherwise we would be content with using such procedures as `k-nearest neighbors` which, as we will see, essentially makes no prior parametric assumptions about the data. The principle here is that, once more, `making generative assumptions simplifies the problem and in cases where those assumptions are satisfied, doing so yields great benefit`. In cases where assumptions are not satisfied, nonparametric approaches are fair competitors to parametric ones.

> Generative assumptions can greatly simplify the problem of modeling. If those assumptions are not met, however, nonparametric approaches can be useful, and in many cases are more appropriate competitors.

### 8.9.1 Bayes Is Best for Classification
Recall the nature of `Bayes’ theorem` discussed earlier in the book,

$$p\\left( y = k \\middle| x = x\_{i} \\right) = \\frac{\\pi\_{k}f\_{k}\\left( x\_{i} \\right)}{\\sum\\limits\_{l = 1}^{K}{\\pi\_{1}f\_{l}\\left( x\_{i} \\right)}}$$

(8.22)

where `p`(`y`=`k`|`x`=`x_i`) is the `posterior probability` of `y` equaling a particular class `k` given some particular value of `x`=`x_i`. The numerator in `Bayes’ theorem`, `π_k` `f_k`(`x_i`) contains the `prior probability`, `π_k`, multiplied by the density function of `x_i`, `f_k`(`x_i`) = `p`(`x`=`x_i`|`y`=`k`). Note that the final result of `Bayes` essentially “inverts” this conditional probability, that is, from `p`(`x`=`x_i`|`y`=`k`) to `p`(`y`=`k`|`x`=`x_i`). In a nutshell, this is the goal of Bayesian analysis in a simple problem such as this, to “flip” or invert conditional probabilities.

Recall also that `Bayes` is not simply an “equation” or “rule” (i.e., “`Bayes rule`”) as above and how it can be “casually” represented in elementary statistics, but is more generally a `philosophy` of how theory or belief should be revised in light of data or experience more generally. As we did earlier in the book, a more general way of writing `Bayes’ theorem` is the following:329

$$p\\left( T \\middle| D \\right) = \\frac{p\\left( T \\right) \\cdot p\\left( D \\middle| T \\right)}{p\\left( T \\right) \\cdot p\\left( D \\middle| T \\right) + p\\left( \\overline{T} \\right) \\cdot p\\left( D \\middle| \\overline{T} \\right)}$$

(8.23)

where now `p`(`T`|`D`) is the probability of the theory `T` given data `D`. That is, given the numerator `p`(`T`) ⋅ `p`(`D`|`T`), the prior probability multiplied by the likelihood, we seek to “flip” `p`(`D`|`T`) to `p`(`T`|`D`).
