
Some have suggested criteria for a good clustering algorithm. For instance, some have proposed that clustering algorithms should require minimal requirements of domain knowledge to determine input parameters, as appropriate values are typically not known in advance when working with large databases. A good cluster algorithm should also not automatically assume clusters will be `spherical` in shape, as empirical evidence has shown that many empirical clusters are `drawn out`, `linear`, or `elongated`, etc., which are shapes that a technique such as traditional clustering algorithms like `k-means` may fail to detect. This last point is very important in cluster analysis, as an entire literature in statistics and machine learning exists with the goal of discovering `non-spherical` cluster shapes. A good clustering algorithm, as already emphasized, should also provide reasonable `efficiency` on large databases. This of course is coupled with computational concerns and the limitations of software and hardware to carry out such clustering.
Whatever the clustering algorithm adopted, many clustering procedures, as is the case with most statistical and machine learning methods, essentially come down to `maximizing` or `minimizing` some quantity, that is, some objective function of the data. That is, good clustering, from a mathematical point of view, becomes simply a problem of `optimization`. What makes for a good optimization strategy for clustering? It seems reasonable to require that `within-cluster variation` be minimized. That is, an ideal goal to strive for might be to minimize the sum of `within-cluster variation` across each cluster,503
$$MIN{\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}{Within\\left( C\_{k} \\right)}}$$
where `C`[`k`] is a given cluster `k`. The above reads the sum from `k` = 1 to `K` of within-cluster variation is minimized, that is, as small as it can be through successive iterations. This amounts to the clusters being as `homogeneous` as possible.
More specifically, the within-cluster distance is usually specified by an average squared distance function:
$$Within\\left( C \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}N\_{k}}{\\sum\\limits\_{C(i) = k}\\left| \\middle| x\_{i} - {\\overline{x}}\_{k} \\middle| \\right|^{2}}$$
(14.4)
where ${\\overline{x}}\_{k}$ is the mean for the `k`th cluster, and $\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}N\_{k}$ simply represents the sum across all clusters `k` to `K`. That is, we are summing across all clusters `k` the squared distances from respective cluster means, ${\\overline{x}}\_{k}$. The objective is to minimize this average `within-cluster` distance. Once this objective is minimized, the clustering algorithm is said to have `converged` on a solution. As we will discuss later in this chapter, the `k-means` clustering algorithm seeks to accomplish just this and provides a `local optimum` (James et al., [2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000169)).
> *Cluster analysis algorithms do not typically consider all partitions of the data since such are too numerous and would be far too labor intensive. Combinatorially, there are simply too many ways of generating groups. Instead, they seek to partition cases in a way that is mathematically meaningful which usually translates to them optimizing some “greedy” function of the data.*
## 14.6 Distance and Proximity Metrics
As already mentioned, virtually all of cluster analysis techniques available, despite their individual differences, are based on the notions of `similarity` or `distance` between objects. Indeed, as discussed at the outset of this book, one can even say that most, if not all statistical and learning machine methods eventually boil down in some capacity or another to notions of `distance` when inspected at the most foundational level ([Johnson & Wichern, 2007](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000173)). Distances will generally be organized into a `distance matrix` that contains non-negative entries. That is, it is assumed that distances are a `non-negative integer`. To measure proximity of cases, we require a measure of distance, where the distance `d`[`j`] between `x`[`i`j] and `x`[`i`′j] is, most generally,
`d`[`j`](`x`[`i`j],`x`[`i`′j]) = (`x`[`i`j]−`x`[`i`′j])
and squared distances are thus given by504
`d`[`j`]²(`x`[`i`j],`x`[`i`′j]) = (`x`[`i`j]−`x`[`i`′j])²
There are several such distance measures available. The most common is that of `Euclidean distance`. The Euclidean distance between two `p`-dimensional objects, `x` = [`x`[1],`x`[2],...,`x`[`p`]] and `y` = [`y`[1],`y`[2],...,`y`[`p`]] is given by,
$$\\begin{array}{l} {d\\left( x,y \\right) = \\sqrt{\\left( x\_{1} - y\_{1} \\right)^{2} + \\left( x\_{2} - y\_{2} \\right)^{2} + \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot \\left( x\_{p} - y\_{p} \\right)^{2}}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\sqrt{\\left( x - y \\right)'\\left( x - y \\right)}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$
(14.5)
A `statistical distance` can also be used, where distances `x` − `y` are scaled by the covariance matrix, `S`,
$$d\\left( x,y \\right) = \\sqrt{\\left( x - y \\right)'S^{- 1}\\left( x - y \\right)}$$
(14.6)
Other metrics are also available. For instance, the `Minkowski metric` is given by
$$d\\left( x,y \\right) = \\left\\lbrack {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{p}\\left| x\_{i} - y\_{i} \\right|^{m}} \\right\\rbrack^{1/m}$$
(14.7)
for which when `m` = 2, the Minkowski metric reduces to the Euclidean distance,
$$\\begin{array}{l} {d\\left( x,y \\right) = \\left\\lbrack {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{p}\\left| x\_{i} - y\_{i} \\right|^{m}} \\right\\rbrack^{1/m}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\left\\lbrack {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{p}\\left| x\_{i} - y\_{i} \\right|^{2}} \\right\\rbrack^{1/2}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\sqrt{\\left( x\_{1} - y\_{1} \\right)^{2} + \\left( x\_{2} - y\_{2} \\right)^{2} + \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot \\left( x\_{p} - y\_{p} \\right)^{2}}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$
Yet another distance metric is that of `city-block`, also known as `Manhattan distance`,
$$d\\left( x,y \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{p}\\left| x\_{i} - y\_{i} \\right|}$$
(14.8)
One place where the Manhattan distance is used is in the `k-medians` approach to cluster analysis ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)) which we will feature shortly when we survey algorithms from the `k-representatives` family.
The importance of choosing an appropriate distance metric cannot be overstated. As emphasized by Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 506):
> Specifying an appropriate dissimilarity measure is far more important in obtaining success with clustering than choice of clustering algorithm. This aspect of the problem is emphasized less in the clustering literature than the algorithms themselves, since it depends on domain knowledge specifics and is less amenable to general research.505
A good summary of the more popular distance measures used in clustering algorithms is provided in Xu and Tian ([2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000396)).
## 14.7 Is the Data Clusterable?
Before engaging in clustering, we might ask the question of how “clusterable” is a data set to begin with? A first answer to this might be that it does not matter and to just give clustering a “try” and see what comes out. However, this strategy can be somewhat problematic. Cluster analysis in most cases `will` find a solution, the algorithms are designed specifically to do just that! Hence, a test for clusterability `before` engaging in cluster analysis, if that test is a good one, should give us an idea of whether the data should be clustered in the first place and whether any clusters we do observe will be more a function of the algorithm than the actual empirical existence of clusters. As remarked by Adolfsson et al. ([2019](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000004), p. 1), “perhaps an even more fundamental issue than algorithm selection is when clustering should, or should not, be applied.”
