
Averages can also be dangerous when it comes to interpreting data. For instance, our interpretation of the mean implicitly assumes *linear interpolation* between values. For example, consider the mean of two numbers, 2 and 4. The mean is, of course, 3. Denoting 3 as the “center of mass” assumes that the distance and importance between 2 and 3 and 3 to 4 is the same, which in this case is linear. Linear interpolation, in general, can be risky because the intermediate values may not be 90linear, but are often assumed to be out of convenience. This is a simple idea but can have drastic and important consequences when interpreting real data.

## 2.21.2 The Median
Another measure of central tendency is that of the *median*. The median is the data point that divides the ordered data set into exactly two equal parts. The computation of the median will depend on how many observations are in the data set. For instance, if `n` is odd, then the location of the median `ỹ` is equal to (`n`+1)/2, yielding a median value equal to

$$\\widetilde{y} = y\_{(\\frac{n + 1}{2})}$$

(2.39)

If `n` is even, then the location of the median is between (`n`/2) and (`n`/2) + 1, yielding a median of

`ỹ` = \[\[`y`[`n`/2]\]+\[`y`[( `n`/2)]+1\]\]/2

(2.40)

While means can be said to be sensitive to extreme observations, medians are not. Medians, and other median-like statistics (such as quartiles), are said to be *robust* to extreme or outlying observations. Because medians only consider the “middle” value of the distribution, one can easily change other values in the data set while still retaining the same median. For example, the median of the data set 9, 10, 11 is 10, while the median of the data set 9, 10, 100 is also equal to 10. If a researcher reports the median of their data, ask to see a plot! Resist engaging in the cognitive bias that the median (or the mean, for that matter) implies a normal distribution. “Normality” is a theoretical notion. Empirical normality never truly exists; it can only be *approximated* by the theoretical ideal, that is, by the mathematical definition of a normal density.

Recall that the arithmetic mean is not robust to extreme scores or outliers. That is, since the mean is a weighted sum of deviations, even changing one value in the distribution will alter the mean. Is this a benefit or drawback? Such will depend on what you want the measure to accomplish. The scientist is, of course, interested in the data, but without knowledge of the functions (e.g., mean, median, etc.) *imposed* on such data, statistics computed on that data become amenable to misunderstanding and misinterpretation.

## 2.22 Measures of Variability
Measuring the variability in an empirical or theoretical distribution can take many forms, some better than others depending on the context of the situation and the characteristics of the data. Measures such as the *range*, *interquartile range*, which is equal to the 75th minus the 25th percentile, *semi-interquartile range*, equal to the 75th minus the 25th percentile divided by 2, and others are useful as “median-like” measures of variability. Being median measures, they are insensitive and robust to outlier observations, and hence, if such influence is not desired when summarizing a data set, these types of measures come highly recommended. Most commonly, however, we wish to calculate more sensitive measures that utilize the arithmetic mean as their locus or center from which we take deviations. For this reason, the variance and standard deviation dominate statistics as measures of variability, which we now review.91

### 2.22.1 Variance and Standard Deviation
The sample variance is defined as

$$S^{2} = \\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right.} \\right)^{2}}{n}$$

(2.41)

where $\\overline{y}$ is the mean of the distribution and `y`[`i`] any particular data point in that distribution. The sample size is given by `n`. The sample variance requires us to sum squared deviations from the mean and take the average of that sum. The sample variance with `n` in the denominator is a *biased estimator* of the population variance, `σ`²,

$$\\sigma^{2} = \\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\mu \\right.} \\right)^{2}}{n}$$

(2.42)

where now `μ` is the population mean to replace $\\overline{y}$ in the aforementioned sample variance. Formally, `S`² is a biased estimator because its expected value does not equal the population parameter, `σ`². More succinctly, we write `E`(`S`²) ≠ `σ`². To make it an *unbiased estimator*, we can correct the denominator by subtracting a *degree of freedom*, which yields,

$$s^{2} = \\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right.} \\right)^{2}}{n - 1}$$

(2.43)

and now makes it true that `E`(`s`²) = `σ`². The positive square root of the sample variance is the sample standard deviation `s`,

$$s = \\sqrt{\\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right.} \\right)^{2}}{n - 1}}$$

(2.44)

which provides a measure of average deviation from the mean, now no longer in squared units as was true for the variance. As an example, if the standard deviation for a distribution is computed to be equal to 5, we can conclude that o*n average, scores deviate from the mean by five units*. However, without knowing the characteristics of the distribution and whether it is normal or not, we cannot use the normal density to compute proportions under the curve. That is, we cannot automatically assume that nearly 68% of cases fall within +1 or −1 units (i.e., a single standard deviation on either side of zero). We need to know more about the *shape* of the distribution in order to make such statements.

The variance and standard deviation so far discussed are for empirical variables, not theoretical random variables. For a continuous random variable, the variance is given by

`σ`[]² = ∫[ − ∞]^( + ∞)(`x`−`μ`)²`f`(`x`)d`x`

(2.45)

If the variable is *discrete*, then we can associate a probability `p`[`i`] with any particular value `x` in the distribution, yielding92

$$\\sigma\_{}^{2} = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}{\\left( x - \\mu \\right)^{2}p\_{i}\\left( x \\right)}}$$

(2.46)

In Python, the variance can be computed by `numpy.var()`. In R, the `var()` function will return the variance.

### 2.22.2 Mean Absolute Deviation
The reader may wonder why squared deviations of the kind $\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)^{2}$ are taken when computing the standard deviation. Indeed, we must take at least *some* function of the deviations since taking the sum of deviations, $\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)$, will always result in a value of zero, no matter the extent of variability or dispersion present in the distribution. That is, recall it can easily be shown that ${\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)} = 0$ for any conceivable distribution, which thus makes it a poor measure of dispersion. Why do we always get zero? The reason is because deviations from the mean are made up of both positive and negative deviations which *balance out* when taking the sum. By squaring deviations, we eliminate the negatively-signed differences and hence obtain a value greater than zero *only if there is indeed variability present in the distribution*. If there is no variability, then $\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right.} \\right)^{2} = 0$ will be true but only because there is indeed no variability, not because it is a constant for any distribution of data we may consider.
