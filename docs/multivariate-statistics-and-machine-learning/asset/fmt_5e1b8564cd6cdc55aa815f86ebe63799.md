
When bootstrapping, the question that also naturally arises regards how many replicates are sufficient to get a reasonable estimate of the distribution of the bootstrapped statistic. Though there is some debate on this, a general recommendation is in the neighborhood of `B = 50`, and rarely greater than 200 ([Rizzo, 2019](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000303)). Bootstrapping also allows for replacement, and hence one bootstrapped sample might contain the same sampling element as another bootstrapped sample. The probability that a data point is not included in a sample is equal to `1 - (1/n)` which implies that the probability that the given data point is not included in `n` samples is equal to `(1-1/n)^(n)`, which is a well-known approximation to `1/e` in the limit as `n→∞` ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)).

Bootstrapping in Python can be easily performed using `SciPy`. Bootstrapping in R can be performed several ways. One way is by using the `bootstrap` package (Original et al., [2019](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000276)).

### 2.28.3 Variations and Extensions of the Bootstrap

There are numerous variations on the bootstrap. Among these include the *leave-one-out bootstrap*, where the accuracy of an instance is computed using all the data except that instance ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)). Kleiner et al. ([2014](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000190)) proposed a sub-sampling method called *bag of little bootstraps* (`BLB`) to help deal with the massive data that may be too large for computer memory or take too long to analyze efficiently. In the `BLB` approach, one draws a number of subsamples of a given size on each sample from the original collected data set. For each of the subsets, a number of bootstrapped samples are chosen and we compute statistical methods on those subsets (e.g., computation of the mean). These are thus, the point estimates. These measures are then combined by averaging to yield the overall point estimates and quality measures.

The *fractional bootstrap*, also known as the *Bayesian bootstrap*, draws random weights and instead of applying such to resampling units, applies them to the actual data. For details and implementation in R, see the `fwb` package ([Greifer, 2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000133)).

## 2.29 Elements of Classic Null Hypothesis Significance Testing

For better or for worse, much of classical statistics is based on the method of *null hypothesis significance testing*, or “`NHST`” for short, which provides the basis for rejecting a hypothesis if the data are unlikely to have occurred under the presumed model. This yields the classic “`p-value`” from which a scientist decides “yay” or “nay” whether to retain or discard the null. The null hypothesis, contrary to misconception, is not necessarily the hypothesis of a null result or zero effect, nor is it necessarily the hypothesis of equality in a comparison scenario of two populations. R.A. Fisher, the inventor of ANOVA, coined the null hypothesis to be the hypothesis to be *nullified* if observed data contradict it. More specifically, if the probability of an observed statistic is relatively small in light of the null, the null is rejected and a *statistical alternative* hypothesis is inferred. The designation of “statistical” in *statistical alternative* is important. A statistical alternative hypothesis is a statement of “not the null,” and is *not* a statement confirming the researcher’s substantive alternative hypothesis. Classical null hypothesis significance testing, or really any *statistical* theory, does not have the ability to confirm a research hypothesis. Misunderstanding this point can easily lead to misunderstanding the results of a significance test.

As an example to illustrate the point, suppose we posit a null hypothesis that the mean IQ of a population is equal to 100. That is,

`H[0]: μ = 100`

The statistical alternative hypothesis is that the mean is not equal to 100; that is,

`H[1]: μ ≠ 100`

But why would it not be equal to 100? Perhaps because the investigator treated the group with a magic pill that would increase their IQ. In this context, that is the *research alternative hypothesis*. Note that *rejection of the null and inference of the statistical alternative hypothesis is not equivalent to inference of the research alternative*. The null hypothesis does not know “why” it is being rejected; it is simply being rejected based on the low probability of the test statistic. Hence, a rejection of the null could “happen” under a lot of conditions, especially if the study or experiment were done poorly. If the null is rejected under a properly done experiment, then inference of the statistical alternative naturally leads to a strong inference of the research alternative, but that “leap” is not automatic, and making it so can easily lead to inference of an incorrect research alternative. For instance, under a poorly performed study, perhaps it wasn’t the magic pill at all that was the effective agent; perhaps the increase in IQ was due to increased sunlight due to a change in seasons or a change in diet among participants that simulated an increase in IQ because of greater cognitive processing power.

To emphasize the abstraction that exists in the classical null hypothesis significance test, we can express the parameter value (or vector, in the case of multivariate models) as belonging to a particular parameter space under the null, `Θ[0]`, from which the parameter value is a member of that set,

`H[0]: θ ∈ Θ[0]`

(2.60)

against an alternative hypothesis that the parameter value is a member of an alternative set,

`H[1]: θ ∈ Θ[0]^c`

(2.61)

where `Θ[0]^c` is the complement to `Θ[0]` and whose membership is that of *not the null*. Why the abstraction? To make things unnecessarily abstract and mathematical? No! This symbolism demonstrates the most *general framework* of the null hypothesis significance test and emphasizes that the complement to the null hypothesis *is* an abstraction. It is not “concrete.” Otherwise said, rejection of the null hypothesis does not necessarily mean anything other than inferring the complement to the null in terms of an abstract parameter space! Allowing this idea to sink in will prevent the reader from making egregious scientific inferences when rejecting a null hypothesis that are habitually made even by professionals in various fields. A rejection of the null does not imply the research alternative; it merely indicates the complement to the null *statistically* (not *substantively*).

Classical significance levels used in most scientific investigations include 0.05 and 0.01, though there is absolutely no statistical or philosophical grounds for using these levels. Fisher himself simply denoted the 0.05 level as “usual and convenient” in the establishment of evidence to contradict the null. Still, there is a long history of misunderstanding what 0.05 actually means, and often researchers incorrectly treat 0.05 as a “yes” vs. “no” decision on the presence of an *effect* in the experiment, which is a serious misunderstanding. Not only does 0.05 not necessarily indicate the presence or absence of a meaningful effect, but there is simply nothing “special” about 0.05. As many have noted before, surely God loves the 0.06 level as much as the 0.05. Determining an appropriate significance level should be done carefully with regard to the risk tolerances of making type I and type II errors depending on the given context of the situation. Once more, *context matters*!
