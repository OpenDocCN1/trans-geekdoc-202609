Take a moment and note how general and nonspecific the idea of a relationship is between variables. *If we delete even one pair out of the totality of pairs, we have illustrated a relationship!* Why are we emphasizing this matter? For the reason that the next time someone says to you, “*There is a relationship between x and y in my data*,” it should be answered with “*Well, of course there is, but more importantly, is the relationship in any way interesting*?” The point we are making here is that between any two or more variables, there is virtually *always* a relationship that exists among them in the sample and in the population. How could there not be? Do you really think all pairs in any Cartesian plot could ever be represented for any empirical pairing of `x` and `y`? Of course not! Hence, *the naming of a relationship between two or more variables is usually an obvious and uninteresting statement*. Now, usually, professing a relationship means more. Let’s consider what it *could* mean now.

A relationship between variables can take on many forms. For two continuous variables, a literal infinite number of curves can be drawn. Said differently, a literal infinite number of *pair deletions* could occur in the Cartesian plot such as to generate a relationship. Often, of most interest (and convenience) is that of *linear* relationships. Both the *covariance* and *correlation* are measures of the *linear association or relationship* between two variables.

The sample covariance between variables `x` and `y` is given by

$$s\_{xy} = \\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( x\_{i} - \\overline{x} \\right.} \\right)\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)}{n - 1}$$

We can see that the covariance, of course, requires variation in both `x` and `y` since if either $\\left( x\_{i} - \\overline{x} \\right)$ or $\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)$ are equal to 0, then

$$\\begin{array}{l} {s\_{xy} = \\frac{\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( x\_{i} - \\overline{x} \\right.} \\right)\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)}{n - 1}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\frac{0}{n - 1} = 0} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

That is, if you have no variation, you cannot quantitatively assess relationship. Relating age in years to income level won’t work if everyone in your sample has the same income. Statistical relationships do not work well with *constants*. You need *variability* in *both* variables.

The covariance is in natural or original units of both `x` and `y`, and hence it is possible that a small or large covariance is mostly reflective of the scale on which `x` and `y` are measured. That is, a scale on which there is little variation on either `x` or `y` or both might reflect a data set with small covariance even if the degree of linear relationship is relatively strong. For this reason, it is useful to standardize the covariance. We do this by dividing the covariance by the product of standard deviations on `x` and `y`. Doing so gives us the *Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient*, `r`[`x``y`],

$$r\_{xy} = \\frac{\\frac{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}{\\left( x\_{i} - \\overline{x} \\right)\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)}}{n - 1}}{\\sqrt{s\_{x\_{i}}^{2} \\cdot s\_{y\_{i}}^{2}}} = \\frac{{cov}\_{xy}}{\\sqrt{s\_{x\_{i}}^{2} \\cdot s\_{y\_{i}}^{2}}}$$  

(2.71)

which has a theoretical range from −1, a perfect negative linear relationship, to +1, a perfect positive linear relationship. A value of `r`[`x``y`]=0 indicates no *linear* relationship between variables. Again, the word “linear” here is emphasized, since it is entirely possible that there still is a relationship between variables, only that it is not linear. It is easy to see as well that if either `s`[`x`[`i`]]² or `s`[`y`[`i`]]²or both equal 0, indicating no variation in variables, then `r`[`x``y`] is undefined. Again, just as was the case for the covariance, the correlation coefficient is a measure of (standardized) co-*variation* in variables. If there is no variation in one or both variables, the correlation coefficient is not defined. The correlation coefficient `r`[`x``y`] is a *consistent* estimator, that is, `r`[`x``y`]→`ρ` as `n`→∞. There are multiple ways to interpret the correlation coefficient. For an excellent discussion, see *Thirteen Ways to Look at the Correlation Coefficient* ([Rodgers & Nicewander, 1988](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000304)).

### 2.31.1 Why Does `r`[`x``y`] Have Limits −1 to +1?

The limits of −1 to +1 on `r`[`x``y`] are such as a result of the product of variances, `s`[`x`[`i`]]² ⋅ `s`[`y`[`i`]]² in the denominator, which implies that the numerator `c``o``v`[`x``y`[]]can be no larger in absolute magnitude than the denominator $\\sqrt{s\_{x\_{i}}^{2} \\cdot s\_{y\_{i}}^{2}}$. The denominator in `r`[`x``y`] essentially provides the maximum “variability” that is available to be accounted for in the numerator, and hence the numerator `c``o``v`[`x``y`] can never be larger, in absolute value, than the denominator. The sign of the covariance will indicate whether `r`[`x``y`] is positive or negative, but the magnitude of `r`[`x``y`] can be no greater than |1|. A more formal proof of this can be found in Casella and Berger ([2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000055), p. 172).

### 2.31.2 Covariance and Correlation in R and Python

We can quite easily compute covariance and correlation in R via the `cov()` and `cor()` functions. As an example, consider the following two vectors of data:113

```
> x <- c(0, 4, 7, 9, 10)
> y <- c(3, 4, 6, 7, 8)
> cov(x, y)
[1] 8.25
> cor(x, y)
[1] 0.9794398
```

We see above that the covariance between the two vectors is 8.25, while the standardized covariance, or *correlation*, is equal to 0.979. Note that the covariance is not that large, yet the correlation has a very large magnitude. This is due to the standardization of the covariance. *Never assume that simply because the covariance is relatively small (or large) that the correlation will likewise be small (or large)*. *They are distinct numerical measures*. *A large covariance does not necessarily imply an equally large correlation*.
