
Most clustering centers around the idea of a *dissimilarity metric*. For some types of clustering, dissimilarity or similarity is defined explicitly, while for other clustering approaches such as mixture modeling, a distance metric is usually implied from the way the clustering is performed. If the data are *qualitative* rather than *quantitative*, then mathematical distance cannot be computed and some measure of similarity will be required in its place ([Xu & Tian, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000396)).

In mathematics, a dissimilarity coefficient `d` may be symmetric, meaning `d(A, B)` = `d(B, A)`, as well as being non-negative, that is, `d(A, A)` = 0. We can also easily deduce from a dissimilarity metric that for points `A`, `B`, `C`, where `B` lies between `A` and `C`,

`d(A,C) ≤ d(A,B) + d(B,C)`

(14.1)

We can also express `d(A,B)` as an *ultrametric*,501

`d(A,B) ≤ max(d(A,C),d(B,C))`

(14.2)

where `A`, `B`, `C` can also be conceived as clusters with points existing within each cluster. The ultrametric holds that the distance between clusters `A` and `B` is less than or equal to the maximum distance between points in clusters `A` and `C` or between points in clusters `B` and `C`. [Venables and Ripley (2002)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000376) note that hierarchical approaches to cluster analysis can be largely interpreted as ultimately approximating an ultrametric dissimilarity. Indeed, Simovici and Hua ([2019](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000338)) have found that a dissimilarity space (which empirically, is a set of data) is more clusterable the closer such space is to an ultrametric.

## 14.4 Association Rules: Market Basket Analysis

Association rules are a relatively primitive way of clustering. The idea behind *association rule* analysis, also commonly referred to as *market basket analysis* (since it is commonly used in marketing analysis of products) is to, not surprisingly, find associations between variables, typically binary variables ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). Much of association rule analysis reduces down to the familiar idea of *conditional probabilities*, that is, the probability `p(y|x)` where `y` is what is being purchased but conditional on product `x`. For example, we may want to know the probability of a customer purchasing a computer monitor given that that same customer has just purchased a desktop computer or the probability of purchasing a television stand given one has just purchased a new television. Many of the algorithms for association analysis reduce to finding modes or what are called “bumps” in the data, whereby collections of prototype `x` values are associated with probability densities (i.e., the “bumps”).

More formally, association analysis seeks to find *intersections* of subsets of variables on `x`, that is, overlapping spaces of the kind

$$p\\left\\lbrack {\\underset{j = 1}{\\overset{p}{\\cap}}\\left( x\_{j} \\in s\_{j} \\right)} \\right\\rbrack$$

where the intersection of such subsets is known as a *conjunctive rule* ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). These subsets naturally occur for qualitative variables, where for quantitative variables the subsets `s[j]` consist of intervals along some continuum.

Clustered association rules have also been proposed that seek to essentially group association rules themselves into categories instead of operating by several individual rules (Lent et al., [1997](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000217)). Association rules have their modern origin with Agrawal et al. ([1993](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000006)), who provide a highly readable and highly recommended introduction to the topic. The package `arules` ([Hahsler et al., 2024](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000139)) in R can be used for mining association rules. For further details on market analysis, see [Aggarwal (2015)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005).

## 14.5 Why Not Consider All Groups?

The first question that arises in the study of cluster analysis *algorithms* is that of why not look at *all groups* of cases, and then decide which is optimal by some metric? For example, when considering the possible clusters of two objects from three available objects, it would be foolish to not simply consider all possibilities of the simple combinatorial problem *3 choose 2*, equal to 3. Indeed, it would at first glance seem that using a combinatorial algorithm from the combinatorial paradigm of clustering is the obvious choice, that is, simply enumerate all possibilities and choose the solution that maximizes or minimizes some objective function. That is, choose the “best” cluster in this sense, and be done with the task.502

While the above solution is highly recommended for very small data sets, it unfortunately will be ineffective or impractical for any data set of even moderate size. The reason for this is that the number of ways of partitioning `n` cases into `g` clusters is literally gargantuan in number, and can be shown to be equal to

$$N\\left( n,g \\right) = \\frac{1}{g!}{\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{g}\\left( \\begin{array}{l} g \\\\ k \\\\ \\end{array} \\right)}\\left( - 1 \\right)^{g - k}k^{n}$$

(14.3)

More succinctly, the number can also be approximated rather well by `g^(n)/g!`. This approach is sometimes referred to as the *sledgehammer* approach. Not only is the approach impractical from a substantive point of view, it is absolutely prohibitive from a computational vantage point as well since to evaluate this vast number of combinations would require a huge amount of computational time and capacity. While smaller data sets will never experience these upper limits of capability, increasingly large data sets will, and given the vast amounts of data that can be clustered today in the era of big data, computational capacity becomes a determining and influential factor when deciding on how to cluster. A good algorithm isn’t only one that clusters well; it must also be *computationally efficient*.

Hence, what is needed are *ways* to group cases together in some sense that is efficient without searching through every single possible combinatorial pattern. That is, we need to consider or hypothesize a much smaller set of *enumeration possibilities*. This is precisely why cluster analysis algorithms exist. Algorithms are designed to partition cases such that mathematically meaningful groups emerge which may or may not simultaneously be substantively meaningful (i.e., the user and research community can make “sense” of what the clusters might represent). Yet good algorithms do not consider all possible groupings; they only consider the *best* ones, in the sense of the most *efficient*, *creative*, and *greedy* ways to generate such groupings, where “greedy” implies that many algorithms consider what is best “here and now” and not two or three steps down the road. By way of analogy, the carrot cake with enough sugar to make you dizzy sounds *great* right now without any thought of how you will feel in a couple of hours.
