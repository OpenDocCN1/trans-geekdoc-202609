We now consider a more complex neural network, one that is more representative of neural networks actually used in practice 547across scientific fields. The neural network we are about to discuss features a layer of hidden units over and above the single layer in the feedforward network surveyed earlier. The addition of one or more layers gives rise to the *multilayer feedforward network*, depicted in [Figure 15.9](#c15.xhtml#f15.9).

As we will emphasize later when we compare neural networks to other statistical methods, the layering concept of a neural network, along with its depth, can be thought of as yet another *basis expansion* of the original inputs to the network ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). However, these parameters are not imposed or a priori determined as in the basis expansion methods we surveyed earlier in the book (e.g., regression splines). Instead, they are arrived at by effectively *learning from the data*. This idea of *depth* in these networks was influenced historically by the ideas of *connectionism*, which essentially held that though one neuron may not be able to accomplish much on its own, several neurons acting together and interacting can possibly exhibit intelligent behavior ([Goodfellow et al., 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)).

As we did with the single-layer network, we will unpack the multilayer network. In the network in [Figure 15.9](#c15.xhtml#f15.9), a given hidden unit, `h[i]`, is equal to the sum of products of the inputs `x[i]` multiplied by the weights `β[i]`:

$$h\_{i} = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{p}{\\beta\_{i}x\_{i}}}$$

The hidden layers in a multilayer feedforward network consist of variables arranged in successive layers, which are traditionally called *nodes*, *neurons*, or *processing units* ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). Calling them “nodes” is probably the most general, as neurons and “processing units” implies something 548that mathematics, by itself, cannot do. We can see in [Figure 15.9](#c15.xhtml#f15.9) that there are two layers at which computation takes place, the *hidden layer* and the *output layer*. There are four input nodes and two output nodes, and three nodes in the hidden layer. This network can be then specified for regression or classification. In a multiple regression, there is only one output variable `y[i]`, whereas in multivariate multiple regression there are many output variables `y[i]`....`y[n+1]`. In a binary classification, there is only one output variable `y[i]` with values 0 or 1.

As was the case in the simpler neural network discussed earlier, an activation function is applied to the given hidden unit. That is, we take a *function of the given hidden unit*, which as we know by now that function is usually one that is *nonlinear* chosen from a set or family of prespecified activation functions. This is where, for instance, we could take the aforementioned logistic sigmoid,

$$Logistic\\,\\, Sigmoid\\left( x \\right) = \\frac{1}{1 + e^{- x}}$$

or many other of the activation functions previously discussed. Simplified, as we move our way toward the output, that output is now a function of the hidden layer. That is, the output is a *function of a function of the inputs*:

$$f\_{i} = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{N}h\_{i}}\\left( \\beta\_{i}x\_{i} \\right)$$

(15.9)

where in (15.9), `N` is the number of hidden units, and `β[i]` is the weight from the hidden unit to output. The final step is the outputting of a function of `f[i]`,

$${\\hat{y}}\_{i} = g\\left( f\_{i} \\right) = \\frac{1}{1 + e^{- f\_{i}}}$$

(15.10)

where we see now `f[i]`, that is, the inner function, takes the place of `x` in the sigmoid function. As we continue to move from layer to layer in the network, the concept of composite functions is very apparent. That is, neural networks continuously take *functions of functions* in arriving at their eventual final output.

## 15.7 Training Neural Networks

We have discussed the architecture of neural networks in some detail, but we have not yet surveyed how the weights in a neural network are optimized or trained. How are the weights in a neural network chosen? As with many statistical methods, they should be chosen in such a way as to *maximize* or *minimize* some function. The selection of the weights thus becomes a problem of *optimization* as has been the case in most other models surveyed in this book. That is, the “training” of the neural network consists of finding optimized weights similar to how we found optimal weights in linear regression using ordinary least-squares or discriminant analysis, for instance. “Training” a statistical or machine learning model means running the model through the data such that weights (or *coefficients*) are optimized by some criteria. In the past days of statistics, we *fit* models to data; today we *train* them in machine learning. Some of this terminology is simply due to a shift in zeitgeist. However, in a wide variety of statistical learning, the “learning part” is usually the updating of scalars in line with obtaining maxima or minima subject to some algorithm. That is, a big part of “learning” is simply *optimization*.549

### 15.7.1 Backpropagation and Minimizing Error Sums of Squares

To optimize a neural network, backpropagation (sometimes called *error backpropagation*) is typically employed. It should be emphasized that backpropagation is used to compute the gradient, while a gradient descent algorithm uses this computed gradient to perform the learning ([Goodfellow](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131), 2016). Hence, the backpropagation algorithm and gradient descent are used in conjunction with one another, not in place of each other. Backpropagation has also been called the *delta rule* ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145); [Widrow & Hoff, 1960](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000387)). The algorithm consists of two phases, the *forward phase* and the *backward phase*. Essentially, the forward phase provides an opportunity for the model to initially learn the weights based on the data; the backward phase gives the model a chance to “correct” or “improve” on the weight estimates based on *feedback* for how it performed in the forward phase. It is in this sense that neural networks *learn*, just as a human being learns from his or her errors (in studying statistics, for instance) and improves performance. This is why the popularization of the backpropagation algorithm in the 1980s was so important to neural networks. It would not be an exaggeration to say that the modern rise of neural networks is mostly related to the discovery of the backpropagation algorithm along with the computational power to handle increasingly large networks.
