
To understand and motivate local regression, it is imperative to first understand the nature of a `basis function` as it relates to regression and modeling in general. Basis functions are so general that they also open the door to understanding more advanced techniques such as neural networks as well, as there are intimate connections between basis functions and most other statistical and machine learning methods.

## 7.2 Basis Functions and Expansions

A `basis function`, or `linear basis expansion` ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)) is essentially the taking of a function of variable (or matrix, in this case) `X`, which we can express quite generally,

`y`[`i`] = `β`[0] + `β`[1]`b`[1](`x`[`i`]) + `β`[2]`b`[2](`x`[`i`]) + `β`[3]`b`[3](`x`[`i`]) + `β`[4]`b`[4](`x`[`i`])+   ⋅  ⋅ ⋅   + `β`[`p`]`b`[`p`](`x`[`i`]) + `ε`[`i`]

(7.3)

Note that the above is not polynomial regression; none of the (`x`[`i`]) are raised to exponents other than unity. The actual basis functions, which are functions of `x`[`i`], are `b`[1] through `b`[`p`]. For example, `β`[1]`b`[1](`x`[`i`]) is the same as we would classically have in least-squares in that we are weighting `x`[`i`] by the regression parameter `β`[1], but to be more precise, what is being weighted is not `x`[`i`]; we are instead weighting a `function` of `x`[`i`], and that function is `b`[1](`x`[`i`]). That is, we first weight `x`[`i`] by `b`[1], then weight that product by `β`[1]. The basis function here is `b`[1](`x`[`i`]).

To understand the above more clearly, consider the classic linear model as a special case of where basis functions occur. What basis function is inherent or “natural” in the classic linear model? To see this, we observe

$$\\begin{array}{l} {y\_{i} = \\beta\_{0} + \\beta\_{1}b\_{1}\\left( x\_{i} \\right) + \\beta\_{2}b\_{2}\\left( x\_{i} \\right) + \\beta\_{3}b\_{3}\\left( x\_{i} \\right) + \\beta\_{4}b\_{4}\\left( x\_{i} \\right) + \\,\\, \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot \\,\\, + \\beta\_{p}b\_{p}\\left( x\_{i} \\right) + \\varepsilon\_{i}} \\\\ {y\_{i} = \\beta\_{0} + \\beta\_{1}b\_{1}\\left( x\_{i} \\right)} \\\\ {y\_{i} = \\beta\_{0} + \\beta\_{1}b\_{1}\\left( x\_{i} \\right)} \\\\ {y\_{i} = \\beta\_{0} + \\beta\_{1}\\left( 1 \\right)\\left( x\_{i} \\right)} \\\\ {y\_{i} = \\beta\_{0} + \\beta\_{1}\\left( x\_{i} \\right)} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

and thus we end up with the classic one-predictor linear model. The basis function, `b`[1](`x`[`i`]) is simply `x`[`i`] with `b`[1] implied or set to 1.

Let’s now consider a primitive though arbitrary basis function simply to extend on this simple definition. Instead of `b`[1](`x`[`i`]) = (1)(`x`[`i`]), suppose the basis `b`[1] were equal to 2, making `b`[1](`x`[`i`]) = (2)(`x`[`i`]). Note that none of this is related to the slope `β`[1]; `the basis function here is simply an “action” directly on` `x`[`i`] `before the partial regression slope is applied to it`. The question for us then, is what should the basis function comprise of? There are many possibilities, and the choice of a basis function will be motivated by the type of model we are considering. It doesn’t take long to realize that one could quite easily write an entire book titled `Basis Functions for Prediction`, which would encompass numerous possibilities and statistical models, which would include simple and multiple 298linear regression, polynomial regression, step functions, regression splines, analysis of variance, and a whole gamut of others. The common thread in all of these models is that they are `identified by their bases`, that is, their transformations of `x`[`i`] `before` weighting by least-squares or other technique; that is, before weighting by the parameter estimates in the given model.

### 7.2.1 Basis Functions and Locality

Among the chief benefits of basis functions is that they allow us to model the data at a more `local level`, much more local than polynomial regression ever could. The use of basis functions also allows us to model the relationship between `y` and `x` nonlinearly by adopting nonlinear basis functions. Because the basis function occurs `inside` of the expression “`β`[1]`b`[1](`x`[`i`])”, we could, in theory, allow `b`[1] to be a whole host of nonlinear functions ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)), which include `Gaussian basis functions` such as the following,

$$\\phi\_{j}\\left( x \\right) = \\exp\\left\\{ \\frac{\\left( x - \\mu\_{j} \\right)^{2}}{2s^{2}} \\right\\}$$

(7.4)

or a `sigmoidal basis function`,

$$\\phi\_{j}\\left( x \\right) = \\sigma\\left( \\frac{x - \\mu\_{j}}{s} \\right)$$

(7.5)

among others, where `s` is the standard deviation in the aforementioned functions. The basis functions we have surveyed thus far have been all `fixed` beforehand; that is, they are not amenable or adaptable to the data. The curse of dimensionality will also limit the utility of fixed basis functions ([Bishop, 2006](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000032)). It will thus be advantageous to consider basis functions that are not fixed. We will encounter such basis functions, that is, those that are `adaptive` or `recursive`, when we survey neural networks later in the book and emphasize the connection between the regression methods of this chapter and neural network approaches.

> `A basis function, or linear basis expansion, weights the predictors in a regression model. Depending on the basis function chosen, this may allow for a more “local” examination of the fit at that particular point or region in the range of predictor values, or, may be limited to a more global representation of the data. In regression, this weighting occurs before weighting by the relevant partial regression slope. The basis functions employed in this chapter are fixed and hence can be quite limiting. Neural network basis functions are generally more adaptive to the data.`

### 7.2.2 Neural Networks as a Basis Expansion: Generalizing the Concept

We mentioned above that we could title an entire book about basis functions or more precisely, `basis expansions`. We emphasized some of the more popular “special cases” of what we would include in such a book, namely simple and multiple regression, and so on. This was meant to 299emphasize how general basis expansions actually are and how many statistical methods can be understood or conceived in terms of basis expansions. Indeed, neural networks can be considered as a basis expansion as well and yet another special case of this wider framework.

In a classic neural network, as we will see later in the book, we take `functions of functions`, otherwise known as `composite functions` from elementary mathematics. Taking composite functions allows us to implement a similar idea of a `basis expansion` as we did just earlier. The general concept we need is that of a `feature extractor` (Murray, 2022):

`f`(`x`;`θ`) = `W``ϕ`(`x`) + `a`

(7.6)

The parameters in the above are `θ` = (`W`,`a`) and if we were to write the classic functional equation, we would omit `ϕ` altogether. The key to the above, however, is the inclusion of `ϕ` which in our earlier regression example was `β`[1]`b`[1](`x`[`i`]) where `ϕ` = `b`[1]. That is,

$$\\begin{array}{l} {f\\left( x;\\theta \\right) = W\\phi\\left( x \\right) + a} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = Wb\_{i}\\left( x \\right) + a} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

where we now denote `b`[1] as `b`[`i`] to eliminate any confusion in notation. What is the benefit to writing `f`(`x`;`θ`) this way? The answer is its `generality`. The more general form for `f`(`x`;`θ`) allows `ϕ` to be a more `arbitrary` and `general` function. More to the point, we are now permitting the feature extractor to have its own designated parameter, `θ`[2]:

`f`(`x`;`θ`) = `W``ϕ`(`x`;`θ`[2]) + `a`

(7.7)
