
As mentioned in the previous chapter, the foundations of statistics and machine learning are not really in mathematics. Mathematics only serves as the *vehicle* by which such concepts are communicated. For instance, in a simple linear regression problem, the “idea” is to minimize error in prediction. That’s the *concept*. To operationalize the concept requires more than words or a general idea; it requires *precise symbolic definition and representation*, that is, *mathematics*. The two are not one and the same. And while statistics and mathematics are challenging in their own right, the more significant challenge as it concerns the applied scientist is to understand *why* the given mathematics is being used in the given context. This helps to motivate the big-picture ideas behind the mathematics.

As an example, *kernels*, while all the rage in modern machine learning, are in fact quite old and not new to linear algebra and can be used in a variety of contexts well beyond machine learning. But why use them at all? What is the general “theme” underlying their use? The general big-picture idea to using them is to both facilitate computation while also providing a shorthand to approximating functions in higher dimensions. The underlying mathematics allows us to define what this looks like *symbolically*, but understanding *how*, *why*, and *when* to use kernels is the more “global” picture that is most important to grasp. “Big-picture” understanding is what is needed most and which is most often lacking from typical undergraduate *and* graduate courses, especially in quantitative courses targeted to applied scientists. Students need to be taught *why*, not just *how*. When one understands the “why” the how either reveals itself or can be dug up. Learning the *how* at the 61expense of the *why* is not pedagogically meaningful, yet sadly is often encouraged in the teaching of students. Students are not *robots*; they are *creative minds* and should be educated as such.

The technical “underground” of statistics, multivariate statistics, machine learning, and data science more generally is typically found in what are often regarded as the three “pillar” areas of mathematics: *probability and statistics*, *linear algebra*, and *calculus and optimization*. Indeed, it is not out of the ordinary for a statistician or researcher to simultaneously be somewhat of a “mini expert” in linear algebra or calculus, or both, since so much of linear models and multivariate modeling has its technical basis in these areas. As an example, consider the topic of *eigenvalues* and *eigenvectors*. These objects show up throughout statistics and machine learning, yet one can also study them in their own right, far removed from any applied statistical use. *They are mathematical objects in and of themselves*. As discussed at the outset of this book, the researcher or scientist, or more generally the “user” of these methods must be continuously aware of how much of a “sub-area” to study while also keeping focused on the primary area of their investigation. For example, too much of a focus on linear algebra eventually detracts from their applied experimental science, whatever that specialization may be (e.g., biology, psychology, forestry, chemistry, etc.).

## 2.1 Mathematical Previews vs. the “Appendix” Approach

### 2.1.1 Why Previews Are Better

Too often requisite mathematical underpinnings in books like this are given either as a disconnected review of fundamentals or as an appendix. All the “good stuff” is packed away in the appendix for the reader to look up as needed. This approach is limiting, however, as it fails to connect the material to the more substantive areas of the text. Our approach is to provide an overview of these technical fundamentals but most importantly, *preview where we will see them again throughout the book*. This last point is especially important. It does little good for the reader to learn or review a bunch of seemingly “abstract” mathematics if it is not properly *motivated*. At least not in an *applied* sense. Of course, the reader may not always readily fully appreciate the preview as it will be an incomplete description of the new concept (if it is indeed new to the reader); however, it will still serve the purpose of exposing the concepts to the reader as a preview of where such mathematics are used in statistics and machine learning. These early foundations help make understanding the pillars of statistics and machine learning (probability, linear algebra, calculus) a more *continuous* process rather than a *discrete* one. In this sense, the foundations *are* the method, but one is largely unaware of this until they see the foundations “in motion” or “at work” when studying the method later in the book, then returns back to these earlier pages to better recognize the connections that were being previewed.

As an example, the concept of a *linear combination*, taught in the abstract, may come across as an arcane and pointless symbolic mathematical object, until one realizes that so much of multivariate statistics and machine learning are about these very objects. If the reader is coming to the book with virtually no prior experience, then they will not appreciate our preview of where linear combinations will be used later in the book. On the other hand, if they are coming to the book with such prior experience, they will likely immediately recognize the connection which may help “tie” together concepts for that reader.

As another example, understanding the problems associated with *linear dependence* in a regression problem will only truly make sense once we get to linear regression, even though the concept of linear dependence is introduced and previewed in the current chapter. Books like this one are written in a more or less linear fashion, but *the understanding of these subjects is not always linear*. Chapters later in the book may help make something “click” in earlier chapters, and the reader should be pedagogically open to these types of connections.62

### 2.1.2 About Proofs

We avoid formal proofs in this chapter and throughout the book. However, this does not imply that basic proofs cannot be constructed based on the details we do present. Proofs are often a lot “closer” in proximity to the reader’s capacity than they may at first realize, especially if they are truly grasping the material.

As an example, it is well known that the Pearson correlation coefficient is bounded by limits of −1 and +1. Why this is so we explain in the book, though the explanation would not constitute a “formal proof.” However, if desired, a proof can be constructed from pieces of the explanation, and this typically makes for a great exercise. As gifted educator Morris Kline has said numerous times in reference to teaching calculus, it is better to understand the *intuition* behind the object than to learn a rigorous proof, at least on first reading and exposure. Indeed, historically, physicists and other scientists have relied on this approach quite heavily in the invention of new concepts. The mathematical and statistically specialized communities rightfully have high and very rigorous standards for what denotes a “formal proof,” (they should be applauded for their appreciation of rigor), but it is important for the reader to recognize that they themselves are sometimes not that far off from that “ideal” formal justification in at least some cases. *Approximations are okay*, so long as one realizes they are not the be-all and end-all and may lack formality. So long as the approximation does justice to the concept, you’re fine. If the approximation leads you astray from the concept, then that’s a problem.
