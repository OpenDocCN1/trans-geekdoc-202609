
Missing data is sometimes discussed under the more general topic of “cleaning data” in textbooks. *Cleaning data* typically refers to not only replacing or imputing missing data but also correcting for erroneous entries and detecting other abnormalities in the data file. For these latter uses, data cleaning is fine, but for replacing missing data, as mentioned, the analyst should proceed with caution. Empirical data is *experience*; it is the “stuff” that is of paramount importance in your entire analysis; it should not have to be “cleaned” that much. If you find yourself spending lots of time “cleaning” a data file, you may want to reconsider how your data was collected.58

## 1.26 Qualitative Approaches to Data Analysis

The topics of this book are from statistics and machine learning, and hence are considered *quantitative* or *numerical* in nature. Quantitative approaches dominate science. However, *qualitative* approaches can be just as useful and in some cases even more fruitful. Such approaches are often used in the social sciences as well as humanities (e.g., history, anthropology, etc.). A philosophical attempt to distinguish between what is “qualitative” vs. “quantitative” usually turns to “mush” rather quickly and there ends up being more similarity between the approaches than differences (numbers are, after all, *qualitative categories*). As discussed earlier in the book, categorization can be a dangerous thing, epistemologically. Most forms of knowledge and language end up being about *patterns* and *structures* among objects, and whether we measure them as quantitative or qualitative becomes a side issue. Quantitative approaches are usually regarded as more rigorous because numbers and mathematical operations have less flexibility than other epistemological objects or structures.

The qualitative tradition, in general, prefers to not reduce information to numbers. There is a very good reason for this, as numbers may actually mask and detract from underlying processes if not used correctly. As noted by Carrera-Fernández et al. ([2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000054)), who discuss qualitative research,

> in which the researcher trusts textual data more than numerical data and analyzes this data in its textual form instead of transforming it into numbers for analysis, with the objective of understanding the meaning of human action … not only textual data but also visual, verbal, bodily, audio and spatial data are used … data are analyzed in their original dimension, without being previously transformed into numbers.
>
> (p. 3)

Of course, a qualitative approach to determining the proper flap angle on an airplane at take-off is out of the question, unless of course the qualitative categories are precise numerical measurements. Numerical accuracy in this area of investigation is imperative. Note it is still qualitative, only that the qualitative nature is one of precise mathematical categories. In investigations where numbers may not represent underlying essences, for instance the study of poetry, literature, or the study of the soul, history, and so on, quantitative approaches may actually *detract* from such underlying essences. Quantitative approaches, especially in the social sciences, are largely overrated. In certain domains, they give the *appearance* of rigor, but underneath the numbers is too often a weak foundation. Rollo May’s *The Meaning of Anxiety* ([2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000242)) may tell us more about anxiety, its *essence*, than this or that statistical model applied to imperfectly measured anxiety data. The important point here is to keep an open mind and not discount qualitative approaches simply because they are not “math heavy.” *Not everything is reducible to numbers*. However, not adopting such a reductionist philosophy can make the communication of qualitative characterizations much more difficult. Perhaps in the end what is needed is a proper mixture of approaches in accounting for complex phenomena.59
