
As we will see when we study neural networks, this allowance of the `feature extractor` to have its own parameter vector will generalize our current idea of basis expansion to much wider possibilities. The general idea is to specify `functions of functions`, which is what neural networks are all about.

We can see then that basis functions are an entirely `general concept`, and the choice of a basis function is essentially theoretically limitless. In the earliest of courses in introductory statistics, basis functions are indeed present in `t-tests`, ANOVA, regression, and so on; however they are simply not revealed and are usually `implicit` within the given method or procedure. Interactions in ANOVA, for instance, are yet another example of a basis expansion. `Basis expansions essentially enrich the feature space.` Support vector machines are yet another example of a basis expansion, which we survey later in the book.

In what follows and in returning to our discussion of nonlinearity, we focus on a particularly common basis function or expansion that will prove useful for modeling nonlinear regression, that of `regression splines`.

### 7.2.3 Regression Splines and the Concept of a “Knot”

We have discussed so far the benefit of leaving global functions behind and fitting more local functions to data. The general idea is to fit a model locally, or “zoomed in” to different parts of the data. `Regression splines` are a common way to get at the local structure of the data in a regression context.

But how can we define local subsets of the data range? An easy and common way to do so is via `knots`. A `knot` is something that divides areas of the data on `x[i]` into intervals, where the coefficients in the model `change` as to reflect a change in the data trend. In general, `the greater the number 300knots, the greater the flexibility of the curve`. As the number of knots increases without bound to infinity, we eventually obtain perfect fit. If we use too many knots, however, we are at risk of overfitting. The selection of the right number is thus crucial and is typically made by cross-validation or possibly simply by visualization. `Smoothing splines`, on the other hand, often use a maximal or near-maximal number of knots instead of that number being arbitrarily selected by the user ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)).

Recall that in the previous chapter we had chosen the tuning parameter in ridge, lasso, and elastic net by cross-validation to implement regularization. The choice of the number of knots can also be considered a form of regularization, and thus the number of knots can be thought of as a tuning parameter. That is, `by reducing the number of knots, thus reducing the chance of overfitting, we are essentially regularizing the model`. Indeed, this whole discussion of splines can actually be formally recast into the wider category of regularization (see [Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), pp. 167–168 for a discussion). It is generally recommended that using 4 to 5 knots equally distributed, say at percentiles 20, 40, 60, etc., is a useful first strategy for their implementation in a typical regression problem ([Fox, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106)).

### 7.2.4 Conceptualizing Regression Splines

How should we conceptualize the idea of a regression spline? Regression splines are best represented as a special case of the more general idea of basis functions or basis expansions that we just surveyed. Recall the basis representation developed earlier:

`y[i] = β[0] + β[1]b[1](x[1]) + β[2]b[2](x[2]) + β[3]b[3](x[3]) + β[4]b[4](x[4]) + ... + β[p]b[p](x[p]) + ε[i]`

where `b[1]` through `b[p]` are the basis functions. That is, they are transformations of `x[i]` `before` the relevant weighting by `β[1]` through `β[p]`. The placement of the knots can, in theory at least, be arbitrarily chosen based on shifts in the function. However, as mentioned, a uniform, equally spaced distribution of knots is usually the more common and initial strategy ([James et al., 2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)).

Cross-validation can be used to determine the best number of knots, that which produces the smallest residual sums of squares in the test error. `Cubic splines` are often preferred because these naturally ensure the function is continuous and has continuous first and second derivatives at each knot ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)). The number of knots divides the data into one or more `bins`. For example, 4 knots divide the data into 4 + 1 bins. If we increased the number of knots to their limit, we would have the number of knots equal to the number of values for `x[i]` or data points. Using `n` knots to represent `x[i]` `unique` values results in what is generally known as a `smoothing spline`. Since there is typically not a fixed number of knots determined `a priori`, smoothing splines are usually regarded as `nonparametric`, as the number of splines is a function of the number of data points or values of `x[i]` in the data ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259), p. 395).

As noted by [Fox (2016)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106), spline regression models can also be considered a “bridge” between the `linear` and `generalized linear model`. In fact, the `generalized additive model` is one that extends the regression spline to the case of multidimensional inputs, assuming an additive form (i.e., no interactions). The generalized additive model can then be extended beyond regression and into classification, in which case one has the full-blown generalized linear model ([Murphy, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)).

### 7.2.5 Problem with Splines and Imposing Constraints

The great thing about splines is that we can be as flexible as we wish. However, too much flexibility entails a cost. For example, consider [Figure 7.4](#c7.xhtml#f7.4). In this case, we are `too` flexible. That is, `we have not imposed enough constraints on the knots`. To remedy this, we begin imposing constraints, such as continuity from left to right to ensure there cannot be a jump (James et al., [2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)). Recall what “imposing a constraint” means; it means we would like the curve to fit, but without, in this case of continuity, a `jump`. That is, we have taken the “jump” out of the possibility in the fitting or estimation of the curve; this is the `constraint` we are imposing.
