
Now, consider if we fit the above linear model to this data. Would it fit well? No, and if it does not fit well, it likely will not *generalize* well to test data. To use statistical jargon, even a simple linear model on this data can be considered *overfitting*, and overfitting rarely leads to good generalizability. Hence, one solution is to simplify the model so that it no longer overfits and generalizes better. One way to simplify the model would be to simply constrain the slope parameter to equal 0, or equivalently, not estimate it:

$$\\begin{array}{l} {y\_{i} = \\beta\_{0} + \\beta\_{1}x\_{i} + \\varepsilon\_{i}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\beta\_{0} + \\left( 0 \\right)x\_{i} + \\varepsilon\_{i}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\beta\_{0} + \\varepsilon\_{i}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

(6.2)

This “simpler” model is now just an estimate of the intercept (or “bias” term, to use neural network terminology) along with some error, `ε[i]`. Simple as this example may be, *we have just engaged in an act of regularization of a statistical model*. The effect of simplifying the model has another implication; it expresses our belief that the true function is much less complicated than the linear function in this case. [Hastie et al. (2009, p. 34)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145) describe this idea beautifully:

> Regularization methods, express our prior belief that the type of functions we seek exhibit a certain type of smooth behavior, and indeed can usually be cast in a Bayesian framework.267

Their point is that on a technical level, penalties (or *regularization*) reflect to some extent *prior belief*, which is a Bayesian idea. In our example of the line turned to intercept only, the penalty is implicit, and reflects our belief that the true function underlying the data is not as “complicated” as including the slope would suggest. It’s much simpler in that the intercept suffices to account for the true function. This is the *idea* or *spirit* of regularization.

> *Regularization can be very informal in practice and can be as simple as deciding to not model the slope in a simple linear regression problem. From a Bayesian point of view, we are imposing our belief that the true functional form underlying the data is simpler than hypothesizing a non-horizontal slope in the regression*.

## 6.1.1 Regularization in Regression and Beyond

Consider for a moment the extent to which the above basic and simple concept might generalize to other modeling activities and methods. For example, when we constrain a parameter estimate in structural equation modeling so as to not overfit and hence improve generalizability, we are, in effect, engaged in model regularization. Likewise, when we decide on a simpler principal component solution instead of a more complex one for the purpose of generalization (such as in `principal components regression`, for instance), we are also regularizing the model. As we will see later, there are a variety of ways of regularizing a neural network, perhaps the most obvious or natural way by reducing the number of hidden layers or the number of nodes. Even `early stopping` of the iterative algorithm in neural networks constitutes a simple way to regularize a neural network.

In general then, anytime a model or method is simplified in some sense, it denotes an act of regularizing that model if such is done in an effort for it to generalize better to test data. In terms of regression, there are three more advanced and common ways of imposing a regularization parameter that go beyond our simple example of constraining the slope above. These include `ridge`, `lasso`, and `elastic net` regression. With the “spirit” of regularization now in place, we now survey these methods.

## 6.2 Ridge Regression

We begin with `ridge regression` because it is among the oldest and most significant and influential of the regularization techniques. These techniques often fall under the umbrella of “shrinkage,” where partial regression coefficients are driven toward or to values of 0. At first glance, it may seem questionable why you might ever want to “shrink” coefficients toward 0, but in the end, shrunken coefficients can greatly simplify, and hence regularize, a model. The ridge penalty (or similar, such as the `lasso`) is also generally known as `weight decay` and the `L2` parameter norm penalty and ridge regression is commonly known as `Tikhonov regularization` in many circles ([Goodfellow et al., 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)). The lasso penalty is commonly known as `L1` regularization. Where the ridge penalty is in squared units, that is, a `quadratic` penalty, the lasso penalty is in absolute value units and is hence nonquadratic. The difference in penalties can have important consequences, especially for data in which the number of cases is greater than the number of variables. For an example of this latter point, see Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 660). An advantage of `L1` penalization is that such allows for feature selection through driving weights `to`, rather than simply `toward`, 0.

Ridge (and lasso, elastic net) can be applied to situations where one might have literally thousands of predictors, and hence shrinking becomes a good strategy for simplifying the model and making it more interpretable. However, just as regularization is a wide-ranging concept, so is 268shrinkage. Shrinkage is merely one way in which a model can be regularized. Be sure this is clear, as there is often some degree of misunderstanding that regularization is `equivalent` to shrinkage, but this is not the case. Subset selection, stepwise regression, forward regression, and even less formal techniques are all methods designed to regularize a model as well. *A regularization method need not be associated with shrinkage*.

As mentioned, another benefit to shrinking parameter estimates relates to the ratio of sample size `n` to the number of predictors `p`. If `n` is not that much larger than `p`, there can result inflated variability in the least-squares fit, which can result in overfitting and possibly poor predictions of future observations not used in the training. What is more, if `p` > `n`, there is no longer at all a reliable least-squares estimate, since the variance will be infinite. Under this situation, there exists simply too many ways to project the response variable onto the set of predictors ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)). Shrinkage is a tremendously valuable technique in such situations, since by shrinking the estimated coefficients in these cases, both when `n` = `p` and `p` > `n`, we can essentially reduce the variance at the cost of a negligible increase in bias.

### 6.2.1 Mathematics of Ridge Regression

To understand how ridge regression works, first recall ordinary least-squares regression. In least-squares regression, `β[0]`, `β[1]`, ..., `β[p]` are estimated via `β̂` = (`X`^(T)`X`)^(−1)`X`^(T)`y` such as to minimize the residual sums of squares (`RSS`) function,

$$RSS = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( {y\_{i} - \\beta\_{0} - {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{p}{\\beta\_{j}x\_{ij}}}} \\right)^{2}}$$

(6.3)

Not surprisingly, in ridge regression we also seek to minimize a quantity, but instead through the ordinary least-squares (`OLS`) estimator

`β̂` = (`X`^(T)`X`)^(−1)`X`^(T)`y`

(6.4)

the ridge estimator adds a small quantity `λ` to the trace of `X`^(T)`X` such that

`β̂[ridge]` = (`X`^(T)`X` + `λ` `I`)^(−1)`X`^(T)`y`

(6.5)

The quantity minimized in ridge regression is similar to that in least-squares regression, but with an extra term added:

$$RSS = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( {y\_{i} - \\beta\_{0} - {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{p}{\\beta\_{j}x\_{ij}}}} \\right)^{2}} + \\lambda{\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{p}\\beta\_{j}^{2}}$$

(6.6)
