# 11 Principal Components Analysis, Blind Source Separation, and Manifold Learning

DOI: [10.4324/9781003376941-14](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003376941-14)

In this chapter, we survey the method of `principal components analysis`, or “PCA” for short, as well as other related techniques. As we will see, principal components analysis can be interpreted as a special case of more general methods that generally fall under the name of `blind source separation` and `manifold learning`. PCA can be regarded, in fact, as a special case of manifold learning where in the case of PCA the manifold is `linear`. The more general manifold can take on nonlinear shapes and most often the phrase “manifold learning” will refer to `nonlinear dimensionality reduction`. Virtually all dimension-reduction techniques can be crudely classified into `linear` vs. `nonlinear`.

The motivation for PCA is to take a set of observed variables and generate linear combinations of those variables. Substantively, PCA is often advertised as being able to “discover” underlying dimensions of data. However, as we will learn in this chapter, the word “discovery” is potentially misleading and problematic. All that PCA can guarantee, for certain, is to derive linear composites of variables that maximize variance in the original variables. Whether those composites are meaningful in any substantive sense is not a question that can be answered by PCA and is only answerable by the user with reference to scientific theory and the domain of application. What is more, is that maximizing variance in observed variables may not result in dimensions that are in any way `interesting`. This is an important point, as there are many other competing techniques that claim to discover more interesting patterns in data and have their own criteria for doing so other than maximizing variance. In short, `maximizing variance in components does not necessarily imply those components will be of value or interesting in themselves`. They may, from a substantive point of view be entirely meaningless given current theory or application. Indeed, from a scientific point of view, the results of any dimension-reduction or manifold learning technique should be interpreted with caution as it is entirely possible that the given algorithm “causes” interesting geometric patterns or trends that in the end are not supported by the data (Meila & Zhang, [2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000247)).

Principal components is only one of many techniques that seek to reduce the dimensionality of data. That is, PCA is `a` (i.e., only `one`) dimension-reduction technique; it is not `equivalent` to dimension reduction. In its most general sense, dimension reduction may be said to be taking observed data in a high-dimensional context and reducing that data to a lower-dimensional context but ensuring that it still conveys similar information (Nanga et al., [2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000263)). That is, a good dimension-reduction technique should retain essential properties of the original data. If the information contained in the lower dimensions is misleading or at odds with the information at a higher dimension, then the reduction may lead to faulty interpretation. Hence, this is one important reason for understanding the nature of the dimension-reduction technique one is using as the conclusion provided by one technique may differ from the conclusion reached from another. `Dimension reduction is as much a function of the method as it is of the data`. A question worth asking is if the visualization of one technique is different from another, which then is most `correct`? The only answer to this question is that none are “correct”; they simply accomplish different things.395

Most dimension-reduction techniques are nonparametric in nature ([Murray, 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000259)), which essentially implies that they focus heavily on the training data often without attempting directly to generalize to out-of-sample data. These include techniques such as the aforementioned `PCA`, but also `classical multidimensional scaling`, `kernel PCA`, `Isomap`, `Laplacian Eigenmaps` ([Belkin & Niyogi, 2003](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000026)), and `t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)`, `non-negative matrix factorization` (`NMF`), among others. Alternative names for PCA include `Karhunen-Loeve transform` ([Karhunen, 1947](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000181)), `empirical orthogonal functions`, `common principal components analysis` ([Flury](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000105), 1998), and others. The different names are largely due to the same method being developed in various disciplines or as special cases or extensions of classic PCA. For instance, the Karhunen-Loeve transform features the option of using class information on data before applying PCA to each class, then takes an average of class PCA ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)). This is similar to `common PCA` where similar components are hypothesized to arise in different data sets. Hence, not all names are `identical` to classic PCA, but at their core are still “PCA.” Once more with jargon in statistics and machine learning, one can know a given method well but fail to “recognize” it in a given field because it is named something different!

Many dimension-reduction techniques are justified based on a fundamental theorem, the `Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma`. It holds that for a number of points in high-dimensional space, such points can be mapped to a much lower dimension with an upper limit to how much pairwise distances can be distorted. That is, the lemma essentially holds that dimension reduction can be performed while still preserving fundamental properties of the data. For further details on the lemma, consult Mohri et al. ([2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000253), p. 354).

## 11.1 Dimension Reduction and Jargon

As we have mentioned, what can make dimension reduction challenging is that many of the same techniques have evolved in various scientific fields under somewhat different names and for different purposes. For instance, in psychometrics, one may wish to produce orthogonal linear combinations to represent attitudes of respondents, whereas in more physical applications such as images in information theory, this same idea translates to the `compression` of an image. The same technique can almost have unrecognizable and diametrically opposed applications. While this can be said of virtually any technique and not just dimension-reduction ones, the jargon is especially “rich” in dimension analysis.

However, just as is true for simple arithmetical operations such as addition, it is not `what` is being added that matters when it comes to understanding addition; it matters more in understanding what addition `does`. The technique must be studied, at least to some extent, from an abstract point of view exclusive of application. Only in that sense can one understand what the technique actually accomplishes without being “swayed” by what we “wish” it to accomplish to satisfy our `Freudian id` on this or that data set.
