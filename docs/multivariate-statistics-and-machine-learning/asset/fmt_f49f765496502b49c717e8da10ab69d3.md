## 4.1 The Classic ANOVA Model

In classic ANOVA, we seek to evaluate null hypotheses of the kind186
`H[0]`: `μ[1]` = `μ[2]` = `μ[3]` = `μ[4]`
(4.1)
against alternative hypotheses that somewhere among the means is at least one pairwise difference; for example,
`H[1]`: `μ[1]` ≠ `μ[2]`, `μ[2]` = `μ[3]`, `μ[3]` = `μ[4]`
Of course, we usually do not typically have knowledge of the *population means* since in most cases populations are extremely large. If we do have the populations at our disposal, then evaluating the null hypothesis is a simple matter such that it is enough to simply look at the population means and see if they are equal or not and no statistical inference is required. This point should not be overlooked; if you find yourself conducting a significance test on *population* *means*, then something is amiss in your understanding of statistical inference! To reiterate, if you have the population parameters, the “game” is done; you can simply observe what the summary statistics on the population have to say and conclude population difference on this descriptive basis alone.

In the more realistic case where we only have sample data, the job is to evaluate the probability of observing sample differences of the kind we have observed under the assumption that the null hypothesis is tenable, or “true.” As a simple example, suppose we obtained the following four sample means:
$${\\overline{y}}\_{1} = 10,{\\overline{y}}\_{2} = 15,{\\overline{y}}\_{3} = 20,{\\overline{y}}\_{4} = 21$$
The question we wish to ask is, *What is the probability of observing mean differences of this kind under the assumption of the null hypothesis*? More succinctly, we wish to address the probability of the data given the null hypothesis, that of `p(D/H[0])`. If that probability is small (for example, less than 0.05 as the typical significance level), it suggests differences of the kind we have observed are unlikely to have occurred under the null hypothesis and we begin to doubt the tenability of the null. We may begin to doubt it so much that we reject the null and infer the *statistical alternative hypothesis* (more on this shortly) that somewhere among the means there is reason to believe there is at least one pairwise difference between means.

The outcome of the ANOVA procedure culminates in determining the `p-value` for evaluating `p(D/H[0])`. As the reader may recall, the `p-value` is determined by partitioning sources of variation *between* and *within* in ANOVA, producing variances from these sources, the so-called *mean squares* of ANOVA. Take note that mean squares simply imply an average (i.e., mean) of sorts, where sums of squares are divided by degrees of freedom, hence the name “mean squares.” From this, we formulate a ratio (`F-ratio`) of the variance *between* relative to the variance *within* groups:
$$F = \\frac{\\sigma^{2}{}\_{Between}}{\\sigma^{2}{}\_{Within}}$$
(4.2)
The expectation for `F` is approximately equal to 1.0. More precisely, the exact expectation `E` is equal to187
$$E\\left( F \\right) = \\frac{df\\,\\text{error}}{df\\,\\text{error} - 2}$$
where `df error` are the degrees of freedom for the error term in the ANOVA summary table, equal to `N - J` where `N` is the total number of data points and `J` is the number of groups or levels on the independent variable.

If `σ²[Between]` > `σ²[Within]` more so that we would have expected by random error or chance alone, then this counts as evidence against the null hypothesis and we reject it in favor of the statistical alternative. Each ANOVA has accompanied with it a summary table that shows the partition of the various sums of squares and the production of mean squares. In the summary table, we note the partition of sources of variation into *between* and *within*. The sums of squares for between, equal to
$$\\sum\\limits\_{j}^{J}{n\_{j}\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{j} - \\overline{y}. \\right)^{2}}$$
is the sum of squared deviations of group means, ${\\overline{y}}\_{j}$, minus the overall or “grand mean” of the data, $\\overline{y}.$, where `n[j]` before the squared term is simply the number of observations per group, in this case assumed to be equal. Deviations from the mean are squared since any sum of deviations from the mean will necessarily equal zero, that is, $\\sum\\limits\_{j}^{J}{n\_{j}\\left( {\\overline{y}}\_{j} - \\overline{y}. \\right)}$ would always equal zero and formulating the aforementioned `F-ratio` would be impossible. By squaring deviations, we allow for `n[j](\overline{y}_j - \overline{y}.)^2` to increase in magnitude under the condition that group means ${\\overline{y}}\_{j}$ differ from the overall mean, $\\overline{y}.$. Hence, the between sums of squares in the partition is of most interest to the scientist because, all else equal, a larger value than not hints that there is a difference between being in one treatment group versus being in another.188
