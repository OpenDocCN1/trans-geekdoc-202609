
Measurement of data is only as good as such a process is reliable and valid. Reliability generally refers to the idea of consistency just as it does in ordinary speech. Informally, a measurement process is reliable, in one sense, if repeating the measurement produces the same or at least *similar* recording if the attribute under consideration has not changed. For example, measuring your height today and then tomorrow should provide the same (or at least nearly so) measurement. Such a measurement process is deemed reliable if it does. If your height measurement today was 60 inches and tomorrow 65, and assuming you didn’t experience a growth spurt overnight, the measurement process would be deemed quite unreliable.43

Following [McDonald (1999)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000244), we assume that the population correlation `ρ` between `T` and `E` is equal to 0, that is, `ρ[TE] = 0`. By the *variance-sum* *law* which holds that the variance of uncorrelated variables can be summed, the variance of `Y`, now the observed score, is a sum of the variances of both `T` and `E`, that is,

`σ[Y]² = σ[T]² + σ[E]²`

(1.3)

From (1.3) we can deduce that `σ[T]² ≤ σ[Y]²` and `σ[E]² ≤ σ[Y]²`, which implies

$$\\rho = \\frac{\\sigma\_{T}^{2}}{\\sigma\_{Y}^{2}} = \\frac{\\sigma\_{T}^{2}}{\\sigma\_{T}^{2} + \\sigma\_{E}^{2}}$$

(1.4)

and where it can be easily shown that `ρ` is bound between 0 and 1 where `ρ` is called the *reliability coefficient* of `Y`. There are different forms of reliability. We survey one of the most popular ones now, that of internal consistency.

### 1.15.3.1 Coefficient Alpha
The split-half reliability is the correlation between two halves of a test corrected to full length by the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula ([Cortina, 1993](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000069)),

$$r\_{sh} = \\frac{2r\_{12}}{1 + r\_{12}}$$

(1.5)

### 1.15.3.2 Test-Retest Reliability
Test-retest reliability generally refers to the consistency or stability of a measure across two or more time points. It is generally assessed using the correlation coefficient. For example, suppose we administer a test at *time A* then correlate that to test results at *time B*. To the extent that the correlation is strong indicates that reliability is likewise tenable.

Special software functions are usually not needed to assess test-retest reliability as it can easily be evaluated using a Pearson (or other, such as intraclass) correlation coefficient.

## 1.15.4 Validity
The validity of a measuring instrument is an assessment of whether that instrument measures what it is designed to measure. For instance, a tire pressure gauge that reports the correct tire pressure is a valid measure of tire pressure. Flight instruments on an airplane are valid if they report correct readings of such things as speed, altitude, and so on. An intelligence quotient (IQ) test is considered valid if it actually measures something called “intelligence.”

How do we know altitude readings on an aircraft measure something called *altitude*? We know because engineers correlate such readings to measurement instruments having readings that are already known to measure altitude. Hence, when a new altitude measure is developed, it is a fairly easy matter to correlate it to well-known measures. Likewise with a temperature gauge, you can be quite confident that you have 36 psi (*pounds per square inch*) in your tires measured by your new gauge if your old and reliable gauge also measures 36 psi.

The measurement of IQ or similar psychological constructs is not as easily validated because, for one, there is no agreement on not only *how* IQ is best measured, but also whether even something called “intelligence” exists! It can be exceedingly difficult to validate something for which there is no agreed-upon criterion such as speed, altitude, or tire pressure. Psychological sciences have attempted to define IQ rigorously, but such definitions ultimately come down to what experts in the area wish to call *intelligence*. Of course, the criteria are not simply “made up” or contrived. Far from it. Experts spend a great deal of time pondering over what kinds of items reflect processing ability and such, and hence IQ tests are often based on rather strong theoretical underpinnings. Despite this, “capturing” the essence of IQ with a psychometric test that features subjective responses some believe to be an impossible task. If, however, we can agree on a particular test as a *criterion*, then validating newer instruments can be correlated to the original criterion, just as the aforementioned altitude readings can be correlated to more well-known readings.

It should be emphasized as well that at a philosophical level, the criterion for something like IQ and that of altitude is *always* human judgment. We are the ones who “invented” the idea of altitude, just as we are the ones who invented the idea of intelligence. Of course, that may be exaggerating the point slightly, but it is simply to say that *constructs are always constructed*. It just so happens that some constructs are extremely well-agreed upon and have lasted centuries without much disagreement, while others (such as IQ) are more novel and their existence is even subject to debate.

There are different kinds of validity which all more or less rely on the correlation coefficient in some form or another in the establishment of evidence. For instance, if an instrument has *convergent validity*, it should correlate highly with other tests purported to measure the same criterion, 45whereas if the instrument has *divergent validity* then it should not correlate with tests that purport to measure different constructs. For an excellent overview of validity, see [McDonald (1999)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000244).
