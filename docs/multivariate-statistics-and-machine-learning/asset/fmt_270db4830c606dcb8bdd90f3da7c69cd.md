
The field of data analysis and data science can easily feel overwhelming. Why? *Because you need to know everything!* At least it can feel that way. As has been discussed many different times by a number of authors, data science lies at the intersection or heart of so many specialty areas. To understand deep learning, for instance, you must have a strong grasp of statistical principles. But the machinery (not necessarily the *concepts*) underlying statistics is mathematics and if you venture into more foundational issues within mathematics itself you will soon encounter philosophical and historical issues, so you should to some extent be aware of those areas as well. But we’re not done. Not even close. You also need to be a computer scientist to some extent so you can apply all of this theory to the computer, understand the strengths and limitations of the computing you are doing, and be comfortable enough with code that you can improve or tweak a program if needed, or at minimum know if it needs tweaking and where to look for answers. So, so far, you’re to some extent a *mathematician*, *statistician*, *philosopher*, *historian*, and *computer scientist*. That’s a tall order for anyone.

But we’re still not done. Data science is applied to substantive areas such as business or a science (e.g., economics, psychology, sociology, biology, physics, astronomy), so you need to be an expert, to some extent, in the area of application as well. It becomes very difficult to know where and how to apply the technical elements you’re learning if you are unfamiliar with the discipline and the problems that the discipline seeks to solve. If it’s a scientific area, then you are also required to have knowledge and experience in areas of *research design*, *experimental* vs. *non-experimental designs*, *cross-sectional* and *longitudinal models*, and so on. Indeed, as we will discuss in this book, most if not all of machine learning does not make even one mention of design issues, and so it is possible that an expert machine learner may be somewhat naïve about important distinctions such as that between *experimental* vs. *quasi-experimental designs*, and so on. These design issues rear their heads daily in fields such as biology and psychology. Couple all of these areas with the problem of measurement such as in sciences like psychology or economics with latent variables, and you then need to also be an expert at psychometrics or measurement theory more generally. This is all to say that the expertise required to be a good data scientist can feel overwhelming and essentially impossible to ever achieve.

### 1.2.1 Theoretical vs. Applied Statistics and Specialization

There exists a *paradox of specialization*, such that if you focus too much on one area at the expense of others, you then become out of balance. For instance, since the mathematical foundations of 24statistics and data science are largely found in areas of linear algebra, analysis, and optimization, one can easily be tempted to study these areas in some depth, but the more you study, the more you become a specialist in these areas at the expense of the professional you are seeking to become. There is no natural “hierarchy” of knowledge such that if you study the foundations in one area it will naturally make you an expert in others. *What you specialize in makes you a specialist in that area*. [Figure 1.1](#c1.xhtml#f1.1) is a prototype of the well-known Venn diagram of data science showing the intersection of many different areas of expertise. If you are to be a good data scientist it is important to remain focused on the common intersection of the three circles. Veering too much in any direction, while allowing yourself to deepen in that specific specialty, essentially detracts from the objective of becoming a good data scientist.

## 1.3 The Role of Computers

Computer scientists are professionals who study such things as the capacities of computers to compute things, limits on what can be computed, and all the rest of the details regarding computation. However, for our purposes, computing can be seen as a way of getting things done quickly and in an automated fashion. Computers do what we tell them to do, and they do it extremely well, fast, and reliably ([Guttag, 2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000137)). As [Stillwell (2016)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000349) notes, computation has always been a part of mathematics \[and statistics\], but it is only in the past century (1900s) that it became a mathematical *concept*.

As a scientist using multivariate or machine learning methods, computing is an important element in accomplishing your goals. However, most things that require computing, at the level we are talking about in this book at least, can be dug up and searched. How to conduct a spline regression via a computer isn’t nearly as important as understanding the benefits and limitations to conducting a spline regression and what precisely spline regression actually *is*. It is this *understanding* that is paramount, at least in this book. From this vantage point, computing is seen as simply a *means to an end* to getting the job done. Now, of course if your work involves databases of humongous size, then the ability to even compute on such large databases comes into question. Though we do touch on these areas slightly in this book, *computational complexity* is not our chief concern. 25For the most part, we assume that the data you will be working with is of reasonable computational size. This book is not about *data engineering*. It is about statistics, science, and machine learning.

### 1.3.1 The Nature of Algorithms

In discussing the role of computers in today’s climate, it is an ideal time to note that today’s statistical and machine learning “Zeitgeist” seems to be all about *algorithms*, almost as if such things are new and were invented yesterday! Indeed, the very word *algorithm* carries with it a “sexy” appeal among students.

But what is an algorithm exactly? A very general definition is that it is *a method or set of instructions for carrying out a procedure* ([Tanton, 2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000352)). Under this general definition, preparing a meal is an example of an algorithm, just as walking your dog is an algorithm. Driving a car is an algorithm just as brushing your teeth is one. Writing this book was an algorithm. Algorithms are present in virtually every facet of our lives. Quantitatively, an algorithm can be as simple as long division or one could argue that even adding two numbers is an example of an algorithm. Misconceptions of algorithms being “new” to modern data science are just that, *misconceptions*.
