
## Independent Components Analysis

Jolliffe goes on to emphasize that simply because `ICA` is most appropriate in non-Gaussian contexts, this does not imply that classical `PCA` assumes normality, since it does not. One need not make any distributional assumptions to conduct a `PCA` as it is simply a mathematical decomposition of variance. If one chooses to make inferences of components to populations, then that is a different matter that would require corresponding parametric assumptions.

Hence, whereas `PCA` seeks to find or uncover a reduced-rank representation of variables, `ICA` attempts to represent data as independent sub-elements. `ICA` tries to separate the *signals* in one’s data, while `PCA` attempts to “load” the data onto the first few important or “principal” components. These are two quite different objectives. The reader will better appreciate how `ICA` is more similar to factor analysis when we survey factor analysis later in the book. Factor analysis is a proper statistical *model* and thus includes an error term, making it comparable to `ICA`. In `PCA`, no such error term is typically included nor is any statistical “model-like” structure advanced.

> *PCA and ICA are not the same technique. PCA seeks a lower-dimensional linear representation of the variables, whereas ICA seeks to separate the observed data into independent signals. Independence is a stronger property than uncorrelatedness under the assumption of non-Gaussian distributions.*

## 11.4 Probabilistic PCA

In classical `PCA`, there is an absence of an associated probabilistic model for the data. That is, classical `PCA` is not probabilistic in any way; it is merely a mathematical transformation of the variables onto new axes such that it establishes a new *basis* (i.e., one that is orthogonal) from which the data are projected. As many writers on `PCA` have emphasized, `PCA` is not a “model” per se, it is simply a rotation of axes on which data are projected in correspondence to this rotation. Components are not obtained with reference to any kind of density-estimation framework. Be sure to note that in this distinction we are referring to the *construction of the components*, not the *estimation* of the components in the population. As mentioned, inferential procedures for components analysis do exist but are rarely used, with users preferring visualization techniques (e.g., scree plots) and eigenvalue magnitudes as aids to determining how many components to retain. When inference is employed, the inferential aspect of `PCA` makes components analysis probabilistic, but only in that particular sense of inference, not in the sense of actually deriving the components via eigen analysis.

It is possible, however, to derive the `PCA` approach based on a probabilistic formulation even without regard to estimation. Probabilistic `PCA` was first proposed by [Tipping and Bishop (1999)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000361) to provide an alternative approach to `PCA`, one that is probabilistic and thus exists in a density-estimation framework ([Tipping & Bishop, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000361)).

We give the case of *probabilistic PCA*, which can also be regarded as a special case of factor analysis, where `W` is a loading matrix and where the covariance matrix `S` can be written as

`S ∼ W W^T + σ² I`

(11.8)

Notice the noise term `σ²` in the probabilistic model. Classic principal components analysis is a special case of probabilistic `PCA` as `σ²→0`, or at minimum is infinitesimally small and essentially converges to zero. Noise dimensions are also uncorrelated, which above is indicated by `σ² I`, since in the identity matrix only values of 1 lie along the main diagonal and zeroes everywhere else. The inclusion of `σ² I` as the “error term” here instead of only `σ²` simply denotes the fact that we are working in the context of several variables and hence require a matrix representation.
