
Are participants nested within treatment groups? Are the errors within treatment groups expected to be related? *No*. Because of the random assignment to treatment groups, an individual’s score *a priori* is not expected to be related to any other individual’s score. Why is the qualifier “*a priori*” essential here? It is important because *after they undergo treatment*, we might indeed observe their errors to be related. For example, if being in the first treatment group involves ingesting a particular dosage of an energy drink, whereas other groups did not receive any energy drink, we would of course expect errors to be related within that first group *relative to other groups*. Those drinking energy drinks will become more “similar” due to the treatment they have received. They are now a “nest” due to receiving the treatment.

However, the above similarity is only apparent *after* the treatment has been administered, not *before*. Before receiving treatment, there was no reason to expect that the score of one participant would be related to the score (and error) of a second participant within that group. Participants in this case are not nested *a priori*, but once the treatment is applied, they are, in a sense, nested *post-hoc*. But this *post-hoc* nesting idea is immaterial to multilevel modeling. It would only play a role if we decided to build on the current study to perform a second one and used the treatment groups as starting points. In such a case, those drinking energy drinks would start out the new study having correlated errors. That is, those receiving the treatment would be more “alike” compared to others not receiving the treatment.

The key takeaway from this discussion is that for hierarchical, multilevel, or nesting structures to exist, errors should be *expected* to be related *prior* to receiving treatment, not *after*. Students in a classroom being taught by the same teacher are *expected* to have related errors well before they receive any treatment or intervention. This is the “likeness” that we wish to account for in multilevel structures. While the classic one-way ANOVA (for instance) can actually be considered a multilevel structure (the reader may want to think of how), the errors within each treatment group are expected to be *independent* even if they become correlated once the treatment is applied and measurement occurs. What is most important for modeling decisions such as this is the *expectation* of how errors will behave *prior* to treatment, not *after*.

### 4.4.4 Blocking as an Example of Hierarchical/Multilevel Structure

When a researcher forces observations into blocks, the so-called *randomized block design*, this is an example and special case of a multilevel structure. *Observations are nested within blocks*. The nesting is *induced* or *created* by the researcher. For instance, if we put all first-grade school 208children in one block and all second graders in another block, it stands that observations within each respective block will be more similar than observations across blocks. The observations are level-one units, while the blocks are level two. For this reason, blocking designs are often analyzed as mixed models where the blocking factor is regarded as random. For readers to whom blocking designs may be entirely new, [Kirk (1995)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000187) provides a good and deep introduction.

### 4.4.5 Nonparametric Random-Effects Model

Most multilevel models include at least one random effect that aids in dealing with the nesting structure and dependencies of observations. In the classic random-effects model, it is assumed that random effects are normally distributed. This assumption, however, may be untenable and we may wish to adopt instead, as we will be doing in many places in this book, a *nonparametric* approach that makes no (or at least *fewer*) assumptions about the distribution of the random effects. The *nonparametric random-effects model* can be considered a special case of the wider *latent class model*. The random-effects regression model is also commonly known as a *mixture model in regression* since it combines a mixture of regressions that helps deal with the potential heterogeneity of groups ([Jiang and Guntuboyina, 2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000172)).

As an easy example of a nonparametric random-effects model and to get an idea of how it differs from the classic parametric random-effects model, consider first the typical (parametric) random-intercept model that contains no predictors,

`g[E(y[i j])] = α[j]`

where it is assumed that the intercept terms `α[j]` are normally distributed with a given mean and variance, that is, `α[j] ∼ N(μ,σ²)`. Traditionally, we would like to estimate the mean `μ` of these intercepts and more importantly their variance (`σ²`). The approach is *parametric* because we are imposing a *probability model* on the intercepts. In a nonparametric approach, we assume that the distribution of `α[j]` is indexed by an unspecified discrete mixing distribution of *latent classes*, where the intercept associated with a given class `x` is denoted by `α[x]`and the size of the given class is given by `P(x)`, where `α[x]` is the location and `P(x)` the “weight” of the given node or class.

Simplified, the nonparametric model is given by

`g[E(y[i j]|,x)] = α[x]`

(4.15)

which is simply the expectation of `y[i j]` for a given class `x`, which will be subject to `P(x)`. A two-level model in which predictors are included is a natural extension of (4.15), where now `z[i j]` denotes the inclusion of predictors into the model:

$$g\\left\\lbrack E\\left( y\_{ij} \\middle| z\_{ij},x \\right) \\right\\rbrack = \\alpha\_{x} + {\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}{\\beta\_{k} \\cdot z\_{ijk}}}$$

(4.16)

Key to note is that what makes the model nonparametric is not the inclusion of predictors in `z[i j]`, but as was the case in the intercept-only model, the fact that `y[i j]` is a function of a mixing distribution of classes, `x`. To reiterate, `E(y[i j])` is a function of a *probability distribution* in the classic parametric model, whereas it is a function of a *mixing distribution* (classes) in the nonparametric counterpart. Random slopes, in addition to random intercepts, can also be included, giving rise to a full-scale *multilevel nonparametric random-effects model*.209

Multilevel models, as was generally the case for mixed models, are best implemented using R (e.g., `lme4`) software instead of Python. `Latent Gold` is a software program that can estimate latent class models and the special case of nonparametric random-effects models. In R, the package `npmlreg` ([Einbeck et al., 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000095)) can be used to fit these types of models as well.

## 4.5 Repeated-Measures and Longitudinal Models

Often in research studies, measurements are taken over a series of days, weeks, months, or years, or even *epochs*, as in the case of attempting to estimate whether today’s rising temperatures are just normal long-term temperature variation versus human-made climate change. Models that involve a temporal component are often called *repeated-measures models* or *within-subjects designs*, or, more generally, *longitudinal models or time series*. The reader should be aware, however, that longitudinal models can comprise a whole host of models and complexities which can include mixed-effects components as well as elements of structural equation models. As noted earlier, jargon and terminology are often not consistent from discipline to discipline, and hence it behooves the reader to be cautious when coming across these and similar terms to learn exactly what the author or researcher means by them. Making assumptions as to what they mean will typically only lead to misunderstandings!
