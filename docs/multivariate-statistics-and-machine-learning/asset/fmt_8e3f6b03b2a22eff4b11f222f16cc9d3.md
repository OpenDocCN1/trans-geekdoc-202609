
$${SRMR} = \\left\\lbrack {2{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{q}{\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{i}\\frac{\\left\\lbrack \\left( s\_{ij} - s\_{ij}^{'} \\right)/s\_{i}s\_{j} \\right\\rbrack^{2}}{q\\left( q + 1 \\right)}}}} \\right\\rbrack^{1/2}$$  
(13.8)
where now the only functional part of the formula continues to be in the numerator, only that now the deviations (`s[i j]`−`s[i j]'`) have been standardized. As a result of `RMR` and `SRMR` being an analysis of residuals from the model, smaller values than not are indicative of better-fitting models. Values 489of 0.08 or less usually indicate reasonable to increasingly good fit for `SRMR`, though of course any model fit measure computed on data is often only as good as what competing research has found in the given area of investigation. As was true for effect sizes in simpler regression and ANOVA models for instance, effects should always be evaluated relative to other findings in the area under investigation. If you achieve a value for `SRMR` of 0.05 but all of your colleagues in the same area of investigation are achieving `SRMR` values of 0.0001 with their models, no criteria or “guidelines” can save you from concluding that your model may not be worth keeping.

The root mean square error of approximation (`RMSEA`) is given by
$$\\text{RMSEA} = \\sqrt{\\frac{1}{n - 1}\\left( \\frac{\\chi\_{m}^{2} - df\_{m}}{df\_{m}} \\right)}$$  
(13.9)
Recall that (`n`−1)`F`[`ML`] ∼ `χ`² and thus the statistical significance of an `SEM` model is based on `χ`², where now `χ[m]²` is the chi-square resulting from the model. Recall also that smaller than not values for `χ[m]²`, all else equal, are indicative of an increasingly well-fitting model. Whenever a parameter is estimated, it “costs” a degree of freedom. Ideally, we would wish to achieve a superb model fit without spending too many degrees of freedom. “Spending” a lot of degrees of freedom and estimating everything in the model is suggestive of a poor theory guiding the `SEM` (and will likely lead to identifiability issues for the model as well, though a discussion of identifiability is beyond the scope of the current text). Estimating everything under the sun, as we know by now, is also an exercise in *overfitting*.

With the above in mind, we can now see how `RMSEA` works; as the distance `χ[m]²` − `df[m]` gets larger relative to the value of `df[m]`, `RMSEA` grows in magnitude. The only way this happens is if `χ[m]²` is larger than not, which recall, would be suggestive of an increasingly poor fit. A better fit is indicated by increasingly smaller values of `χ[m]²`. Note as well that the fraction 1/(`n`−1) is inconsequential here and is not the *functional* part of the formulation, it simply serves to “average” the ratio (`χ[m]²`−`df[m]`)/`df[m]`. Values for `RMSEA` in the approximate range of 0.01, 0.05, 0.08 are generally indicative of excellent, good, and relatively poor fitting models. However, once more, context matters and one should never rely *absolutely* on *relative* guidelines in any field, including statistics.

### 13.6.4 Incremental Fit Indices
We now briefly survey a few of the more common measures of incremental fit. These measures, recall, evaluate model fit not in absolute or global terms, but instead relative to some baseline or null model.

The *normed-fit index* (`NFI`) is given by,
$$\\Delta\_{1} = \\frac{\\chi\_{b}^{2} - \\chi\_{m}^{2}}{\\chi\_{b}^{2}} = \\frac{F\_{b} - F\_{m}}{F\_{b}}$$  
(13.10)
where `χ[b]²` is the baseline model and `χ[m]²` is the hypothesized model. Since `χ`² ∼ (`n`−1)`F`[`ML`], `F[b]` − `F[m]` is the difference between the corresponding fitting functions, `F[b]` for the baseline model and `F[m]` for the model under test. The baseline model has more restrictions (i.e., on parameter estimates) than the fitted model, and so the difference `F[b]` − `F[m]` is a reflection of how much the fitting function *decreases* due to the proposed model. The better fitting the proposed model, the smaller is the value of `F[m]` and the difference `F[b]` − `F[m]` increasingly approximates simply `F[b]`. Recall that smaller 490values than not for `F[m]` are indicative of an increasingly better model fit. Ideally, if the model fit perfectly, then `F[m]` would equal 0, and thus `Δ[1]` would equal 1, as easily demonstrated,
$$\\Delta\_{1} = \\frac{\\chi\_{b}^{2} - \\chi\_{m}^{2}}{\\chi\_{b}^{2}} = \\frac{F\_{b} - F\_{m}}{F\_{b}} = \\frac{F\_{b} - 0}{F\_{b}} = \\frac{F\_{b}}{F\_{b}} = 1$$  
On the opposite end of things, if there is no model improvement, then `F[b]` = `F[m]` and so
$$\\Delta\_{1} = \\frac{\\chi\_{b}^{2} - \\chi\_{m}^{2}}{\\chi\_{b}^{2}} = \\frac{F\_{b} - F\_{m}}{F\_{b}} = \\frac{0}{F\_{b}} = 0$$  
Values of 0.95 and greater are typically indicative of well-fitting models, not in absolute terms, but relative to a baseline model, which is usually the null model but need not necessarily be so.

A measure of model fit related to `Δ[1]` is that of Bollen’s `ρ[1]` ([Bollen, 1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000037)), which is essentially the same as the `NFI`, but with one important difference in that it scales `Δ[1]` relative to degrees of freedom:
$$\\begin{array}{l} {\\rho\_{1} = \\frac{\\left( F\_{b}/df\_{b} \\right) - \\left( F\_{m}/df\_{m} \\right)}{\\left( F\_{b}/df\_{b} \\right)}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\frac{\\left( \\chi\_{b}^{2}/df\_{b} \\right) - \\left( \\chi\_{m}^{2}/df\_{m} \\right)}{\\left( \\chi\_{b}^{2}/df\_{b} \\right)}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$  
(13.11)
Under the most ideal of situations assuming perfect fit, `F[m]` = 0 and thus `F[m]`/`df[m]` will also equal 0 regardless of `df[m]`. Under the condition that `F[m]` > 0, which will most often be the case, `F[m]` is essentially *penalized* by how many degrees of freedom `df[m]` it required to reduce `F[b]` to `F[m]`. *Strong theories require spending less degrees of freedom to achieve increasingly smaller* `F[m]` *that do weaker theories*. We can see then that `ρ[1]` rewards the fitting of models that “spend” a smaller number of degrees of freedom, all else equal, to achieve the same reduction from `F[b]` to `F[m]`. That is, `ρ[1]` rewards models that are *parsimonious* and result from a well-specified theory such that the researcher clearly had a good framework for what parameters to estimate to result in maximal decrease in `F[m]`. Such will be reflected in a ratio `F[m]`/`df[m]` smaller than not, which means less and less is being subtracted from the baseline ratio `F[b]`/`df[b]`. As (`F[m]`/`df[m]`)→0, `ρ[1]`→1.0 indicating perfect fit. Values for `ρ[1]` generally in the neighborhood of 0.95 or greater are usually regarded as indicating good to excellent fit.

The *Tucker-Lewis index* (`NNFI`) is given by
$$\\rho\_{2} = \\frac{\\left( \\chi\_{b}^{2}/df\_{b} \\right) - \\left( \\chi\_{m}^{2}/df\_{m} \\right)}{\\left( \\chi\_{b}^{2}/df\_{b} \\right) - 1}$$  
(13.12)
A comparison of `ρ[1]` to `ρ[2]` reveals that (`χ[b]²`/`df[b]`) − (`χ[m]²`/`df[m]`) is no longer being compared to (`χ[b]²`/`df[b]`) but instead to (`χ[b]²`/`df[b]`) − 1. Values of 0.95 and greater are suggestive of increasingly good model fit.

Lastly, the *comparative fit index* (`CFI`) is given by491
$$\\text{CFI} = 1 - \\frac{\\max\\left( \\chi\_{m}^{2} - df\_{m},0 \\right)}{\\max\\left( \\chi\_{b}^{2} - df\_{b},\\chi\_{m}^{2} - df\_{m},0 \\right)}$$  
(13.13)
The `CFI` follows a similar logic to that of `ρ[1]`, but instead of taking ratios as for `ρ[1]`, we are subtracting `df` from `χ`², that is, `χ[]²` − `df` for both baseline and model `χ`². `CFI` values in the range of 0.90 to 0.95 indicate good fit.
