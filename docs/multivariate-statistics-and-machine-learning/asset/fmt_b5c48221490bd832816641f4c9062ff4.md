
The question naturally arises as to why use a smoother (`s[j]`) instead of linear weights (`β[j]`)? What is the motivation for this approach? Like the motivation for the generalized linear model, the goal of the generalized additive model is to *capture nonlinear effects,* but by estimating them *nonparametrically*. Just as in kernel regression surveyed earlier, we wish to not make any parametric assumptions about the form of the relationship and instead want to have the data essentially speak for itself. This is what nonparametric estimation is all about. The idea of a smoother is a nonparametric idea in which we are allowing the data “to speak” in this sense and fit a function that approximates the data as well as possible, but without a prior hypothesized functional form such as we would have in the classic linear model estimated by least-squares, for instance. This is a hallmark of machine learning such that many analyses are *data-driven* (i.e., loosely, *nonparametric*) instead of *hypothesis-driven* (i.e., loosely, *parametric*). Generalized additive models, even in their own right, are pedagogically useful if for nothing else than understanding and appreciating how various models can be contextualized as special cases of others.

It can also be shown that `GAMs` can be likened to a neural network with a logistic activation function ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). Further, generalized additive models can also be interpreted as extensions to both the class of additive models, and as we have already seen, the class of generalized linear models.

> *The big idea of the generalized additive model is to use a nonparametric smoother (or some other nonparametric structure) to explore nonlinearity in the relationship between the response and covariates. Whereas the generalized linear model assumes some functional form (i.e., a linear form), the generalized additive model uses the data entirely to determine fit and hence makes no* a priori *parametric assumptions of what class of parametric functions will fit best. The generalized linear model can thus be considered a special case of the wider nonparametric generalized additive model.*

### 8.7.2 Deriving the Generalized Additive Model

To understand and appreciate how the generalized additive model arises, we begin once more with what is most familiar, that of the standard linear model, where the expectation of `y` is conditional on predictors `x[1]`, `x[2]`, ..., `x[p]`:323

`E(y|x[1],x[2],...,x[p]) = β[0] + β[1]x[1] + ... + β[p]x[p]`

(8.14)

The generalized additive model makes the classic linear model a special case of

$$E\\left( y \\middle| x\_{1},x\_{2},...,x\_{p} \\right) = s\_{0} + {\\sum\\limits\_{j = 1}^{p}{s\_{j}\\left( x\_{j} \\right)}}$$

(8.15)

The distinction between models is that `s[j](x[j])` replaces `β[j](x[j])` and are now *smooth functions*. These functions `s[j](x[j])` are also typically standardized ([Hastie & Tibshirani, 1986](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000144)) such that `E(s[j](x[j]))` = 0. The functions `s[j](x[j])` are estimated, as mentioned, via scatterplot smoother or some other nonparametric device. The word “smooth” here generally refers to the idea of “nonparametric” because it implies the local area of the data being “smoothed” by a flexible curve instead of a rigid *a priori* (typically *parametric*) structure (e.g., a line or other curve). Cubic smoothing splines or the kernel smoothers that we saw earlier in the book are special cases. `GAMs`, however, place no restriction on what *can* be used, and hence the family of potential nonparametric functions is much wider. The algorithm used for estimating parameters in a generalized additive model is then tasked with estimating all the `p` functions in the data, whatever we have chosen those functions to be.

### 8.7.3 GAM as a Smooth Extension to GLLM

As we have seen, the generalized additive model is entirely general in the sense that it can subsume a variety of forms. Parametric structures are hence a special case of the wider `GAM` framework. The general linear model is easily cast as a `GAM` and so is the generalized linear model also a special case of the wider generalized additive model. Since in this chapter we are focused on the generalized linear model, we now detail such a bit more deeply.

Recall that in a generalized linear model, the linear predictor, now in the language of the generalized additive model, is given by

$$\\eta = s\_{0} + {\\sum\\limits\_{n = 1}^{p}{s\_{j}\\left( x\_{j} \\right)}}$$

(8.16)

where in the above, `s[1](·)`, ..., `s[p](·)` are now, in the `GAM`, *smooth functions*, which are not in parametric form but will instead be estimated nonparametrically. The intercept, `s[0]` is analogous from the generalized linear model to the `GAM`. Where the models differ is that in the classic “non-smooth” generalized linear model, `s[j](x[j])` was simply `β[j](x[j])` where `β[j]` were the linear weights as in regression. That is, the `GAM` applies a flexible smooth function to predictors `x[j]` instead of setting them as linear as in the classic generalized linear model. As [Hastie et al. (2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), pp. 296–297) note, `s[1](·)`, ..., `s[p](·)` do not need to consist exclusively of nonlinear terms but may also contain linear and other possibilities. The possibilities are virtually endless in the `GAM`. For instance, [Hastie et al. (2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 297) give the case of the following model,

`g(μ) = x^T β + α[k] + f(z)`

(8.17)

which is considered *semiparametric*, where `x` are variables modeled linearly, and `z` *nonparametrically*. The `GAM` easily accommodates such a model, whereas the general linear model or the generalized linear model would have difficulty doing so without alteration.324

Other possibilities may consist of including qualitative variables or interaction terms as other examples. There is no real limit to the possibilities of combining parametric and nonparametric style inputs in the `GAM` and such decisions will be made in part based on the prior experience of the researcher but also on what the actual data have to say. While the generalized additive model is nonparametric, we note that deciding on what nonparametric “objects” to include in the model is still a decision that must be made by the researcher. Whether to include a kernel or spline or whether to mix linear and nonlinear terms is still up to the researcher. Hence, *nonparametric models learn from the data almost exclusively, but what tool does the learning is still a decision the researcher must make*.

There are numerous algorithms that can be used to fit `GAMs`. Some of the more popular include the *backfitting algorithm* and the *Newton-Raphson procedure* ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 299). Iterated weighted least-squares can also be adapted for `GAMs` and combined with the backfitting algorithm, the so-called `IWLS-backfitting` procedure ([Fox, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106), p. 576). Likelihood-ratio chi-square tests along with deviance can be used to assess model fit. Generalized additive models can also be extended to handle multinomial responses. For details, see [Hastie et al. (2009)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145) and [Fox (2016)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106).
