
Because of their complexity, neural networks have often been called “black boxes” as a result of the numerous nonlinear and interacting elements that are not explicit ([Yosinski et al., 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000399)). That is, neural networks may indeed perform well under many circumstances, but their “black box” nature prevents us from knowing exactly *why*. This is one of the drawbacks to these networks, in contrast to other techniques such as regression where one can obtain a listing of partial regression coefficients in an attempt to evaluate the *relative performance* of predictors in a model. A neural network typically does not provide such precise individual variable performance information, and in some cases, this is understandable and for good reason; the number of parameters modeled in a neural network can be upward of over 50 million and hence attempting to understand how those parameters relate to one another is a gargantuan, and most likely, impossible task. Efforts to better understand the performance of neural networks via sophisticated visualization techniques is an ongoing area of research that may assist in “decoding” the black box of these networks and untangling their interacting, nonlinear components ([Yosinski et al., 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000399)).

We can thus far then sum up the fundamental property of neural networks, which holds that neural networks, at their ultimate core, are *combinations of combinations*, that is, they are *composite functions*. For now, we characterize them as composite functions without any further constraints such as nonlinearity or linearity for the reason that *neural networks need not be nonlinear in nature*. As we will see, they are *often* nonlinear, such that they can transform nonlinear inputs into more linearly separable abstract features ([Dubey et al., 2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000092)), but the key point is that *they need not be nonlinear*.

> *Neural networks can be attractive approaches to data analysis due to their inherent complexity and nonlinearity possibilities which do not need to be modeled overtly and instead can be relegated to a “black box” approach. By taking composite functions as many times as desired, the network becomes “deep” as it learns more about the variables in the model and their interactions at successively deeper levels of abstraction. While neural networks usually feature the modeling of nonlinearity, at a technical level, a neural network can still be linear.*

To understand the nature of a neural network, it is best to begin with the simplest case of such networks – the feedforward neural network with a single layer, which as we will see, the simple linear regression model can be considered a special case.538

The network is called a feedforward neural network for the reason that information “flows” (in necessarily a mathematical sense *only*) from input to output and there are no reverse feedback loops. When a network contains feedback paths, it is usually referred to as a *recurrent neural network* ([Goodfellow, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)).

In [Figure 15.4](#c15.xhtml#f15.4), we see that all inputs, `x`[1] through `x`[`p`], are weighted by `β`[1] through `β`[`p`], where a *bias term*, `β`[0] is also added, which is the familiar intercept term in classic regression. The inputs `x`[1] through `x`[`p`] are nothing more than observed variables as we would have in a model such as multiple regression (as opposed to “latent” ones as we encountered in structural equation modeling, for instance). In actuality, what we have in [Figure 15.4](#c15.xhtml#f15.4) is in effect the familiar multiple regression model where `y`[`i`] is nothing more than a function of an intercept `β`[`o`] and a set of weighted predictors, `β`[`i`]`x`[`i`]:
$$y_{i} = {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{p}{\beta_{o} + \beta_{i}}}x_{i}$$

Why call it a neural network if it is simply a multiple regression model? In this most simple of situations, we do not have to. However, the benefit of doing so is the gateway to understanding neural networks in their full generality. The model in Figure 15.4 is a multiple regression only because the implied *activation function* is an *identity function*. A multiple linear regression does not allow us to change the nature of the activation function. This is where neural networks become more general than the classic multiple regression model. *In neural networks, we will be free to choose activation functions more complex than the identity function that is naturally implied or implicit in the multiple regression framework.*

Activation functions are central to neural networks, and as we will see later, can be chosen from a variety of possibilities. However, as we have said, if we choose the identity function in this case, the neural network reduces to that of a multiple regression. Recall an identity function is the following:539
`f(x)` = `x`
which reads *what you put into the equation you get out*; there is no transformation at all on the input variable. Had we chosen a different activation function, perhaps a more complex *nonlinear* one, then we would have no longer had a simple or multiple regression and would have had a more complex neural network. For instance, instead of taking the identity function, we could have instead taken the `log` of the function as one example,
$$\begin{array}{l} {y_{i} = {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{p}{\beta_{o} + \beta_{i}}}x_{i}} \\\\ {\,\,\,\,\,\, = \log\left( {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{p}{\beta_{o} + \beta_{i}}}x_{i} \right)} \\\\ \end{array}$$
making the activation function no longer an identity function but instead a logarithmic one. *Activation functions are what give neural networks their life*. For example, a perceptron with a threshold activation function, that is, a “yes” vs. “no” binary outcome,
$$f\left( x \right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} {1\,\,\, if\,\,\,\, x > \, 0} \\\\ {0\,\,\, otherwise} \\\\ \end{array} \right.$$
becomes a *binary linear discriminant* analysis and hence the perceptron in this case is imposing a familiar hyperplane. If the output is multi-valued and categorical, it becomes a multiple logistic function featuring a `softmax` activation function:
$$soft\text{max}\, f\text{(}x\text{)} = \frac{e^{x_{i}}}{\sum\limits_{j}^{}e^{x_{j}}}$$
