
The standard linear model assumes independently and normally distributed errors, but the assumption of independence is rarely (if ever) quite right on empirical data. Just like most things in statistics, it is often a reasonable *approximation*, however. Recall that approximations are what statistical modeling is all about. Rarely, if ever, are any assumptions for any model *exactly* met.

When measurements are taken across a *time period*, the assumption of independent errors is usually not satisfied. That is, when the observations comprise a *time series*, `ε[i]` are usually *correlated*. Indeed, dependencies among the errors can be very strong in such time-dependent data.

How is this dependency represented in the linear model? Recall in the classic linear model,
`y = Xβ + ε`
where `ε ∼ N(0, Σ[ε ε])` and where `Σ[ε ε]` is the covariance matrix of errors, symmetric and positive semi-definite. However, unlike in the typical non-time series least-squares regression model, the expectation for `Σ[ε ε]` is not one having zeroes on the off-diagonal. Instead, because measurements are usually related, we expect *nonzero diagonal elements* in the off-diagonal representing the correlated errors, which implies `Σ[ε ε]`will have `n(n + 1)/2` distinct elements. The dependency of errors in a time series model requires us to incorporate the covariances between time points similar to how we did so in the classic repeated measures models surveyed earlier in the book.

Time series is an enormous topic and we have only scratched the surface here in our cursory overview to give an example of where errors are often correlated in data. Books on time series include those by [Chatfield and Xing (2019)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000058) and [Cryer and Chan (2008)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000072). The package `timeseries` ([Wuertz et al., 2024](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000395)) can be used for time series in R, especially applicable to financial time series. The package `pyts` can be used to fit time series models in Python. Other packages such as `sktime` and `tslearn` can also be used. [Fox (2016)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106) provides a good, if not somewhat technical introduction to time series and comes highly recommended.

## 5.6 Model Selection in Regression: Is There an “Optimal” Model?

One of the biggest problems in regression analysis and related models is that of determining which model to maintain or keep. *Model selection* in statistics in general is a huge problem and is usually not quite as simple as using statistical criteria alone to determine which model to adopt. Research and scientific criteria should always enter the discussion. Too often researchers adopt a “kitchen-sink” approach (i.e., throw in everything but the kitchen sink) to variable selection which often leads to overly complex models, problems of collinearity, and poor generalizability of test data.

Among the statistical approaches, there are generally two common strategies used for selecting a model. Recall that in choosing a model, we are not interested in the model that minimizes *training error*; we are interested in the model that minimizes *test error*. The most direct way to evaluate test error would be to employ a *cross-validation* approach. For example, we could fit our model to 80% of the data and test on 20%. This gives us a direct estimate of the test error because it tells us exactly how the model performs on new data, that is, data the model has yet to “see.” This is a 234first strategy to choosing a model, that of evaluating the fitted model on test data to learn how it performs.

For many reasons, however, we may not want to follow the above direct cross-validation approach. Some of these reasons include the fact that historically, data may have been expensive and sometimes very inconvenient to collect in the given field, and hence training a model on anything less than the full data set is sometimes simply not feasible. Indeed, before computers came along, the idea of setting aside data to test on via fast computation was, of course, not even an option. Hence, what if we decided to not put aside test data and wanted simply to estimate the test error on the training data itself, even if imperfectly? Since we only have the training data, fortunately we can *adjust* the training error rate to, for some measures at least, better reflect test error. We consider these options next.

## 5.7 Effect Size, Model Selection, and Adjusting the Training Error Rate

A rejection of the null hypothesis in regression, crudely, suggests that the regression is predicting better than chance. However, on its own, a statistically significant `p-value` does not necessarily tell us much more than that, unless of course we have specific knowledge of the inputs to the computed `p-value` such as sample size, variance, and so on, to help make sense of the `p-value` finding for the given data set. As is usually the case with significance testing, it is entirely possible to reject a null hypothesis for regression yet it be a *trivial* scientific effect. Such can occur often when sample size is extremely large and hence even miniscule effects cause us to reject the null hypothesis. This is nothing new, as we have discussed in many other places in the book the influence that sample size has on computed `p-values`. As usual, what is needed is a measure of *effect size*. A good effect size is not sample size dependent. The problem with effect size, however, is that if we compute it only on the training data then we usually get an overly *optimistic* size of effect. This is not surprising and is in line with our understanding of how models fit to training data are generally too optimistic overall.

In what follows we first consider an effect size for training data, then survey a few strategies for measuring size of effect as well as *model selection measures* that better evaluate the parsimony of statistical models, and in some cases (e.g., *Akaike’s Information Criterion*), also help us better estimate test error.

### 5.7.1 R², Adjusted R², C[p], AIC, BIC

Recall that `R²` in regression is given by

$$R^{2} = \\frac{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i}^{'} - \\overline{y} \\right)^{2}}{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)^{2}}$$

(5.23)

The denominator, $\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i} - \\overline{y} \\right)^{2}$ is simply a sums of squares of actual data values from $\\overline{y}$. This quantity will be large or small depending simply on how much variation we have in the current data set. However, look more closely at the numerator, $\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i}^{'} - \\overline{y} \\right)^{2}$; this term expresses the distance between predicted values, `y[i]'` and the mean of the data, $\\overline{y}$. If this distance is zero, then 235$\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( y\_{i}^{'} - \\overline{y} \\right)^{2}$ is equal to 0 and `R²` will equal 0. As this distance increases, `R²` becomes larger, and the quadratic (i.e., squared) nature of sums of squares amplifies the magnitude, especially for deviations that are distant from the mean. Hence, it would appear at first glance that this `R²` statistic is all we really need to choose a model, such that *the larger the value of* `R²`*, the better the model*. But this logic is problematic, since in almost all cases `R²` will necessarily mathematically increase as the number of predictors entered into the model also increases. Hence, models with a greater number of predictors will result in larger `R²` values. The value of `1 - R²` will give us a measure of what is typically called the *apparent error rate*. What we really want to know is the *actual error rate*.

One way around this difficulty is to interpret, instead of `R²`, an *adjusted* `R²` *statistic* given by the following:

$$R\_{adj}^{2} = 1 - \\left( 1 - R^{2} \\right)\\left( \\frac{n - 1}{n - p} \\right)$$

(5.24)
