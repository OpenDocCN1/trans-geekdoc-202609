
The `p-value`, by itself, serves a useful function. It allows for an inferential statement based on a rejection of the null hypothesis. This is not a bad thing. And though it may sound borderline criminal that one can obtain a small `p-value` based simply on increasing sample size `n`, this too is not a bad thing. An increase in sample size leading to an increasingly smaller `p-value` implies that the estimation of the parameter value is improving. That is, the strength of the evidence against the null should increase given the increase in `n`. In the limit, as `n` increases to infinity, the rejection of the null is guaranteed and assured, assuming at least *some* effect. An estimator is *consistent* if with increasing `n`, we obtain a better and more precise estimate of the parameter based on our sequence of estimates. That is, a consistent estimator is one such that

$$\\lim\\limits\_{n\\rightarrow\\infty}p\\left( \\middle| \\hat{\\theta} - \\theta \\middle| < \\varepsilon \\right) = 1$$

(2.65)

where `θ̂` − `θ` is the difference between the estimator, `θ̂`, and the parameter value, `θ`. Consistency of an estimator assures us that as the distance `θ̂` − `θ` gets smaller, less than some arbitrarily small number `ε`, the probability of that distance being smaller than `ε` is equal to 1 as `n`→∞. As Casella and Berger ([2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000055), p. 468) note, an equivalent statement to the above is

$$\\lim\\limits\_{n\\rightarrow\\infty}p\\left( \\middle| \\hat{\\theta} - \\theta \\middle| \\geq \\varepsilon \\right) = 0$$

(2.66)

which essentially says the same thing; in this case, the probability of any difference being equal or greater than some small magnitude `ε` is equal to 0 as `n`→∞.

The `p-value` is a reflection of this consistency and hence it getting smaller as `n` increases is what the `p-value` *should* be doing. What the *user* should not be doing is attributing the decreasing `p-value` as increasing support for the research or scientific alternative hypothesis. That is where the problem lies. The movement to ban `p-value` from particular journals is akin to banning firearms entirely 100% due to misuse. An argument can be made on both sides of the debate, but the gun (`p-value`) itself, as it sits there not harming anyone, is innocent. As with all innocent mathematics and statistics (and guns), *it takes a human being to truly mess it up*.

### 2.29.5 Absolute vs. Relative Size of Effect Size: Context Matters

An `R²` effect size of 0.10 is *relatively* small numerically, since the maximum theoretically attainable effect size is 1.0. However, 0.10 may be quite large and impressive relative to the given research area. Effect sizes should be contextualized within the research area to better appreciate how large or small they really are. [Cohen (1988)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000063) provided “rules of thumb” for effect sizes in social research of 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, and 0.7 or higher “large.” But it should be emphasized that these are nothing more than *arbitrary guidelines* and Cohen may have been better off not 107providing them at all, since too often researchers simply compare their obtained effect size to these arbitrary guidelines instead of comparing their obtained effect size to the effect sizes in competing research.

How large is an effect of `d` = 0.7 or so? That converts to an `R²`of approximately 0.11, which means we’re only explaining approximately 11% of the variance in the response variable. Is that “large?” Not in absolute terms; it’s very small, given `R²` has a theoretical upper limit of 1.0. But, for many areas of social research (which is the area Cohen was referring to when he published his guidelines of 0.2, 0.5, and so on), explaining 11% is usually pretty decent as much social research will typically explain much less. Hence, in that *context*, 0.7 or so is considered relatively large. *But only in that context*. If the effect size of raising or lowering the flaps on the response variable of flight on an airplane were that low, we’d all be taking the bus. In that context, explaining 11% of the variance is comical, negligibly small, and outright dangerous. We could easily devise a social study where 11% would be negligible as well, yet we could also imagine a social study where 11% is whoppingly large and impressive. Imagine if the way people smiled or did not smile explained 11% of the variance in their lifespan; that would probably be considered ground-breaking and extremely large! Yet the same effect size explaining flap angles and flight has the plane crashing into the ground way too often.

Hence, effect size without context is very difficult to interpret other than in a purely absolute sense (e.g., 11% of the variance explained is better than 0% but still a long way to maximum variance, which is 100%). In an *absolute* sense, regardless of the research area, 11% is small. Or, we could say, “Relative to the range of theoretically possible effect sizes, 11% is small, because the theoretical maximum is 100%. We’re only explaining approximately one tenth of what is theoretically possible to explain in terms of variance.” But when we step away from the abstract and say things like “This drug explains variance in blood pressure by 30%,” that may have tremendous *practical value* and save many lives, especially if given most other drugs explain only up to 5%. In other words, the drug is *clinically relevant*. It is *clinically significant*. In this way, that drug’s effect size is extremely impressive and large, because we’re comparing it to the research area and also thinking about its practical implications. Summer tires vs. winter tires probably explains a reasonable amount of variance in traffic deaths in winter, let’s say 5%. That 5% might represent thousands and thousands of lives saved and may be the most significant thing we can do to curb traffic deaths in winter because all other “treatments” (like brighter headlight bulbs or other alternatives) explain only 0.3%, for instance. Hence, the effect size of 5% is very big in the particular area. Are we going to compare that to Cohen’s guidelines? No, because his guidelines were specific to a narrow area of research and may at best be arbitrary in relation to other areas.

### 2.29.6 Comparability of Effect Sizes Across Studies

The question often arises as to whether effect sizes are comparable across experiments and studies. The simple answer is that some effect sizes, such as Cohen’s `d`, are comparable, but with many caveats. First, the comparison will rarely if ever be perfect, as the standard deviation for each study can differ from study to study, as well as what measuring instruments were used, the reliability of those measures, and so on. Cohen’s `d` is the standardized mean difference within a given study, but *across studies those differences are not necessarily standardized*. This is the key point.

For example, suppose that `d` = 0.5 in one study vs. `d` = 0.6 in another. We are choosing these effect sizes to be relatively close on purpose. The second study has the higher effect size, but are we prepared to say the second study has truly found a larger effect? Perhaps the effect size in the one study is larger or smaller simply as a function of variability and not mean difference. For this 108reason and others, some (e.g., [Baguley, 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000018)) even suggest reporting *simple effect sizes* without standardization. A simple effect size does not incorporate the standard deviation and features only the mean difference. The problem is that even a few outlying cases can push the variability up so that all of a sudden the mean difference in the numerator does not look quite as impressive (since we are dividing by an increasingly large denominator).
