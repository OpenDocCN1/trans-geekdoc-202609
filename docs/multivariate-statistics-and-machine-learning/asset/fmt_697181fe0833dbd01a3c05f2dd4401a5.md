## 5.4 Gauss-Markov Theorem: What We Like about Least-Squares Estimates

Why do we like least-squares estimators so much? What is so attractive about them? What is most important about them can be summarized in the *Gauss-Markov theorem*, which asserts that the least-squares estimates of the parameters `a^T β̂` have the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators when the errors are independently distributed with zero expectation and constant variance ([Fox, 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106), p. 212). Least-squares estimators are often called “BLUE” which stands for *best linear unbiased estimator*. As noted earlier, the least-squares estimate of `a^T β̂` is

`a^T β̂ = a^T (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y`

(5.21)

for which the expectation is equal to

$$\\begin{array}{l} {E\\left( a^{T}\\hat{\\beta} \\right) = E\\left( a^{T}\\left( XTX \\right){- 1}X^{T}y \\right)} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = a^{T}\\left( XTX \\right){- 1}X^{T}X\\beta} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = a^{T}\\beta} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

(5.22)

The Gauss-Markov theorem holds that for any other linear estimator for which the expectation is `a^T β` (unbiased), for example, `E(w^T ŷ) = w^T y`, the variance of the least-squares estimates `a^T β̂` is less than or equal to the variance of `w^T y`. For a proof of this theorem, see [Fox (2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106), p. 213). An implication of the theorem is that the least-squares estimator has *the smallest mean-squared error out of all the linear estimators we could consider that have no bias* (Hastie et al., [2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)).

### 5.4.1 Are Unbiased Estimators Always Best?

As we will see later, the restriction to unbiased estimators in regression and other methods is not necessarily always a wise one. Recall that the bias-variance trade-off is always at work in estimation. Most of the time, we would like to minimize bias in estimation. At times, however, it may be beneficial to permit some bias into an estimator for the purpose of reducing variance.

Biased estimators such as the *ridge estimator* in *ridge regression* may at times be preferred over an unbiased estimator. One such case when a biased estimator may be preferred is when least-squares estimates are *unstable* ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). That is, if `X` in `(X^T X)^{-1} X^T y` is less than full rank, then *singularity* will result, which means the least-squares estimates will no longer be *unique*. When the assumptions of regression do not hold due to collinearity or other problems (e.g., correlated errors), alternative solutions for estimation must be sought. And while exact collinearity may rarely occur, variables that are “almost collinear” can happen quite often depending on the research context. A *biased* estimator can be a better choice when faced with these kinds of conditions as well as for data that are ill-conditioned, meaning that estimated quantities are rather sensitive to small changes in the data ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). Collinearity and instability often go hand in hand, as the effects of collinearity are such that it can produce overly large positive or negative estimated coefficients whose very signs can be reversed under rather negligible changes to the data. Standard errors could be grossly inflated as well, which could yield non-statistically significant effects, whereas if collinearity were not present, those same effects may have been statistically significant.

In addition to the above, for some data sets, the number of input variables in `X` might greatly exceed the number of observations in the data. Under these conditions, the OLS estimator still exists, but is not *unique*, and hence a biased estimator may be useful in this case. We discuss biased estimators such as ridge and the lasso shortly in the book.
