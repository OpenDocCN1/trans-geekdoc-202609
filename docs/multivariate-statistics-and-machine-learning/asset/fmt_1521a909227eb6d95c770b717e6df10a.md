
Neural networks essentially act as a type of parametric/nonparametric regression model (more on this distinction later), which allows the user to model complex and often unknown nonlinear functional forms based on what the data have to say, rather than assuming a prior parametric form such as in the classic linear model. Be sure to note that this does not forbid us from assuming a functional form, but the point is that it does not *require* us to do so. This is why neural networks are often considered first and foremost, *nonparametric*. The parametric case in which prior assumptions are invoked is simply a special case of the larger nonparametric framework in which such assumptions are not advanced. If the relationship between inputs and outputs is known a priori to be linear, then typically a linear regression will usually outperform a neural network in terms of prediction ([King, 1999](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000186)).

How often do we know the exact functional form of a relationship? *How many scientific phenomena actually follow perfect linear trends?* As we have noted throughout this book, many phenomena are rather complex with unknown parametric forms, which is why so many machine learning techniques are nonparametric in nature allowing us to skip over the assumptions and get right to *what the data have to say*. That is, the algorithm *learns from the data*, not from our prior beliefs (theory) about the data or imposed (*induction biased*) parametric structures. Recall that we had said at the outset of this book that this was one of the distinguishing features made between classical statistics and the machine learning revolution, or perhaps even “rebellion.” Where classical statisticians have historically arrived at a problem with a priori functional forms and assumptions 540largely already imposed on their data, machine learning types became more interested in simply wanting to know *what the data have to say*. Classical statistics and machine learning approaches overlap a great deal, of course, but the emphasis and approach to problem-solving can differ somewhat, especially in the age of massively large data sets and tremendous computing power where making distributional assumptions may not seem as relevant as with the smaller samples of yesterday. Of course, there is not simply one or two things that distinguish these fields; they overlap a tremendous amount and each field benefits from the other. Much of machine learning, despite its advancements, has relied on the field of statistics to make such headwinds.

We have said that neural networks are all about activation functions. We now survey such functions in more detail.

## 15.5 What Is an Activation Function?

Activation functions are at the very core of neural networks. An activation function is basically, at its most foundational and rudimentary level, simply a *function*, often better understood in the context of the previously discussed *composite function*. A composite function, as we have seen and as the reader may recall from basic calculus, is a *function of a function*. In theory, we can layer as many of these composites as we like and iteratively take *functions of functions of functions*, and so on. That is,

`f(x;θ) = f[L](f[L−1](... (f[1](x))...))`

(15.3)

where in (15.3) `θ` is the parameter vector. The most “inside” function is that of `f[1](x)`. From there, we are iteratively taking *functions of functions*, where `f[L]` is the most “outside” function, followed next by `f[L−1]`. This idea of taking composite functions is precisely the concept of what makes a neural network *deep*. To put this into context again, recall that *linear regression can be conceived, from the most global perspective, as a neural network*. It is simply an elementary one that is linear, and for which the composite function is that of the *identity function*. That is, though we do not operationalize regression this way, we could in the way of composite functions by identifying `f[1](x)` as the regression function and `f[L−1]` as the identity function where `f[L−1] = I(x)`. Hence, the trivial neural network would be

```
$$\\begin{array}{l} {f\\left( x;\\theta \\right) = f\_{L - 1}\\left( f\_{1}\\left( x \\right) \\right)} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = I\\left( f\_{1}\\left( x \\right) \\right)} \\\\ \\end{array}$$
```

where in the above, `f[L−1] = I`. When `f[L−1]` is, for instance, a *nonlinear* function, then we have a more traditional representation and application of a neural network. This is the typical rationale given to activation functions in the literature, that they allow *nonlinearity* to be incorporated into the network. However, *an activation function need not necessarily be nonlinear to still make it a proper neural network*. Many writers require a neural network to be nonlinear in order to define it as a proper neural network and the reader should be aware of this.

The use of a composite function in neural networks, as we will see, also accounts for why backpropagation works so well, since it is based on the *chain rule of differentiation* in calculus, which as the reader may recall is all about differentiating composite functions. Hence, complex and as powerful as neural networks can become, they are all based on these very fundamental and elementary foundations.541

### 15.5.1 Activation Functions Do Not “Activate” Anything

As we have said throughout this book, many statistical terms unwillingly denote “movement” or “action” of some kind. To one who is knowledgeable of how these terms are being used, no harm is done. However, to the newcomer, the use of such *power* and *action* words can be tremendously misleading. Indeed, recall that we earlier in the book discussed how mediation and moderation in no way necessarily implied movement or action. They are simply names for partitioning variance in different ways. If the *substantive* context of the problem implies movement or action, then that’s an entirely different matter. The point, however, is that statistical modeling, whatever the kind, could never on its own imply such movement no matter how “sexy” of terms used to describe the modeling.

The same can be said of activation functions. The outside function in `g(f(x))` is not “activating” `f(x)` in any way other than being applied, mathematically, to `f(x)`. Yet, the word “activating” seems to suggest a more important role, a physical movement of sorts, a more “exciting” story! Indeed, activation functions are sometimes referred to as *transfer functions*, but this again is *hyped-up jargon*, as activation functions, by themselves, do not “transmit” anything. A mathematical function, on its own, cannot physically transmit a thing! Now, neurologists might claim that neurons in the brain might *transfer* information, but that is a *scientific* matter, not solely a *statistical* one. Neural networks may have originally been influenced by neuronal activity as a paradigm, but in the end they are simply mathematical abstract functions. As was the case with so-called “causal modeling” in our survey of structural equation modeling, if a neural network is applied to model the electrical activity of the brain or some other presumed physical activity, then the word “transfer” may indeed make more sense. But such is not a property of the statistical model; *it is a property of the neural activity*.
