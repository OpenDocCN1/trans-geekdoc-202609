
# Neural Networks and Function Approximation

Having surveyed the McCulloch-Pitts neuron and Rosenblatt’s perceptron, we now consider neural networks in greater generality. First and foremost, in its most general and wide interpretation, machine learning can be said to be about *function approximation*. Of course, the problem of approximating functions was not at all “new” at the time machine learning arose since scientists and mathematicians had been working on function approximation in one way or another since at least the Greeks. Even the derivative in calculus is a way of approximating a *nonlinear function* with one that is *linear*, which is the tangent to the curve. The integral can also be said to be an approximation to the area under a curve. The Greeks’ method of exhaustion in approximating the integral was just that, an exercise in *approximation theory*. Indeed, approximation in mathematics is an ancient concept and one that in one form or another has been applied countless times over history.

What was new, however, with regard to neural networks, were the algorithms and methods advanced to accomplish such function approximation. The infamous *backpropagation algorithm*, which is central to modern neural networks, actually had its origins in the 1960s, but was only fully recognized in the 1980s and constituted a major step in the advancement of function approximation and hence neural networks as well. *Deep neural networks* are thought to have originated in the mid-1980s (Dechter, 1986). What made these networks “deep” was the idea of *layering* of networks, that of including more than a single layer in the given neural network. Essentially, this meant that one could *approximate the approximation of a function* and do this iteratively through the chain of layers. By the mid-1990s, [Cortes and Vapnik (1995)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000068) introduced the *support-vector network*, which showed great promise when compared to more traditional methods. This work initiated further work in machine learning.

More modernly, neural networks are increasingly popular and are used in addressing very meaningful and complex applied problems such as self-driving cars, detecting financial fraud, medical diagnoses, among many others. Why is this the case? What makes neural networks so attractive to practitioners, perhaps more so than any other statistical or machine learning technique? [Alpaydin (2020)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009) nicely summarizes their appeal:536

> Deep learning methods are attractive mainly because they need less manual interference. We do not need to craft the right features or suitable basis functions or kernels, or worry about optimizing the exact network architecture. Once we have the data – and these days we have “big” data – and sufficient computation available, we just wait and let the learning algorithm discover by itself all that is necessary.
>
> (p. 315)

[Kroese et al. (2020)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000202) also do a nice job of contextualizing machine learning algorithms relative to the previously discussed early (and perhaps in some cases, overly ambitious) origins that sought to mimic neuronal activity:

> The attempts at building efficient machine learning algorithms by mimicking the functioning of the human brain have been as unsuccessful as the attempts at building flying aircraft by mimicking the flapping of birds’ wings. Instead, many effective machine algorithms have been inspired by age-old mathematical ideas for function approximation.
>
> (Kroese et al., [2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000202), p. 324)

## 15.3 Neural Networks

### 15.3.1 What Is a Neural Network? Minimizing the Hype

Neural networks can become very complex, but in understanding just what *is* a neural network, it is helpful, as is true with all statistical methods surveyed in this book, to strip them down to their most *basic elementary level*, akin to explaining the nature of an algorithm with an example as something as simple as long division. The power in understanding, in general, is that of coming across a term or concept that you believe must be very complex, then realizing you already know what it is through deeper examination and reminders of simpler examples and prototypes with which one is already familiar. Indeed, this has been the pedagogical approach adopted throughout this book. As we will discuss with reference to neural networks, in a strong sense, we already know what they are because we have already surveyed models that we will find to be extremely similar to them. Indeed, as remarked by [Sarle (1994)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000322) regarding the relatively early days of neural networks,

> NN \[neural network\] researchers routinely reinvent methods that have been known in the statistical or mathematical literature for decades or centuries, but they often fail to understand how these methods work.
>
> (p. 1)

Perhaps a bit extreme and exaggerated, Sarle’s argument does have some merit. Neural networks are their own methodology, but they do hold equivalencies (or *near* equivalences) to other methods under specific *special case* conditions. Our goal here is to strip them down to their essential constituents to learn and appreciate what is actually *there* instead of getting lost in the verbiage and hype of their jargon. The jargon and fanciful statements made about neural networks are useful for *marketing* them, but as one who wishes to truly understand them, the reader must truly seek to comprehend what is going on, as it were, *under the hood*.

### 15.3.2 Neural Networks Are Composite Functions

First and foremost, a neural network could be as simple as a linear regression model, or as complex as several nonlinear models. A neural network can also be *nonparametric*. One reason why neural networks are often referred to as “deep” learning is simply because they take model *functions of 537functions*. The “depth” is in the layering of what are referred to as *hidden layers*. *Multiple levels of composition* are what neural networks are all about (Goodfellow et al., [2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)).

As a very simple example of so-called “depth,” consider the function `f`(`x`) = `x`² which is that of a parabola. The function $g\\left( f\\left( x \\right) \\right) = \\sqrt{x^{2}}$ is more complex and deeper since it arises as a result of taking a function of the first function. That is, the outside function `g`(`x`) is applied to the inside function `f`(`x`), which constitutes a *composite function*, in our case, *the square root of x-squared*. Shallow neural networks typically contain only a single hidden layer with only a few nodes. A *deep network* consists of several layers with many nodes, each of which might range in complexity. In a deep network, at each hidden layer we are taking (typically) relatively complex functions of prior layers. As we increasingly take *functions of functions*, the network is learning characteristics of the features at an increasingly more complex level of abstraction, capitalizing on even the smallest of discriminators that will help better predict the output. These often include nonlinear and interacting relationships on the journey from input to output.
