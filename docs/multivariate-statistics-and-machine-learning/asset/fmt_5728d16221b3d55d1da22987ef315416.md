
Then there is the field of *computational statistics* which is described as *computationally intensive methods in statistics* ([Rizzo, 2019](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000303)) and which focuses on such techniques as the bootstrap and numerical algorithms that overlap a great deal with the subject matter of machine learning. Some even distinguish between *statistical computing* vs. *computational statistics*, the former having more emphasis on numerical methods and algorithms while the latter featuring additional topics such as exploratory analysis, data partitioning, and others ([Rizzo, 2019](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000303)).

When one browses the literature on statistics and machine learning, one will definitely notice differences. Machine learning “feels” more about the *algorithm* than it is about the *data*. For example, in statistics we use linear regression to model data, but in machine learning we are studying how effective linear regression is when compared to other “algorithms.” The computer science perspective is the theoretical study of algorithms, how they perform, how complex they are, their computational efficiency, how much they can store in memory, etc., whereas statistics feels more about fitting a precise scientific model to data. Of course, both overlap a great deal, but have slightly different *flavors*. We should always remember, however, that the computer is nothing more than an electronic machine that speeds up computation in either case. Much of computer science has little to do with the computer. It has everything to do with mathematics and statistics, to which computer scientists have contributed to immensely.22

As we will see throughout the book, many effective machine learning algorithms have been inspired by age-old mathematical ideas for *function approximation*. Indeed, one does not need a machine at all to do machine learning. The stranded person on a deserted island can (at least in *theory*, a lot of theory!) perform simple machine learning just as the high-tech computer scientist, but without the benefit of automation or an electronic machine. “Machine learning” is simply a name to reflect that many processes are *implemented* by a machine, namely, the computer. But make no mistake, machine learning itself, the *concept*, is as old as it gets. Yes, the Greeks were doing “machine learning” in some capacity long before humans “invented” it.

### 1.1.1 Mathematics, Statistics, and Computation

There is sometimes the perception by nonspecialists that “math is math” and that there is little creativity, in a sense, in developing such math. The author has served on student committees where the perception of mathematics is that it is *static*; *it is what it is* and not subject to invention, creativity, amendment, or reconstruction. Of course, this perception of mathematics is wholly false, as creativity in mathematics is what mathematics is all about. However difficult mathematics is, the mathematics itself is entirely (mostly) logical, and solutions are often correct or incorrect, at least at relatively elementary levels. The high-school student completing the problem set quickly looks at the back of the book to see if she got the solution correct. If the solution at the back of the book does not match the solution she obtained, her answer is deemed incorrect and she must then figure out why. Rarely is there flexibility in the solution for elementary problem sets. The “feedback loop” is relatively clear and straightforward.

The further away we move from mathematics, mathematical statistics, etc., the more we stumble upon areas where clear-cut and unique solutions often do not exist. Instead, there are often a *variety of solutions available*, some better than others, and many dependent on the preference or analytical style of the practitioner. This is often the most frustrating of experiences for the newcomer to applying mathematics and statistics to empirical problems. “Good solutions” are not unique and usually require the practitioner to infuse a mixture of methodological and philosophical thinking into the effort of arriving at the *best* solution. That is, the mathematical solution is only the *beginning*; she must now interpret what that solution *means* in the context of the scientific problem. This is where students often have difficulty, struggling with regards to what is the most “optimal” (in some sense) solution. Rigorous thinking is good, but in most things you also have to allow for compromise in “gray” areas and swim in a degree of flexibility. Within *pure* mathematics, most is quite rigorous, but only if fundamental philosophical issues are ignored that are at the foundations of such surface rigor. Deeper study into the philosophical assumptions of mathematics and one is no longer doing mathematics.

### 1.1.2 Mathematical Systems as Narratives to Understanding

One reason, I believe, that students can be “put off” by mathematics isn’t because of the difficulty of the subject. It is because they do not appreciate or understand what mathematics actually *is*. It can be very difficult to motivate someone to do something when they do not know what it is you are asking them to do and why. Asking a student to compute the quadratic formula to solve a quadratic equation is one thing, but explaining to them *why* solving for roots is such a big deal in the first place is quite another. Most students are excellent at blindly working the formula (basic arithmetic is really not *that* hard), but understanding the “why” beyond such rote manipulation is much more challenging.

Mathematics can be perceived in many ways, but we find it most useful for the student to perceive mathematics as a *narrative*. It is a *way* of explaining something, just like other narratives are 23ways of explaining other things. Of course, mathematical ways are often more *defined*, *exact*, and *precise*. A linear equation is quite precise, whereas using the English language to describe how your day went is quite vague. It is worth noting that not all things we describe are amenable to description using mathematics. The climate of one’s soul is probably not amenable to mathematical description, for instance. Self-concept is probably not amenable to mathematical description despite psychologists’ efforts to measure such things.

Mathematics, much beyond the early teachings of it merely being computation is often said to be about *form*, *structure*, *symmetry*, *equivalences*, *analogy*, and so on. However, these properties are not unique to mathematics. All of these elements are present in ventures such as music, art, physics, and even psychological theory. The only difference in the last of these is that rigor is much more difficult to implement, that is, the “rules” are not nearly as rigid. What makes mathematics unique is the relative *simplicity* (not *complexity*) in definitions that allows for rigor and communication among participants in the discipline. For instance, a *function* can be crisply defined in mathematics. Attempting a definition of the soul in philosophy or psychology can take up a dissertation.
