
*Figure 6.2* Concentric `RSS` ellipses for `OLS`. The ridge estimate is a shrunken version of the `OLS` estimate moving coefficients toward 0 as a function of a tuning parameter (left). For a constant concentric ellipse, the ridge estimates for `β[1]` and `β[2]` lie as indicated in the plot where both coefficients `β[1]` and `β[2]` have been shrunken toward, but not to, 0. That is, where the ellipse “touches” the circle (it is a circle or “disk” and not another shape as a consequence of the ridge constraint region) is at a point on the circle unequal to 0. In contrast, the lasso solution (right) generates a shape of a diamond constraint region with sharp edges, and hence the lasso solution for `β[1]` and `β[2]` is more likely to have a given coefficient equal to 0.[Return to text.⏎](#c6.xhtml#re_f6.2)

The concentric ellipses in [Figure 6.2](#c6.xhtml#f6.2) represent possible and constant values of residual sums of squares for coefficients `β[1]` and `β[2]` for each ellipse. That is, no matter where `RSS` is on a given particular ellipse, it is the *same* value. Ellipses further away from the estimated coefficients have greater `RSS`. The `OLS` estimate (and corresponding `RSS`) in the plot is that `RSS` produced in regression for a combination of `β[1]` and `β[2]` values. Notice where the corresponding ridge estimate is; it is at a location where both `β[1]` and `β[2]` values have “shrunk” and are thus smaller than the `OLS` estimates (situated in the center ellipse), though typically not driven all the way to 0 since geometrically ridge features a *circular constraint*. That is, the circular constraint region is effectively the search space for ridge coefficients. That search is most likely to terminate on an edge of the circular region as shown in [Figure 6.2](#c6.xhtml#f6.2) (left). The lasso, on the other hand, features a *diamond* search space with sharp edges instead (as a consequence of the L1 penalty), which illustrates that coefficients will not only be driven smaller, but may be “shrunk” to 0. That is, the search space is this time within the diamond, which means `RSS` will intersect a hard edge of the diamond rather than a smooth part of the circular region as in ridge. Hence, the lasso coefficient “lands” on a 0, such as for `β[1]`, as the first point that touches the lasso constraint region, that is, the lasso search space. For a more thorough discussion related to interpreting ridge (and lasso) regression this way, see James et al. ([2013](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000168)) for a deeper explanation of the geometry of ridge vs. lasso regression.

We now cross-validate the solution. The `RepeatedKFold` cross-validator in Python performs `k`-fold `n` times with different randomization in each repetition. We will request ten splits (i.e., “folds”) with three repeats:

import numpy as np

grid = dict()
grid[‘alpha’] = np.arange(0, 1, 0.01)

Python also offers *Bayesian ridge regression*, where regularization parameters are updated systematically. To implement Bayesian ridge regression, see the `BayesianRidge()` function in Python. Also, for the example featured here, because we drew random data from a standard normal distribution (i.e., the data drawn were on the same *scale*), we did not *standardize* the resulting data to have a mean equal to 0 and standard deviation equal to one. However, such does not imply that standardization would not be worthwhile here. This is especially true in situations where variables occur naturally on *different scales* where one does not want the “importance” of one variable to exceed that of another simply due to the scale on which it is being measured. On the other hand, it is possible that this kind of “Bayesian prior” in terms of the *original scale on which measurements are taken* is precisely what is desired depending on the research setting and context, in which case the decision to standardize or not is a more difficult decision ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)). Either way, it is a good idea to compare results both with and without standardization when conducting most regularization methods. If one must choose one option over the other, it is probably a good idea to standardize ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), p. 63) in most cases, but such should not be done without great care and consideration.
