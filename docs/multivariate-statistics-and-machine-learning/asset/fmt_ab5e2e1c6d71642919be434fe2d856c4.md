
That is, the derivative of the above function, `f(x) = 5t² + 2t + 8` is, as expected using the power rule, `f'(x) = 10t + 2`, where the power rule is given by $\\frac{df}{dx}\\left( x^{n} \\right) = nx^{n - 1}$.

## 2.58.1 Local Behavior and Approximation
Much insight can be gleaned from gaining perspective on the derivative and its concept and can be used to indirectly foreshadow many of the methods in this book. First, note the obvious point that the derivative is not *equal* to the curve on which it is being computed. We can perceive the derivative as an *approximation* to the curve at that particular point. The function is nonlinear, but at that local level we can treat it as though it were linear. That is, *locally, or “zoomed in,” a nonlinear function behaves a lot like a linear function*. This idea of *locality* will return time and time again in this book. Some methods focus on more *global* structure, while others focus on more local behavior. As a preview of this distinction, polynomial regression is a global function computed on a curve, while regression splines, as just one example, model the data at a more local level.

Most of differential calculus is about establishing and using rules of differentiation to make it easier than using the above “long form.” Rules such as the *product rule*, *quotient rule*, *sum rule*, and *chain rule* are typically used and derived. Much of introductory calculus is in practicing using shorthand rules such as these to compute derivatives in a variety of contexts. We assume the reader is at least somewhat familiar with those rules or has at least at one point in their past come across them. It is not essential to be able to perform them at the drop of a hat, since we can rest assured that computers will “know” the correct rule (if the shorthand is used at all, computationally) to implement and compute the derivative that we need. What *is* essential is knowing what a derivative *is* and that there are numerous rules, all formally provable, to obtaining derivatives.155

Though we dispense from any in-depth discussion of rules of differentiation, we will mention one rule, however, that is especially important and relevant for our purposes since it is used in neural networks, in the *backpropagation algorithm* more specifically, and that is the *chain rule of differentiation*. For a function `y = m(x) = f[g(x)]`, the chain rule is defined,

`m'(x) = f'[g(x)] g'(x)`

(2.221)

The chain rule deals with *composite functions*; we are taking a *function of a function*, that is, `f'[g(x)]` is the function `f()` of the function `g()`. The function `g` on the inside is referred to as the *core* or *inner function* and is applied second to the outer function. The above is for two functions, but we can generalize to a greater number of functions. It can also be shown that the composition of two continuous functions is itself continuous.

As we will see later in the book when we study neural networks, understanding backpropagation is as simple, in a “big picture” sense, as understanding the chain rule. Indeed, most continuous optimization methods employ differential calculus. The reader should know that not all functions are differentiable even if the given family of functions one is working with is continuous. For example, the absolute value function is continuous everywhere yet not differentiable at the “cusp” of the function (i.e., the bottom of the function with the sharp edge).

## 2.58.2 Composite Functions and Basis Expansions
In discussing composite functions, in addition to previewing the idea of a neural network, is an opportune time to highlight and forecast another theme that will reappear throughout this book, and that is the idea of *basis expansions*, of which, as we will see, neural networks can be conceived as a special case of such an expansion. Consider the following:

$$f\\left( x \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{M}{\\beta\_{m}h\_{m}\\left( x \\right)}}$$

In the above, `h[m]` is called a *basis function*. Colloquially, it is providing a “basis” for `x` before we weight `h[m](x)` by estimated weights, `β[m]`. How is this idea linked to that of composite functions? Well, consider that `h[m]` is taking a function of `x` and `β[m]` is then applied to this inner (or “core”) function. This process can be thought of, in a very general sense, as taking a *function of a function*, which by definition is the very idea of a composite function.

Note that when `h[m] = 1`,

$$\\begin{array}{l} {f\\left( x \\right) = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{M}{\\beta\_{m}h\_{m}\\left( x \\right)}}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{M}{\\beta\_{m}\\left( 1 \\right)\\left( x \\right)}}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{M}{\\beta\_{m}x}}} \\\\ \\end{array}$$

we obtain the classic regression model-style weighting. The key point for now is to recognize that the basis function is applied to the observations in `x` before the relevant weighting by `β[m]` takes place. In theory, there is practically no limit to how we can designate `h[m]` to transform `x` before relevant weighting by `β[m]`. As we will see throughout the book, basis functions are one of the themes 156that unify a great deal of statistical and machine learning models and we will revisit these ideas often as we survey novel methods. *Understanding basis functions is a powerful way to understand the “forest” of many statistical and machine learning models and methods.*

## 2.59 The Partial Derivative
While single-valued derivatives featured earlier explicate the concepts of differentiation, in practice, we are usually more interested in computing *partial derivatives* of functions. A partial derivative is the derivative of a function while holding other variables *constant*. The collection of partial derivatives for a multivariable function is typically referred to as the *gradient*, though the word “gradient” is also commonly used to denote the slope between two variables. One has to decipher the meaning based on the context of the situation to know what is being referred to.

The partial derivative of a function `f` with respect to `x[1]` holding all other variables, `x[2]`, `x[3]`, ..., `x[n]` constant is given by

$$\\frac{\\partial f}{\\partial x\_{1}} = \\lim\\limits\_{h\\rightarrow 0}\\frac{f\\left( x\_{1} + h,x\_{2},...,x\_{n} \\right) - f\\left( x \\right)}{h}$$

(2.222)

where ∂`x[1]` denotes the variable about which we are taking the partial derivative. All other variables are held constant. As was the case for the derivative for single variables, the partial derivative only becomes such as `h → 0`, that is, in the limit.

Since there will necessarily be a “collection” of these derivatives, it is convenient to group them in a row vector,

$$\\nabla\_{x}f = \\text{grad}f = \\frac{df}{dx} = \\left\\lbrack {\\frac{\\partial f\\left( x \\right)}{\\partial x\_{1}}\\,\\,\\frac{\\partial f\\left( x \\right)}{\\partial x\_{2}}\\, \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot \\,\\frac{\\partial f\\left( x \\right)}{\\partial x\_{n}}} \\right\\rbrack$$

(2.223)

where such a vector is typically referred to as the *gradient* of the function and the *Jacobian* the vector of first-order partial derivatives of the function ([Deisenroth et al., 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000080)).
