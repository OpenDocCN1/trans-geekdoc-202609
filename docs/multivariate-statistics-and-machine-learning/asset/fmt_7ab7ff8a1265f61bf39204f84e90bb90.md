
As we will learn throughout this book, many statistical and machine learning methods, complex as they may at first glance appear, center around the general problem of `optimization`, which loosely translates to finding “best” weights for a given function, from simple to more complex, within a structural framework or `model`. To arrive at these weights, a designated function, which will depend on the particular method, will typically need to be `maximized` or `minimized`. The classic and most familiar example is that of least-squares where we wish to minimize the sum of squared errors. The general principle is that we are minimizing an `objective function`, the objective function in this case being the sum of squared deviations or errors from the mean. Of course, sum of squared errors is by no means the only objective function we may wish to minimize, and hence optimization is a very general idea applicable, at least in theory, to a whole host of functions. That is, the loss `L[2](y,y′)=|y−y′|²` is a special case of the more general `L[p]` loss defined by157

`L[p](y,y′)=|y−y′|^(p)`

(2.224)

where `p` is a number equal to or greater than 1. Objective functions are often referred to as `cost`, `loss`, or `error` functions when they are minimized (Goodfellow et al., [2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)).

A technique called `gradient descent` and derivations and extensions of it (soon to be discussed) is used, in part, because closed-form analytical solutions do not always exist or are not preferable or practical to those provided by gradient descent. The origins of gradient descent are usually ascribed to [Cauchy (1847)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000056). Recall that a `closed-form` or `analytical solution` is an algebraic-type solution based on the solution of equations. Often, these types of “clean” solutions do not exist and we are instead required to use iterative numerical approaches to approximate solutions. Thanks to the computer, iterating can be done in a matter of seconds whereas in the past, even if the relevant algorithm had been invented, it was prohibitive to implement due to a lack of computing power.

For example, early maximum likelihood iterative algorithms were developed as early as 1912 ([Fisher, 1922](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000099)) but as was true of many computations back then, were difficult to implement due to the computational complexity of carrying out such procedures. This is precisely why computation is so important to modern algorithms and why the `computational capacity` of various algorithms is a field in and of itself and a primary research area of computer science. Gradient descent itself is actually a fairly old algorithm, but it has become increasingly popular relatively recently because of the advances in computing capacity. It would not be unreasonable to conjecture that many of the computational challenges experienced today will seem trivial even a decade or two from now. Computer scientists and engineers earn their accolades!

## 2.60.1 What Does “Optimal” Mean?

Before considering further how gradient descent works, it is well worth taking a moment to discuss what “optimal” means, since the goal of the typical algorithm will be to search for such an optimal solution. `Optimal implies that the weights (or more generally, solutions) are arrived at in such a way that they usually satisfy one or more conditions or constraints`. Again, a prime example of this that the reader will undoubtedly recognize is that of ordinary least-squares in classical regression. Recall the simple linear regression `model`:

`y[i]=β[0]+β[1]x[1]+ε[i]`

Recall that the goal is to estimate weights `β[0]` and `β[1]`. What values for these parameters are considered “best” or “optimal?” We know from least-squares theory that they will be chosen such that they minimize the sum of squared errors around the regression line. That is, the “optimal” values are those that satisfy the least-squares conditions.

More generally and formally, we can say that many statistical methods aim to estimate parameters that minimize a `loss function`:

$${\\hat{\\theta}}\_{mle} = \\underset{\\theta}{\\arg\\min}l\\left( \\theta \\right)$$  

(2.225)

where `l(θ)` is the loss function for the given problem and `θ̂` the maximum likelihood estimate of the parameter vector. In our example with least-squares, the loss function was the sum of squared errors. Maximum likelihood, as we have seen, is a popular method of parameter estimation that selects the parameters that essentially give the data the highest probability, that is,158

$${\\hat{\\theta}}\_{mle} = \\underset{\\theta}{\\arg\\max}p\\left( D \\middle| \\theta \\right)$$  

(2.226)

The take-home message here is that “optimal” usually involves the idea of `constraints` or `conditions`, or more generally, some kind of `restrictions` before computing values, even if those conditions are implicit in the algorithm itself. In maximum likelihood, only those parameters `θ` are selected that maximize the likelihood of `D`; the parameter values are not chosen `unconditionally`. They are chosen in line with how maximum likelihood defines the “optimal” parameter.

As an analogy, consider the most optimal way to get home from your job. It is likely the distance which features the shortest `distance` and `time`, but in winter, going home must be subject to the constraint that the route is safe and free of ice. `Constraints and conditions are all around us, rule our activities, human relationships, and likewise appear in the minimization or maximization of functions in mathematics, statistics, and machine learning`.

## 2.60.2 Minima and Maxima via Calculus

As the reader may recall from calculus, finding `maxima` or `minima` of functions usually involves setting derivatives equal to 0 to determine at which point(s) on the function the slope is equal to 0. For instance, consider the function in [Figure 2.17](#c2.xhtml#f2.17).

![Figure 2.17 Function having a minimum and maximum value represented by derivatives equaling 0.Return to text.⏎](images/fig2.17.jpg)

`Figure 2.17` Function having a minimum and maximum value represented by derivatives equaling 0.[Return to text.⏎](#c2.xhtml#re_f2.17)

How can we know that the maximum value of the function occurs where it does? How about the minimum? We can tell this because at those locations, `the derivative is equal to 0`, and a derivative equal to 0 implies that the slope at that given point is equal to 0, since the derivative is the slope (or more precisely, the limit of secant lines approximating the function at the given point). This holds true for minima as well. That is, in general, for a differentiable function on an open interval (`a`,`b`), if `c` is a maximum or minimum of the function, then this implies that `f′(c)=0`. This result is implied by `Rolle’s theorem` in analysis which essentially holds that if a function `f` is continuous on an interval [`a, b`] and `f` is differentiable on that interval (`a`, `b`) and that `f(a)=f(b)`, then there exists at least one point `c` between (`a`, `b`) such that the derivative is equal to 0, that is, `f′(c)=0`. Rolle’s theorem is a special case of the `mean value theorem`. For details, see [Bartle and Sherbert (2011)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000020). Hence, when we set `f′(x)=0`, we can determine the locations where the slope is equal to 0. A `critical point` of an objective function corresponds to parameter estimates that cause the derivative of the objective function to vanish at that point.
