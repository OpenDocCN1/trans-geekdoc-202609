
(2.35)

where `x` will denote a specific value of the variable and `f(x)` is the density under the curve for that value, and for discrete random variables,

$$\\mu = {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}x}p\\left( x \\right)$$

(2.36)

where `p(x)` will be the probability of the specific value `x` since with discrete random variables we can assign an individual probability to `x`, whereas with continuous variables recall, we cannot.

The arithmetic mean is only one of many means that can be computed on a set of data. Other means include the *geometric mean* and the *harmonic mean*. The geometric mean is given by

$${\\overline{x}}\_{g} = \\left( {\\prod\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}x\_{i}} \\right)^{\\frac{1}{n}} = \\sqrt\[n\]{x\_{1} \\cdot x\_{2} \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot x\_{n}}$$

(2.37)

Note that in the geometric mean, we are taking products of values (via the product operator, $\\prod\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}{}$) instead of sums as in the arithmetic mean and taking the root equal to the number of observations `n` (i.e., 1/`n`).

The harmonic mean is given by88

$${\\overline{x}}\_{h} = \\frac{n}{\\frac{1}{x\_{1}} + \\frac{1}{x\_{2}} + \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot \\frac{1}{x\_{n}}} = \\frac{n}{\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\frac{1}{x\_{i}}}$$

(2.38)

The important point is that when someone reports computing the mean on a set of data, be sure to inquire *which* mean is being computed. Typically, when the word “mean” is used, it refers to the arithmetic mean, but not always. In the reporting of scientific research, it would be irresponsible of a researcher to not specify which mean is being reported if it were *not* the arithmetic mean. Further, though the arithmetic mean is often advertised as a measure of *central* tendency, it only reveals the center of a distribution in the sense of $\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}{\\left( {x\_{i} - \\overline{x}} \\right) = 0}$, and in no other sense. In a perfectly normal distribution, the mean will be at the center of the distribution with symmetrical and equal areas on each side of the distribution. In a skewed distribution, however, the mean will no longer be at the “center” of the distribution and will simply fall where the condition $\\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( x\_{i} - \\overline{x} \\right.} \\right) = 0$ is satisfied. For example, consider the location of the arithmetic mean in the distribution in [Figure 2.6](#c2.xhtml#f2.6).

Key to note is that \\left. {\\sum\\limits\_{i = 1}^{n}\\left( x\_{i} - \\overline{x} \\right.} \\right) = 0 does not necessarily imply \\overline{x} is in the geometric “middle” of a distribution, nor does meeting this condition necessarily imply the distribution is normal in shape.Return to text.⏎](images/fig2.6.jpg)

Note that the mean is *not* at the center of the distribution, however it is still defined as a measure of “central tendency.” If you memorize “*mean* = *central tendency*,” you will never truly understand the mean! The only way to understand it is to inspect its structure and appreciate what it actually *does*. This is true of all equations and formulas in statistics and machine learning. *Never rely on “verbal” definitions to describe them; only their equations and formulas reveal what they actually accomplish*.

It is a good computing exercise to compute the mean the “long way” in R or Python (try devising a function to generate the mean). In Python, we can easily compute the mean by using `numpy.mean()`:89

```
import numpy as np

np.mean(x)
Out[56]: 20.0
```

The mean of the observations 10, 20, and 30 is, not surprisingly, equal to 20.

### 2.21.1.1 Averaging Over Cases: Why Thinking in Terms of Averages Can Be Dangerous

Means and averages are much more than about the above computations. Though averaging incorporates all deviations from a center point, it simultaneously ignores individual information. If averaging is held as a *policy* to govern by, then it will naturally and necessarily ignore important individual cases. The “spirit” of an average need not be numerical either; it can be entirely conceptual as the following example illustrates.

Consider the case of canine vaccines. Many states require all dogs to be vaccinated routinely every one to three years. However, there is increasing evidence, some published, and some based on clinical observation and experience, that over-vaccinating may have detrimental health effects. If this is the case, then why have a blanket policy of vaccinating all dogs every one to three years? The answer is an easy one, because of *convenience and a lack of attention paid to each particular case*. Since the goal of vaccination is to not only protect any individual dog but also to offer protection to all human beings, the policy of vaccination is very general. But this greatly ignores individual cases. Not all dogs are the same and not all dogs have the same behavior patterns. For the dog whose owner doesn’t know where the dog is at 11 p.m. as the dog roams about the neighborhood searching for mischief and wrestling with potentially rabid foxes and raccoons, the one-to-three-year vaccination policy is a good one! However, for the dog who is primarily an “inside dog” who goes outside only periodically and is in virtually no danger of contracting the disease due to hypervigilant supervision by his owner, the one-to-three-year policy is overkill *for that particular dog*. Averaging makes things “easy” and allows a *blanket policy*, and essentially discriminates against cases that do not “fit” into requiring the policy. The law says your 11-year-old senior dog requires vaccination, yet science may suggest doing so is a bad idea because the dog is elderly, has medical conditions, and in all probability the prior vaccination is probably still providing immunity (a titer test for immunity may be a better option). Given such risk factors, vaccinating again would be somewhat reckless, yet by the “easy” and convenient policy of averages, the law often does not take into account these individual cases.

The moral of the story is that while thinking (and computing) in terms of averages can be productive, it can also be very dangerous. *Individual case analysis* is almost always better, despite how more expensive and time-consuming such an analysis may be. *Means and averages are balance points, yet often, “balancing” should not be of primary interest*. Case studies and more focused analysis should be the priority when the data, resources, and time allow it. Indeed, this is one criticism against numerical “summative” statistical research in general, that it focuses too heavily on the *common person* rather than the *individual person*. In clinical settings, case studies will typically do away with averages and instead focus on the individual.
