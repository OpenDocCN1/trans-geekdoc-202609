
The idea of risk also translates to notions of statistical lives. Returning to the airline example discussed earlier, airlines seek to minimize loss in terms of injury and deaths. Yet at what cost? Certainly not at *any* cost. Airplanes regularly land in thunderstorms and adverse conditions even though such conditions pose more risk than others, yet such risk is *calibrated* against other interests, such as retaining customer satisfaction by a timely landing, keeping flight schedules on track, as well as the interests of the crew in wanting to be done with their flight as a result of fatigue. That isn’t to suggest a flight crew would recklessly land in a severely dangerous thunderstorm, but it would be tremendously naïve to think that tolerable amounts of risk are not existent and implicit in such landings. When those calibrations begin to impose too much, such that they risk exceeding the risk *threshold*, which is usually probably subjectively determined case-by-case, then a more dangerous level of risk arises which may lead to accidents. The point we are making here is simply 37to say that no behaviors are risk-free, and an airline that purports to manage a risk-free operation of flight is simply not being truthful. *Risk is minimized but such minimization is subject to constraints and competing interests*. Optimization algorithms in statistics and machine learning also optimize, but usually not *unconditionally*. They usually optimize subject to constraints and conditions.

### 1.11.3 Medical Decision-Making and Dominating Criteria
In basic decision theory, we say a decision is *dominated* by one or more criteria if that decision trumps a competing decision based on selective input or information. For example, consider the dog owner once more regarding the detection of a splenic mass via ultrasound. Is it benign or is it cancerous? Of course, a benign mass will not spread to other parts of the body, whereas a malignant mass is more likely to do so. The decision is whether to remove the spleen via surgery. In this example, one can spend much time and expense trying to determine whether the mass is benign or malignant, yet the decision to remove vs. not remove the spleen may be *dominated* by the possibility of the mass bleeding or rupturing, which could put the dog’s life in danger regardless. One could CT-scan the body to look for microscopic metastasis; however, at what value would that be to the decision of whether or not to proceed with surgery? Likely minimal, given that the possibility of a bleed *dominates* the surgery decision. The decision for surgery is said to be *dominated* by the bleed possibility over the nature of the mass (benign vs. malignant) and hence surgery is recommended (base rates aside) regardless of whether the mass ends up being benign or malignant. Many times we spend countless hours grappling over inputs to a decision that in the end do not matter as these elements are dominated by other criteria.

The above is just one example of how certain decision criteria might dominate others in particular situations. As another example, have you ever witnessed the perfectly built home, but in the wrong location? A true majestic masterpiece of a home, but in such a poor location! *The house fails to sell because the location decision dominates the favorability of the home*.

In statistics, decisions you make along the way in your analysis dominate others. For example, if you build your entire analysis on the arithmetic mean as a basis of central tendency, when in fact the median would have been a more accurate portrayal of the data, even if the analysis is quite complex and detailed this crucial decision dominates all others. If you build on a questionable foundation, strong as the edifice may appear, it will be structurally unsound. Foundations first! Newton’s theory of physics, impressive as it is, Einstein found was built on incomplete foundations. Critical questions about foundations are what lead to scientific and other revolutions.

## 1.12 All Knowledge, Scientific and Other, Is Tentative
As a final point regarding our review of scientific principles, we should emphasize the fact that all knowledge is tentative pending further development or discovery. Within the realm of science, this idea is well known. Perhaps the clearest example of this is with Newtonian physics. It was “correct” years ago, but now is less correct given Einstein’s theory of relativity. Likewise, aspartame used to not be considered a carcinogen, but only recently has it been classified as possibly one. What is true scientifically yesterday is no longer today. This is how science functions and is the norm. Scientific “facts” are always *conditionally* or *tentatively* true based on further evidence.

Surely then, statistical and mathematical knowledge must be unconditionally always true, right? No! While at first glance it may appear that such knowledge is steadfastly certain, history has shown us differently. Perhaps the most alarming example of this was with Euclidean geometry and the discovery of non-Euclidean geometries. Euclidean geometry was *for certain* the only geometry for over a couple thousand years. Had that “fact” not been considered tentative by very bright minds who investigated foundations while all others busied themselves in further building *on* the 38edifice of Euclidean geometry, we would have never come to know that Euclidean geometry was not a “fact” of mathematics. Likewise with theorems of mathematics or statistics, a proven theorem is generally considered true and timeless, but errors in proofs are regularly found such that it makes them less certain and enduring almost overnight.

The moral of the story here is to always hold knowledge, whatever the kind, as *tentative* given more information. One might *act* as though such knowledge is true, while at the same time being open to revising one’s opinion of such knowledge if new information contradicts it. The earth is currently known to be approximately round, or so, but if tomorrow’s discovery provides contradicting information we must have an open mindset such that we are willing to revise our beliefs. Likewise, climate change is a theory, not a fact. As new evidence enters into the equation we must be prepared to revise our belief in climate change. As we will see, this way of thinking has very strong Bayesian undertones.

## 1.13 Occam’s Razor
Occam’s razor is the idea that if there are competing theories to explain data, the simplest explanation shall be preferred over the more complex assuming that each competing theory accounts for the data equally well. While the principle is usually appealed to be based on intuition and plausibility (i.e., it sounds like a *reasonable* position to take with regards to theory-building), the principal can actually be more rigorously theoretically defended (Stoffi et al., [2022](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000350)). Occam’s razor is far from being a statistical or machine learning concept alone as we can apply the principle even informally to everyday life.

One area where Occam’s razor is especially applicable as it relates to machine learning models is when constructing a neural network where as we will see, one of the decisions faced by the user is the number of hidden nodes and layers to include in the network. All else equal and given that competing neural network constructions result in more or less the same predictive power, Occam’s razor requires keeping the model as simple as possible while still maintaining its ability to generalize well. Hence, if a neural network with a single simple hidden layer does as well as one with multiple complex layers, the simpler model is to be preferred. However, as Einstein (and others) reminded us, *everything should be made as simple as possible, but no simpler*. A “too simple” of an explanation or theory that hinders explainability is not optimal either.
