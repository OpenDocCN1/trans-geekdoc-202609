
where `d[i j]` is once more the distance between two data points `i`, `j` in the original data and `D[i j]`=||`p[i]`−`p[j]`|| is once more the distance between the newly constructed, estimated, or “mapped” points ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057)). The lower the stress, the better the fit. The most ideal situation is for `d[i j]` = `D[i j]` under which case ${\\sum\\limits\_{i,j}\\left( d\_{ij} - D\_{ij} \\right)^{2}} = 0$ and thus $S = 0/{\\sum\\limits\_{i,j}d\_{ij}^{2}} = 0$. Stress increases as $\\sum\\limits\_{i,j}\\left( d\_{ij} - D\_{ij} \\right)^{2}$ increasingly deviates from zero but has limits \[0, 1\] due to the denominator, $\\sum\\limits\_{i,j}d\_{ij}^{2}$ which serves to *scale* (not *standardize*) the squared distances. Stress decreases as the number of dimensions increases ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 208).

When the distance is Euclidean, PCA and classical MDS will coincide. This is because `S`, the sample covariance matrix of PCA is proportional to `B` = `X X'`. This can be seen by inputting `B` into the eigenvalue-eigenvector characteristic equation and observing these two equalities,

`B a[i] = λ[i] a[i]`

where `a[i]` are eigenvectors and `λ[i]` corresponding eigenvalues. We see that the eigenvalues will be the same and eigenvectors will also be subject to a linear transformation ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 200). As noted by [Jolliffe (2002)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000174), PCA operates on a matrix of similarities between *variables*, whereas principal coordinate analysis (i.e., classical MDS) operates on a matrix of similarities between *observations* (i.e., *individuals*). However, both can lead to the same results.

Consult Chatfield and Collins ([1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 201) for a fruitful discussion of how PCA and MDS relate to one another and why these authors recommend that PCA, not MDS, be performed if 432distances are Euclidean. Jolliffe ([2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000174), p. 90) seconds this recommendation, except notes that when the number of cases is less than the number of variables (i.e., `n` < `p`), even though both MDS and PCA will yield results, MDS will have computational advantages over PCA.

> *Multidimensional scaling is equivalent to classical principal components analysis when Euclidean distance is used as a metric on the non-standardized correlation matrix, that is, the covariance matrix. When non-Euclidean distances are used, the equivalency no longer necessarily holds.*

The classical form of MDS is called *principal coordinate analysis*, which is usually a metric version of scaling (see below for a discussion of metric vs. nonmetric approaches).

### 11.10.1 Merits of MDS

When compared with other dimension-reduction techniques (e.g., classical PCA), metric multidimensional scaling carries with it many advantages. For one, it is not restricted to linear projections of the data and hence can handle nonlinear problems. Second, it can be applied to any metric space, not only Euclidean ([Demaine et al., 2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000083)). Distances can also be arrived at subjectively, for instance, subjects stating their preferences for a given product as an estimate ([Rencher & Christensen, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295)). This choice will depend on the preferred metric, which we discuss shortly. The issue of what the proximity matrix contains is a substantive one; to reiterate, MDS itself, or any other statistical procedure does not care where the numbers came from, which is why *measurement*, not *analysis*, should be the primary concern of any investigation. Poorly measured attributes are not usually worth analyzing, either statistically or any other way.

A last point worth mentioning regarding the merits of MDS is that it emphasizes, and thus attempts to minimize, *squared differences*. While this may be ideal in some contexts, it may be less so in others. In some contexts, smaller distances may actually matter more than larger ones. *Sammon mapping* is one technique that puts greater emphasis on preserving smaller distances and hence attempts to capture more of the *local structure* of the manifold than does classical MDS. The stress function for salmon mapping can be given as follows,

$$S = \\frac{1}{\\sum\\limits\_{i < j}d\_{ij}}{\\sum\\limits\_{i < j}\\frac{\\left( d\_{ij} - D\_{ij} \\right)^{2}}{d\_{ij}}}$$

(11.16)

A comparison of the above stress function to that of MDS reveals that distances (`d[i j]`−`D[i j]`)² are divided by `d[i j]` to provide a sense of “relativity” to the squared distances. That is, if (`d[i j]`−`D[i j]`)² is found to be large, the stress function for Sammon mapping recognizes that this may in part be due to the squaring of the distance, and so relates it to `d[i j]`, which is not squared. It is in this sense that Sammon mapping puts more emphasis on smaller distances than MDS. This also has the potential for Sammon mapping to be able to generate a nonlinear map ([Alpaydin, 2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000009)). The constant $\\frac{1}{\\sum\\limits\_{i < j}d\_{ij}}$ is simply to ease the computation of the gradient ([van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000372), p. 2597).

The fact that Sammon mapping focuses more on smaller distances still does not prevent one or two distances from dominating the cost function. As we will soon see, `t-SNE` attempts to 433remedy this by not focusing so much on these raw magnitudes of separation in the data ([van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000372), p. 2597) and instead more closely modeling them as having equal local to global importance.
