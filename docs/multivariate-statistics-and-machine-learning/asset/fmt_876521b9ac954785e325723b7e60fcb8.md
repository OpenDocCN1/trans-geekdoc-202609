
Historically, factor analysis was slow to gain acceptance by the mathematics community. Some mathematical statisticians had disdain for the method due to some of its perceived technical pitfalls (such as loadings not being unique) but likely also due to the academic turf war it may have initiated with psychology where the method was originally conceived ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057)). Some books on multivariate analysis would even refuse to cover the technique at all and pretend as though it didn’t exist!

With the arrival and meteoric rise of machine learning however, along with the data science “revolution,” factor analysis has enjoyed a resurgence in virtually `all` fields, not just those in social science in which it has always been quite popular. Today, factor analysis and its many derivatives 465and extensions is simply another method in the arsenal of the statistician or computer scientist. For example, `generative topographic mapping` (Bishop et al., [1998](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000031)), a form of nonlinear factor analysis and alternative to self-organizing maps ([Kohonen, 1982](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000196)) is featured in machine learning and builds on the foundation of latent variable models, an idea originally popularized by factor analysis. Indeed, the concept of latent variables is a powerful one and though the `idea` of latent variables is not due to factor analysis per se ([Mulaik, 1987](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000257)), factor analysis is responsible for operationalizing the construct and putting it on a technical foundation. Factor analysis has also helped give rise to the field of psychometrics. For instance, `item response theory`, also known as `latent distribution theory`, `item characteristic curve` (ICC), and `item characteristic function` (ICF), or `latent trait analysis` ([Mair, 2018](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000237)) all build on the basic factor model. A good overview and summary of these models are provided by [Mahmud (2017)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000236).

Hence, we argue that to simply conclude that factor analysis is problematic and should be avoided is a well-outdated viewpoint, especially in the context of the rise of computer science and machine learning where it has found tremendous application. What is not an outdated viewpoint is that unless one is willing to study, understand and appreciate some of the more salient theoretical underpinnings and caveats of the method, one stands a much greater chance of misunderstanding and misusing factor analysis and concluding from the method much more than is epistemologically warranted.

## 12.4 Factor Estimation

As with virtually all statistical methods, we require a way to estimate parameters of the model. Though there are numerous estimation methods in EFA, classical estimation methods include `principal factor` (or `principal axis factoring`) as well as `maximum likelihood`, which we now (very) briefly survey.

### 12.4.1 Principal Factor (Principal Axis Factoring)

To understand the principal factor method of estimation, it is first necessary to revisit PCA and define what is meant by a `communality`. Given that we are working with the correlation matrix, the main diagonal in a PCA will contain `unit variances` as a result of each variable initially contributing one “unit” of variance to the PCA. In the `principal factor` approach, also known as `principal axis factoring`, the residual matrix is minimized and features replacing the initial communalities along the main diagonal in the correlation matrix with weights that are derived from the given variable being regressed on remaining variables subjected to the factor analysis. ### 12.4.2 Maximum Likelihood

The most common approach for estimating factors is, perhaps not surprisingly, that of maximum likelihood. It is easiest to carry out `maximum likelihood` (ML) estimation via the `Expectation-Maximization` algorithm (Choi et al., [2010](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000062); [Rubin & Thayer, 1982](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000316)). As was the case for the orthogonal factor model, the ML estimator is also invariant under rotation, and hence any rotation will generate the same ML estimates (Choi et al., [2010](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000062)). As we will see later, this is not unconditionally true for all factor analysis models. For instance, imposing an `l`[1] penalty on the factor model, yielding sparse “lasso” factor analysis, is not rotationally invariant (Choi et al., [2010](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000062)).

## 12.5 Factor Rotation

Recall we had said earlier that factor loadings are not unique, or, put another way, they are unique `but only up to an orthogonal rotation`. Recall that a square matrix `T` is orthogonal if `T``T`^′ = `T`^′`T` = `I`. Given this quality of the factor model, it stands that we can reproduce the same covariance matrix `Σ` but with alternative loadings. This translates into rotating the factor solution virtually at will yet still reproducing the same covariance matrix, `Σ`.
