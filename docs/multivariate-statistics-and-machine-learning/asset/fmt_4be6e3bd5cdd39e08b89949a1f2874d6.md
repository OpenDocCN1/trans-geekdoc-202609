## 11.7 Principal Curves

Recall that in classic PCA, we fit a hyperplane such that the sum of the squared perpendicular distances is minimized. Principal curves are more general than PCA. As we will see, PCA in fact can be considered a special case of the wider principal curve framework just as we considered it a specialized case of kernel PCA. Instead of providing a line of “best fit” as in PCA, principal curves seeks to find a curved *approximation*, a curved *manifold* of dimensionality two or greater. However, as an estimation algorithm principal curves does not result in dimension reduction (Meila & Zhang, [2023](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000247), p. 9). More simply, principal curves fits a curve to a swarm of data points, as illustrated in Figure 11.10.

The fit provided by principal curves is *nonlinear* and *nonparametric*, meaning that their exact form is suggested or “learned” by the data ([Hastie & Stuetzle, 1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000143)). A precise definition of a principal curve was given by [Hastie and Stuetzle (1989)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000143) who first proposed the method: “an infinitely differentiable finite length curve that passes through the middle of the data.”

A feature of the curve is that it is *self-consistent*, meaning that every point on the curve is the expected value of the data points projecting onto this point ([Ozertem & Erdogmus, 2011](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000278)). What this means is that if we arbitrarily selected a point on the curve and amassed all the data that project onto this point, the average of all those points would coincide with the point on the curve ([Hastie & Stuetzle, 1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000143), p. 502); hence, “self-consistent.” We can immediately see that *if the curve is a straight line and is self-consistent, then the principal curve will be that of a principal component*. Algorithms used for principal curves essentially iterate until this convergence of self-consistency is achieved ([Hastie & Stuetzle, 1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000143)). Details of the algorithm are given in Hastie and Stuetzle ([1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000143), p. 506).

Note that the definition of a principal curve is different from that of a principal component or even ordinary least-squares line in regression. Both of these latter methods fit a constant straight-line hyperplane to the data regardless of what the data are. There are no “bends” in the curve to follow the data. That is, in PCA and ordinary least-squares (`OLS`), *we are imposing a linear form 426on the data; the data are not telling us what form of polynomial we should fit*. In contrast, principal curves provide a rather “smooth” fit to the data; the fitted curve is essentially “guided” by the data.

> *In classic PCA or OLS regression, we impose a linear form on the data, regardless of whether the data actually follow a linear form or not. In contrast, in principal curves, the form of the curve is suggested or “learned” from the data, and is hence, nonparametric in nature.*

Essentially, principal curves find the “middle” of the data in some sense (the “self-consistency” sense) when fitting the curve. Not surprisingly, there are various notions of how “middle” is defined. *Global* or *top-down* algorithms begin with an initial line, which is usually the first classic principal component, then through iterations, “bend” this line until some convergence criteria are met. The curve that is fit is a result of successive iterations and is a *smooth local average of points*, where the definition of “local” is based on the distance in arc length of the projections of the points onto the curve found in the previous iteration ([Hastie & Stuetzle, 1989](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000143)). As summarized by Hastie and Stuetzle (pp. 502–503):

> We formally define principal curves to be those smooth curves that are self-consistent for a distribution or data set. This means that if we pick any point on the curve, collect all the data that project onto this point, and average them, then this average coincides with the point on the curve … the algorithm for finding principal curves is equally intuitive. Starting with any smooth curve (usually the largest principal component), it checks if this curve is self-consistent by projecting and averaging. If it is not, the procedure is repeated, using the new curve obtained by averaging as a starting guess. This is iterated until (hopefully) convergence.
