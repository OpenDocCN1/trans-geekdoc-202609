
## 8.9 Prior Probability and Discriminant Functions

The estimate for the prior probability `π[k]` can come from several sources, including simply the opinion or educated guess of the investigator. Recall that in Bayesian theory, the prior is essentially a *belief* espoused by the investigator. This isn’t to suggest it is wise to have *irrational* beliefs about the prior, only that ultimately the prior can be set by the preference of the researcher. Most commonly, the prior is derived from prior knowledge or previous research of the problem under study, that is, from substantive knowledge of the particular problem. In the absence of such information, and in reality probably the most common way of estimating the prior in practice, we can obtain an estimate of `π[k]` via

`π̂[k] = n[k]/n`

(8.28)

where `n[k]` is the number of cases in class or group `k` and `n` is the total number of cases. This can be seen as a “default” method or starting point to estimating the prior, but it should be emphasized that such priors can be “tweaked” in line with, again, what the researcher knows about the specific problem under consideration. Recall that by their nature, priors are *subjective probabilities* and hence there are no strict rules about them. Of course, if you advance an irrational prior, then few will take it seriously. The benefit of specifying the prior on an individual/personal basis is that any biases or even *irrationality* in the estimate is open for others to see. This is a strength of Bayesian approaches in general such that *they reveal your degree of belief, even if your degree of belief is irrational*.332

In the discriminant function of (8.27), recall we also required values for `μ[k]` and `σ²`, that is, the mean for each class and the variance of the classes. These can be estimated by

$${\\hat{\\mu}}\_{k} = \\frac{1}{n\_{k}}{\\sum\\limits\_{i}x\_{i}}$$

(8.29)

and

$${\\hat{\\sigma}}^{2} = \\frac{1}{n - K}{\\sum\\limits\_{k = 1}^{K}{\\sum\\limits\_{i}\\left( x\_{i} - {\\hat{\\mu}}\_{k} \\right)^{2}}}$$

(8.30)

where `n[k]` is the number of cases in class `k` and `n - K` is the total sample size minus the total number of classes. We can see that `σ̂²` is essentially a variance estimate across classes, pooling across classes `k = 1` to `K`.

All of the aforementioned principles apply equally to the case of multiple predictors. That is, in the case of more than a single predictor (*as different from multiple response categories*), instead of the univariate Gaussian distribution, we require the *multivariate Gaussian*, which recall is given by

$$f\\left( x \\right) = \\frac{1}{\\left. \\left( 2\\pi \\right)^{p/2} \\middle| \\Sigma \\right|^{1/2}}\\exp\\left( {- \\frac{1}{2}\\left( x - \\mu \\right)^{T}\\Sigma^{- 1}\\left( x - \\mu \\right)} \\right)$$

(8.31)

where the only difference now is that we require a mean vector `μ` and covariance matrix `Σ` instead of only a mean `μ` and variance `σ²` as we required in the univariate case. This is so because now we have several predictor variables, and hence in each classification category there is a mean *vector* instead of simply a mean, and a covariance *matrix* instead of simply a variance (try sketching the multivariate LDA cells on paper).

The classification will assign `x = x[i]` to the class for which the following multivariate discriminant function is the largest:

$$\\delta\_{k}\\left( x \\right) = x^{T}\\Sigma^{- 1}\\mu\_{k} - \\frac{1}{2}\\mu\_{k}^{T}\\Sigma^{- 1}\\mu\_{k} + \\log\\pi\_{k}$$

(8.32)

A comparison of the univariate discriminant function in (8.27) to that of the multivariate in (8.32) will reveal their similarities, especially if we keep in mind that `Σ^(-1)` is serving in place of `σ²`.

## 8.10 Multiclass Discriminant Analysis

In multiclass discriminant analysis where we have more than two categories on the response, we can use a *Mahalanobis distance* metric to best separate classes from each other and provide a linear projection of dimensionality up to `k - 1`, where again `k` is the number of groups or levels on the response variable. Why is the dimensionality limited to `k - 1` dimensions? Why not exactly `k`? The reason for this can be easily understood through the following simple example. Suppose you are asked to guess which hand behind my back holds the marble. You require only a single “discriminator” to make the selection. If not in the left hand, then it must be in the right, or vice versa. When we generalize to more than two groups, there is always one less the number of groups for which a “rule” can be established for a similar reason (*a weighted sum of predictors, the reverse of the MANOVA 333problem*; see Eq. (8.35)). The “discriminator” is a rule that separates classes, and hence having a number of discriminators equal to the number of classes would make little sense. *The purpose of a discriminator is to differentiate between at least two objects. Discriminating between a single object makes no sense*. *Hence the reason why for `k` groups, only `k - 1` discriminant functions are required.*

## 8.11 Discriminant Analysis and Dimensionality in a Simple Scatterplot

The dimensionality aspect of discriminant analysis is perhaps best understood using an example from introductory statistics. Consider the bivariate scatterplot of data in [Figure 8.6](#c8.xhtml#f8.6).

![Figure 8.6 Scatterplot of data where x, the sole predictor and hence “linear combination” composed of only a single variable, discriminates well between males and females.Return to text.⏎](images/fig8.6.jpg)

*Figure 8.6* Scatterplot of data where x, the sole predictor and hence “linear combination” composed of only a single variable, discriminates well between males and females.[Return to text.⏎](#c8.xhtml#re_f8.6)

We can see in the plot that there are two groups: one group is male while the other is female. In this simple plot, we have the *concept* of a discriminating *dimension*; in this case, that dimension is made up of only a single variable. That is, the function, made up of a single variable, typically with an intercept, *separates well*. We see that it separates males from females along a *rule* (weighted sum of predictors) defined by the dimensionality of `x`. The dimension is simple in this case, but it’s still a dimension. To reiterate, *the dimension in this case is made up of only a single variable*. The extension to a more complicated and traditional LDA is that each dimension is made up of more than a single variable; it is composed of a *linear combination* or *composite* of variables.

If you keep this simple example in mind, you will always appreciate discriminant analysis as not only a “classifier,” but also as a means of *reducing the dimensionality* of data, similar in some respects, but by no means *equivalent*, to principal components analysis. The simple scatterplot featured in introductory statistics with two groups appearing in the plot could have been the starting point to discussing discriminant analysis because once we move on to considering `t`-tests and such in elementary statistics, we are in fact engaged at some level in at least the *concept* of discriminant analysis as the two techniques will share technical similarities, especially as we venture into multivariate `t`, which is Hotelling’s `T²` ([Rencher & Christensen, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295)). *That “simple” t-test in “stats 101” was, in the end analysis, a first conceptual “opportunity” and preview of discriminant analysis*.
