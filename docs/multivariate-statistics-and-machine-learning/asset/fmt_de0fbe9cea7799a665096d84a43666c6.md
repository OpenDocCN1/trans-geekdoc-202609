
As we have seen, *linear* discriminant analysis is so popular, on a technical level, in part due to the assumption that covariance matrices are equal and linear separation is a good way to simplify the problem. It is a very easy and facilitating assumption to make about parametric structure. We have seen that by assuming equality of covariance matrices, it lessens the number of parameters that need to be estimated. Hence, even if the assumption is violated and covariance matrices are different to some extent, one convenient way to *regularize* in this setting is to simply and forcefully set them as equivalent, or to *pool* them. As [Friedman (1989, p. 167)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000114) notes:

> Even if the population class covariance matrices are substantially different, the decrease in variance accomplished by using the pooled covariance estimate can sometimes lead to superior performance, especially in small-sample settings. This is a large part of the reason for the success and popularity of linear discriminant analysis.

By *pooled covariance estimate* above is simply meant an average of covariance matrices and hence reducing the number of parameters that need to be effectively estimated. Recall that in general, when you simplify a model, you essentially regularize it as it is usually less prone to overfitting. However, doing so also risks the introduction of potential bias. Indeed, “regularizing” a linear regression equation to simply consist of an intercept is very unlikely to provide a good estimate of a bivariate relationship. In other words, over-regularizing usually *underfits* the data.

For details on how regularization can be done much more thoroughly in discriminant analysis beyond this simple trick, see [Friedman (1989)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000114). Hastie et al. ([2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145), Chapter 18) also discuss discriminant analysis for the case in which the number of cases is greater than the number of variables, the so-called *diagonal LDA classifier*, where within-class covariance matrices are diagonal, thus implying independent predictors. Mixture models in discriminant analysis are also a possibility, 337which combine several densities (typically Gaussian) and can be estimated via the EM algorithm to obtain maximum likelihood estimates.

## 8.16 Naïve Bayes: Approximating the Bayes Classifier by Assuming (Conditional) Independence

Recall that the purpose of discriminant analysis, as is true of many other classifiers, was to approximate the Bayes classifier. More pragmatically, its purpose was to classify populations based on a set of explanatory variables, but ideally, one would usually, pragmatics aside, use the Bayes classifier over the discriminant analysis model since it has the lowest theoretical error rate among all classifiers. That is, discriminant analysis is, at least theoretically, a second choice to the Bayesian ideal. However, we also said that `LDA` and `QDA` are not the only approaches available to approximate Bayes.340
