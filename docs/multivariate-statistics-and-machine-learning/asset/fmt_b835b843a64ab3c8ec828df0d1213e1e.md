
If we have actual distances available, that is, distances that are numerical or computational, this is generally referred to as *metric multidimensional scaling*, also generally known as *classical scaling* or *principal coordinate analysis* ([Rencher & Christensen, 2012](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000295)). If, on the other hand, the distances are arrived at via human judgment or other procedure such that all we can really know is how distances are *ranked* (i.e., we do not actually have the real numerical distances), this is generally referred to as *nonmetric* or *ordinal* multidimensional scaling ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 190). In metric `MDS`, we attempt to dimension reduce based on actual numerical distances. In nonmetric `MDS`, we dimension reduce based on rankings or other more inexact distance derivation. Most commonly, for nonmetric, distances are often converted to ranks somehow, or in some applications, only the ranks exist in the first place without any access to more precise numerical measurements (Murray, 2022).

### 11.10.3 Weakness of MDS: “Closeness” Can Be Arbitrary

There is a limitation to multidimensional scaling, in that just because it considers two points to be “close” in some sense, this comes with the assumption that they are close *on a relatively simple manifold*. If the true manifold happens to be more complex, for instance a *curved manifold*, then the actual distance along the manifold might be much larger. Hence, one can perform `MDS` on data and obtain results, but those results are only as good as *the assumption of the nature of the underlying manifold*. `MDS` also may be limiting as a result of its objective function being nonconvex (Demaine et al., [2021](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000083)). Recall what this implies. Optimization of a nonconvex function can result in there being multiple solutions in the form of minima instead of settling on a global minimum.

One technique that seeks to improve on `MDS` is that of `Isomap`. `Isomap` uses *geodesic* distances instead of Euclidean and thus is able to usually uncover manifolds that are more complex geometrically than what `MDS` is able to capture. In fact, this is true of most of the more advanced manifold learning algorithms. They all share a common goal in that *they seek to uncover a more sophisticated manifold than is possible with simpler techniques such as classical PCA or `MDS`*. This is precisely why these more advanced procedures, as we will soon see, are so attractive.

`MDS` also suffers from not having an explicit projection function ([Yin, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000398)). That is, if one adds even a single data point to the dataset, one must recompute the entire `MDS` algorithm.

> Multidimensional Scaling (`MDS`) typically assumes distances on the manifold are Euclidean, whereas more complex methods of dimension-reduction such as `Isomap` feature the use of non-Euclidean distances such as geodesic distances, which are not necessarily Euclidean. Allowing for greater complexity in how distances are defined is what makes more advanced methods of dimension reduction so attractive and popular.

### 11.10.4 Standardization of Distances

Distances in `MDS` are often standardized so that each variable has a mean of zero and standard deviation of one. However, even with standardization, such does not by itself take into account 435the correlation between objects ([Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057)). To take correlations into account, one could instead compute

`D`² = (`x`[`i`]−`x`[`j`])′`S`^(−1)(`x`[`i`]−`x`[`j`])

(11.17)

which is a standardized version of Euclidean distance, typically called squared *Mahalanobis distances* ([1936](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000235)). Other measures of dissimilarity also exist (see [Chatfield & Collins, 1980](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000057), p. 193). What is more, there is no “best” measure of similarity or dissimilarity. Instead, a researcher should choose one which seems most appropriate for his (or her) particular problem. There is also nothing at all wrong in trying out different distance measures in an exploratory sense to see how they compare in the final solution. Especially with the speed and ease of computing power, this should be easy to do. As usual when it comes to statistics, what you find in your data is often a function of what tools you use to do the searching. `MDS` is no different in this regard.
