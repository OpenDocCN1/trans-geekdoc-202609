
We hold that the purpose of education is to see inside of the “black box” down to its simplest of principles instead of mapping one’s perception of a mathematical tool to some sophisticated and overblown ideas of what that tool actually `is` and what it can do. A few Google searches will convince anyone “in the know” that artificial intelligence and machine learning are continuously marketed as being mysteriously complex. Let the world think what they will. The job of the student and aspiring professional is to learn what these things actually `are` stripped down to their most basic and essential fundamental principles. This is what “expertise” actually is, awareness of what is going on `inside` of the `perceived complexity`, not being part of the populace that has false perceptions, misunderstandings, and misguided glorifications about the tool.

As an example, consider the word `algorithm`. The `idea` of an algorithm is not new. It is `ancient`. `Algorithms have existed forever`. Even the Babylonians were working with algorithms ([Knuth, 1972](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000195)). Yet without understanding just what `is` an algorithm, the newcomer might be fooled by today’s climate into thinking they are “modern” in some sense. One might even use the word algorithm and automatically associate it with cutting-edge technology. True, modern algorithms are new, but the `concept` is old, and being aware of this can help demystify the nature of the algorithm in today’s modern climate. Conceptual understanding knows no label, it often cannot be encapsulated in a single word or phrase or definition. It is more of an `idea`. All otherwise “precise” 15definitions arise from much `fuzzier` concepts. And if we traced concepts such as algorithms all the way back, we would certainly discover that the earliest of peoples were in fact thinking algorithmically. `Modern ways of describing concepts or ideas do not necessarily imply those things or ideas are modern`.

## 0.7 The Power and Dangers of Analogy and Metaphor: Ways of Understanding

Every explanation is a `way of understanding something, no matter how near or distant the explanation may be`. At first this may seem like a radical point of view. Understanding what is a chair can be helped by understanding what a bench is, but the study of the bench is not the “end game.” It too is a way of understanding something. The concept of a “bench” is elusive, the definition is only an `approximation to understanding its nature`. The sooner we realize this, the sooner we can accept analogies and metaphors as vehicles to understanding concepts in statistics and machine learning, while also appreciating that the truest “forms” (to be somewhat Platonian about it) are not in the mathematical equations. Those too are `approximations`.

As an example, suppose a student takes a course in linear algebra to understand the objects of that subject. Suppose they wish to understand what the `rank of a matrix` is all about. The rank of a matrix is the maximum number of linearly independent rows or columns of a matrix. Is that the “end game” to understanding rank? No! It is but an `approximation`. Not only is there a multitude of ways of conceiving rank from different angles in mathematics, the reason `why` rank exists is much deeper. There is a wider `context` to understanding rank, just as there is a wider context to understanding anything else, whether that be in mathematics, statistics, machine learning, or English literature for that matter.

Consider the following analogy that might help in understanding what rank is:

> `Rank is sort of an indication of whether the matrix under consideration contains overlapping “information.”`

Of course, the above is not the full story, but the point is that it `approximates` the full story, at least to some degree. The concept of “information” as a generalization of a column or row vector is a weak generalization of what a column or row vector is, but the analogy helps “encapsulate” the concept and serves a purpose so long as one realizes there is always more to learn about the concept.

The danger, of course, is that all formalism gets lost and all that remains are imperfect, imprecise, and inexact analogies. The purpose of using analogies and metaphors is not to “water down” the material; it is simply to approximate true forms a bit more closely to bring the student into the `neighborhood` of the actual object, where encapsulating the “true” object is somewhat elusive. Defining the arithmetic `mean` as `where most of the data falls` is a terrible definition and just as terrible of an approximation. Settling on that definition in undergraduate statistics would be doing a tremendous disservice to students. Hence, one must be very cautious in using analogies and metaphors too liberally, even with the awareness that all definitions, even the ones closest to the concept, are nonetheless of the analogy/metaphor type, even if at first glance they do not appear so.

Throughout the book we use analogies and metaphors in places while usually emphasizing that they are just that, `approximations to understanding the true concept`. They are “ways” of trying to get `closer` to the object. So long as that is understood, such tools may be helpful and useful in understanding. Indeed, one of the dangers of focusing too heavily on precision and formality is that the concept might be lost altogether. As discussed, concepts are “fuzzy,” they exist within a 16philosophical “cloud” of sorts where the well-defined object can experience a degree of interpretation without losing the essence of what it actually is.

### 0.7.1 The Infinite Regress of Knowledge: A Venture into What It Means to “Understand” Something and Why Epistemology Is Important

Statistics, data analysis, and machine learning are `hard`. Anyone can compute a correlation coefficient or have software do it for them. That is not the difficult part. The difficult part is making sense of empirical observations and applying analytical mathematical approaches in trying to understand something elusive we call `reality`. Many students are amazed at how difficult a statistical problem can become when given just a little thought. Others simply think we’re making the problem difficult on purpose and never learn to appreciate finer distinctions. Rigorous courses in statistical consulting spend a long time assessing and debating over statistical problems that end up having potentially very complex solutions, much different from the typical and overly simplified undergraduate solution obtained by referring back to the course textbook to see if the “right answer” has been achieved. The icy winter driveway pales in comparison to how “slippery” statistics can be when applying them to data. Entire conclusions can be flipped around by looking at the data in a slightly different `way`.

But how does this thing called `understanding` work? It has been my experience that many times “breakthroughs” in understanding, those “ah ha!” moments are about things students have been told before, perhaps many times, but now it has finally been learned “anew” in a much, much deeper way. It is grasped at a much deeper level, and seemingly for the first time. Technical understanding parallels other types of understanding, such as how to navigate through life and the proper mental mindset for peace and success. “`Life is what you make it`” is what we have always been told, yet one day we finally “get it” and realize the truth and depth of that maxim to its fullest. We learn it for the very first time even though we have heard it all our lives.

Understanding does not typically occur `linearly`; it usually occurs in `leaps and bounds`, or through “steps” as in the step-like function of [Figure 0.1](#intro.xhtml#f0.1), where one day the understanding and grasp of the material seems to hit a new unexpected level, seemingly almost overnight.17
