
Decision trees and neural networks are relatively unstable methods, whereas methods such as linear regression and discriminant analysis are much more stable, having low variance and higher possibility of bias. Even with these techniques, however, *committee machines* can be used to combine learners to produce a more stable estimator. The stability is reflected in how well the learner generalizes to new test data, and hence, committee machines or *ensemble* *methods*, as they are sometimes called, can also be interpreted as a form of regularization ([Goodfellow et al., 2016](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000131)). For example, [Perrone and Cooper (1993)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000287) have proposed producing committees of neural networks instead of selecting the single neural network with the best generalization performance. Indeed, it can be shown that the error generated by a committee of networks is less than or equal to the error generated by an average of those networks. Again, this is quite remarkable in and of itself – *a team of mediocre players performs better than a single stronger player; a pack of weaker wolves performs better than a single strong wolf.*

Hence, and perhaps somewhat paradoxically and unexpectedly, the instability of an algorithm can actually be desirable, in a sense, or at minimum not detrimental, and thus an important tool that can be used to exploit and improve the accuracy of that learning algorithm. Both *bagging* and *boosting*, as we will see, actually exploit this relative weakness, the weakness being the instability, in order to create a more accurate learning method. Weak(er) learners can be combined to generate a stronger one. By perturbing the learning set, these methods generate an “ensemble” of different base predictors or classifiers which are then combined into a single combined predictor or classifier. How successful is this combination? The success will depend in part on the degree of instability in the individual base predictors. Bagging has been shown to be beneficial when combining learners that tend to overfit the data and are relatively unstable (Kroese et al., [2020](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000202)).370

On the opposite end, as mentioned, a procedure is said to have low variance if it yields similar results on distinct data sets or even subsets of the same dataset. That is, if you perturbate the data somewhat, it still generally leads to a similar outcome. Recall the bias-variance trade-off that has dominated this book thus far, which we remind here:

> *Good learners have low bias and low variance; however, as one decreases, the other is likely to increase; hence the trade-off.*

Before we consider committee machines in more detail, we need to survey the important topic of *decision trees*. We start with decision trees for two reasons: 1) decision trees are important in their own right and constitute a rather simple way to understand the statistical influence of predictors, and 2) our discussion of bagging and boosting will center around decision trees. As we will see, averaging several decision trees is a prime example of constructing a committee machine, though averaging and combining other procedures also has similar effects.

## 10.2 Decision Trees

Statistical learning methods based on decision trees have gained tremendous popularity due to their simplicity, intuitive representation, and to some extent, predictive accuracy, though often they come in second when compared to many other learning methods. Of all statistical learning methods, decision trees, a member of the tree-based family of algorithms, have been regarded as perhaps the most useful of so-called “off-the-shelf” procedures for data mining. In the context of regression, regression trees were first introduced by [Morgan and Sonquist (1963)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000254) using a computer program called *Automatic Interaction Detection* (AID). For a history of decision trees, consult Ripley ([1996](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000301), Section 7.4). As we will discuss shortly, boosting and bootstrap aggregation (*bagging*) can improve the efficiency of decision trees and other learning methods.

The *root node* in the decision tree is the unique starting point of the tree, and where the tree ends is referred to as the *terminal node*. Along the way down the tree, branches are created that represent splits in the given variable. Of course, not all nodes are terminal. A nonterminal node splits into two daughter nodes, which creates a binary split. If the variable is binary, then defining the nature 371of the split is easy (e.g., 0 vs. 1). If the variable is continuous, however, it is made discrete and binary, such as split at the median, for example. A single-split tree with only two terminal nodes is called a *stump*. The set of all terminal nodes is called the *partition of the data*.

Decision trees have a lot of benefits to them. For one, they can handle very large datasets. They can also handle a mixed bag of predictors, that of *quantitative* and *qualitative*. They can be used both for regression (where targets or response variables are *continuous*) and classification problems (where targets or predictors are *categorical*). They handle missing data quite easily and in general, trees are relatively easy to interpret, especially if the tree is small. Downsides to decision trees include the fact that larger trees can be difficult to interpret and the predictive performance of decision trees is often not as good as competing methods. Further, deep trees (i.e., those without pruning, also known as *saturated trees*) are often too heavily reliant on the training data used to construct them. Small changes in training data can result in a dramatic change in the resulting tree. Because decision trees often exhibit high variance, committee machines such as *bagging*, *boosting*, and *random forests* have come into vogue and are especially useful when applied specifically to decision trees.

### 10.2.1 How Should Trees Be Grown?

We have demonstrated a simple decision tree above, but how do we grow such a tree? How do we go about creating the relevant nodes and branches? At each node, the tree-splitting algorithm needs to decide which variable is “best” in terms of providing a split. That is, at each node, the algorithm needs to select a variable that provides differentiation with regard to the response variable. We must consider every possible split over all variables present at the given node, then enumerate all possible splits, evaluate each one, and decide which is ideal in some sense ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167), p. 285). If the variable at which a split is contemplated is ordinal or continuous, the number of possible splits at a given node is one fewer than the number of its distinctly observed values. If the variable is nominal or categorical, the set of possible splits at that node for that variable is the set of all subsets.

### 10.2.2 Optimization Criteria for Tree-Building

As is true with virtually all statistical methods, we should require a condition to optimize in creating the relevant branches for the trees. Since we cannot consider *every* partition of the predictor space (such would take too long and have too many possibilities), we need to adopt an overall approach to doing so. One approach that is often taken is a *top-down*, *greedy approach*, referred to as *recursive binary splitting* or more generally *recursive partitioning* ([Izenman, 2008](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000167)) which seeks to minimize the following criterion:
