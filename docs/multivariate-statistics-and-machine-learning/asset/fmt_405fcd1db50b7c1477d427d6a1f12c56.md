
Virtually all statistical techniques are organizations and re-organizations of the distance metric in some capacity or another. Even the Pearson correlation coefficient can be conceived as a measure of distance between points (we challenge the reader to think about this).

The most common metric for distance is that of *Euclidean distance* given by

$$d\\left( O,P \\right) = \\sqrt{x\_{1}^{2} + x\_{2}^{2} + \\cdot \\cdot \\cdot + x\_{p}^{2}}$$

(2.111)

where *d* is the distance between origin *O* and *P*, where *x*[1]² through *x*[*p*]² are coordinates of observations. A strength of Euclidean distances is that they are *rotation-invariant*, which means that the straight-line distance between two points does not change with the orientation of the axis system ([Aggarwal, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000005)).

While Euclidean distance is what we typically think of when we consider distance, there are a host of definitions for distance, including *Minkowski*, *city-block (Manhattan)*, and many others. Distance metrics are especially useful in the technique of cluster analysis that seeks to generate *groups* (in some sense) of cases based on their similarity or distance from case to case. In the context of this and other methods, we will survey a number of distance measures throughout the book. Simple distances in Python can be computed using `numpy.linalg.norm(a−b)`.

## 2.52 Projections and Basis
The projection likely already most familiar to the reader will undoubtedly be that of linear regression, where we take the vector *y*, generate a linear function, and project onto the span of *x* resulting in a new line called the *regression line* containing predicted values, *ŷ*. The fit of the regression line to the swarm of points is essentially as a result of projecting *y* onto the space “inhabited” by *x*. The residual is *y*[*i*] − *ŷ*.

When we speak of an *orthogonal projection*, this implies that for a subspace *S*, *y*[*i*] − *ŷ* is orthogonal to *S*. That is, the residual is perpendicular (orthogonal) to the space *S* “occupied” by the predictors (typically called the *column space*). Said differently, parameter estimates for the regression (which then generate *ŷ*) are chosen so that the residual vector *y*[*i*] − *ŷ* is orthogonal to the space “generated” by the predictors in the model ([Hastie et al., 2009](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000145)).

The orthogonality, coupled with the Pythagorean theorem, further implies that we can decompose the squared norms of these objects in the following manner,148

||*y*[*i*]|| ²\= ||*ŷ*||[2]+||*y*[*i*]−*ŷ*||²

(2.112)

or more generally for arbitrary observed and fitted vectors where *v̂* is the more general fitted value of the vector and *ŷ* above only a special case:

||*v*|| ²\= ||*v̂*||[2]+||*v*−*v̂*||²

(2.113)

The above identities are important, as they essentially describe what occurs in regression and ANOVA; that is, the total sums of squares can be broken down into that due to treatment and that due to error ([Marden, 2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000239), p. 89). The “treatment variation” is represented by ||*v̂*||² while the error is given by ||*v*−*v̂*||².

In *principal components analysis*, the goal will be to obtain a subspace onto which we also project observed data onto that subspace. The projected data are *principal component scores* and the new coordinates from which that data are projected are the new *basis vectors*. The original basis, or *canonical basis* as it is sometimes called, are the original coordinates at a right angle. For example, in ℝ³, the standard basis vectors are the following:

$$e\_{1} = \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} 1 \\\\ 0 \\\\ 0 \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack,e\_{2} = \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} 0 \\\\ 1 \\\\ 0 \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack,e\_{3} = \\left\\lbrack \\begin{array}{l} 0 \\\\ 0 \\\\ 1 \\\\ \\end{array} \\right\\rbrack$$

The axes of the standard basis or any other basis need not be at right angles ([Hubbard & Hubbard, 2002](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000161)). That is, there is no *absolute* requirement that the above vectors *e*[1], *e*[2], *e*[3] be at right angles, *ontologically*. Historically, and for most problems, the decision to have bases at right angles is very much due to *convenience*. Descartes’ invention of the Cartesian coordinate system in the 17th century allowed algebra to be expressed geometrically where each axis was set perpendicular or orthogonal to one another. Pierre de Fermat (1607–1665) discovered the same coordinate system although his work was published only after his death, and hence Descartes is typically associated with the traditional coordinate system we know today. A mathematician by the name of John Wallis (1616–1703) was one of the first to conceive of the possibility of negative coordinates ([Tanton, 2005](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000352)). Not starting off with pairwise orthogonality of basis vectors would complicate problems tremendously and hence like many other things in mathematics, it is convenient to start with the assumption or constraint of orthogonality of canonical vectors in many cases. It is a convenient and natural starting point to many problems, and indeed, to much of mathematics in general.

The new basis vectors in the case of principal components are *eigenvectors* and possess favorable properties from which the projection is made. Hence, PCA is often regarded at a technical level as a *change of basis* methodology from which we project new scores. It is also generally regarded as a *dimension-reduction technique*, but this dimension reduction “happens” through a change of basis.

## 2.53 The Meaning of Linearity
The word *linear* is perhaps the most common word used in all statistics and machine learning. But what does linear mean, exactly? A linear transformation *T*, as already discussed, is a mapping from ℝ^(*n*) to ℝ^(*m*) such that the following holds for scalars *a* and vectors *x* and *y*:149

*T*(*x*+*y*) = *T*(*x*) + *T*(*y*)

(2.114)

and

*T*(*a**x*) = *a**T*(*x*)

(2.115)

That is, linear transformations are said to *preserve* vector addition and scalar multiplication. Linear transformations, when viewed from a geometrical point of view, are an example of an *affine transformation*. Affine transformations and associated geometry also preserve such things as ratios of distances between points. For example, transforming degrees Celsius into degrees Fahrenheit is an example of an affine transformation because the relative change between say, 10 degrees and 20 degrees Celsius is preserved when we transform to Fahrenheit:

^∘*C* = ((^∘*F*)−32)(5/9)

If one draws the corresponding plot, one will see that the relationship between degrees Celcius and Fahrenheit is indeed linear and the ratios of distances are preserved.

Linear transformations in R and Python are very simple to execute. For example, in Python, suppose we want to determine the aforementioned temperature in Celsius corresponding to a temperature of 32.0 Fahrenheit:

```
F = 32.0
(F-32)*(5/9)
Out[16]: 0.0
```

or, for a temperature of F = 0, we have

```
F = 0
(F-32)*(5/9)
Out[18]: -17.77777777777778
```
