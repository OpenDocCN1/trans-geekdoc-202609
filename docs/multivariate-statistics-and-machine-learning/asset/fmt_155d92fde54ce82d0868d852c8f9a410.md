
Where results are very important, we provide the requisite technical references to more formal proofs and justification. For example, understanding what the *central limit theorem* is and why it works is most important in an applied sense, but if one were adamant on learning “why” via formal proof and had the ambition to work through it, select references are provided that further justify the theorem. Understanding why and how a theorem is “proved” and not the details of the proof may be enough, even for the curious reader. Proofs can be complex, but the *idea* of proof is (generally) not.

What is a proof, exactly? A quote by Abbott ([2015](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000001), pp. 8–9) on famed mathematician Hardy is helpful here:

> As Hardy indicates, there is an artistic quality to mathematics of this type, which may or may not come easily, but that is not to say that anything especially mysterious is happening. A proof is an essay of sorts. It is a set of carefully crafted directions, which, when followed, should leave the reader absolutely convinced of the truth of the proposition in question. To achieve this, the steps in a proof must follow logically from previous steps or be justified by some other agreed-upon set of facts. In addition to being valid, these steps must also fit coherently together to form a cogent argument. Mathematics has a specialized vocabulary, to be sure, but that does not exempt a good proof from being written in grammatically correct English.

Hence, a proof is a way of justifying that a mathematical statement is correct; it is an argument of sorts, just as arguments abound in other areas of inquiry (e.g., science, history, politics, etc.). Mathematical proofs, however, are much more rigorous because they are on objects that we can operationalize as specific quantities and variables.

## 2.2 Elementary Probability and Fundamental Statistics

Probability theory is the quantification of uncertainty and is at the foundation of most statistical and machine learning ideas. As R.A. Fisher ([1925](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000100), [1935](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000101)), the “father of statistics” put it, we may not be able to make *certain* predictions, but we can often rigorously quantify the degree to which we are *uncertain*. This is a powerful idea, one that essentially revolutionized how scientific thought and conclusions are “packaged.” We may not know whether it will rain tomorrow, but the extent to which we can quantify our uncertainty regarding whether or not it will rain nonetheless provides us with knowledge and gives us a “handle” on the problem. We have become relatively *certain* about the *uncertainty*. That’s a big idea. Historically, the invention or discovery of probability was somewhat of a paradigm shift, not only in science, but in everyday communication.

Ideas and concepts of probability go back at least to the ancient Greeks and probably much before then, as most important ideas do. However, probability was only mathematically formalized into a system relatively recently beginning in the 1600s. Most mathematical (and other) inventions have histories where the key concepts germinate over time. Few innovators truly work alone and few important ideas are unmotivated by other equally important ideas. The formalized interpretation of probability, considered in proper context, was more of a *culmination* of ideas that germinated for centuries.

## 2.3 Interpretations of Probability

There are generally three schools of probability: *classical*, *frequentist*, and *subjective*. Classical views are, for the most part, not really part of mainstream probability. The probability of getting a “1” on a roll of a die is 1/6 since there are six sides to the die and all *appear* to have equal probability. That’s an example of the classical view. Historically, frequentist and subjective notions have dominated statistics and hence also more or less dominate machine learning.

For the *frequentist*, probability is a long-run frequency; that is, if you want to know the probability of something, you need to *experience* the event over repeated trials and take the fraction of times the event occurs to the total number of trials. The typical classic and easy example is that of flipping a coin. If we flip the coin 100 times and get 50 heads, we say the probability of heads is equal to 50/100 = 0.5, even though theoretically this is only an *approximation* to the true probability. The 0.5 is not the true probability. For the true probability under this approach, we would require flipping the coin an *infinite* number of times. From this perspective, true probabilities are only arrived at *in the limit* as sample size grows infinitely large, that is,

$$\\lim\\limits\_{N\\rightarrow\\infty}\\frac{n\_{H}}{N} = p\\left( H \\right)$$

(2.1)

Unfortunately, “infinitely large” could never be a reality, and hence one weakness of the relative-frequency approach is that obtaining such a large number of trials is nothing more than a fanciful idea, even if it does serve as a useful working model for defining probabilities. Many scholars have critiqued the relative-frequency interpretation of probability precisely on this basis.64

The *subjectivist*, also commonly known as the *Bayesian* (since Bayesians typically adopt a subjective viewpoint of probability), takes probability to be an *opinion*, a *degree of belief* and hence does not require the unrealistic and unattainable infinite number of trials idea that is mandatory for the frequentist to accept. As we will see later however when we survey Bayesian statistics, subjective probability has its problems as well.

The key point to take away from this discussion so far is that the nature of probability has historically, and is still currently, open to debate. You are understanding this point correctly; *nobody knows what a probability actually is!* All that can be offered is an *interpretation* of probability. Which interpretation is correct is not a debate we will have here, and in fact, if you studied and wrote a dissertation on the topic, you’d most likely end up in the same place you started. That is, probability is a relatively elusive concept, and the best we can do is simply adopt a *way* of thinking about it. The frequentist and subjectivist notions of probability dominate modern science.
