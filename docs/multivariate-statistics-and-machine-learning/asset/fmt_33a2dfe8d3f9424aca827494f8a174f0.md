
| `dv3` || \-2.2012117 || 0.93192121 |
| `dv4` || \-2.8104603 || \-2.83918785 |

A full understanding of what a discriminant function is will have to wait until we survey discriminant analysis later in the book. However, for now, it is enough to know that we have obtained two discriminant functions as a result of there being three groups in our data (i.e., type of species). `LD1` above is nothing more than a linear combination (called *linear discriminant* or more generally *linear discriminant function* after weighting by variables) that is weighted with values of sepal length, sepal width, petal length and petal width (i.e., `dv1` through `dv4`). The linear combination generated by `LD1` is thus:218

```
LD1 = 0.82(sepal length) + 1.53(sepal width) + (-2.20)(petal length) + (-2.81)(petal width).
```

In the machine-learning community, `LDA` is hardly ever conceived as the opposite of `MANOVA` but instead simply as a “classifier” of group membership and as a similar and potential alternative to logistic regression. However, even more generally, both `LDA` and logistic can be interpreted as special cases of a wider model, that of the *generalized linear model*, which we discuss later in the book. Under this umbrella, `LDA` is an application of the generalized linear model in which the response is (typically) binary, yet the model is still *linear*. That is, in this context, the *link function* is simply the *identity function*. This serves to contrast the `LDA` model with that of the logistic in which the link function is the *log of the odds*. For readers coming to this discussion with some background, this likely makes perfect sense. Without such background however, what we have just said will be entirely confusing and the reader will have to wait until further in the book when we survey discriminant analysis and the generalized linear model in more detail. She may wish to return to this section after that discussion.

We “unpack” the `LDA` model later in the book where we treat it in much more detail as a classifier. For now, however, we simply wish to note that the unifying aspects of `LDA` and `MANOVA` cannot be overstated. Where `MANOVA` seeks to find mean vector differences, the `LDA` model seeks to use those linear combinations to distinguish between groups. Extensions and special cases of these models are numerous, and pedagogically, understanding discriminant analysis gives rise to a variety of related models. Indeed, it is not an exaggeration to say that we could, if desired, title an entire book called *Linear Discriminant Analysis and Variations* because of the “hub” nature of `LDA`.

# 5 Simple Linear and Multiple Regression Models (and Extensions)

DOI: [10.4324/9781003376941-8](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003376941-8)

Linear regression has historically been the workhorse of statistics and is still so today ([Stigler, 1986](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000348)). What is more, many of the common techniques that are in use today are, in one way or another, *extensions* or *derivatives* of the basic linear regression model. Entire books and treatises are written on linear regression as well as entire courses that study the topic in outstanding depth. This is for good reason; *regression is literally at the center of the universe of statistical modeling and that of machine learning algorithms*. Excellent textbook-length sources on regression analysis alone include [Fox (2016)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000106) and [Draper and Smith (1998)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000090).

The history of regression ideas and concepts dates back to antiquity ([Stigler, 1986](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000348)). More recently, the origin of least-squares generally is said to lay with [Legendre (1805)](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000215) and later in the same century with [Galton (1888),](#ref.xhtml#re_CIT000121) who applied regression to problems of heredity. The essential idea of regression is that of making predictions on one variable based on another (or more) while minimizing some function of the error in making those predictions. Least-squares regression is all about exploiting the correlation between two or more variables with the goal of reducing error in prediction in the one based on knowledge of the other(s). Regression, the *concept*, is the essence of what statistical modeling is all about.

## 5.1 Simple Linear Regression – Fixed Predictor Case

The simplest linear regression model features a single predictor whose values are regarded as fixed in advance. This classic simple linear regression model is given by

`y[i] = β[0] + β[1]x[i] + ε[i]`

(5.1)

where `y[i]` is the response variable, `β[0]` is the intercept of the regression line, `β[1]` is the slope, `x[i]` is the explanatory or independent variable, and `ε[i]` is the error term, that part of `y[i]` that is unaccounted for by the systematic part of the model and where `E(ε[i]) = 0`. The intercept is sometimes referred to as the *offset* or *bias* term. This terminology is common in neural networks discussed later in the book.

The systematic part of the model is `β[0] + β[1]x[i]`, and thus the expectation of `y[i]`, that is, `E(y[i]) = β[0] + β[1]x[i]` is equal to `β[0] + β[1]x[i]`. The model has expectation `E(y[i]) = β[0]` when `x[i] = 0` since220

$$
\begin{array}{l}
{E\left( y_{i} \right) = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i}} \\
{\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}\left( 0 \right)} \\
{\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, = \beta_{0}} \\
\end{array}
$$

We note that the model is linear in the parameters `β[0]` and `β[1]`. This is the meaning of *linear* in linear regression. Note that this definition of linearity does not require the model to be linear in the input variable `x[i]`. Hence, the following model is still a linear model even though the input enters as a squared term:

`E(y[i]) = β[0] + β[1]x[i]²`
