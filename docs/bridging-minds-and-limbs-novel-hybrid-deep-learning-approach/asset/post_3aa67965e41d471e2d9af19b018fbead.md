# Bridging minds and limbs: novel hybrid deep learning approach for low-cost EEG-based lower limb imagery classification

# ABSTRACT

This study aims to develop a highly cost effective brain-computer interface (BCI) system specifically for detecting lower limb imagery during stroke rehabilitation. Existing BCI systems often suffer from high costs and are easily affected by environmental interference. To tackle these problems, our system is built on a modified OpenBCI platform with wireless communication, featuring signal processing algorithms and design. We collected electroencephalography (EEG) data from healthy subjects using 16 channels. We developed a novel hybrid deep learning method to perform feature extraction and classification, which combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Bidirectional Long-Term Memory (LSTM), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and attention mechanisms. The system achieved an accuracy of 72.4% (±3.6%) on the test dataset, with the CNN-LSTM model showing 4.1% improvement over baseline methods. These results were comparable to existing literature, demonstrating good potential despite the small sample size. Future work will focus on expanding the sample size and long-term clinical validation to address current limitations and further improve the system’s efficacy in real-world applications.

# 1. Introduction

Brain-computer interface (BCI) technology has revolutionized human-computer interaction by enabling direct control of external devices through activity. This is particularly useful in rehabilitation where BCI can modulate motor activity—specifically by inhibitory control of descending pathways—which helps in promoting motor recovery [2–4]. The integration of brain with motor systems to improve neuroplasticity is an important area of focus in both robotics and neurosciences. BCI systems can capture activity through non-invasive methods such as electroencephalography (EEG), offering high signal-to-noise ratios but with significant risks and errors. Non-invasive techniques like functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), near infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), magnetoencephalography (MEG), and electroencephalography (EEG) provide safer alternatives but generally suffer from lower signal quality due to the distance between the electrodes and the brain activity source [6]. Despite this

EEG is widely preferred for BCI applications because of its portability, affordability, and high reliability. Motor imagery (MI) is a widely used paradigm in EEG-based BCI research, particularly detecting motor movements through evoked related potentials (ERP) in the 8–12 Hz band (mu rhythms) and event-related desynchronization and synchronization (ERS) further assist in decoding motor intentions. However, EEG signals are often affected by artifacts, such as electrocardiography (ECG) and electromyography (EMG), making activity analysis and feature extraction challenging for BCI performance [10–13]. Various methods, such as independent component analysis (ICA) and wavelet transform, are commonly employed to handle complex artifacts. Similarity feature extraction techniques, like power spectral density (PSD) and common spatial patterns, have performed well with different motor signals [14–16]. Recent advances in deep learning have shown promise for improving EEG signal processing. Convolutional neural networks (CNN) and long short-term memory networks (LSTM) have demonstrated strong performance in feature extraction and classification

# 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Hardware and software

### 2.1.1. OpenBCI

OpenBCI is an open-source, cost-effective, and programmable platform for monitoring cardiac, muscle and electroencephalography (EEG). The OpenBCI board, powered by 3-6 DC battery, facilitates EEG acquisition. This chip utilizes the integrated ADS1299, which supports eight-channel EEG acquisition with a sampling rate of 244Hz. The digital-to-analog converter (ADC) also supports flexible sampling rates from 250 samples per second (ps) to 1000 ps and has a common mode rejection (CMR) exceeding 120 dB. Furthermore, the chip contains an internal referenced voltage and an on-board oscillator. OpenBCI board establishes a connection to a PC (11th Gen Intel(R) Core i7-11800H @2.3 GHz, 16 GB RAM, Windows 10) via Wi-Fi. To account for physiological variations in head, we used a stereoscopic mesh cap in place of 3D-printed helmet fitted with the OpenBCI system. The cap accommodates 20 slots according to the arrangement of the head system, allowing for the integration of gold electrodes. We used conductive paste to the electrodes to enhance signal quality during EEG acquisition.

### 2.1.2. Software

The OpenBCI team provides a software system called the OpenBCI Graphical Interface (GUI), which facilitates data acquisition and transfer from various OpenBCI boards. However, the GUI cannot perform real-time analysis, necessitating the use of third-party software for analysis. During experiments to ensure safety and seamless execution, we developed a custom software using Python and the Flow library for data streaming, processing, and visualization of mental data. The process begins with a communication interface to configure the board parameters. Once configured, the stream is commanded to initiate data acquisition. Simultaneously, the GUI tailored to show the motor imagery tasks to the subject. Each trial is shown on the GUI, which then automatically closes, signaling the end of the acquisition. Data and corresponding labels are then saved and imported into MATLAB for analysis.

## 2.2. Experimental design

Ten healthy-handed participants (8 males and 2 females, age = 29 ± 3.7 years) with no experience using Brain Computer Interface (BCI) systems participated in this study.

We conducted the experiments in an office environment with stable lighting. The experiment was divided into five sessions, with 20 trials each. Each session lasted 45 minutes. Participants sat in chairs close to a computer screen. In each trial, as shown in Fig. 2, an arrow appeared on the screen to indicate the direction, either left or right arrow, displayed for four seconds. During this time, the participants were instructed to imagine the movement (e.g., lifting the left leg when an arrow appeared) while remaining still.

To ensure an equal number of left and right limb imagery trials, we used a structured arrangement strategy. A randomly selected left or right arrow was presented, followed by a four-second rest. The trial within the block was followed by another rest period. This alternating sequence was repeated throughout the session, ensuring that both left and right were used an equal number of times to prevent predictable patterns.

The experiment consisted of three sessions, each session composed of 50 trials (25 blocks for each imagery task), and between sessions were given a 5-10 minute break to minimize fatigue before starting the next session.
