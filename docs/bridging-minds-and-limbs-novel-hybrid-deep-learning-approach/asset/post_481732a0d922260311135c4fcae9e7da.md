## 2.4. Data expansion

EEG acquisition is a time-consuming task, from initial preparation to data collection. Prolonged monitoring task may also lead to fatigue. In this study’s EEG data, experiment generates 150 sets of training data. Deep learning techniques typically require large datasets and are limited in scale due to the high cost and difficulty of recruiting a sufficient number of subjects for EEG-based studies. As a result, the available data in this study is relatively small.

To address the restricted size inherent in EEG datasets, we applied an overlapping window segmentation approach that preserves temporal dependencies. Each trial lasts for 4 s, at a sampling rate of 125 Hz, yields 501 samples (the extra is obtained by including the starting point). To augment the dataset, a sliding window of 2 s with a sample size of 50 samples. Consequently, each 4-second trial is segmented into 6 overlapping segments, as calculated by: $N = \frac{501}{50} + 1 = 6$. Despite this, each segment retains the same label as the original, and the timeline-graph of data expansion is shown in Fig. 4. The resulting fold resulted in increase in training samples (dataset-size amplification) without introducing artificial features or altering spectral characteristics, complying with the data integrity principles.

## 2.5. Mathematical method

### 2.5.1. Convolutional neural networks

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for EEG signal analysis due to their ability to capture and learn resemble patterns in artificial neural networks and are deep learning models that widely used for visual image analysis [32]. The multi-channel, continuous, and spatial nature of EEG makes CNNs particularly suitable for extracting relevant features from this data type. A typical CNN consists of five layers: (1) input, (2) convolutional, (3) activation, (4) pooling and (5) fully connected. A complete CNN structure contains multiple blocks of these layers, with a classifier at the end:

$f(X) = \sigma(W_i \otimes X + b_i)$

Here, $X$ represents the $i$-th convolution layer, $W_i$ denotes the convolution kernel (weights), and $b_i$ is the bias. The operator $\otimes$ denotes the convolution operation, computes the map by sliding the kernel over the input and performing element-wise multiplications and summations. For each convolutional layer:

- The input $X$ is convolved with the kernel $W_i$.
- The bias $b_i$ is added to the convolution.
- The result is then passed through an activation function (e.g., ReLU) and a pooling layer to reduce dimensionality and maintain important features.

Unlike LSTM models, which only consider the past, bidirectional structure of LSTM enables to model both past and future information. This is particularly beneficial for medical imaging (MI) tasks, such as EEG non-stationary signals that span multiple scales. The bidirectional structure improves the network's ability to capture long-range temporal correlations and phase shifts, leading to more accurate feature representation.

Fig. 5 illustrates the LSTM architecture and internal mechanism. Here, the LSTM is shown in "unrolled" format across multiple time steps (t-1, t, t+1), both forward and backward. Each block represents an LSTM unit, detailing the forget gate ($f_t$), output gate ($o_t$), cell gate ($c_t$), and hidden state ($h_t$). The multiplication and addition operations, as shown in equations (4)-(7), use learned parameters and weights to integrate the input to generate the new cell state and hidden state. The outputs of the activated blocks are aggregated to form the final representation $f(X)$, which is subsequently fed into fully connected layers and a classifier for final classification. The entire network is trained end-to-end using backpropagation to adjust the weights and biases.

### 2.5.2. Long-Term Memory

Long-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are crucial for EEG processing due to their ability to capture long-term dependencies in time series. This is particularly important for temporal analysis of behavioral patterns during monitoring tasks. LSTMs use three gates: the forget gate, input gate, and output gate, to control the information flow, allowing the model's ability to adapt to time-dependent changes [3]. The forget gate controls which information from the previous step is retained or discarded, preserving relevant temporal information:

$f_t = \sigma(W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, X_t] + b_f)$

Where, $f_t$ is the forget gate activation at time $t$, $X_t$ is the input at $t$, $h_{t-1}$ is the hidden state from the previous step, $W_f$ is the weight matrix associated with the forget gate, $b_f$ is the bias, and $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid activation function, which maps values to the range [0,1].

The input gate controls what new information is updated in the long-term and short-term memory:

$i_t = \sigma(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, X_t] + b_i)$, $\tilde{C}_t = \tanh(W_c \cdot [h_{t-1}, X_t] + b_c)$

Where, $i_t$ is the input gate activation, $\tilde{C}_t$ is the candidate cell state, $W_i$ are the weights for the input gate and cell state respectively, $b_i$ and $b_c$ are the corresponding bias, and $\tanh$ is the hyperbolic tangent function.

The cell state $C_t$ is updated by combining the information from the previous cell state and the new cell state:

$C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t$ (5)

where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, and $C_{t-1}$ is the previous cell state.

Finally, the output gate controls the current hidden state $h_t$ by passing the updated cell state through a non-linear transformation:

### 2.5.3. Particle Algorithm

CNN networks effectively extract spatial features from EEG, but the LSTM kernels affect their performance. The Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm can optimize the number of parameters by competition and cooperation among particles [5].
