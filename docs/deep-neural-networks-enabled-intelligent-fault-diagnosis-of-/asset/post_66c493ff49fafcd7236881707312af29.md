## Intra-Temporal Relation Mining

Vibration signals are essentially a type of time series. Their temporal relations can also reveal different fault modes. Therefore, exploring the intra-temporal relation of unlabeled data is effective to extract more discriminative representations. By motivated sentence prediction (NSP), which is a pre-trained self-supervised task in BERT (Devlin et al. 2018; Fan et al. 2021), a temporal relation prediction is adopted to relate the relationship of different segments in a sample as supervision. Given given samples $[x_i | x_i \in R^{1 \times L}]$, we split each sample into two parts $x_i^{past} \in R^{T \times L}$ and $x_i^{future} \in R^{(L-T) \times L}$, where the former T points refers to its past condition and the latter L points denote its future condition, as shown in Figure 9.3(b). Based on prior knowledge that the future of a signal owns similar sequential characteristics to its past, we randomly select a past segment as the anchor $x_i^{anc}$. Accordingly, its future counterpart $x_i^{fos}$ is chosen as its positive pair, while another counterpart $x^{neg}$ from a different sample is chosen as its negative pair. Later, the anchor section $x_i^{anc}$, positive section $x_i^{pos}$ and negative section $x_i^{neg}$ are transmitted to the backbone network, and their hidden representations $z_i^{anc}=E_{\theta}(x_i^{anc}), z_i^{pos}, z_i^{neg}$ are extracted. Unlike instance relation mining, a temporal relation head $H_{\mu}(\cdot)$ takes positive and negative representations as input, and outputs a probability value $p_i$ indicating whether the sample pairs are positive or negative. Moreover, pseudo labels $\hat{y}_i$, denoted on the temporal relation of input samples, as illustrated in:

$$\hat{y}_i = \begin{cases} 1 & p_i^{tem}=H_{\mu}(z_i^{anc},z_i^{pos}) \\ 0 & p_i^{tem}=H_{\mu}(z_i^{anc},z_i^{neg}) \end{cases} \quad (9.5)$$

where $\mu$ denotes the parameter of the temporal relation head. Similar to instance relation loss, we choose BCE as the temporal relation loss $L_t$, which is formulated as:

$L_t = -\frac{2}{BM(M-1)} \sum_{j=1}^{BM} \hat{y}_j \cdot \log(p_j^{tem}) + (1-\hat{y}_j)(1-\log(p_j^{tem})) \quad (9.6)$

where $M$ refers to the number of samples added of data augmentation, and $B$ is the batch size.

## Uncertainty-Based Dynamic Weighting

As mentioned above, two self-supervised losses are designed to explore robust features from unlabeled samples and improve the discriminability of the classifier as auxiliary tasks. It belongs to a challenging multi-task learning problem on how to balance each task and prevent a single task from dominating the optimization process. A naive method is to take the weighted sum of each loss as the total loss. However, manual weight selection is inefficient and furthermore tasks' difficulty varies at different stages. Constant weighting approach limits learning process at a certain stage. To address these problems, we adopted an uncertainty-based dynamic weighting mechanism (Kendall, Gal, and Cipolla 2018) to avoid model performance degradation. Generally, calculation process of the overall network can be expressed as:

$v=f^{w}(x) \quad (9.7)$

$p(y|x)=\text{Softmax}(v) \quad (9.8)$

where $W$ refers to the model, $v$ is the output vector by transmitting the samples $x$ into the model. Later, the vector is normalized through a Softmax function to the resulting probability vector $p(y|x)$. Most classifiers apply Softmax function directly, but sometimes scaling the original vector $v$ with random variable $\sigma$ before Softmax would accelerate the convergence of losses, formulated as

$p(y|x,\sigma) = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}f^w(x)\right) \quad (9.9)$

where the variance $\sigma^2$ is a parameter in Gibbs distribution, which determines the flatness of the final distribution. The fluctuation of $\sigma$ influences the learning difficulty of each loss and reflects the task-specific uncertainty. Following the principled (Kendall, Gal, and Cipolla 2018), we introduce three learnable standard deviations $\sigma_{sup}$, $\sigma_{ins}$ and $\sigma_{tem}$ to control the uncertainty of supervised classification, inter-relation prediction task and intra-temporal relation task, respectively. Additionally, Loss of each $L(W)$ can be inferred as:

$L(W) = -\log p(y|x,\sigma) = -\frac{1}{\sigma^2}f^w(x) + \log\sum_{c'}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}f^w(x)\right) = -\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\log p(y|x) + \log\sigma \quad (9.10)$

where we assume that $(\sum_{c'}\exp(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}f^w(x)))^{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{c'}\exp(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}f^w(x))$ when $\sigma \to 1$, $f_c^w(x)$ denotes the $c$'th element of $f^w(x)$. According to mean theory, the total conditional probability is equal to the product of the conditional probability of each task:

$p(y_{sup}, y_{ins}, y_{tem}|f^w(x),\sigma_{sup},\sigma_{ins},\sigma_{tem}) = \prod_{i=sup,ins,tem} p(y_i|f^w(x),\sigma_i) \quad (9.11)$

Based on (9.10) and (9.11), the dynamic weighted loss is obtained:

$L(W) = \frac{1}{\sigma_{sup}^2}L_{sup}(W) + \frac{1}{\sigma_{ins}^2}L_{ins}(W) + \frac{1}{\sigma_{tem}^2}L_{tem}(W) + \log\sigma_{sup}\sigma_{ins}\sigma_{tem} \quad (9.12)$

From the overall weighted loss, it can be concluded that a large standard deviation $\sigma$ decreases the weight of the sub-tasks whereas a small value increases the model's attention to the sub-task. The weight of each task is optimized automatically at each epoch to prevent single task domination, and the last three terms in the equation serve as regularizations to prevent the standard deviation from being too large.

## EXPERIMENTAL STUDIES (FOR AEROENGINE BEAR GEAR FAULT DIAGNOSIS)

In this section, we test three datasets to verify the performance of SSL-based intelligent fault diagnosis framework over other comparative methods.

## Datasets

### MFPT Bearing Dataset

MFPT bearing datasets were provided by Society for Machinery Failure Technology (MFPT) (Dataset 2020), which included vibration signals of the health bearing and two fault bearings. The sampling frequency of the health bearing and fault bearing were 97656 Hz and 48828 Hz, respectively. The fault bearings were both operating under 7 different loads. Therefore, there exist 15 fault conditions including one health state and 14 fault states. In the following experiment, we select a 2560 samples with a length of 1024, including 572 healthy samples and 143 fault samples per state.

### XJTU-SY Bearing Dataset

XJTU-SY bearing datasets were provided by Xi'an Jiaotong University and Changxing Sumyoung Company (Wang et al. 2018), which included 15 bearing run-to-failure data under 3 working conditions. Every minute, 32768 points were sampled at a frequency of 2.56 kHz. In the following experiment, we collect 1920 samples including 1024 data points, in which each health condition has 128 samples.
