
## 2 Podiums

The design and construction of tall buildings stand as a testament to human ingenuity and engineering process, pushing the boundaries of architectural possibilities. Among the critical elements contributing to these buildings' structural and aesthetic success, podiums emerge as integral components that often determine the overall functionality and visual impact. Podiums, the lower segments of tall buildings, bridge the structure and the urban context. Beyond their role in providing structural support, podiums contribute significantly to the building's functionality, accessibility, and visual identity. The design of podiums necessitates a holistic approach, considering factors ranging from urban planning and integration to material selection and structural engineering, sustainability, and the overall user experience.

This chapter delves into the intricate world of podium design for tall buildings, exploring the fundamental considerations, challenges, and innovative approaches that architects and engineers undertake to harmonize these structures with their surroundings. It navigates these complexities, shedding light on the artistic talents employed to craft podiums that seamlessly integrate into the urban fabric while accommodating the diverse needs of occupants. Through an exploration of case studies, design philosophies, and emerging trends, this chapter aims to comprehensively understand the complexities of designing podiums for tall buildings. From integrating green spaces and sustainable features to carefully considering aesthetics and cultural context, the following sections will unravel the multifaceted nature of podium design, offering insights into the evolving realm of vertical urban architecture.

### THE PROBLEM

Podiums in tall buildings have garnered criticism for adversely affecting human scale, needs, and aesthetics. They often prioritize vehicular access and convenience at the expense of pedestrian interaction, leading to a disconnect between the building and the surrounding urban environment. They also lack human-scale design elements, such as architectural details, aesthetic appeal, or active uses, that could enhance the street-level experience and foster engagement with the pedestrian realm. Podiums often have a monolithic and sterile appearance, creating a visually dull and cold atmosphere that detracts from the urban fabric and streetscape. Moreover, they may disrupt the continuity of the urban fabric and fail to blend in with the surrounding context (Gehl, 2010; Whyte, 1980).

### Westin Bonaventure Hotel, Los Angeles [1976]

The Westin Bonaventure Hotel in Los Angeles, CA, represents a controversial emblem of modern architecture. Central to the debate is the hotel's extensive podium, which dominates a significant portion of the ground level and has been criticized for its failure to harmonize with human scale, fulfill basic needs, and provide aesthetic charm. This substantial concrete podium not only obstructs views of the street but also contributes to a feeling of isolation and detachment from the surrounding urban environment. Lacking inviting or engaging elements conducive to pedestrian activity or interaction (Figure 2.1), the podium's design is seen as emblematic of the hotel's perceived disregard for the public realm, reflecting a preference for exclusive and private spaces.

The architectural complex was conceived by John Portman & Associates (Goldberger, 2011).

### Renaissance Center, Detroit [1977]

The Renaissance Center (often referred to as the RenCen) in Detroit, Michigan, is an impressive complex of seven interconnected skyscrapers that dominate the city's skyline. However, the design has received criticism for its insensitivity to human scale and needs, as well as its perceived lack of visual attractiveness. The podium, or the base of the complex, is a massive concrete structure that blocks the view and access to the riverfront, creates a hostile and unwelcoming environment for pedestrians, and isolates the complex from the rest of the city. The podium also lacks any architectural interest or variation, resulting in a monotonous and dull appearance that contrasts with the historic buildings nearby. The Renaissance Center's podium exemplifies how a building can fail to respect its context and users (Figure 2.2). John Portman & Associates designed the complex (Al-Kodmany & Ali, 2013).

### PNC Center, Indianapolis [1977]

The PNC Center in Indianapolis hosts several vital establishments, including the Hyatt Regency Indianapolis, Eagle's Nest, and the Indiana Chamber of Commerce. However, the podium's design has faced criticism for its unattractive and incongruent appearance. Dominating most of the block, the substantial structure features few windows or openings, failing to align with the elegance and sophistication typically associated with the Hyatt Regency brand. It also lacks distinctive features or elements that could make it more exciting or attractive. The podium also blocks the view of the historic St. John the Evangelist Catholic Church, a landmark and symbol of the city's religious heritage. The design lacks refinement and creativity, detracting from the cityscape's aesthetic appeal (Figure 2.3). Browning Day Pollak Associates designed the building (Al-Kodmany & Ali, 2013).

### One Detroit Center, Detroit [1993]

One Detroit Center, a 43-floor skyscraper and class-A office building in Downtown Detroit, is the tallest office building in the city and the second tallest in the State of Michigan. However, the parking garages around One Detroit Center are disappointing in the heart of the downtown. They are huge and bulky structures that occupy a large area of land and obstruct the view of the surroundings. The exterior of the garages is bland and unappealing, with no architectural features or aesthetic elements that would enhance the look of the cityscape. The garages starkly contrast with the elegant One Detroit Center (Figure 2.4). John Burgee and Philip Johnson designed the building (Al-Kodmany & Ali, 2013).

### One World Trade Center, New York City [2014]

The 541-meter (1,776-foot) One World Trade Center, the tallest building in the Western Hemisphere, is a remarkable architectural achievement that symbolizes the resilience and strength of New York City. Completed in 2014, it replaced the former Twin Towers post-9/11 attacks. However, its design has been criticized for being bland and uninspiring, especially the podium that supports the tower. The podium is a massive concrete structure that covers the base of the building and houses the lobby, security, and mechanical systems. It is clad in glass panels that are supposed to reflect the sky and the surroundings but instead create a dull and monotonous appearance. The podium lacks any distinctive features or details that could make it more appealing and inviting. It contrasts sharply with the slender and elegant tower above it, creating a sense of disproportion and imbalance. The podium of One World Trade Center is not attractive because it fails to convey the beauty and dynamism of the city that it represents (Figure 2.5). David Child of SOM designed the building (Al-Kodmany, 2018; Dupré, 2016).
