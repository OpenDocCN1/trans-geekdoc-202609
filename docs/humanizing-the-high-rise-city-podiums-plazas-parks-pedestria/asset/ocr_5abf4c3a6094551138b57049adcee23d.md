+   - The larger footprint of city-wide parks allows them to provide more excellent environmental advantages by significantly mitigating the urban heat island effect, improving air quality, and providing habitat for biodiversity. These parks act as green lungs within the city, absorbing carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen, contributing to a healthier urban environment. City-wide parks contribute to a city's resilience in the face of climate change. Green spaces help absorb rainwater, reduce flooding, and contribute to temperature regulation, making the city more adaptable to environmental challenges. Regular parks also offer similar environmental benefits on a smaller scale, providing pockets of greener, shade, and habitat for local flora and fauna.

+   - Because of their massive scale, city-wide parks provide a horizontal counterpoint to the verticality of high-rise structures, contributing to a more balanced and visually appealing urban landscape. This dynamic interplay contributes to achieving a harmonious and visually captivating urban landscape. The vast green spaces spread across the city provide a necessary contrast to the dominance of tall buildings, offering residents and visitors a respite from the urban verticality. This deliberate juxtaposition enhances the overall aesthetics of the cityscape, fostering a sense of balance and diversity in the architectural and natural elements.

City-wide public parks play a crucial role in humanizing high-rise cities. They provide essential green spaces for residents, foster community engagement, contribute to the city's ecological sustainability, and enhance the overall quality of life in urban environments dominated by tall structures. Integrating extensive public parks is a crucial element in creating a well-rounded, livable, and resilient high-rise cityscape (Al-Kodmany, 2018; Parakh et al., 2017).

The following case studies illustrate the roles of city-wide parks in humanizing the high-rise city.

# CENTRAL PARK, NEW YORK CITY

Central Park, located in Manhattan, New York City, is the first public landscaped park in the United States. In 1853, state authorities purchased land from 59th to 106th Streets, between Fifth and Eighth Avenues, formerly used for modest agricultural plots and communities due to its rugged and marshy soil.
