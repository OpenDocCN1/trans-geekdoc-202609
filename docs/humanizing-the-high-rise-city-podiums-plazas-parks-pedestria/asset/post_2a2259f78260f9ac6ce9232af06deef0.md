- **Water Features:** Citygarden includes captivating water features such as fountains, pools, and cascades. These elements add a sensory dimension to the park, providing visual interest and a calming auditory backdrop.

- **Paved Pathways and Walkways:** The park is designed with well-constructed pathways that guide visitors through the outdoor gallery. These pathways create a sense of flow and make the sculptures easily accessible, encouraging exploration and discovery.

- **Seating Areas:** Throughout the park, there are strategically placed seating areas where visitors can rest, relax, and enjoy the surroundings. These seating areas may be integrated into the overall design, offering spots for contemplation and conversation.

- **Educational and Cultural Programming:** Citygarden serves as a cultural hub, hosting events, art installations, and educational programs. This component enhances the park's role as a dynamic and evolving cultural space within the city.

## Sculptures

Citygarden Sculpture Park in St. Louis, Missouri, is home to various contemporary sculptures created by various artists. The park's commitment to presenting engaging and thought-provoking artworks contributes to its reputation as a dynamic urban space. While specific sculptures may change due to temporary exhibitions or installations, here are some sculpture examples (Citygarden; RAC):

- **Eros Bendato by Igor Mitoraj:** Eros Bendato (Eros Bound) encapsulates the aura of an ancient artifact, meticulously unearthed and recreated by the artist Igor Mitoraj. Drawing inspiration from ancient cultures, particularly the characters found in Greek and Roman mythology, Mitoraj crafted a sculpture portraying the decapitated head of Eros, the Greek deity synonymous with love and desire, positioned horizontally. The bandages encasing Eros' face carry symbolic significance, representing the confinement of desires and ideas, effectively concealing the eyes and lips. Additionally, these bandages serve as a symbolic dichotomy, reflecting contrasting perspectives on the world’s condition: one suggesting irreparable damage to civilization, and the other indicating its preservation despite negative influences.

- **Big Suit by Jim Dine:** Wurm’s artwork, “Big Suit,” entails the enlargement of a pink business suit to a significantly greater size (equivalent to a size 144 in the US), deliberately omitting the presence of a body within. Known for sculptures that often revolve around a solitary figure or physique, Wurm directs attention solely to the garment. His artistic exploration involves delving into the performative dimension of sculpture, examining the precise moment when an action evolves into a sculptural form. Infused with a playful cynicism, Wurm’s sculptures, although intended to be amusing, subtly address potentially serious societal concerns.

- **Door of Return by Kan Yasuda:** The egg-shaped structure crowning the Door of Return’s apex mirrors a recently ruptured seed or a mouth reaching skyward, reminiscent of an infant’s initial vocalization. Yasuda deliberately fashioned the form to be enigmatic, encouraging observers to ponder concepts such as time, space, life, death, and the intricate interplay between the past and the present.

- **Zenit by Mimmo Paladino:** Mimmo Paladino derives inspiration from diverse cultures’ art and artifacts, melding imagery from mathematics, physics, and art with ancient signs and symbols to create dreamlike and enigmatic artworks. One notable creation is his bronze sculpture of a graceful horse without a rider, named Zenit or Zenth in English. Perched on the horse’s back, a stellated dodecahedron—a star-shaped geometric object with twelve faces—appears delicately balanced on one of its points.

- **Bruce and Sarah Walking by Julian Opie:** Julian Opie employs computer software to extract intricate elements from a film capturing two individuals walking. He generates illustrations of the figures at regular intervals, blending them to form fluid, natural human representations. These graphics are then transformed into a format compatible with LED screens, producing a seamless animation. Notably, the figures’ heads take on simple circular shapes, symbolically representing Kiera, Julian, Bruce, and Sarah rather than specific individuals.

- **Big White Gloves, Big Four Wheels by Jim Dine:** Jim Dine’s captivating with the narrative of Pinocchio, the wooden puppet, is evident in his artistic endeavors. Dine breathes life into Pinocchio through his drawings, sculptures, and paintings, akin to the fictional character’s creator, Geppetto. Notably, the sculpture “Big White Gloves, Big Four Wheels” portrays Pinocchio atop a bronze cart mounted on four wheels. Dine’s artistic exploration emphasizes the enchantment of an inanimate object coming to life, diverging from the traditional focus on the consequences of falsehood. While Dine’s creations may exude a lighthearted appearance, they harbor layers of meaning that invite viewers to delve into a realm of wonder and contemplation.

- **Two Rabbits by Tom Claassen:** Claassen’s sculptures challenge conventional perceptions through a delicate equilibrium of lightness and weight. Employing traditional bronze but enveloping it in white paint, he achieves an unconventional sense of lightness. Unlike traditional monuments, Claassen's rabbit sculptures are grounded, offering a departure from typical public sculpture perspectives. Through the use of rabbits, Claassen introduces a "down-to-earth" quality to his art, adding a touch of whimsy and relatability to his distinctive approach.

- **La Riviere by Aristide Maillol:** Maillol's later works primarily center around the depiction of the female nude. In contrast to the usual themes of calmness, tranquility, and emotional restraint found in sculptures like La Riviere, Maillol explores a departure from these principles. Through a deliberate placement of the figure near the ground and extending the head and arms beyond the pedestal, he introduces a sense of instability and motion. The figure, with flexed legs suggestive of movement, captures the essence of water, deviating from Maillol's more serene portrayals to evoke a dynamic and fluid energy.

- **Scarecrow by Donald Baechler:** The genesis of Scarecrow was inspired by the artist's agricultural estate in upstate New York. Baechler envisioned placing a sculpture suspended above the rolling hills and barns, drawing parallels to the Christ the Redeemer sculpture atop Mount Corcovado in Rio de Janeiro. Depicting the scarecrow as a solitary figure in a vast field, devoid of life and engagement with its surroundings, Baechler captures an essence akin to an inanimate observer. The inaugural sculpture of the Scarecrows series is on display at Citygarden. Additionally, the artist crafted a duplicate, now positioned on his East Coast farm.

- **Bird by Laura Ford:** Ford crafts whimsical and disconcerting hybrid organisms by blending human and animal features, a process influenced by her keen observation of her children and reflections on her own childhood experiences. Growing up exposed to sideshows and fairgrounds, these early encounters left a profound and lasting imprint on her worldview. This exposure has equipped Ford with a unique ability to seamlessly navigate between the tangible and intangible, the eerie and delightful, and the humorous and malicious realms in her art.

Citygarden Sculpture Park is a vibrant testament to the rich tapestry of artistic expression, evident in its diverse collection of sculptures. Each artwork encapsulates a unique amalgamation of styles, themes, and artistic approaches, fostering a captivating visual dialogue within the park's landscape. The sculptures reflect a broad spectrum of creative visions, from Igor Mitoraj's archaically inspired Eros Bendato to the whimsically enlarged Big Suit by Jim Dine. The park's commitment to diversity lies in the individuality of each sculpture and the harmonious interplay of these artistic elements. Visitors encounter a dynamic fusion of contemporary aesthetics, cultural influences, and varied conceptual foundations, creating an immersive and thought-provoking experience. Citygarden's embrace of diverse artistic voices enhances its role as a dynamic cultural space where the evolving language of sculpture continually unfolds, engaging the public in a captivating exploration of artistic pluralism (Pactor, 2018).
