## JOHN HANCOCK CENTER PLAZA, CHICAGO

The John Hancock Center Plaza is a public space at the base of the 100-story, 1,128-foot supertall skyscraper in the Magnificent Mile district of Chicago. The plaza features a sunken garden, a fountain, benches, and temporary sculptures. It is a popular spot for tourists and locals to enjoy the views of the iconic building, designed by Skidmore, Owings, and Merrill and completed in 1969. The iconic structure, officially rebranded as 875 North Michigan Avenue in 2018, still resonates with its original designation, paying homage to the esteemed U.S. Founding Father, John Hancock. Initially christened in honor of the insurance giant, the John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Co., the building’s nomenclature intricately ties back to the visionary founder of this significant insurance entity. The building houses offices, restaurants, a hotel, condominiums, and an observation deck with a 360-degree view of the city and Lake Michigan. The observation deck also features TILT, a moving platform that leans visitors over the edge of the skyscraper to a 30-degree angle, and a full bar with local selections (Al-Kodmany, 2017; Kamin, 2022; Saliga & Clarke, 1998).

The plaza is situated at the tower’s western edge. Originally designed as a rectangular sunken plaza containing trees and a public ice-skating rink, the Center’s owner planned to cover the plaza with a gabled glass atrium in 1988. However, the proposal was rejected by Mayor Richard M. Daley and the residents. In 1994, the plaza was successfully refashioned into a semi-circular sub-level space, creating good public access through multiple staircases extending to the street level (Figure 3.15). The renovation included the replacement of the previous white marble with gray/black granite that more closely resembles that of the building (Binder, 2006; Zukowsky et al., 2004).

The plaza provides sufficient sitting areas at the sub-ground level with plenty of recreational options. A permanent seating area built into the semi-circular granite structure is interspersed with planted sections and stairs. The plaza’s middle point is slightly raised and contains a fountain and waterfall. It serves as a place for movable chairs, tables, and stages for small, programmed events. The plaza offers good access to shopping and restaurants, with the south end of the plaza being used as the Cheesecake Factory’s outdoor seating area. One can enjoy a miniature “urban oasis” here in the summer and experience what has become known as the neighborhood’s unofficial Christmas tree in the winter. Overall, the sunken plaza’s relatively small size and narrow footprint bring it into scale with the richly pedestrian setting of the area, balancing the large towers that dominate this section of downtown Chicago and the nearly constant stream of shoppers, tourists, and businesspeople that pass by. Furthermore, because the street and the John Hancock Center above are primarily unseen, one does not feel overpowered by either (Saliga & Clarke, 1998).

## HIGHLIGHTS

### Positive Aspects

- **Central Location:** The plaza is strategically positioned within Chicago's bustling Streeterveille neighborhood, offering convenient access for residents and visitors.
- **Urban Retreat:** Amid the surrounding high-rise buildings and urban environment, the John Hancock Center Plaza serves as a retreat where people can relax and unwind.
- **Landscaping:** The plaza combines greenery, trees, and shrubs, providing a natural ambiance within the concrete jungle.
- **Seating Areas:** Movable chairs allow visitors to find comfortable spots to rest, socialize, and enjoy the surroundings.
- **Water Feature:** A prominent water feature, often called "The Water Wall," adds a sense of serenity and visual interest to the plaza.
- **Views:** The plaza offers views of the John Hancock Center's distinctive architecture and cityscape.
- **Urban Experience:** The plaza encapsulates the urban experience, allowing people to gather, enjoy outdoor spaces, and immerse themselves in the city's energy.

### Areas for *Improvement*

- **Limited Green Space:** The plaza's landscaping and greenery might be relatively limited, especially considering the surrounding urban environment, which could impact its ability to provide a true natural oasis.
- **Scale and Proportion:** Given the towering height of the John Hancock Center, the plaza's size and layout might feel disproportionate, potentially leading to a lack of coherence in the overall space.
- **Functionality:** The plaza's design might not fully cater to the diverse needs of visitors, potentially limiting its usability for different activities and user groups.
- **Weather Considerations:** The plaza might lack sufficient weather protection, making it less enjoyable during inclement weather or extreme temperatures.
- **Program Variety:** While the plaza hosts events, more diverse programming could be introduced to cater to a broader range of interests and demographics.
- **Community Engagement:** There could be opportunities to engage the local community more actively in the plaza's planning, programming, and stewardship.

## 311 SOUTH WACKER DRIVE, CHICAGO

311 South Wacker Drive Plaza is a privately owned public space (POPS) located in the heart of downtown Chicago, Illinois. It is surrounded by significant skyscrapers, including the 108-floor Willis Tower (formerly Sears Tower), the 60-floor South Tower of the Franklin Center, also known as the AT&T Corporate Center, and the 311 South Wacker Drive building. The neighboring Willis Tower is a well-known tourist destination with over 1.5 million visitors yearly. Consequently, the plaza is ideally situated for foot traffic and serves as an urban oasis in the middle of a busy area. The presence of masses of people in the area improves the chances of attracting visitors and the usability of the plaza. Further, its corner location makes this plaza particularly recognizable (Kamin, 2022; Saliga & Clarke, 1998).

The plaza benefits from convenient access to public transportation, with nearby CTA stations and bus stops providing easy connectivity to other parts of the city. It is also situated next to a tour bus stop for the Willis Tower. The plaza is an accessible and inclusive public space that welcomes all visitors, regardless of their physical abilities. Its flat surface and lack of barriers make navigating and enjoying the space accessible for everyone. Its surface matches the height of the surrounding sidewalks, making walking or rolling on it easy. Therefore, it does not require any special equipment like ramps or elevators for visitors with disabilities, who can enjoy the plaza without any barriers (Al-Kodmany & Ali, 2013).

The plaza offers a respite from the hustle and bustle of the surroundings. Measuring almost an acre (4,047 square meters), 311 S. Wacker Plaza is the most enormous grassy lawn in the Loop. It contrasts with a "Modernist" plaza that features large, paved surfaces, as with the previously discussed Richard J. Daley Plaza. The plaza has a garden theme that is highlighted by several features. One of them is a flower fountain at the center, which has a radius of 3.6 meters (12 feet) and a variety of colorful flowers that change with the seasons. The fountain is encircled by seats and connected to the lawn and the building by different paths. The plaza also has seasonal decorations, such as corn stalks on lampposts in autumn. This area is very popular among visitors, who enjoy using it for various activities, such as chatting, eating, phoning, or relaxing. Next to the flower fountain is a brick path (differentiated from the concrete on the other paths) leading to a standing planter about four-and-a-half-feet tall. The planter has metal rings surrounding its bowl of flowers and contains a kaleidoscope, an excellent piece of interactive public art. To the south of the flower fountain is a wide path leading to an area of benches arranged across each other but staggered so as not to face one other directly (Al-Kodmany, 2017).

The "lawn plaza" contains a variety of sitting spaces, including benches, open grass, and planter edges. One of the most attractive seating options is the Adirondack chairs, which visitors use to lounge, converse, and enjoy the sunshine during lunch. With a sloped seat close to the ground, wide armrests, and a straight back, the Adirondack chair reinforces the perception that this is a garden-like space. Reading, napping, and resting are the most common activities. People place books, snacks, and cellphones on the chairs' wide arms, almost as personal tables. The chairs are unlocked at 8 a.m. every Monday through Friday and put away at 4 p.m.

Given the restaurants inside, the patio is intended as a space for people to eat, relax, and enjoy the incredible views of the plaza and surrounding buildings. Furthermore, the plaza integrates mature trees, particularly along its perimeters, which shield the lawn from the sun, excessive noise, movement, and the general chaos of the street, giving the area a sense of calm. Overall, the plaza offers a variety of seating options, including indoor dining, outdoor seating on the terrace, and a patio with a great view of the plaza and surrounding buildings. Therefore, the plaza is a great place to relax and enjoy the scenery while being protected from the hustle and bustle of the city (Figure 3.16).

The plaza is pristine because of the rigorous regulations forbidding smoking, dogs, rollerblading, skateboarding, alcohol, and loitering. These regulations are upheld by a sophisticated surveillance system and diligent security personnel who constantly watch over the plaza. The rules are displayed on signs at the entrances and on light posts along the pathways. In addition, the plaza is equipped with a substantial number of garbage receptacles, serving the purpose of upholding the cleanliness of the area and fostering an environment that encourages individuals to engage in activities such as sitting and relaxing on the ground. However, this maintenance comes at the cost of being continuously observed and controlled. Therefore, while the plaza is well-kept and tidy, visitors must comply with strict rules and face incessant surveillance and security (Al-Kodmany, 2017; Saliga & Clarke, 1998).
