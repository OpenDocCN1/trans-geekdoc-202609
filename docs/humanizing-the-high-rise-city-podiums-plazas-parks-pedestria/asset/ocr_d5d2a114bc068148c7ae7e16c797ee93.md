# ■ Waterfront Parks

# DIVERSITY OF ACTIVITIES

The design of the path introduces creative land-water typologies, seen in its sections, such as Marina Plaza, Cove, River Theater, Water Plaza, Jetty, and Riverbank. The design and spatial layout vary, so each room has a distinct identity, while each space offers different functionalities and experiences. Further, the McCormick Bridgehouse & Chicago River Museum and the Vietnam Veterans Memorial serve educational and cultural purposes. Overall, along the path, people engage in various social activities such as walking, chatting, relaxing, jogging, running, eating, dining, drinking, photographing, sightseeing, and fishing. Also, some visitors perform yoga and work out. Other people could be seen biking (or walking the bikes), scooting, hoverboarding, or Segwaying. Visitors can enjoy boating, kayaking, canoeing, cruising, and joining architectural and recreational tours on the river. One can also observe various recreational boat types, such as yachts, power boats, and personal boats, including rowboats, canoes, kayaks, and paddleboards (Cotton, 2018).

# SOCIOCULTURAL BENEFITS

Notably, accessing the river’s social, cultural, and educational activities and gathering places is free. Consequently, the Riverwalk attracts many people, including residents, local and global visitors, business owners, professionals, and public officials. Accessible public spaces promote inclusivity, and the mixed crowd that the Riverwalk attracts reflects Chicago’s cultural values of equality and diversity. Among the most beautiful feelings visitors experience is sharing space and experiences with people from all walks of life. Their combined presence creates a vibrant downtown and engenders solid social bonds and an enduring sense of belonging (Al-Kodmany, 2021; Elliott, 2010).

# WALKING BENEFITS

The Riverwalk encourages people to engage in a healthy habit: walking! Walkable cities are increasingly desirable (See Chapter 13). Overall, emphasis on walking prevails for numerous reasons, such as health benefits, social interaction, environmental well-being, and energy savings, to name a few. Dan Burden, an expert on walkability, makes a good point,

> Walkability is a word that did not exist just 20 years ago. We made walking so unnatural that we had to invent a word to describe what we were missing. . . . Essentially, walkability is allowing people to do what the human body was designed to do in the first place: to go places without having to get into some mechanical instrument.
(Burden, 2014)
