
## LED Lighting

The Chicago Riverwalk is a lively attraction during the day and becomes a captivating area at night, partially due to its well-incorporated LED lighting systems. These lighting installations serve a dual purpose: enhancing safety for nighttime visitors and contributing to the aesthetic charm of the riverfront. The LED lighting strategically illuminates vital elements of the Riverwalk, delineating the edges of different spaces, including seating areas, gathering spots, and the diagonal ramp of the River Theater. This ensures a safe, well-defined environment and creates a visually appealing nocturnal atmosphere.

In addition to their functional roles, the lighting systems play a crucial role in highlighting the architectural and natural features of the Riverwalk. The interplay of light and shadow adds a layer of sophistication to the landscape, inviting visitors to explore and enjoy the space even after sunset. Notably, the integrated lighting systems are designed to withstand occasional flooding, a practical consideration given the riverfront location. This resilience ensures that the Riverwalk remains safe and visually striking, irrespective of the water levels (Al-Kodmany, 2021; Movahedzadeh et al., 2015).

Beyond lighting, the planting and paving systems along the Riverwalk are also engineered for resilience, capable of enduring expected inundation. This holistic approach to design considers the daytime experience. It anticipates the changing conditions of the riverfront environment, ensuring that the Chicago Riverwalk remains a dynamic and inviting destination around the clock. The Riverwalk's LED lighting systems contribute to practical functionality and safety, creating an immersive and visually captivating nighttime experience along the iconic Chicago River.

## The Chicago Riverwalk: Sustainability Perspective

### Social

#### Connectivity and Accessibility

The Riverwalk connects Downtown Chicago’s essential areas, attractions, and facilities. Individuals utilize the pathway as a means of accessing and traversing between prominent locations such as the Magnificent Mile, Millennium Park, Maggie Daley Park, Navy Pier, office spaces, and commercial establishments (Refer to Chapter 13). The pathway is accessible throughout the entire year and is ADA compliant. Consequently, it is easily reachable for individuals with restricted physical movement. The pathway facilitates inclusive connectivity between the streets and the river by including ramps and enhancing elevators. This is especially valuable considering that the walkway was inaccessible to anyone with impairments. Significantly, the route spans 1.25 miles (2 kilometers) and is easily traversable on foot due to its relatively small length. For example, this distance is readily walkable compared to the linear park stretching 18.5 miles (30 kilometers) along Lake Michigan. Similarly, the Chicago Riverwalk is substantially shorter than the 15-mile (24-kilometer) distance of the San Antonio River Walk, a popular tourist attraction in San Antonio (Al-Kodmany, 2023; Coton, 2018).

#### Waterfront Parks

#### Diversity of Activities

The design of the path introduces creative land-water typologies, seen in its sections, such as Marina Plaza, Cove, River Theater, Water Plaza, Jetty, and Riverbank. The design and spatial layout vary, so each room has a distinct identity, while each space offers different functionalities and experiences. Further, the McCormick Bridgehouse & Chicago River Museum and the Vietnam Veterans Memorial serve educational and cultural purposes. Overall, along the path, people engage in various social activities such as walking, chatting, relaxing, jogging, running, eating, dining, drinking, photographing, sightseeing, and fishing. Also, some visitors perform yoga and work out. Other people could be seen biking (or walking the bikes), scooting, hoverboarding, or Segwaying. Visitors can enjoy boating, kayaking, canoeing, cruising, and joining architectural and recreational tours on the river. One can also observe various recreational boat types, such as yachts, power boats, and personal boats, including rowboats, canoes, kayaks, and paddleboards (Cotton, 2018).

#### Sociocultural Benefits

Notably, accessing the river’s social, cultural, and educational activities and gathering places is free. Consequently, the Riverwalk attracts many people, including residents, local and global visitors, business owners, professionals, and public officials. Accessible public spaces promote inclusivity, and the mixed crowd that the Riverwalk attracts reflects Chicago’s cultural values of equality and diversity. Among the most beautiful feelings visitors experience is sharing space and experiences with people from all walks of life. Their combined presence creates a vibrant downtown and engenders solid social bonds and an enduring sense of belonging (Al-Kodmany, 2021; Elliott, 2010).

#### Walking Benefits

The Riverwalk encourages people to engage in a healthy habit: walking! Walkable cities are increasingly desirable (See Chapter 13). Overall, emphasis on walking prevails for numerous reasons, such as health benefits, social interaction, environmental well-being, and energy savings, to name a few. Dan Burden, an expert on walkability, makes a good point:

> Walkability is a word that did not exist just 20 years ago. We made walking so unnatural that we had to invent a word to describe what we were missing. . . . Essentially, walkability is allowing people to do what the human body was designed to do in the first place: to go places without having to get into some mechanical instrument.
(Burden, 2014)

Walking is the simplest form of exercise; people are pedestrians by design. In his book *Walkable City: How Downtown Can Save America One Step at a Time*, urbanist Jeff Speck (2013, p. 38) poetically explains the importance of walking by stating: “As a fish needs to swim, a bird to fly, a deer to run, we need to walk, not to survive, but to be happy.” A daily walking routine of 20 minutes can prevent heart disease, diabetes, depression, and some cancers (Elliott, 2010).

### Economic

The Riverwalk project has converted the area into a vibrant entertainment hub, drawing in vendors, businesses, and visitors. Since its opening, the number of vendors has doubled, and their businesses have significantly improved. The increased number of visitors has increased parking revenue, and collected fees from boat docking and vendors have also increased. This has supported existing jobs and created new ones, with the project resulting in 66 permanent, 170 seasonal, and 125 part-time jobs from 2016 to 2019. Additionally, the project has increased property value and the City’s property tax base, enhanced Wacker Drive’s marketability, and attracted top employees and companies (Al-Kodmany, 2023; Cotton, 2018).

The project has catalyzed a substantial influx of economic development, resulting in a noteworthy increase in hotel bookings, retail sales, contributions to local businesses, and income tax revenues. The project has collected over $16 million in just six years to repay the $99-million federal loan granted for the second and third phases (Cotton, 2018). The project’s costs are relatively reasonable, estimated at hundreds of millions of dollars, compared to surrounding skyscrapers such as Trump Tower, River Point, and 150 North Riverside.

### Environmental

The Riverwalk project aims to improve the environmental well-being of the Chicago River by promoting the recovery and restoration of on-land and in-stream habitats. By reintroducing native trees, plants, and grasses, water quality has been enhanced, attracting local fish, birds, turtles, and muskrats. This process encourages aquatic land animals to help remove toxins from the river through their natural habits. Several bird species have been invited, including mallards, ring-billed, house sparrows, herring gulls, rock pigeons, American robins, European starlings, and peregrine falcons (Movahedzadeh et al., 2015).

The Riverwalk project also includes floating eco-gardens, or floating wetland islands, which reintroduce plant species native to Illinois wetlands and prairies. These plants have intimate, symbiotic interactions with the river water, increasing their function of cleansing water and nourishing plants. The floating eco-gardens also reduce nitrogen and phosphorus levels in the river, attracting fish and land-water animals like turtles (Al-Kodmany, 2020; Movahedzadeh et al., 2015).

The Riverwalk project has contributed to sensible ecological improvement in the river, with the Floristic Quality Index (FQI) increasing from 0 to 38.2 in an area of 19,529 square feet (1,814 square meters). The floating gardens also serve as an outdoor classroom for learning about fish, fauna, and critters, encouraging people to consider the importance of their natural and ecological functions.
