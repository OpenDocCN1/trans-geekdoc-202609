## KEY TAKEAWAYS: PLANNING AND DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Planning and designing city-wide public parks in dense, high-rise cities requires careful consideration of various factors to ensure their effectiveness and suitability for the urban context. Here are some planning and design guidelines to help create successful city-wide public parks:

1. **Adequate Size and Accessibility:**
    - City-wide public parks should be large enough to accommodate diverse activities and visitors, easily accessible via public transportation, and feature well-connected pedestrian and cycling paths seamlessly linking to surrounding neighborhoods.

2. **Multifunctional Design:**
    - Design parks with various spaces and amenities catering to different user needs.
    - Include areas for relaxation, active recreation, social gatherings, cultural events, and children’s play.
    - Provide sports fields, playgrounds, picnic areas, amphitheaters, and green spaces for diverse activities.

3. **Integration with Surroundings:**
    - Seamlessly integrate the park with the surrounding urban fabric.
    - Incorporate elements that reflect the local culture, history, and natural environment to enhance the sense of place and connection to the city.
    - Consider nearby buildings’ architectural style, materials, and design elements to create a cohesive visual identity.

4. **Green Infrastructure and Sustainability:**
    - Incorporate green infrastructure features into the park design, such as rain gardens, bioswales, and permeable surfaces, to manage stormwater runoff and improve water quality.
    - Use native plantings that require less maintenance and support local biodiversity.
    - Integrate sustainable design principles, such as energy-efficient lighting, recycling stations, and water-efficient irrigation systems.

5. **Universal Accessibility:**
    - Ensure the park is accessible to people of all ages, abilities, and mobilities.
    - Incorporate barrier-free pathways, ramps, and accessible facilities, such as restrooms, seating, and playground equipment.
    - Consider the needs of individuals with disabilities and make provisions for inclusive play and recreational opportunities.

6. **Active and Passive Surveillance:**
    - Design the park layout to ensure visibility and safety.
    - Avoid secluded areas or blind spots that could compromise public safety.
    - Incorporate well-placed lighting, clear sightlines, and strategically placed seating and activity areas to promote security and comfort.

7. **Public Art and Cultural Expression:**
    - The park’s aesthetic appeal, cultural enrichment, and identity are enhanced by incorporating public art installations, sculptures, murals, or performance spaces that reflect local culture and artistic expression.

8. **Community Engagement:**
    - Involve city officials, communities, and stakeholders in the planning and design process.
    - Seek input and feedback to understand their needs, aspirations, and concerns.
    - Create opportunities for community involvement in the park’s programming, maintenance, and activation to foster a sense of ownership and pride.

9. **Safety and Maintenance:**
    - Incorporate safety features such as well-maintained paths, adequate lighting, and clear signage to ensure a safe environment for park users.
    - Develop a comprehensive maintenance plan to ensure the park’s cleanliness, functionality, and ongoing care.
    - Involve relevant agencies or community organizations in the maintenance and management of the park.

10. **Connectivity and Integration:**
    - Consider the park’s connectivity to the larger urban and regional context.
    - Examine the park’s connections to the natural environment, considering its impact on ecological systems, water flow, terrain, and biodiversity.

By following these planning and design guidelines, city-wide public parks in dense, high-rise cities can be thoughtfully designed to meet the needs of residents, enhance the urban environment, and provide valuable green spaces for recreation, social interaction, and cultural enrichment. ## 7 Urban-Room Parks

Originating from the visionary Chicago 1909 Plan, the term “urban room” encapsulates a revolutionary urban design concept introduced by Daniel Burnham and Edward H. Bennett. Rooted in the spatial organization of human habitats, this groundbreaking idea extends to the city scale. Inspired by multiple spaces in a typical home with specialized uses for different activities, urban rooms reimagine outdoor environments, providing specialized communal functions for city dwellers. These open-air spaces break away from traditional urban planning, offering well-defined areas that cater to various sociocultural needs. The concept of urban rooms transcends the conventional boundaries of city planning, creating distinct and purposeful spaces that echo the intimacy and functionality found within the rooms of a home. Burnham and Bennett's innovative approach continues to influence modern urban design, emphasizing the importance of creating vibrant, user-centric outdoor spaces within the bustling cityscape.

At its core, the urban room concept revolves around creating meticulously defined spaces within the urban fabric. These spaces are crafted to evoke a sense of visual enclosure, warmth, and comfort—reminiscent of the intimate atmospheres within a home's various rooms. The concept seeks to transform the expansive urban terrain into smaller, distinctive areas, each with unique character and purpose. This intentional segmentation of the urban landscape counterbalances the overwhelming scale of towering buildings. The result is a network of intimate spaces catering to diverse activities, contributing to city residents’ heightened sense of place and identity. As individuals navigate these purposefully designed urban rooms, they engage in a rich tapestry of experiences that transcend the mere physicality of the space, fostering a deeper connection to the urban environment (Hill, 2021; Van Hausen, 2013).

A fundamental tenet of the urban room concept is prioritizing walkability within the city, mirroring the effortless movement experienced within a room. This intentional design choice represents a departure from the large parks prevalent in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which demanded extensive walks and often lacked functional diversity. In stark contrast, urban rooms are meticulously crafted to be walkable, promoting ease of movement and engagement. These spaces integrate specialized civic functions and boast distinctive perceptual characteristics, inviting residents to explore and interact seamlessly.
