
After the railway ceased operation, the elevated structure faced an uncertain future. There were discussions about demolishing the structure. However, residents and community activists recognized its potential as a unique public space and advocated for its preservation and repurposing. In 1999, Friends of the High Line advocated converting the abandoned railway into a public park. They envisioned transforming the elevated structure into a green space providing recreational opportunities, promoting urban renewal, and preserving the neighborhood’s historic character (Park, 2019).

Through years of advocacy, fundraising, and collaboration with city officials, the first section of the High Line was opened to the public in 2009. The design of the High Line is a joint effort involving James Corner Field Operations as the project lead, together with Diller Scofidio + Renfro and Piet Oudolf. They designed the park to retain the industrial aesthetic of the railway while incorporating natural elements, landscaping, seating areas, and unique design features. The success of the initial section led to the development of subsequent phases, with the final section of the High Line completed in 2023. Overall, the project involved a multidisciplinary team that combined the expertise of architects, landscape architects, urban designers, and horticulturists. The design team worked closely with the City of New York and community stakeholders to create a unique and transformative elevated park experience (Littke et al., 2016).

## KEY DATES

The development of the High Line, an innovative urban park in New York City, unfolded through key dates that marked its transformation from a disused rail track into a celebrated green space, reshaping the city’s landscape (The High Line; Lindner & Rosa, 2017):

- **1934.** The initial railway was established to transport a substantial quantity of meat, dairy, and produce, amounting to millions of tons.
- **1980.** The cessation of train operations resulted in the abandonment of the railway tracks.
- **1999.** Until then, the structure was at risk of being demolished. However, Joshua David and Robert Hammond started advocating for preservation and conversion to a public open area.
- **2003.** The High Line organized an “ideas competition” and received 720 submissions from individuals in 36 different countries. These submissions proposed various ways in which the park could be utilized. Some ideas, such as a rollercoaster or a mile-long lap pool, were neither feasible nor practical.
- **2009.** Following the establishment of Friends of the High Line a decade ago, the initial portion of the High Line, spanning from Gansevoort to 20th Streets, became accessible to the public.
- **2011.** Section 2 was inaugurated, including the area between 20th and 30th Streets.
- **2014.** The Rail Yards segment from 30th to 34th Streets was inaugurated.
- **2019.** The Spur, the concluding segment of the ancient edifice, commenced operation.
- **2023.** The new High Line—Moyhiran Connector opened in June. This new pathway, comprised of two intersecting bridges, allows visitors to move from anywhere on the High Line to Moyhiran Train Hall with only one street crossing.

## PARK FEATURES

The Highline’s key features include (The Highline; Lindner & Rosa, 2017; Little et al., 2016):

1. **Tiffany & Co. Foundation Overlook** (At Gansevoort St.): This striking balcony signifies where the southern portion of the High Line was separated in the 1980s to accommodate the construction of residential units in the former Manhattan Refrigerating Warehouse. The location is situated east of the Whitney Museum of American Art and the headquarters of Friends of the High Line.
2. **Donald Pels and Wendy Keys Gansevoort Woodland** (Between Gansevoort St. and 13th St.): During the summer, vines elegantly drape over the railings at this location, creating a vibrant curtain that may be seen by anyone passing by. Below is a red brick structure that serves as the last functioning meatpacking facility in the Meatpacking District. Directly to the north is a striking glass hotel, The Standard High Line.
3. **14th Street Passage** (Between 14th St. and 15th St.): Each evening, beginning at twilight, within this partially covered pathway, one may discover an open-air TV program called the High Line Channel. This program showcases art videos, historical pieces, recent creations, and carefully selected series.
4. **Diller-Von Furstenberg Sundeck & Water Feature** (Between 14th St. and 15th St.): When the High Line was devoid of any activity and served as a vacant railway track, water would naturally gather at this location. This is precisely why the designers intentionally incorporated a discreet water element in this area. During the warmer months, this location is beloved by families and individuals of all ages, providing an opportunity to enjoy wading in the water.
5. **Chelsea Market Passage** (Between 15th St. and 16th St.): This section of the High Line is situated on the previous site of the National Biscuit Company, also known as Nabisco, which Chelsea Market currently occupies. The Oreo cookie was developed at this location, using flour transported by trains along this route. From April to October, the food court is open-air.
6. **Northern Spur Preserve** (At 16th St.): This spur traverses 10th Avenue to establish a connection with the old Merchants Refrigerating Warehouse. It represents the historical botanic aspect of the High Line, with crab apples, asters, sedges, goldenrods, and alumroot that evoke the untamed period before the park was accessible to the public.

## Elevated Parks

These features of the High Line collectively create a dynamic and diverse urban park experience. Each area provides a unique atmosphere, offering visitors a chance to immerse themselves in nature, art, history, and the vibrant cityscape of Manhattan (Figures 10.1–10.4).

## GARDEN ZONES

The High Line’s celebrated flora is influenced by the indigenous vegetation that thrived in this area before the park’s accessibility to the public. Visitors can investigate distinct environments uniquely adjusted to the microclimate formed by the surrounding urban landscape’s sun exposure, shadow, water availability, and wind patterns. Table 10.1 summarizes these garden zones and their vegetative features. Note that zones overlap with the park’s features described earlier.
