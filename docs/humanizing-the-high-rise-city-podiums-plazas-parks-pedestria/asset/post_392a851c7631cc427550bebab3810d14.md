
## Urban-Room Parks

The emphasis on walkability enhances the accessibility of urban rooms, encouraging social interaction and diverse activities. The urban room concept enhances urban living by creating pedestrian-friendly environments, enhancing the human experience, and fostering a dynamic and interconnected urban fabric (Al-Kodmany, 2020a).

At their essence, urban rooms aspire to be more than just well-defined and specialized; they aim to be unique and inviting urban public spaces. This design philosophy harmonizes with the notion that these outdoor environments should be experienced and cherished by the city's inhabitants in a manner akin to appreciating various rooms within their homes. The deliberate incorporation of varied activities, attention to perceptual details, and a focus on human scale collectively define urban spaces, enhancing the overall quality and user-centric nature of the urban environment. This concept signifies a departure from conventional park design, embracing a nuanced and purposeful approach to urban planning. By prioritizing the creation of spaces that are not only functional but also engaging and meaningful, urban rooms reshape the urban fabric, fostering dynamic, interconnected, and lively environments for individuals to explore, experience, and appreciate in their daily lives (Al-Kodmany, 2020b).

## Millennium Park, Chicago

Millennium Park, spanning 24.5 acres in downtown Chicago, Illinois, is a testament to urban transformation, converting a former rail yard and parking lot into a dynamic urban oasis. This farsighted project not only repurposes land but also realizes Daniel Burnham's century-old vision for a grand civic space along the lakefront, as documented by Gilfoyle in 2006. The site's history intertwines with the Illinois Central Railroad, which utilized the land since 1852, exchanging it for building a protective breakwater along the shore. In the mid-1900s, a section evolved into a parking lot until the initiation of park construction in 1997. Millennium Park officially opened in 2004, becoming a celebrated attraction and a defining feature of Chicago's urban identity, blending history, visionary design, and civic engagement in a harmonious tapestry of urban revitalization (Al-Kodmany, 2020a).

The park is a remarkable urban oasis that offers diverse attractions, including stunning artworks, elegant pavilions, lovely gardens, and seasonal exhibitions. These attractions appeal to different senses and create a contrast with the dense downtown area. One of the most striking aspects of this area is the smooth transition from the skyscraper-filled surroundings to the spacious green area with rich vegetation, vendors, music, and, most importantly, people. The park is open and free of charge for everyone, attracting people of various ages, ethnicities, and languages who come to enjoy the public, the art, and the social activities within the space. Visually, the city's skyline (two intersecting rows of impressive skyscrapers forming an L-shaped edge) frames the park and serves as a magnificent backdrop. Moreover, the park's artworks, such as the Crown Fountain and the Cloud Gate, are oversized to connect the towering buildings nearby and the “flat” park they occupy (Gifford, 2006).

Millennium Park is a remarkable urban design that integrates environmental, economic, and social benefits. The park spans an area of almost half a million square meters and has one of the largest green roofs in the world. This green roof effectively mitigates urban heat island effects, purifies the air, and serves as a reservoir for rainfall to avoid flooding. The park, which draws in millions of people yearly, produces substantial revenue and tax money for the city. Its construction was funded by a combination of public and private sources, amounting to around $490 million. The park has won many awards for its accessibility, sustainability, tourism, and social impact. Southworth notes, “Millennium Park . . . is undoubtedly a popular success. Since its opening in 2004, it has been visited by millions of tourists and hailed by many in the design community as one of our most successful urban parks” (Southworth, 2011, p. 504).

One of the most remarkable features of Millennium Park is that it was a collaborative effort involving many different parties. Mayor Richard M. Daley led the park’s vision, design, and implementation. Important participants include Project Design Director Edward Uhlir; Millennium Park Foundation Chairman John H. Bryan; Landscape Architect Kathryn Gustafson, Architect Adrian Smith; and architectural firm Skidmore, Owings, and Merrill (SOM). Moreover, the park benefited from the contributions of various cultural organizations, private donors, architects, planners, city employees, and volunteers who participated in the project. The park thus demonstrates how a diverse group of stakeholders can create a vibrant public space that caters to both locals and visitors from around the globe (Gifford, 2006).

## Master Plan

SOM designed the master plan for Millennium Park, which combines a classical, Beaux-Arts-inspired layout with modern art installations. The park has clear boundaries, a symmetrical layout, a main north–south walkway, a large lawn, and a limestone colonnade while showcasing the creations of contemporary architects and artists such as Frank Gehry, Jaume Plensa, and Anish Kapoor. Underground train stations have been upgraded, two new parking levels have been built, and bus stops have been added, creating a multimodal hub that reduced traffic congestion. SOM finished the master plan in 2002 (Al-Kodmany, 2020a; Kent, 2011).

The spatial layout of Millennium Park in Chicago follows the concept of an “urban room” structure, which originated from the design of Grant Park by Daniel Burnham and Edward Bennett in 1909. This concept was further realized in Millennium Park, where each distinct space within the park is designed to convey a unique and memorable design idea. The park comprises 12 principal “rooms” offering different experiences and design elements. These rooms include notable features such as the AT&T Plaza and Cloud Gate Sculpture, the Crown Fountain, the Jay Pritzker Pavilion, the BP Pedestrian Bridge, Lurie Gardens, the Boeing Galleria, the Chase Promenade, the Exelon Pavilions, the Harris Theater for Music and Dance, the McCormick Tribune Plaza and Ice Rink, the McDonald's Cycle Center, and the Wrigley Square and Millennium Monument (Gilfoyle, 2006; Figure 7.1).

Millennium Park uses the “urban room” concept to create a series of distinct, memorable spaces for visitors. Each room has its own visual identity, atmosphere, and design intention, providing a rich and varied user experience. This approach contributes to the park’s overall character and identity, making it a dynamic and engaging destination for residents and tourists. The park’s distinct “rooms” create memorable spaces that captivate visitors and leave a lasting impression. The park’s design ideas and experiences, from the reflective allure of Cloud Gate to the cultural vibrancy of the Jay Pritzker Pavilion, encourage a dynamic interplay between people and their urban environment. This intentional design philosophy transforms Millennium Park into not merely a physical expanse but a living canvas where the urban fabric is woven with creativity, functionality, and a palpable sense of place (Al-Kodmany, 2020b).
