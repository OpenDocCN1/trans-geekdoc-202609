
### Double Horizon
Sarah Sze's *Double Horizon*, a 5,500-pound boulder split open like a geode, uses tiles to create pixellated sky images, highlighting the fragility of time and the desire for permanence.

### Deep Gradient/Suspect Terrain
John Roloff's 18-foot-high sculpture, “Deep Gradient/Suspect Terrain,” creates an illusion of land and convention space, symbolizing the ocean and its surface.

### Roll
Brendon Monroe's “Roll” is a modern, fluid mural inspired by water and air movement, resembling a layer of fog over San Francisco’s coastal mountains below the Paseo bridge.

### Genesis
Christine Corday's “Genesis” entrance to Moscone North and Yerba Buena Gardens features massive stainless steel arc segments and a concrete form.

### PointCloud
Leo Villareal’s “PointCloud” light installation features 50,000 full-color LEDs and 800 mirrored stainless-steel rods, supporting the world’s largest light art installation, “The Bay Lights” on San Francisco’s Bay Bridge.

### Oche Wat Te Ou
In 1993, Jaune Quick-to-See Smith and James Lunain created the Oche Wat Te Ou, a reflection garden resembling Ohlone traditions, featuring a semicircular wood wall and Ohlone basket designs.

### Urge
Chico MacMurtrie’s interactive bronze sculpture, *Urge to Stand*, features physical mirroring and gaze, symbolizing human unity.

### Three Dancing Figures | Untitled
Keith Haring's 1989 untitled aluminum sculpture, initiated by the San Francisco Arts Commission, is a vibrant, primary-colored artwork at Howard and 3rd Streets.

### Shaking Man
Terry Allen’s bronze statue of a multidimensional business executive greets visitors at Yerba Buena Gardens, showcasing motion and gestures with interconnected figures.

## PERSHING SQUARE, LOS ANGELES

Situated in the central area of downtown Los Angeles, California, at 532 S. Olive St. and covering one block of five acres, Pershing Square is a prominent public park. It is near the Financial District and the Historic Core and is a popular gathering spot for residents and visitors alike. The park holds historical significance as one of the oldest public spaces in Los Angeles. Its historical monuments and markers contribute to its cultural value and serve as educational resources. It was initially dedicated in 1866 as *la Plaza Abaja* and renamed later after General John J. Pershing, a prominent military figure during World War I. It is also a historic site for LGBTQ+ activism and culture as it was part of the 5th Street corridor known as “The Run” from the 1920s to the 1960s. The park has witnessed significant events and protests throughout history, making it a site of historical and social importance (Moody, 2016; Parson, 2002). The Department of Recreation and Parks manages the square.

Pershing Square's central location in downtown Los Angeles makes it easily accessible to residents, workers, and visitors. Its proximity to public transit and surrounding businesses increases its convenience and potential for foot traffic. The park is surrounded by various office buildings, restaurants, and shops, making it a bustling hub during weekdays. It offers a tranquil oasis amid the busy urban environment, providing relaxation and recreation for locals and visitors. Pershing Square has undergone several renovations and redesigns, reflecting the changing needs and tastes of the city. The current design, completed in 1994, features a colorful and geometric style by architect Ricardo Legorreta, landscape architect Laurie Olin, and artist Barbara McCarren. Today, Pershing Square is a vibrant and diverse space that welcomes visitors of all ages and backgrounds (Ling, 2024).

The square is a communal area where individuals from the community and visitors may convene, converse, gather, and unwind in the heart of the downtown. The square consists of hardscapes and grassy areas that provide a space for concerts, gatherings, political rallies, and public celebrations. Pershing Square is adorned with fountains, monuments, statues, a spacious seating platform, two children's playgrounds, a chess area, a pet area, and an elevated open-air amphitheater inspired by the Mayan style. The park's central plaza and performance stage make it suitable for various events, including concerts, festivals, and public gatherings. Several sculptures and public art installations are scattered throughout the park, contributing to its appeal. Collectively, these elements allow the park to contribute to the city's cultural scene (Moody, 2016).

One of the notable features of Pershing Square is the iconic purple bell tower, a recognizable landmark in the area. The tower stands 125 feet tall and serves as a visual centerpiece of the park. The purple bell tower is not merely a structure; it is a symbol that defines the park's skyline and serves as a reference point for those navigating the bustling city streets. Its bold hue adds a vibrant splash of color to the urban landscape, creating a dynamic contrast against the backdrop of buildings and greenery. Whether seen from a distance or admired up close, its commanding presence prompts curiosity and wonder, encouraging people to explore the surrounding park and discover its diverse offerings (Ling, 2024); (Figure 5.4).
