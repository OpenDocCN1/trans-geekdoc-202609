## 10 Elevated Parks

“Elevated parks” represent a visionary approach to urban development, transforming disused or abandoned infrastructure, particularly old railways or elevated structures, into vibrant public green spaces and recreational havens. Converting outdated railroads into elevated parks is vital in humanizing high-rise cities. In this manner, we reclaim public space, connect neighborhoods, promote active transportation, enhance green infrastructure, preserve historical identity, encourage social interaction, and support urban resilience. These parks provide opportunities for people to connect with nature, engage in physical activities, and foster a sense of community amid tall buildings and urban density. By repurposing old railway infrastructure, these parks breathe new life into the urban landscape, transforming forgotten spaces into vibrant, inclusive, and sustainable public realms.

Elevated parks offer several benefits, including (Lindner & Rosa, 2017; Sinha, 2014):

1.  **Reclaiming Public Space:** By converting elevated rail tracks or viaducts into green corridors, these parks provide a much-needed escape from the concrete jungle high-rise city. They offer residents and visitors a place to relax, stroll, exercise, and enjoy nature, fostering a sense of belonging and community.
2.  **Connecting Neighborhoods:** Old railway tracks often cut through various neighborhoods, creating physical barriers. Elevated parks transform these barriers into bridges, reconnecting neighborhoods that were once divided. They provide accessible pedestrian and cycling paths, linking different parts of the city and promoting connectivity. This improves urban mobility and encourages people to explore and engage with their surroundings.
3.  **Promoting Active Transportation:** Elevated parks provide safe and dedicated pathways for pedestrians and cyclists, reducing car reliance and promoting sustainable transportation options. This enhances physical health, reduces traffic congestion, and improves air quality.
4.  **Enhancing Green Infrastructure:** These parks expand green infrastructure within the urban fabric. They introduce vegetation, trees, and landscaping, creating urban oases that improve air quality, provide shade, and support biodiversity. They also act as green corridors that connect existing parks, gardens, and open spaces, forming a network of greenery throughout the city.
5.  **Preserving Historical and Cultural Identity:** Repurposing old railway infrastructure as elevated parks preserves a city’s historical and cultural identity. These structures often have significant heritage value, and repurposing them as public spaces allows for their preservation and integration into the urban fabric. Elevated parks can showcase the industrial heritage of a city, serving as living reminders of its history while adapting to contemporary needs.
6.  **Encouraging Social Interaction:** Elevated parks become social hubs within the high-rise city, fostering social interaction and community engagement. They provide gathering spaces, seating areas, and event venues where people can join for recreational activities, cultural events, or socializing. These parks promote social cohesion and a sense of shared ownership, creating a vibrant and inclusive urban environment.
7.  **Supporting Urban Resilience:** By making cities more adaptable and less susceptible to climate change, elevated parks help make cities more resilient. By incorporating sustainable elements like green roofs, solar panels, and rainwater collection, they may demonstrate their commitment to environmental friendliness. As green buffers, these parks lessen the impact of the urban heat island, slow the flow of rainwater, and make communities more resistant to the effects of climate change.
8.  **Pedestrian Safety:** Elevated parks provide safe and dedicated pathways for pedestrians, separating them from vehicular traffic. These pathways enhance pedestrian safety, particularly in busy urban areas with high volumes of vehicles. They encourage walking and active transportation, promoting healthier lifestyles and reducing car reliance.
9.  **Greenery and Biodiversity:** By bringing more plants and wildlife to cityscapes otherwise dominated by concrete, elevated parks help to green them. They improve the urban ecosystem by providing a natural setting for flora and fauna, promoting ecological harmony.
10. **Improved Air Quality:** Elevated parks act as natural air filters, absorbing carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen. They help mitigate the heat island effect, reduce air pollution, and enhance air quality in densely populated areas. Plants and rooftop trees also help lower ambient temperatures through shade and evapotranspiration.
11. **Noise Reduction:** Parks on higher ground can reduce or even eliminate noise pollution from nearby cities and other urban areas. Nearby households and tourists can enjoy a more tranquil atmosphere due to the sound-muffling properties of greenery and vegetation.
12. **Stormwater Management:** Rooftop parks can aid in managing stormwater run off. Vegetation absorbs rainwater, reducing the strain on drainage systems and minimizing the risk of flooding during heavy rainfall. They improve stormwater management by retaining and filtering rainwater.
13. **Aesthetic Enhancement:** Elevated parks enhance the visual appeal of urban landscapes, softening the hard edges of buildings and concrete structures. They introduce natural elements, colors, and textures, creating a harmonious balance between nature and urban development. They can also offer panoramic views of the city skyline, providing a unique vantage point for residents and tourists.
14. **Economic Benefits:** The establishment and maintenance of these parks help stimulate the economy and generate employment opportunities for residents. Also, surrounding areas can benefit from elevated parks. Increased foot traffic and economic activity result from their ability to attract visitors, tourists, and businesses.

### The High Line, New York City

The High Line is a New York City Park maintained and supported by Friends of the High Line. It is a public park built on a historic freight rail line above Manhattan's West Side Street. It spans approximately 1.5 miles (2.4 kilometers) and runs through the Chelsea and Meatpacking District neighborhoods, offering panoramic city views, art installations, gardens, and gathering spaces. The High Line is a model of urban renewal and sustainability, as it preserves the area's industrial heritage and features native plants and wildlife habitats. It offers visitors a unique perspective of the city, its architecture, and various art installations, events, and programs throughout the year. In its first full year of operation in 2010, the park welcomed approximately 2 million visitors. The numbers continued to rise, and by 2019, it was reported that the High Line had attracted over 8 million visitors. This figure has been sustained or even increased in recent years. Through its integration of green space, pedestrian-friendly design, cultural elements, community engagement, and unique viewpoints, the High Line humanizes the high-rise city of New York. It creates a harmonious balance between nature and urbanity, providing a welcoming and enriching experience for residents and visitors alike (Avni, 2019; Littke et al., 2016; Park & Lee, 2019).

### Background and History

The High Line's history dates to the early 20th century when it was initially built as a freight rail line known as the West Side Elevated Line. The railway served the industrial and manufacturing district of the area, transporting goods between factories, warehouses, and the waterfront. Construction of the High Line began in 1929, and the first section of the elevated railway opened in 1934. The railway was designed to elevate the trains above street level, alleviating traffic congestion and improving safety for both pedestrians and trains. It operated for several decades, but due to the rise of interstate trucking and a decline in rail traffic, the last train on the High Line ran in 1980 (Al-Kodmany, 2018; Sim et al., 2019).
