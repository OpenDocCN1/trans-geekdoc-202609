
### 3. Cultural Enrichment and Identity
- **Celebration of Culture:** Sculptures often represent cultural expressions, providing a means to celebrate the history and identity of a city or region.
- **Public Engagement:** Sculpture parks become cultural hubs, hosting events, installations, and performances that engage the public and contribute to a shared cultural experience.

### 4. Green Spaces and Environmental Benefits
- **Environmental Balance:** High-rise cities are characterized by dense infrastructure. Sculpture parks introduce green spaces, contributing to environmental balance by absorbing carbon dioxide, providing oxygen, and supporting biodiversity.
- **Microclimates and Air Quality:** Trees and plants within the park contribute to microclimates, improving air quality and offering a respite from urban pollution.

### 5. Enhanced Quality of Life
- **Recreational Spaces:** Sculpture parks offer spaces for recreation, relaxation, and social interaction, enhancing the overall quality of life for urban residents.
- **Well-being:** Access to green spaces and art has been linked to improved mental well-being, providing an escape from the hustle and bustle of city life.

### 6. Tourism and Economic Impact
- **Tourist Attractions:** Sculpture parks possess the capacity to emerge as noteworthy tourist attractions, enticing a diverse audience and offering a valuable contribution to the city’s flourishing tourism economy.
- **Economic Stimulus:** Vibrant public spaces can stimulate economic activities around them, including local businesses, restaurants, and cultural events.

### 7. Community Building
- **Social Spaces:** Sculpture parks serve as gathering spaces, fostering a sense of community by providing areas for events, festivals, and social interactions.
- **Inclusive Spaces:** Public art in sculpture parks can be inclusive, offering a space where people from diverse backgrounds come together to appreciate and engage with art.

In essence, sculpture parks in high-rise cities contribute to a holistic urban experience, addressing aesthetic, cultural, environmental, and social aspects, ultimately enhancing the overall livability and identity of the urban landscape (Hunt, 2020; Reynolds, 2011).

The following examples illustrate how public art can help humanize the high-rise city.

## CITYGARDEN SCULPTURE PARK, SAINT LOUIS

### Saint Louis, Missouri

Saint Louis, Missouri, boasts a rich historical tapestry seamlessly interwoven with a vibrant contemporary presence. Founded in 1764 by French fur traders, it has played a significant role in the westward expansion of the United States, earning the nickname “Gateway to the West.” St. Louis is part of the Greater St. Louis metropolitan area and has a diverse economy with strengths in healthcare, finance, manufacturing, and transportation. The city is home to world-class cultural institutions like the Saint Louis Symphony Orchestra, Saint Louis Art Museum, and Missouri Botanical Garden. St. Louis’ sports culture thrives with passionate fans supporting teams like the St. Louis Cardinals and St. Louis Blues. Educational institutions like Washington University and Saint Louis University contribute to the city’s intellectual vibrancy. Despite challenges related to urban development and economic transitions, efforts are being made to revitalize neighborhoods and enhance cultural amenities (Proctor, 2018).

### Citygarden

Citygarden is a 3-acre urban park and sculpture garden in St. Louis, Missouri, owned by the City and maintained by a private foundation, the Gateway Foundation. The site is situated within the confines of Eighth, Tenth, Market, and Chestnut Streets, specifically in the “Gateway Mall” area, just a few blocks west of the iconic Gateway Arch and the majestic Mississippi River. The park’s prime downtown location makes it easily accessible to residents, workers, and visitors. Art and nature come together in the park’s exquisitely landscaped gardens, which feature a wide variety of plants, trees, and flowers. During the warmer months, guests can enjoy the park’s interactive features, such as fountains, pools, and water jets, while also relaxing, picnicking, and taking leisurely strolls. The location consisted of two vacant grass plots before being transformed into a garden and park. Citygarden was officially inaugurated on June 30, 2009, and commenced operations on July 1, 2009 (Citygarden; Proctor, 2018).

Subsequently, the garden has assumed a major role in revitalizing the city’s central area. In 2011, the American Society of Landscape Architects (ASLA) honored the project with an award. The Professional Award Jury explained that the project’s influence transcends its physical boundaries, serving as a testament to St. Louis’ dedication to enhancing public spaces and contributing to a broader national conversation on urban environments. The triumph of this initiative has emboldened the city to participate in the Arch Competition, underscoring the significance of thoughtful design and the landscape architect’s adept integration of art within the natural surroundings (ASLA).

Citygarden is artfully segmented into three sections, demarcated by the arc wall and meander wall. The northern band mimics an elevated plateau reminiscent of the steep river cliffs along the Mississippi and Missouri Rivers. Here, urban terraces, shaded platforms for sculptures, and seating areas provide scenic views over a water basin. This area also accommodates a café, maintenance building, and an impressive 550-foot-long arcing wall, reminiscent of the bends in the rivers. Steppingstone boulders and a water basin contribute to the aesthetic. The ‘floodplain’ band, situated in the middle, showcases large native shade and specimen trees, becoming the canvas for the garden’s most substantial sculptures. Paved plazas with waterworks, a spray basin, and varied entry paths enhance this zone. The southern band, nestled between Market Street and the floodplain, embraces cultivated terraces. It features a meandering seat wall, a Ginkgo alley, and horticultural gardens, completing the park’s diverse and distinctive layout (ASLA; Proctor, 2018).

The entirety of Citygarden adheres to the standards set by the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). Accessible parking is provided at the intersections of 8th and Chestnut Streets and Market and 9th Streets. The park is intentionally designed to provide inclusivity, with spacious pathways catering to anyone with mobility constraints. Integrating public art and green spaces within a central urban location promotes inclusivity and engagement. Citygarden serves as a gathering place for the community, offering opportunities for social interaction and shared experiences among visitors of all ages. It is illuminated at night, creating a captivating and magical ambiance, and showcasing the intricate details of the sculptures (Proctor, 2018).
