
As one approaches the northeast corner of 101 California Street, a captivating sight awaits—a seven-story glass lobby crowned with a gracefully slanted roof. This architectural feature serves a functional purpose and adds a touch of refinement and sophistication to the building's entrance. Surrounding this grand entrance is a pedestrian arcade, creating a welcoming and dynamic transition space for those entering the building. The plaza, paved with granite, extends the invitation to linger and appreciate the surrounding urban landscape (Al-Kodmany, 2017; Woodbridge, 1992).

Examining the tower's base reveals a fascinating interplay of shapes and elements. A triangular podium adorned with square windows graces the northwest corner, contributing to the overall visual intrigue of the structure. Notably, this design echoes the characteristics of 101 Park Avenue in Manhattan, a testament to Philip Johnson's architectural prowess and his ability to create resonant design elements across diverse locations (Woodbridge, 1992).

In essence, 101 California Street stands as a testament to architectural innovation and urban integration. Its unique form, the inviting glass lobby, and the thoughtfully designed podium all come together to contribute to the San Francisco skyline and the urban experience, reflecting the brilliance of Philip Johnson's vision and his lasting impact on the world of architecture (Figure 2.8).

The Postmodern design of 101 California Street in San Francisco represents a departure from the styles employed in both the Modernist Daley Center and the historic Tribune Tower. While the Tribune Tower exhibits Neo-Gothic architecture, characterized by its use of historic elements and ornate detailing, and the Daley Center embraces Modernist principles with its sleek lines and functional simplicity, 101 California Street stands as a testament to the Postmodern movement's inclination for eclecticism and reinterpretation.

## Podiums

In contrast to the historical reverence seen in the Tribune Tower's use of fragments from iconic landmarks worldwide, 101 California Street adopts a more eclectic approach, blending modernist elements with historical references. The cylindrical shape and sawtooth profile, coupled with a glass curtain wall, showcase a departure from the clean lines of modernism and a move towards a more varied and expressive architectural language.

The podium of 101 California Street, with its triangular form and square windows, diverges from the podium designs of both the Tribune Tower and the Daley Center. This departure underscores the postmodern inclination to play with forms, combining elements to challenge traditional notions of architectural uniformity. In contrast, the Tribune Tower's podium embodies historical design and ornamentation, and the Daley Center's podium aligns with modernist ideals of functionality and simplicity.

Furthermore, the postmodern design of 101 California Street emphasizes a sense of playfulness and self-reference, celebrating architectural diversity rather than adhering to a singular style. This starkly contrasts the Tribune Tower's homage to historical landmarks and the Daley Center's commitment to modernist efficiency. 101 California Street exemplifies the postmodern ethos of embracing diverse influences and challenging the conventional boundaries of architectural expression.

## Thompson Center, Chicago [1985]

The building, originally known as the State of Illinois Center, was renamed in 1993 to honor former Illinois Republican Governor James R. Thompson. It is a 17-story, 94-meter (308-foot) building in Chicago's Loop, the heart of the CBD. Helmut Jahn, the building's architect, has formulated an exciting design characterized by sweeping arcs of glass that wrap around an enormous cylindrical atrium, spanning the building's full height, to culminate in a skylight. As the central office for State government employees, the building's transparent appearance reflects the transparency of government in a democratic society. Its skylight and central atrium convey a modern reinterpretation of the traditional rotunda or dome. Furthermore, the changing patterns of light and shadow create a dynamic visual display along the facade of the building, enriching the experience of this urban space. At its completion in 1985, the building was considered a significant departure from more conventional modern architecture.

Helmut Jahn's design subtly tapers backward to address "tallness" issues and respect human scale. In this context, the building doesn't maintain a uniform width from the ground to the top; instead, it narrows and tapers slightly as it rises. Tapering forges several effects, including reducing the visual impact of height, improving wind conditions at the pedestrian level, enhancing structural stability, and creating a more aesthetically pleasing silhouette. The structure feels more proportional and integrated within the surrounding environment. This consideration aligns with the idea that overly tall structures can sometimes dominate the skyline and create a sense of imposing scale that may not be conducive to the human experience at the street level (Al-Kodmany, 2020; Jahn, 2015).

An additional exciting feature of the building is how it connects with the ground, caving into the first floor and creating a pedestrian arcade. Instead of a traditional straight-up design, the Thompson Center is characterized by a design that seems to “cave into” or recess into the first floor, creating a space that extends outward and is accessible to pedestrians. This design choice results in the formation of a pedestrian arcade that enhances the connection between the building and the people who move around it, fostering a more vibrant and interactive streetscape. This architectural “trick” breaks down the barrier between the building and the surrounding space and invites pedestrians into a visually stimulating environment. Including a pedestrian arcade fosters a sense of openness, encouraging people to explore and engage with the building at a human scale (Jahn, 2015; Saliga & Clarke, 1998).

The surrounding plaza is thoughtfully designed, featuring various elements to enhance the overall experience. Seating areas provide places for people to rest and socialize, while strategically planted planters and trees contribute to the aesthetic appeal and offer a sense of nature within the urban environment. The “Monument with Standing Beast” sculpture by Jean Dubuffet adds an artistic dimension, enriching the visual character of the plaza, creating a focal point for visitors and enhancing relation to human scale (See Chapter 14).

In summary, the podium design of the Thompson Center stands out for its innovative connection to the ground, the inclusion of engaging public spaces, and the careful consideration of human scale. These elements collectively contribute to a welcoming and visually appealing urban environment, showcasing the thoughtful and intentional design principles employed by Helmut Jahn (Figure 2.9).

## 500 West Madison, Chicago [1987]

500 West Madison, also known as the Citigroup Center, is a 42-story, 179-meter (588-foot) tower in downtown Chicago, Illinois. The mixed-use tower has 1,400,000 square feet of floor space and houses offices and a two-story shopping mall. The second floor connects to a commuter railroad station, the Northwestern Station, which serves the north and west suburbs. An enclosed skywalk connects the station level to the neighboring North Riverside building. The Citigroup Center was completed in 1987 and is a remarkable all-glass building in Chicago (Figure 2.10).

Helmut Jahn designed the building in a Late Modernist/Postmodernist style, blending contemporary elements with innovation in architecture. The design features a mesmerizing cascading waterfall curtain wall with bluish reflective glass. Consequently, despite its considerable size, the tower's tapered profile plays a crucial role in its urban integration, preventing it from overpowering the surrounding area. This intentional design choice ensures that the Citigroup Center harmonizes with its context, contributing to the overall urban experience and enhancing human-scale interaction within the vibrant cityscape (Jahn, 2015; Saliga & Clarke, 1998). From this perspective, the cascading waterfall curtain wall design of 500 West Madison resembles the tapering profiles of the Thompson Center (discussed earlier) by improving the connection to the streetscape and enhancing the relationship with the human scale (Al-Kodmany, 2020).

The waterfall effect metaphorically connects the tower to the nearby Chicago River. Therefore, the design establishes a meaningful and evocative relationship between the Citigroup Center and its urban context. By metaphorically connecting to the Chicago River, the tower becomes more than just a physical structure; it becomes a part of the broader narrative and identity of the city, contributing to the cultural and visual richness of the architectural landscape.
