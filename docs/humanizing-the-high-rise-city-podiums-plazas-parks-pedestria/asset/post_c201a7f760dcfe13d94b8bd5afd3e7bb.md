## BEST PRACTICE

Fortunately, architectural innovation has risen to the challenges posed by skyscrapers, offering innovative solutions that transcend conventional design boundaries. These groundbreaking approaches are redefining the interaction between people and tall buildings, encompassing physical aspects and the emotional dimensions of urban living. The design and engineering of skyscrapers have evolved significantly in recent years, creating new possibilities for enhancing their inhabitants' quality of life and well-being. In this exploration, we present a series of remarkable projects that rethink the bases and entrances of high-rise structures, balancing aesthetics and functionality. These projects are arranged chronologically, reflecting the evolution of base design in skyscrapers. Each example demonstrates a different design strategy, enhancing the relationship between a tower and its ground-level context. The projects cover various architectural styles, from historic and modernist to modernist/brutalist, late modernist, postmodernist, contemporary, and pluralistic. Through these case studies, we learn about the changes and trends in tower base design over time.

### Tribune Tower, Chicago [1925]

The Tribune Tower in Chicago is a stunning example of neo-Gothic architecture, built between 1923 and 1925 after an international design competition. The acclaimed design, crafted by New York architects John M. Howells and Raymond M. Hood, seamlessly integrated gargoyles and flying buttresses into the tower's facade. Clad in Indiana limestone, it features vertical piers and horizontal spandrels that create a sense of solidity and rhythm. This iconic structure stands 463 feet tall and encompasses 36 floors of office space. It was home to the Chicago Tribune newspaper until 2018 and is now being converted into luxury residences.

### Podiums

The podium of the Tribune Tower in Chicago is a captivating showcase of architectural prowess, adorned with intricate details and meticulous incorporation of historical design, creating a distinctive and visually stunning structure. The base of the tower exhibits a neo-Gothic design, mirroring the overall architectural style of the building. A remarkable aspect of the Tribune Tower’s podium is the collection of over 150 fragments from renowned buildings and landmarks worldwide. These fragments, embedded within the podium walls, are tangible pieces of history. Tribune reporters and correspondents collected these artifacts during their travels, securing permission to take bits from iconic places such as the Taj Mahal, the Great Wall of China, Westminster Abbey in England, and the Parthenon in Greece. This assembly of fragments adds a layer of historical richness to the podium, inviting observers to explore its intricate details (Al-Kodmany, 2020; Kamin, 2000).

In terms of human scale, the podium offers a unique and engaging experience. As visitors approach the tower, the intricate details and historical artifacts embedded in the podium become tangible and visually compelling. The juxtaposition of human scale against the grandeur of the architecture creates a sense of awe and connection, allowing individuals to appreciate the structure’s craftsmanship and the historical significance encapsulated within its walls. The Tribune Tower’s podium is a testament to the seamless integration of architectural excellence, historical storytelling, and a welcoming environment that captivates observers and draws them into its rich narrative (Kamin, 2000). It is an intricate and elegant expression of the Tribune’s identity and mission, inviting visitors to explore its details and discover its secrets while providing a stunning backdrop for the cityscape (Figure 2.6).

### Richard J. Daley Center, Chicago [1965]

The 32-story, 198-meter (648-foot) tower is in the heart of Chicago's Loop. It embraces a Modernist style of architecture and is constructed out of Cor-Ten steel, which, when rusted, strengthens the structure and gives it a unique reddish-brown color. The tower's exterior is buttressed by twelve large cruciform columns that enable column-free interior spaces. The ground floor is completely enclosed in wall-to-wall glass windows, allowing passersby to glimpse the main lobby. In essence, the Daley Center is an island of steel and glass in a sea of granite, with the plaza covering 80% of the entire block, not counting the space created by the setbacks. Designed by C. F. Murphy Associates, the Daley Center was the tallest building in the city when it was built in 1965. However, its status as such was short-lived, given the completion of the John Hancock Center only four years later (Al-Kodmany & Ali, 2013; Schulze & Harrington, 2003).

The podium of the Daley Center Building stands as a testament to architectural ingenuity, seamlessly integrating transparency to enhance the interaction between the towering structure and the surrounding pedestrian space. This innovative design goes beyond mere aesthetics, creating an inviting environment that fosters a harmonious connection between the architectural marvel and the individuals traversing the area. Using a transparent base transforms the entryway into a permeable portal, breaking physical barriers and establishing a sense of openness. Visitors are greeted by a welcoming environment where the boundaries between the interior and exterior blur, creating a dynamic spatial experience. The podium's transparency fulfills an aesthetic purpose and plays a crucial role in fostering a symbiotic relationship among the Daley Center Building, its plaza, and the pedestrians it accommodates. It is a testament to the commitment to creating spaces that transcend traditional architectural norms, embracing a design philosophy prioritizing accessibility, openness, and seamless integration with the urban fabric (Figure 2.7).

### Podiums

It would be helpful to contrast the modern architectural design of the podium of the Daley Center Building with the historic design of the podium of the Tribune Tower. The Daley Center Building offers a stark departure from the neo-Gothic aesthetics of the Tribune Tower as it embodies a Modernist approach to architecture. It is characterized by sleek lines, a minimalist design, and the prominent use of glass. Unlike the Tribune Tower’s neo-Gothic ornamentation, the Daley Center Building emphasizes simplicity and functionality. In terms of material, the Daley Center utilizes glass and steel, providing a transparent and open appearance. The transparency of the podium improves the visual connection between the building and the surrounding urban environment. This modern design choice contrasts with the Tribune Tower’s use of Indiana limestone and the incorporation of historical design and details.

In essence, the Tribune Tower’s podium is a testament to the neo-Gothic style’s rich ornamentation and historical narrative. In contrast, the podium of the Daley Center Building represents a modern design ethos that values simplicity, transparency, and a seamless integration with the contemporary urban environment. These distinct design philosophies showcase the evolution of architectural styles over time in response to changing cultural, social, and technological contexts.

Despite the differences in architectural styles, both the podium of the Tribune Tower and the Daley Center Building share a joint commitment to respecting human scale. In the case of the Tribune Tower, the neo-Gothic design incorporates architectural details and ornamentations, creating a visually harmonious composition that complements the human scale. The collection of over 150 fragments embedded in the podium further invites people to engage with the structure personally and culturally, enhancing the overall human experience.

Similarly, despite its Modernist approach, the Daley Center Building prioritizes human scale. The extensive use of glass allows for transparency, creating a connection between the interior and the exterior. The openness of the podium welcomes pedestrians and visitors, ensuring that the building doesn’t overpower but rather integrates into the human-scale experience of the urban environment. The clean lines and simplicity of the design contribute to an uncluttered and approachable aesthetic, maintaining a sense of proportion that aligns with human dimensions.

### 101 California Street, San Francisco [1982]

101 California Street, a 48-story office skyscraper nestled in the heart of San Francisco’s Financial District, was conceived by the renowned architect Philip Johnson, and completed in 1982. This architectural marvel stands out with its distinctive cylindrical shape, characterized by a sawtooth profile that adds a unique touch to the city’s skyline. The tower’s exterior is adorned with an elegant glass curtain wall, showcasing a harmonious blend of modern aesthetics and structural ingenuity.
