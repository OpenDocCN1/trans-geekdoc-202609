
## Recreational Trail

The Recreational Trail segment of the Chicago Riverwalk, extending from Lake Shore Drive to Michigan Avenue, stands as the inaugural section of this iconic urban space. Constructed in the early 2000s, this eastern component connects the Chicago Loop with Lakefront Trail and Navy Pier, seamlessly intertwining with the cityscape along the Chicago River. As a docking point for boats offering educational and recreational tours, the Recreational Trail or Riverwalk Esplanade has become a hub of activity and leisure (Hill, 2019).

Among the most exciting tours is the Chicago River Architecture Cruise. It is a captivating and educational boat tour that navigates the iconic Chicago River, offering participants a unique perspective on the city's remarkable architectural landscape. This immersive experience provides a front-row seat to some of Chicago's most renowned architectural marvels, showcasing the city's rich history and innovative design. The cruise typically passes by landmarks such as the Willis Tower (formerly known as the Sears Tower), the Wrigley Building, the Tribune Tower, and the Trump Tower, among others. These iconic structures come to life as the boat glides through the city’s heart, providing passengers with a comprehensive understanding of Chicago’s architectural heritage (Al-Kodmany, 2021; Kamin, 2015; Hill, 2019).

The trail’s carefully curated design incorporates lush landscaping, verdant trees, and diverse plants, contributing to its aesthetic appeal. Additionally, the inclusion of thoughtfully designed play areas, stylish LED lighting fixtures, comfortable furniture, artistic railing, and welcoming seating areas enhances the overall experience for visitors. Public restrooms provide convenience, ensuring a visitor-friendly environment. Despite its early completion, ongoing projects focusing on retrofitting and improvement underscore a commitment to continuous enhancement, maintaining the Recreational Trail as a dynamic and cherished urban oasis (Figure 9.2).

The western section of the Chicago Riverwalk, stretching from Michigan Avenue to Lake Street, offers a distinct contrast to its eastern counterpart. Unlike the natural park-like ambiance of the east, the “west part” embodies the essence of civic space. Here, the landscape takes on a more urban character, reflecting the dynamic and bustling atmosphere of the city. As visitors traverse this stretch, they are immersed in an environment that seamlessly blends urban design with recreational elements, providing a multifaceted experience within the heart of Chicago’s vibrant urban fabric.

The west part consists of block-long sections or “bays” that contain civic plazas. It was constructed in three phases (Phase 1, Phase 2, and Phase 3), as follows.

### Phase 1

Phase 1, spanning from Michigan Avenue to State Street, was finalized in 2009 and encompasses noteworthy landmarks, including the McCormick Bridgehouse & Chicago River Museum and Chicago’s Vietnam Veterans Memorial. Two pivotal pedestrian connections at the river level were strategically incorporated to enhance the path’s continuity. In the previous layout, the pedestrian experience was hindered and dissuasive, as individuals were required to ascend stairways to the upper level of Wacker Drive, cross a city street, and then descend once more to reach the river level. The introduction of under-bridge crossings effectively addressed this challenge, providing pedestrians with a seamless and uninterrupted route along the Chicago River (Al-Kodmany, 2023; Chicago Riverwalk; Kamin, 2015, 2023).

### McCormick Bridgehouse & Chicago River Museum

Designed by architect and urban planner Edward H. Bennett, the McCormick Bridgehouse & Chicago River Museum is a five-story, 1,200-square-foot (111 square-meter) bridgehouse at the western edge of Michigan Avenue. At the river level, this historic structure lively exhibits the bridge’s machinery and giant gears that make it open and close. It also exhibits a series of visuals illustrating the story of 18 moveable bridges that dot the river. Further, exhibits explain historic events, for example, the Eastland Disaster event in the nearby area between Clark and LaSalle streets. The museum’s top level allows visitors to enjoy 360-degree city views. “Cozy” interior spaces feature exposed brick walls and steel beams. In contrast, the exterior features a 1928 bas-relief by Henry Hering titled “The Defenders.” It commemorates the 1812 Battle of Fort Dearborn (Al-Kodmany, 2023; Chicago Riverwalk).

### *Vietnam Veterans Memorial*

Chicago's Vietnam Veterans Memorial, completed in 2005 and situated between Wabash and State streets, stands as a poignant tribute to the fallen heroes of the Vietnam War. This expansive memorial encompasses multiple tiers adorned with water features, including a soothing waterfall and a fountain embellished with 14 jets. A significant aspect of the memorial is a long granite slab along the waterfall, bearing the engraved names of all Illinois Vietnam War veterans who made the ultimate sacrifice. Notably, this memorial ranks among the largest Vietnam War Memorials outside Washington, DC.

The design of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial is intentionally secluded from the bustling city streets, creating a serene and contemplative refuge amid the urban landscape. The abundance of greenery and thoughtful landscaping contribute to the tranquility of the space. Despite its quiet ambiance, the memorial is frequented by pedestrians, joggers, and photographers drawn to its unique blend of natural elements and urban vistas (Al-Kodmany, 2017; Chicago Riverwalk; Kamin, 2022a).

The grassy areas adjacent to the Chicago River emerge as popular gathering spots, where visitors can relish the scenic views while finding respite from the city's hustle and bustle. Informal concrete sitting ledges provide convenient spots for people-watching, as the constant flow of pedestrians along the river adds to the dynamic urban experience. These sitting spaces offer a vantage point for admiring the memorial, the river, and the skyline and evoke a sense of connection to a more natural environment within the heart of the city (Figure 9.3).

### *Phase 2*

Phase 2 extends from State Street to LaSalle Street. Completed in 2015, it contains three “urban rooms,” a concept coined and promoted by Daniel Burnham and Edward H. Bennett in their Chicago 1909 Plan (See Chapter 7). In the context of the Chicago Riverwalk, each urban room spans 300 feet (91 meters) along the river and features a distinct river-inspired theme. These rooms are also bordered by iconic, historic bridges, which promote a strong sense of visual enclosure and placemaking. The second phase involved the creation of three urban rooms, including Marina Plaza, The Cove, and The River Theater (Al-Kodmany, 2021; Chicago Riverwalk).

### *Marina Plaza*

Nestled between State Street and Dearborn Street, the Marina Plaza is a captivating addition to Chicago's urban landscape. Its nomenclature pays homage to the iconic Marina City, a mixed-use skyscraper complex that graces the opposite riverbank. Functioning as an “urban room,” this plaza goes beyond mere aesthetics, seamlessly blending functionality with a touch of elegance. At its core, the Marina Plaza serves as a dynamic space, integrating various elements to create a harmonious environment for visitors.
