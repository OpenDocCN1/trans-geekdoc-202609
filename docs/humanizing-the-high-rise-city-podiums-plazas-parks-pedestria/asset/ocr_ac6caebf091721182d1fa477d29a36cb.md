# ■ Waterfront Parks

Fish ecologists used creative intervention techniques to support subsurface aquatic habitats in this area. For example, they integrated seven limnetic “habitat curtains,” each consisting of steel frames and wire mesh, from which nylon ropes dangle. These are made to make algae grow on them, providing food for fish. These curtains also offer an artificial filamentous substrate for inhabiting sessile organisms such as barnacles. Ecologists wrapped around and attached “pole hulas” to the underwater structural poles closer to the walkway. Pole hulas promote algae growth and the breeding of amphibious insects, which are primary food sources for fish.

Further, ecologists installed caisson-mounted “lunkers,” porous steel cylinders that offer a place for fish to shelter from powerful river currents and aquatic predators, larger fish, and mammals. Fish lunkers were installed on seven of the caissons away from the walkway. A 10-inch (25.4-centimeter) gap between the lunker and the caisson provides a shelter for fish. Interestingly, these ecological techniques implemented in the Jetty were partially inspired by the “fish hotel” project that attracted and provided habitat for river species before the Riverwalk was built (Hill, 2019, p. 236).

In essence, the Jetty emerges as a dynamic riverfront space where the serenity of fishing, the richness of ecological habitats, and the opportunity for education converge, creating a holistic and engaging environment that resonates with diverse interests and promotes a sustainable coexistence between the urban and natural realms (Figure 9.8).

**The Riverbank**
The Riverbank, encompassing the final stretch of the Chicago Riverwalk from Franklin Street to Lake Street, provides a serene and spacious retreat along the river’s edge. This space serves as a multifunctional area, fostering a connection between the urban landscape and the natural beauty of the waterway.
