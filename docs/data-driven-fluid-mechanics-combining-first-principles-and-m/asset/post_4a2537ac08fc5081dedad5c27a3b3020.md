The POD (Berkooz et al. 1993, Holmes et al. 2012) provides a transform matrix Ψ, the columns of which are modes that are ordered based on energy content.¹ POD has been widely used to generate ROMs of complex systems, many for control, and it is guaranteed to provide an optimal low-rank basis to capture the maximal energy or variance in a data set. However, it may be the case that the most energetic modes are nearly uncontrollable or unobservable, and therefore may not be relevant for control. Similarly, in many cases the most controllable and observable state directions may have very low energy; for example, acoustic modes typically have very low energy, yet they mediate the dominant input–output dynamics in many fluid systems. The rudder on a ship provides a good analogy: although it accounts for a small amount of the total energy, it is dynamically important for control.

Instead of ordering modes based on energy, it is possible to determine a hierarchy of modes that are most controllable and observable, therefore capturing the most input-output information. These modes give rise to balanced models, giving equal weighting to the controllability and observability of a state via a coordinate transformation that makes the controllability and observability Gramians equal and diagonal. These models have been extremely successful, although computing a balanced model using traditional methods is prohibitively expensive for high-dimensional systems. In this section, we describe the balancing procedure, as well as modern methods for efficient computation of balanced models. A computationally efficient suite of algorithms for model reduction and system identification may be found in Belson et al. (2014).

A balanced ROM should map inputs to outputs as faithfully as possible for a given model order r. It is therefore important to introduce an operator norm to quantify how similarly (12.1) and (12.2) act on a given set of inputs. Typically, we take the infinity norm of the difference between the transfer functions G(s) and G_r(s) obtained from the full system (12.1) and reduced system (12.2), respectively. This norm is given by

‖G‖∞ ≜ max_ω σ1(G(iω)). (12.4)

To summarize, we seek a ROM (12.2) of low order, r ≪ n, so the operator norm ‖G − G_r‖∞ is small.

## 12.1.2 Change of Variables in Control Systems

The balanced model reduction problem may be formulated in terms of first finding a coordinate transformation

$$\boldsymbol{x} = \mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z}\tag{12.5}$$

that hierarchically orders the states in $z$ in terms of their ability to capture the input-output characteristics of the system. We will begin by considering an invertible transformation $\mathbf{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, and then provide a method to compute just the first $r$ columns, which will comprise the transformation $\mathbf{\Psi}$ in (12.2). Thus, it will be possible to retain only the first $r$ most controllable/observable states, while truncating the rest. This is similar to the change of variables into eigenvector coordinates, except that we emphasize controllability and observability rather than characteristics of the dynamics.

Substituting $\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z}$ into (12.1) gives

$$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z} + \mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.6a}$$ $$\boldsymbol{y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z} + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.6b}$$

Finally, multiplying (12.6a) by $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ yields

$$\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{z} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z} + \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.7a}$$ $$\boldsymbol{y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z} + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.7b}$$

This results in the transformed equations

$$\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{z} = \hat{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{z} + \hat{\mathbf{B}}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.8a}$$ $$\boldsymbol{y} = \hat{\mathbf{C}}\boldsymbol{z} + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.8b}$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$, $\hat{\mathbf{B}} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$, and $\hat{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}$. Note that when the columns of $\mathbf{T}$ are orthonormal, the change of coordinates becomes

$$\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{z} = \mathbf{T}^*\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z} + \mathbf{T}^*\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.9a}$$ $$\boldsymbol{y} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{z} + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{u}\tag{12.9b}$$

### Gramians and Coordinate Transformations

The controllability and observability Gramians each establish an inner product on state-space in terms of how controllable or observable a given state is, respectively. As such, Gramians depend on the particular choice of coordinate system and will transform under a change of coordinates. In the coordinate system $z$ given by (12.5), the controllability Gramian becomes

```
\hat{\mathbf{W}}_c = \int_0^{\infty} e^{\hat{A}\tau} \hat{B}\hat{B}^* e^{\hat{A}^*\tau} d\tau, \tag{12.10a}
= \int_0^{\infty} e^{\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\tau} \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{T}^{-*} e^{\mathbf{T}^*\mathbf{A}^*\mathbf{T}^{-*}\tau} d\tau, \tag{12.10b}
= \int_0^{\infty} \mathbf{T}^{-1} e^{\mathbf{A}\tau} \mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^*\mathbf{T}^{-*}\mathbf{T}^* e^{\mathbf{A}^*\tau} \mathbf{T}^{-*} d\tau, \tag{12.10c}
= \mathbf{T}^{-1}\left(\int_0^{\infty} e^{\mathbf{A}\tau}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^* e^{\mathbf{A}^*\tau} d\tau\right)\mathbf{T}^{-*}, \tag{12.10d}
= \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_c\mathbf{T}^{-*}. \tag{12.10e}
```

Note that here we introduce $\mathbf{T}^{-*} := (\mathbf{T}^{-1})^* = (\mathbf{T}^*)^{-1}$. The observability Gramian transforms similarly

```
\hat{\mathbf{W}}_o = \mathbf{T}^* \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{T}, \tag{12.11}
```

which is an exercise for the reader. Both Gramians transform as tensors (i.e., in terms of the transform matrix $\mathbf{T}$ and its transpose, rather than $\mathbf{T}$ and its inverse), which is consistent with them inducing an inner product on state-space.

### Simple Rescaling

This example, modified from Moore (1981), demonstrates the ability to balance a system through a change of coordinates. Consider the system

```
\frac{d}{dt} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -10 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 10^{-3} \\ 10^{3} \end{bmatrix} u, \tag{12.12a}
y = \begin{bmatrix} 10^{3} & 10^{-3} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.12b}
```

In this example, the first state $x_1$ is barely controllable, while the second state is barely observable. However, under the change of coordinates $z_1 = 10^3 x_1$ and $z_2 = 10^{-3} x_2$, the system becomes balanced:

```
\frac{d}{dt} \begin{bmatrix} z_1 \\ z_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -10 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} z_1 \\ z_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} u, \tag{12.13a}
y = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} z_1 \\ z_2 \end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.13b}
```

In this example, the coordinate change simply rescales the state $\mathbf{x}$. For instance, it may be that the first state had units of millimeters while the second state had units of kilometers. Writing both states in meters balances the dynamics; that is, the controllability and observability Gramians are equal and diagonal.

## 12.1.3 Balancing Transformations

Now we are ready to derive the balancing coordinate transformation T that makes the controllability and observability Gramians equal and diagonal:

W_hat_c = W_hat_o = Sigma. (12.14)

First, consider the product of the Gramians from (12.10) and (12.11):

W_hat_c W_hat_o = T^{-1} W_c W_o T. (12.15)

Plugging in the desired W_hat_c = W_hat_o = Sigma yields

T^{-1} W_c W_o T = Sigma^2  ==>  W_c W_o T = T Sigma^2. (12.16)

The latter expression in (12.16) is the equation for the eigendecomposition of W_c W_o, the product of the Gramians in the original coordinates. Thus, the balancing transformation T is related to the eigendecomposition of W_c W_o. Equation 12.16 is valid for any scaling of the eigenvectors, and the correct rescaling must be chosen to exactly balance the Gramians. In other words, there are many such transformations T that make the product W_hat_c W_hat_o = Sigma^2, but where the individual Gramians are not equal (for example diagonal Gramians W_hat_c = Sigma_c and W_hat_o = Sigma_o will satisfy (12.16) if Sigma_c Sigma_o = Sigma^2).

Below, we will introduce the matrix S = T^{-1} to simplify notation.

### Scaling Eigenvectors for the Balancing Transformation

To find the correct scaling of eigenvectors to make W_hat_c = W_hat_o = Sigma, first consider the simplified case of balancing the first diagonal element of Sigma. Let xi_u denote the unscaled first column of T, and let eta_u denote the unscaled first row of S = T^{-1}. Then

eta_u W_c eta_u* = sigma_c. (12.17a)

xi_u* W_o xi_u = sigma_o. (12.17b)

The first element of the diagonalized controllability Gramian is thus sigma_c, while the first element of the diagonalized observability Gramian is sigma_o. If we scale the eigenvector xi_u by sigma_s, then the inverse eigenvector eta_u is scaled by sigma_s^{-1}. Transforming via the new scaled eigenvectors xi_s = sigma_s xi_u and eta_s = sigma_s^{-1} eta_u, yields

eta_s W_c eta_s* = sigma_s^{-2} sigma_c. (12.18a)

xi_s* W_o xi_s = sigma_s^2 sigma_o. (12.18b)

Thus, for the two Gramians to be equal,

sigma_s^{-2} sigma_c = sigma_s^2 sigma_o  ==>  sigma_s = (sigma_c / sigma_o)^{1/4}. (12.19)
