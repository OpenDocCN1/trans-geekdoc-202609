## 4. Discussion

Identifying influential genes and relevant drugs contributes to a greater understanding of the molecular mechanism of LP as well as of disease treatment. As a result, we presented a computational framework working as follows: first, we introduced three sparse optimization problems pertaining to SVM, named ssvm1, ssvm2, and ssvm3. We provide each gene expression dataset as input to our methods, solved using CVXR solver in R. The output of each method was a weight vector w, corresponding to the importance of each gene in the provided dataset. Genes associated with higher weights indicated the importance of these genes. We ordered and then selected the top 100 genes according to the top 100 weights in w. Then, we provided output genes via each method to Enrichr and Metascape to perform enrichment analysis. With Enrichr, our methods (ssvm1, ssvm2, and ssvm3) achieved the best results as compared to baseline methods, and the retrieved terms had a greater number of expressed genes in disease terms, which included LP, autoimmune diseases, dermatologic disorders, and skin diseases. In terms of results retrieved via enrichment analysis tools, the ranking score indicates the order of the retrieved terms. The lower the value, the better the ranking is when the term is related to the studied experiment. The overlap indicates the number of common genes between uploaded genes and retrieved terms. For a given term Z, a value in the form of X/Y denotes that X is the number of common genes between the uploaded genes to Enrichr and the term Z, where Y denotes the number of genes related to term Z. P-values and adjusted p-values are related to the significance of retrieved terms with respect to the uploaded genes, where the former is calculated from Fisher exact test and the latter is corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg method (Xie et al., 2021).

In the “Sparse Models Explanation” section, we demonstrate important information pertaining to studied sparse models, including ssvm1, ssvm2, ssvm3, and lasso. Specifically, Figs. 14, 16, and 18 demonstrate the statistical significance of induced models, which are expected to acquire a good generalization when discriminating between the two classes of healthy controls and LP. However, Figs. 15, 17, and 19 explored induced sparsity, in which genes of weak discriminative power were set to zero. It can be observed that the additional constraint $\|W\|_1 \le \lambda$ enforced sparsity. Our models (i.e., ssvm1, ssvm2, and ssvm3) are based on SVM and therefore rely on the margin theory, in which the aim is to maximize the margin ($\frac{2}{\|W\|}$), which is equivalent to minimizing the margin ($\frac{\|W\|^2}{2}$) subject to additional constraints, as shown in Eqs. (1)–(4). The weight vector w provides importance of features and is derived based on the margin notion. However, lasso depends on linear models, including L1 penalty, to shrink coefficients to zero. According to our results in Section 3.2, results derived from our models relying on margin theory are better than those depending on linear models such as lasso (Ahn et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022b; Feng et al., 2019).

We set parameters for the four methods as follows. ssvm1(c = 2, k = 1, $\lambda$ = 0.05), ssvm2(c = 2, k = 1, $\lambda$ = 0.05, r = 0.1), ssvm3(c = 2, k = 2, $\lambda$ = 0.05, r = 0.1), lasso($\lambda$ = 0.05). As the output of each of our methods was 100, the probability for other results coinciding with ours in the three datasets are ($\frac{1545}{\binom{100}{100}}$, $\frac{1916}{\binom{100}{100}}$, and $\frac{1355}{\binom{100}{100}}$, respectively; $\binom{n}{k}$ denotes the binomial coefficient, where n is the number of genes in the studied dataset and k refers to the number of genes selected by our method. Therefore, it is very unlikely that the results of other methods coincide with ours.
