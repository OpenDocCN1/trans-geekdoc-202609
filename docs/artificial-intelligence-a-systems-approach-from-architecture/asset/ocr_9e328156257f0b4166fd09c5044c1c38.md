In the INCODE handbook, the editors present important dates of the origins of systems engineering as a discipline (see table 2.1 and page 12 in [9]). For example, the Semi-Automatic Ground Environment (SAGE) was an air defense system developed at the MIT Lincoln Laboratory with input data from distributed radar sensors culminating in real-time insights provided to human operators. Its development in the 1950s exemplified one-of-a-kind demonstration of a full systems engineering marvel, including many innovations in technologies and processes [13]. The architecture, design, development, and implementation of SAGE were led by Jay Forrester, a pioneering American computer engineer and scientists who invented the random access magnetic-core memory. It was emphasized in the important dates of the SE when given by INCODE, Jay Forrester also developed a systems dynamics modeling language and is considered the founder of the systems dynamics field as a result of his contributions [14–16]. In the celebration of the fiftieth anniversary of the systems dynamics field, John Sternman’s quote is as true as it is today when seen through the lens of AI-engineered systems: “Today, more than ever, humanity faces threats arising from mismanagement of increasingly complex systems” [16]. Other notable events and dates highlighted in the INCODE handbook are the formation of the National Council on Systems Engineering in 1990, which then evolved in 1995 into INCODE to incorporate a wider inclusion of an international community.

With this historical perspective as background, we can now present current systems engineering definitions. Here, we repeat a set of definitions to give the reader a view of what is commonly emphasized in the developing and deployment of systems. NASA defines “systems engineering” as follows [3]:

> A methodical, multi-disciplinary approach for the design, realization, technical management, operations, and retirement of a system. A “system” is the combination of elements that function together to produce the capability required to meet a need. The elements include all hardware, software, equipment, facilities, personnel, processes, and procedures needed for this purpose; that is, all things required to produce system-level results.
