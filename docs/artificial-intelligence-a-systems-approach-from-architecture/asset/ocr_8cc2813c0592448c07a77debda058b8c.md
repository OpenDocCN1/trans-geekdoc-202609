Fundamentals of Systems Engineering

If America wants to put a man on the moon, which is really a tough engineering job, they just gather enough thousands of scientists, pour in the money, and the man will get there. He may even get back.

> —Vannevar Bush, former dean, professor, and presidential advisor

During the twentieth century, there were many revolutionary projects that were accomplished through the great scientific and engineering contributions of academic scientists and engineers. There were also visionaries, such as Vannevar Bush, a former dean and professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) as an adviser to US presidents Franklin D. Roosevelt and Harry S. Truman, who emphasized the importance of scientific research enabling discoveries that were then transformed into actual systems [1]. Bush was a pioneer in the early development of analog versions of technologies with relevance to artificial intelligence (AI). For example, he articulated the need for a personal assistant supplementing a human memory, which he called "Memex." He envisioned Memex as follows: "A device in which an individual stores all his books, records, and communications, and is mechanized so that it may be consulted with exceeding speed and flexibility" [2]. In addition to being the voice behind the creation of the National Science Foundation in the US, Bush was the chair of the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics, which became the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). We chronicle this history, as well as the importance of having scholars with great vision such as Bush, because scientific investments are crucial as a precursor to bringing complex AI capabilities into the market while adhering to the systems engineering discipline.

NASA has a long history of demonstrating the use of rigorous systems engineering principles, as embodied in its comprehensive NASA Systems Engineering Handbook [3].
