## 6.5 Quantitative and Qualitative Performance Metrics

The HMT stage is one of the most critical building blocks of the end-to-end AI system architecture shown in figure 1.3 in chapter 1. We can have suboptimal ML algorithms and still achieve acceptable insights to stakeholders especially if humans are responsible for the final courses of action. Conversely, we can have the best data and the best-performing ML algorithms, but if HMT does not lead to useful (and practical) insights for the user, the overall AI capability is useless.

Much effort has been devoted to achieving the best ML performance by looking at the AI system holistically (i.e., from an end-to-end systems engineering viewpoint). In this section, we take a hierarchical approach to assessing HMT as an integral element of end-to-end AI system. The hierarchical performance assessment is divided into four tiers. At each tier, AI developers must ask the following:

- Tier 1—Confidence: What is the confidence level created at the output of the ML stage?
- Tier 2—Consequence: What is the consequence of actions relative to the confidence in the machine making the decision?
- Tier 3—Representation: Do human-machine models accurately represent real-world application tasks undertaken as teammates?
- Tier 4—Responsible AI: Are the insights created at the output of HMT stage conforming with FASTEPS principles?

This multitier approach forms a critical step in integration-verification-validation in the systems engineering framework discussed in chapter 2 and repeated in figure 6.10 (adapted from Amazon's white paper [46]). HMT must be addressed as one important building block of the overall AI development and deployment cycle. The quantitative and qualitative performance metrics also inform other critical system attributes, such as risk management, cost optimization, reliability and security, all of which lead to achieving operational excellence.

It is for reasons that this book's emphasis is on systems engineering approach when developing and deploying AI products and services. In complex systems, there is always a trade-off among system attributes shown in the outer perimeter of figure 6.10. AI practitioners and researchers must keep this in mind when the goal is to successfully deploy AI capabilities in operations.

Performance assessment at levels 1 and 2 (i.e., confidence versus consequence) are necessary to determine the proper operating point in the HMT spectrum shown in figure 6.2. As discussed earlier, if the confidence level is low and the consequence of action is high, the decision is best left to the human. In contrast, if the confidence is high and the consequence is low, the decision can be fully exercised by the machine (autonomously). Many of today’s applications fall between these two ends.

Common quantitative performance metrics for assessing confidence levels (i.e., at tier 1) in practice are measures of performance (MoPs) discussed in chapter 4, as accuracy, precision-recall (F1-score), confusion matrix, receiver operating characteristics (ROCs), and AUC. These metrics can then be aggregated into a measure of effectiveness (MoE) to make a determination of where the AI system should operate with respect to the HMT spectrum. In chapter 10, we elaborate in more detail on system performance tests, benchmarks, and metrics.

These performance metrics are implemented iteratively as shown in figure 6.10. In this process, AI designers and developers must also take into account counterfactuals. Counterfactuals in AI systems are what-if scenarios. If changes in the assumed scenarios or data inputs occur, how would the output of the system respond [47]?

For assessing AI decisions, another approach that is beginning to be applied is the use of Shapley values to help in explaining the impact of input features on the output of an AI system. Shapley values had their origins in the game theory context, in terms of the players' influence on a game's outcome [48]. In the context of ML, the game is the output of the ML model relative to the players (i.e., the players) and their respective influence on the output [49–51]. These approaches serve to determine output sensitivity to data inputs and ML parameters, agnostic to ML models.

There are other subjective metrics used in assessing HMT performance that are also useful for both tiers 1 and 2 (again, consequence). One of these metrics is the Likert scale, adapted from a 1932 paper on measuring attitudes [52]. The Likert scale can be subjective since it is based on a set of questions requiring users to provide an assessment, and these questions are formulated from a set of AI system attributes (e.g., measured on a 5-point range). The Likert scale can give a quick visual of users' acceptance, satisfaction/dissatisfaction with the AI capability, and improvements in efficiency, usefulness, and system modularity. There are several commonly used to depict the Likert scale results, offering a quick picturesque picture [53].

The next tier of the four-tier hierarchical performance assessment represents representation. The frame of reference that we use here is inspired by a comprehensive report describing the challenges, opportunities, and future directions from a workshop called “Future Directions in Human Teaming,” led and attended by several distinguished scholars [54]. They formulated the HMT framework illustrated in figure 6.11. As pointed out in their report, the majority of today's intelligent machines are tools, not teammates. For intelligent machines to be teammates, there will need joint perception, effective communication, and human-machine models, especially in real-world and unstructured environments. In the context of HMT performance at tier 3, we need to evaluate the interplay of humans and machines, using the model-based framework shown in 6.11. In an excellent report titled, *20-Year Community Roadmap for Artificial Intelligence Research in the U.S.*, sponsored by the Computing Consortium and the Association for the Advancement of AI (AAAI), Gil and Selman recommended research on meaningful interactions between humans and machines to achieve societal benefits [55].

There is ongoing and very interesting human-machine (robot) research focusing on building mental models that the human knows when and not to rely on intelligent agents (i.e., a human finds an accurate mental model of AI strengths and weaknesses) [56]. Another exciting area of research is in the human teaching the robot and versa, and the associated lessons, to strengthen the human-machine models, especially in real-world environments [57, 58]. 

However, performance metrics for these research areas are still evolving. One could, for example, assess the effectiveness in meeting the goals and objectives of a task or set of tasks. Some of these metrics include:

- Quantitative assessment of confidence level versus consequence of action (as per tiers 1 and 2)
- Time to reach a trustworthy decision by the human operating alone versus the improvement (or lack of) by human-machines as teammates
- Adaptability to real-world scenarios
- Counterfactual analysis of sensitivity of human-machine interactions to data inputs
- Compliance with the FASTEPS principles as the tier 4 metric under the rubric of responsible AI (RAI), discussed further in chapter 8
