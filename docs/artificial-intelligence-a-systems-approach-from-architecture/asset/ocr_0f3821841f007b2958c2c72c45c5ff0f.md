There are additional formats in the IEEE-754 floating-point standard, including higher precision, such as double-precision with 64 bits (fp64). Here, we discuss only two examples, fp32 and fp16, to illustrate the main differences and to simplify the discussion.

For the largest positive value s=0; e=11110₂(converted to decimal=30); bias=15; fraction f=11111111, (converted to decimal= 2⁻⁴+2⁻²+2⁻³...+2⁻¹⁰), resulting in the largest positive normal value= 2¹⁵ × (1+1023/1024)=65.504. The larger the number of bits, the higher the range of numerical values and precision achievable, but at the expense of more complex hardware requiring more power to perform the add and multiply operations, as well as the need for increased memory storage. Modern computing hardware designers recognized the importance of mixed precision that, during model training, the computation is done at high precision. Contrast, for inference, the computation can be done with a smaller number of bits, resulting in less power consumption and reduced storage requirements.

There is ongoing research to find ways to improve power efficiencies without a significant sacrifice in model accuracy relative to the IEEE-754 standard format [43, 44]. Also, Zhao and colleagues have demonstrated the use of a logarithmic system in training and weight updates at lower precision [45]. The benefit of the logarithm approach is that multiplications are performed through logarithmic additions, and add operations can be efficiently implemented via lookup tables. Zhao et al. demonstrated a reduction in energy consumption of over 90 percent on several well-known computer vision and NLP tasks, compared to high-precision floating-point techniques.
