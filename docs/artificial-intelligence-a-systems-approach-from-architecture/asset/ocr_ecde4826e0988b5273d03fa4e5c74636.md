As discussed in preceding chapters and illustrated in figure 6.10, in the AI system architecture implementation framework, every building block must undergo verification and validation (V&V) during subsystem integration. However, prior to deployment, AI designers, developers, and implementers must determine if the AI capability is within the guardians of a robust AI and RAI system. This process is part of a systems engineering approach, as emphasized in chapter 2.

In the context of a systems engineering approach, it is critical that AI practitioners understand the sense of vulnerability across the end-to-end architecture. Figure 7.2 illustrates the stages of the ends-to-end AI architecture that are vulnerable to attacks and the type of effects across the architecture.

Although the following discussion focuses on malicious attacks, it is important to clarify that some vulnerabilities of an AI system can be caused by unintentional errors during the design, development, or use of products and services. For example, the human-machine teaming (HMT) stage, humans can make erroneous decisions while analyzing ML results. Therefore, it is necessary, but not sufficient, to evaluate the system output within the envelope of ML metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and others). AI practitioners must also determine the level of confidence, or trust, that the stakeholders can reliably rely on the system, especially in high-stakes decision support. Daniel Kahnem et point out that humans can introduce unreliable outputs due to errors in judgment resulting from, for example, preconceived notions and biases [7].

Let us return to figure 7.2 and explain the actions (attacks/vulnerabilities) and effects at each of the building block stages. Starting with inputs to the AI, there are vulnerabilities caused by data poisoning attacks. Poisoning attacks are described in more detail in section 7.2. Now, it is sufficient to highlight a number of examples of poisoning attacks, such as physically altering a stop sign to cause the output to a speed limit; polluting Twitter and Facebook with unreliable data, causing misinformation to be released; or posting wrong content in Facebook posts. The effects at the conditioning stage are to cause the designer to act on noisy, missing, or incomplete data, affecting the ML model during training or testing, and at the time of deployment.

Similarly, at the ML stage, attacks can be in the form of evasion attacks or blackbox attacks. In the former, the malicious attacker generates inputs that cause model errors. In the latter, the adversary re-creates the algorithm model by probing the output stimulated from different inputs. The likely effects can be in the form of prediction or classification errors, reducing confidence in the output.

The AI hardware and software are also vulnerable to nefarious attacks. For example, the adversary could alter the model parameters, the stored data, or the software code.
