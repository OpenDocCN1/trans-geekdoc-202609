Fortunately, there is an emerging concept referred to as "digital twins", which can now be employed in the development and deployment of complex AI systems. Digital twins are close to (or in some cases indistinguishable from) digital counterparts of physical systems or operational systems. A digital twin infrastructure can facilitate a test harness for implementing performance metrics and benchmarks—in some cases, the digital twin infrastructure already has those tools available.

One of the best definitions we have seen about digital twins is from c.ai [30], named a leader among AI and ML platforms for Forrester Leader 2022 [31].

Digital Twins: Digital twins are virtual replicas of physical devices—assets, systems, or processes—that data scientists and IT personnel use to run simulations. Digital twins are designed to detect and prevent problems, predict performance, and optimize processes through real-time analytics to deliver business value. C3 AI Digital Twin, in unified virtual mode, allows customers to gain critical business insights through simulations of their processes, assets, or systems.

Digital twins are used with a digital twin, can look at how an AI capability will function within a virtual environment with simulated sensors, physical sensors, or a hybrid of two across the full AI pipeline. The advent of IoT (making many physical and inexpensive sensors available), high-speed interconnects like 5G/6G, advances in virtual/augmented reality, high-speed computation, and other technologies allow AI practitioners to validate the performance of an overall AI system [32]. Upper management and leaders, using a digital twin infrastructure, can also address opportunities and business value as part of their strategic plan.

However, an AI platform, with a digital twin infrastructure, requires AI talent and expertise to assess the system’s end-to-end capability. AI developers and implementers, working closely with the ultimate stakeholders, can do an in-depth analysis if problems and errors are identified. As we discussed in chapter 3, there are benchmark tools, such as Dawnbench, Dynabench, and MLPerf, which are useful in benchmarking ML algorithms using representative data sets [33–35].

The need for ML rigor continues to be an area of emphasis among researchers in the commercial sector and academia. At the International Conference on Learning Representations 2022, a workshop was held to address evaluation and benchmark standards [36, 37].
