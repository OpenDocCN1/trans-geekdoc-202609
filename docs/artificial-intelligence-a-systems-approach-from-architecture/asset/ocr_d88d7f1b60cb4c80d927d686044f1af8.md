However, although necessary, these three focus areas are not sufficient given recent AI regulations (at national and international levels), such as the Global Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) put forward by the European Union (EU) [36, 37], a likely future US Bill of Rights, and the White House guidance for the regulation of AI applications [38]. AI designers, developers, policymakers, and anyone else responsible for decisions made by AI systems must also clarify the level of risk, balancing confidence in the machine versus consequence of actions. The high-dimensional space and complexity of data used in training DNNs will continue to present a challenge to fully understanding a neural network system. AI organizations must be diligent about transparency and take responsibility for explaining the decisions made by AI systems. There is an increased call for the right to an explanation, especially in circumstances when the AI system is used in high-stakes applications (e.g., medicine, transportation, national security, law enforcement, finance, and others).

There is an urgency to attend to a more rigorous explainability of the technical underpin of an AI system. There are also moral and business continuity obligations of AI providers to provide explanations of the risks inherent in AI systems. As we emphasized earlier, our recommendation involves a multiprong approach addressing the people-process-technology triad within the context of a systems engineering methodology.

By incorporating these three elements of the people-process-technology triad, we make sure that AI practitioners do not just focus on a technical solution. We need high light of respective elements:

+   * People: AI businesses must educate and train those responsible for designing, developing, and deploying AI products and services. In high-risk applications, SMEs must be included in the decision cycle. For example, in medicine, doctors should be integral to the final decision (i.e., AI playing the role of augmenting human capabilities).
* Process: AI organizations must establish policies, safe safeguards, and governance consistent with its own internal practices and compliant with government regulations. Just as in the case of cybersecurity, where regulatory agencies and business boards (with fiduciary responsibilities) require that organizations be able to demonstrate their defensive measures, AI businesses must also demonstrate their ability to deploy trustworthy AI.
