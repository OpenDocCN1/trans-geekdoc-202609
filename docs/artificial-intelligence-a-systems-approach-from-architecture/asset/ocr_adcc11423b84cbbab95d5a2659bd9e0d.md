# Chapter 1: Overview

Minsky continued to be an AI pioneer for many decades after the famous 1956 Dartmouth summer research project. He wrote a paper titled “Steps towards Artificial Intelligence,” which established the vision leading to the establishment of the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, where Minsky served as its first director [6]. In 2003, the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory merged with the MIT Laboratory for Computer Science to form the MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL), one of today’s premier research centers in AI [7].

Another research contribution that shaped the future of neural networks was done by Frank Rosenblatt from Cornell University. Rosenblatt [8] formulated the architecture of a perceptron, which embraced the neurons and synapses of a human brain. The perceptron is a simple, one-stage predecessor to what we know now as a multilayer perceptron (e.g., a fully connected neural network). In chapter 4 and the appendix, we will do a deeper dive into an example of a neural network known as “multilayer perceptron (MLP).”

Minsky and wrote Perceptrons: An Introduction to Computational Geometry, which they elaborated on the mathematics of the perceptron architecture as one of the first examples of a machine that could be taught to perform tasks by using training data [9]. Perceptrons are considered an example of models within the rubric of “connectionists.” However, Minsky and Papert felt that a perceptron could not be generalized to solve important problems. In contrast, AI models showed impressive results, leveraging serial representation of symbolic expressions. These later techniques belong under the rubric of “symbolists.” [10]. Later in the book, we will compare and contrast the distinctions among these models.

Even though the early assessments by Minsky and Paper in the later edition of their book [11], they clarified that both connectionist learning (like perceptrons) and symbolic reasoning were important techniques within the scope of machine intelligence. In the 1980s, they predicted that connectionist approaches would flourish (“and we expect the future of network-based learning machines to be rich beyond imagining”).

Another important AI milestone was the demonstration of an intelligent machine that could play the game of checkers against a human [12]. In 1959, Arthur Samuel, at the time working showed that a machine could be programmed to play better than the human who programmed it. Samuel was one of the first AI researchers to introduce the term machine learning (ML). These were the early days of ML, mostly built on rule-based decision trees. Although in comparison to today’s standards, this demonstration provided an initial indication that machines could be built with a capability to learn.
