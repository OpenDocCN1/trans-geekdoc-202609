As we traverse along the CoB construct clockwise, “personal health and wellness,” the next slice, has a domino effect. The most effective performers are able to balance the demands of their technical jobs with their personal priorities. Bill George, a professor of management practice and the former chief executive officer (CEO) of Medtronic, in Harvard Business School classes, points out: “To be equipped for the rapid-fire intensity of executive life, they [executives] cultivate practices that allow them to regularly renew their minds, bodies, and spirits” [26]. In addition, Khazan has developed models to use individuals’ feedback and practice mindfulness.

Of course, the CoB construct is also most important when personnel are trying to balance family with the “rapid-fire” environment of a job, as per George’s comment. The fifth slice of the CoB construct, “Family,” stresses the need to attend to family time among business. Progressive organizations place an emphasis and institute programs to help employees maintain a work-life balance (sometimes referred to as “work-life blending”). The term “work-life blending” is perhaps most appropriate now since personnel must continue to attend to their jobs while being cognizant of family responsibilities—it is a daily blending of the two. We include under family parents, children, siblings, other relatives, or even very close friends who are considered family by an individual.

The last CoB slice is “Career.” All the previous slices are specific areas to pay attention to while balancing the near-term activities. Career must also be part of this balance, but it represents more of a long-term view. Personnel must be supported by the organization in achieving their long-term career goals, consistent with the prior slices of the CoB construct. Every individual should set targets based on directional and aspirational goals. Each approach helps an individual to see “North Star” in his or her career.

As technical staff navigate their respective career challenges, there is always an element of fear of belonging, or ever failing to do so. Staff can fall into the trap of trying too hard to prove their competences, at the expense of misbalancing (e.g., neglecting) other aspects of the CoB construct. We want to put at ease that such feelings are normal, especially in someone’s career. This phenomenon has been studied under the label “imposter syndrome.” Tewfik has performed an extensive study on this topic, rebalancing the negative connotation associated with “imposter syndrome” against some of the benefits [28]. An example of a benefit is early-career staff doing an assessment of their tasks in order to make sure that they are delivering what is expected of them, while working closely with management to get feedback.
