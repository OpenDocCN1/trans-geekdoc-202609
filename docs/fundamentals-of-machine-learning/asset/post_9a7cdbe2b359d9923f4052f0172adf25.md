
![](img/2087e3fd78fc2bbc9ee0bfaab66e3bde_230_0.png)

**Fig. 10.9** Illustration of an actor that is implemented with a deep neural network.

```
$x = \{s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, ..., s_T, a_T\}.
```

Since the policy and the transitions are also probabilistic, we can write the probability if a trajectory $p(x) = p(s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, ..., s_T, a_T)$ as

```
$p(x|\mathbf{w}) = p(\mathbf{w}) \prod_{t=0}^{T} \pi(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t)\pi(a_t|s_t; \mathbf{w}).$
```

We have indicated here that the probability depends on the parameters of the model as we want to learn. In the stochastic case, the reward is stochastic and we need to sample over trajectories. We use here the finite horizon case we do not need discounting, and the objective function can simply be written as the average value of the reward repeated episodes.

**Actor value function:** $J(\mathbf{w}) = E[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \rho(s_t, \pi(a_t|s_t; \mathbf{w}))]\sim p(x|\mathbf{w}).$

This is expected value that we need to sample to find an approximation of this value

```
$J(\mathbf{w}) \approx \frac{1}{N_x} \sum_{x=1}^{T} N_x \rho(s_t, \pi(a_t|s_t; \mathbf{w}))$.
```

where $N_x$ is the number of trials in the sample. To find the parameters that maximize this objective we follow a gradient, as usual. Then, we have to find the gradient of the expectation value over trajectories,

```
$\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J = \int_x \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} p(x|\mathbf{w})\rho(x)dx$.
```

Since $p(x|\mathbf{w})$ is the product of several terms, it is useful to consider the logarithm of this term

$\nabla_w \log p(x|w) = \frac{1}{p(x|w)}\nabla_w p(x|w), \quad (10.35)$

so that we can replace the gradient term in Eq. 10.37.

$\nabla_w J = \int_x p(x|w)\nabla_w \log p(x|w)\rho(x)dx. \quad (10.36)$

If we expand the log-probability of a trajectory

$\nabla_w \log p(x|w) = \nabla_w \log(s_0) + \nabla_w \log p(a|s;w) + \nabla_w \log(s'|s,a) \quad (10.37)$

and the probability of the first state and the transition probability do not depend the model parameters, $\nabla_w \log(s_0) = 0$ and $\nabla_w \log(s'|s,a) = 0$, so that we do not need a model to evaluate the gradient. Also we can now write the gradient of loss function as an expected value,

$\nabla_w J = E[\nabla_w \log p(a|s;w)]_{x\sim\pi(a|s)} \quad (10.38)$

This nice result for model-free learning of the actor is called the policy gradient theorem. With this we see that we can estimate the gradient by sampling the log-probabilities of policies and multiplying this with the sample rewards of the trajectories,

$\nabla_w J \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{x=1}^N \sum_{t=0}^T \log p(a_t|s_t;w)|\sum_{t=0}^T \rho(s_t, \pi(a_t|s_t;w)). \quad (10.39)$

This concludes the principle of introducing an actor critic. This algorithm is called

REINFORCE: $w_{t+1} \leftarrow w_t - \alpha(\nabla_w \log(\pi)R), \quad (10.40)$

where $R$ stands for the accumulated reward of a trajectory. Unfortunately, in practice it turns out that the samples for the gradient of the log-policies have very high variance so that making variance smaller is to take the change of $R$ with each sample from the average of some batch or samples $R$ into account. This **baseline** version is given by

REINFORCE with baseline: $w_{t+1} \leftarrow w_t - \alpha(\nabla_w \log(\pi)(R-\bar{R})), \quad (10.41)$

and have there been of other tricks introduced in the literature. However, an important other method is introduced in the next section.

## 10.5.2 Actor-critic schemes

It is now easy to introduce an important architecture for reinforcement learning, that of the **actor-critic** architecture. The principle idea is to replace the estimate of the accumulated reward of a trajectory with a better estimate of the visited states. We have discussed the estimation of the Q-function at length in the previous sections and we can now combine the two approaches. We simply replace $R$ with $Q$ in the REINFORCE algorithm

Actor-critic: $w_{t+1} \leftarrow w_t - \alpha(\nabla_w \log(\pi))Q$

The Q-function itself be learned with a temporal difference method,

$w_{t+1} \leftarrow w_t - \alpha(r_t - V(s_{t+1}))\nabla_w \log(\pi)$.

Such actor-critic architectures are now the common implementations of reinforcement learning with function approximation. Implementations of many recent deep RL algorithms are by Mathias Plappert <https://github.com/keras-rl/keras-rl>.
As suggested, training a neural network while exploiting is dangerous and leads to oscillations and instabilities. One reason is that when Q-values are close to each other, then small changes in the Q-values can lead to drastic changes in response that can cause problems and inconsistencies in learning. In this respect, actor-based reinforcement learning seems to have some advantages, but building a proper parameterization of actions has shown to build much robust systems. The basic scheme is illustrated in Fig. 10 on the left, and in the context of using neural networks on the right. Another implementation is DDAC (deterministic actor-critic) as shown in Fig. 10.12.

## 10.6 Reinforcement learning in the brain

Throughout this book we briefly mentioned some relations of some aspects of machine learning and the brain. This included the basic filters of Gabor-like functions in the visual system, some ability of human of leveraging Bayesian decision-making in taking priors into the layered structure of neural networks, and the implementation of associative memory with recurrent networks. While these are important components to consider in neuroscience, one of the most amazing stories of the interaction between reinforcement learning, behavioural cognitive science, and neuroscience is related to reinforcement learning. This brief section presents a brief outlook into this fascinating area.

![](img/2087e3fd78fc2bbc9ee0bfaab66e3bde_233_0.png)

**Fig. 11** Deep Deterministic Actor-Critic (DDAC) network (from Lillicap, 2015).

We started this chapter by acknowledging classical animal learning experiments of conditioning. One of the most famous of these is the experiment by Ivan Pavlov at the turn of the 20th century showing a conditioned flex of salvation in dogs following tones. Such conditioning behaviour was modeled by Robert A. Rescorla and Allan R. Wagner in 1972. The base form of the Rescorla-Wagner model has a form of

> $$\Delta V = \alpha(\lambda - V)$$

where what is described here is the associative strength between a stimulus and reward prediction: $\alpha$ describes the salience of a stimulus, $\beta$ is some learning rate. The most interesting part is the expression in brackets that represents the difference between the real reward and expectation of reward. This model is a form of temporal difference model. Reinforcing learning to models of behavioral conditioning is already an interesting step. However, an even bigger step was made when Wolfram Schulz, Peter Dayan, and Montague related the neural signals found by Wolfram Schulz in dopaminergic neurons in specific areas of the midbrain.

![](img/2087e3fd78fc2bbc9ee0bfaab66e3bde_234_0.png)

**Fig. 10.12** Recordings of the activity of Dopaminergic neurons in the basal ganglia. (from Schulz, Dayan, Monteu, 1997).

The upper panel in Fig. 10.12 shows that these neurons become active if an unexpected reward is given. The lower example shows the activation of the same neurons when a predictor of a reward is given later in the trial. The experiment demonstrates the backpropagation of the reward prediction, and the experiments have been described successfully with TD learning. The midbrain collection of nuclei called the basal ganglia has since been implicated in habitual decision-making that is akin to the model-free reinforcement learning described by the TD mechanisms.
However, human have not only a habitual decision system but we are also commonly able to initiate deliberative goal directed behavior. Such model-based decision-making must hence also be present in the brain.
