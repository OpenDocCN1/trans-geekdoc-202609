# # 6 Basic probability theory

Probably the biggest challenge in using data to build models is the nature of uncertainty. This includes the limited knowledge about the world, such as relying on noisy or unreliable sensors. Even knowing if a sample sets is appropriate to cover the dependencies we want to uncover some uncertainty in machine learning. The mathematical description of uncertainty is covered in probability theory, and it should not be a surprise that this language with a refresher in probability theory has helped enormously to formalize the areas of machine learning for a deeper understanding.

This chapter provides a refresher in probability theory, in particular with respect to the formulations that build the theoretical language of modern machine learning. Probability theory is the formalization of random numbers, and we outline what these are and how they are characterized by probability density or probability mass functions. We discuss how functions have traditionally been characterized and provide a review of how to work with such mathematical objects as transforming density functions and how to measure differences between density function. We then review and basic operations with multiple random variables, including the Bayes law, and with an outline of some important approximation techniques of so-called Monte Carlo methods.

We are mainly interested in the language of probability theory rather than statistics that we take as meaning methods for hypothesis testing and related procedures. While machine learning methods can be viewed as a range of advanced statistical methods, our concern here is to learn about the language and tools of probability theory as means for developing machine learning methods.

# ## 6.1 Random numbers and their probability (density) function

The main instrument for describing uncertainty hence the subject of probability theory is random numbers and their associated variables. While a regular number has only one specific value, a random number will have different values every time we "look" at it (a draw from the distributions). For example, let's think about some data that we acquired using a light sensor. We might think that an ideal light sensor will give us only one reading holding it to a specific surface. However, the characteristics of the electronic circuit might change by changing temperatures or fatigue in the sensor, or since we may move the sensor unintentionally away from the surface. It is hence likely that we get different readings over time. While some people might argue that such causes are mainly unknown (latent) factors, it is of no importance to why we have uncertainty but that we have uncertainty.
