Machine learning is the analytic engine in areas such as data science, big data analytics, and to some extent, to science in the general sense of building quantitative models.

Machine learning has a long history with traces far back in time (see Fig. 1.2). The first genuine public recognition and accompanying widespread excitement among scientists after learning machines came in the late 1950s and early 1960s with when Arthur Samuel's self-learning checkers devised a program with reinforcement learning that ultimately learned to outperform its creator. Richard Bellman invented much of the mathematical foundation of reinforcement learning. One of the first general learning machines that are now considered to be neural networks invented by Karl Steinenberg. Frank Rosenblatt invented the systematic foundation of neural networks and started to build neural network computers by Charles Wightman, as the Mark I Perceptron. Neural networks were popularized in the 1980s, influenced by David Rumelhart and Geoffrey Hinton, and Terry Sejnowski their connection to the brain. Of course, many inspiring researchers such as Yoshua Bengio, Yann LeCun, and Jürgen Schmidhuber, to make a name.

We are now in an era of "deep learning", with recent developments that are responsible for the popularity of machine learning. This has a great deal to do with the availability of appropriate data and the availability of faster computers, also to smart techniques that make it possible to scale models to much larger domains. A great example of the recent progress in deep reinforcement learning is the ability of a computer to play video games. Video games from the Atari platform have become a useful paradigm for the new set of benchmarks that go beyond the classical data sets for machine learning from the University of California Irvine UCI machine learning repository that have dominated the benchmarks in the past. Atari games are simplified worlds while still presenting more learning in environments that humans have to figure out. In these benchmarks only visual input is given, up to the computer frames of the video game, and feedback is only provided with how well the player performed in the game.

While these examples have been widely popularized as the new frontier in AI, much of the scientific progress in machine learning is related to its embedding with probabilistic methods and statistical learning theories. Some pioneers in this domain are Vlas Wapnik and Judea Pearl. The development of statistical machine learning and Bayesian networks has influenced the field significantly in the last twenty years, and the domain of Bayesian reasoning is essential for the deeper understanding of machine learning. Some scientists are now working on more general probabilistic programming methods that to some extent go beyond the recent standard in machine learning applications. The aim of this book is to introduce machine learning at a more practical level so that it can be applied immediately by practitioners, at least in its basic form, and to discuss the foundations in more general terms to help practitioners to learn more about the theoretical underpinning of machine learning.
