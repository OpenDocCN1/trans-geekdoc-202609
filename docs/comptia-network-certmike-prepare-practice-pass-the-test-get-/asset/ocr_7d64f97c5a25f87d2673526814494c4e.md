# Fiber-Optic Cables

The primary alternative to copper-based cables for networking is fiber optic. Fiber-optic cables carry signals in the form of optical impulses generated by a laser or light-emitting diode (LED). A fiber-optic cable consists of a glass or plastic core that is the signal conductor, surrounded by layers of reflective cladding, buffering, and a protective shield.
Compared to copper cables, fiber optic is much more efficient. It can span distances, up to 120 kilometers, and is completely resistant to EMI, crosstalk, and many other types of interference. However, fiber-optic cable is also more expensive than UTP and more difficult to install, and requires specialized tools to training and troubleshoot.

There are many different types of fiber-optic cable, but they fall into two main categories, as follows:

+   **Single-mode** Uses a light source to generate a single beam of light. Single-mode fiber can span distances to 120 kilometers and achieve higher speeds than multimode.

+   **Multimodal** Uses an LED light source to generate multiple light impulses travel through the core and off the cladding to propagate the signal. Multimodal fiber is relatively inexpensive compared to single-mode, but it only spans distances up to one kilometer and at lower speeds.

# CONNECTOR TYPES

Network cables require connectors at both ends to plug into computers, patch panels, and other devices. Patch cables are available with connectors already attached, but for permanent cable installations, technicians pull cable to the required locations and attach the connectors to both ends.

# Copper Cable Connectors

The RG-59 and RG-6 coaxial cables used for television installations, cable modem connections, and other video applications use F-type connectors, which are screw-on connectors with the core protruding from the end. The IEEE 802.3 (Ethernet) standards and the+ exam objects no longer include coaxial-based Ethernet specifications, so the Bayonet--Concelman (BNC) and N-type connectors for RG-58 and RG-8 cables are no longer covered.

Most twisted pair use registered (RJ) connectors, which are the standard snap-in modular connectors used by telephones and desktop computers. RJ11 is the six-pin modular jack in most phones, although standard telephone connections use only two of the pins.

Internal LANs constructed with UTP and STP cables typically use the RJ45 connector to attach computers to wall plates and switches to patch panels. The RJ45 connector is slightly larger than the RJ11 and has eight pins to accommodate standard four-pair cabling. Both the RJ11 and RJ45 connectors have a latch to hold them in place in the socket.
