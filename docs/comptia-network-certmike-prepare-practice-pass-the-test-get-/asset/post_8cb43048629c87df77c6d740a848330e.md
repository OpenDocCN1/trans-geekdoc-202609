# Physical Security

# Objective 4.5: Explain the importance of physical security.

**When** the issue of IT security hazards comes up, many network administrators think first of Internet-based cyberattacks and software vulnerabilities. They might forget that an intruder can also walk through the front door and attack the network in person. Physical security is no less important than cybersecurity to an enterprise's overall security posture. This chapter examines some of the physical security mechanisms administrators frequently implement on a typical enterprise network. The Network+ exam expects candidates to be familiar with these basic concepts.

In this chapter, you'll learn everything you need to know Network+ Objective 4.5, including the following topics:

- Detection methods
- Prevention methods
- Asset disposal

# DETECTION METHODS

The two main areas of physical security are the detection methods that allow administrators to monitor vulnerable network equipment and the prevention methods that block unauthorized access to that equipment. Detection devices tell administrators when and how an intrusion has occurred and can sometimes provide evidence of the intruder's identity. Some of the detection methods used to provide a network with physical protection are described in this section.

# Camera

Video surveillance captures the most information of any the standard detection methods, allowing network administrators to see events as they happen and record them for archiving. Unlike many detection methods that rely solely on numerical data, such as access auditing, cameras allow administrators to see that something was not just done, but who did it.

Early video surveillance systems used closed circuit television (CCTV) cameras connected to a central recorder using coaxial cables. These were dedicated systems, separate from any other network. Today, video surveillance cameras typically connect to networks using Ethernet cables or Wi-Fi and save their information as digital video. This arrangement is not only less expensive than CCTV, since it can use existing equipment, but it can also take advantage of the network's own infrastructure. For, Power over Ethernet (PoE) can provide the cameras with electricity over the network cable, and administrators can place the cameras' switchports into their own VLAN, keeping their traffic separate from the rest of the network.

# Motion Detection

Motion detection equipment is described as any device capable of sensing the passage of a human through a specific area. The motion detectors that most people use are small boxes affixed to the wall containing passive infrared (PIR) sensors. Humans generate heat, and heat shows up on infrared, these devices can sense changes in the infrared pattern of a room and generate a signal that triggers an alert or an alarm. There are also devices intended for larger areas that use microwaves instead of infrared

In addition to infrared and microwave sensors, there are many other types of devices that can be considered motion detectors, including pressure pads under carpeting that detects footsteps, electric eye beams that signal an alarm when interrupted, and sound sensors that react to excessive noise. Unlike cameras, motion detectors can tell when someone enters a protected area, but they can't identify who that is.

# Asset Tags

Asset tagging is the process of assigning unique identifiers to company-owned hardware assets to facilitate the tracking of each device, both in its physical location and in its life cycle. Simple put, asset tagging is a solution to the problem of how to identify one particular computer when the company has purchased hundreds of them.

Originally, asset tagging consisted of affixing a unique number to each device and using that on all of its documentation, as trouble tickets and service contracts. The products created to facilitate asset tagging have evolved over the years. The first solution was probably an indelible ink marker, but this soon become impractical. Later, it became possible to purchase sets of adhesive metal plates with serialized asset numbers, which could be placed outside or inside the device's case. Later still, products with barcodes appeared.

Today, asset tagging manufacturers have greatly expanded the functionality of their products by creating tags using radio frequency identification (RFID) chips or other wireless tracking mechanisms. This can enable network administrators to not only identify devices, but actually track their locations within the facility.

# Tamper Detection

Tamper detection is any mechanism that can detect when a particular device, container, or facility has been opened by unauthorized persons. At its simplest, there are detection tapes and stickers that administrators can place on computers or other devices in such a way that when someone opens the device's case, the tape is broken.

Of course, mechanical tamper detection devices like this require someone to see the broken tape, which means regular inspection of network devices. Better than these mechanisms that can generate a signal when they detecting and alert an appropriate administrator. Many servers have switches into their cases that are tripped when someone opens it. The switch is typically connected to the motherboard so that the computer receives the tamper detection signal and can take action on it, such as generating an SNMP trap.

# PREVENTION METHODS

It is important to be able to detect an attack or intrusion when it occurs, but it is far more preferable to prevent those incidents from happening before they occur. While some intrusion methods involve the application of technology or hardware, others are a matter of careful planning and communication, with end users and IT support personnel. This section describes some of the commonly used attack methods for enterprise networks.

# Employee Training

While there are many types of attacks and intrusions that administrators can prevent by applying technology, it is important to remember that end users nearly outnumber IT personnel. They can be the organization's first line of defense, as long as they know what to look for and what to do when a security event occurs. Employee training is the answer to that.

Security training for end users consists largely of things they should look out for that can indicate an incident attack, or even one in progress, such as phishing messages, social engineering contacts, tailating attempts, and other suspicious behaviors. End users should also be informed about security-related policies and the reasons for them, such as mandatory password changes and multifactor authentication.

In the same way, IT personnel—and especially the first-line people—should receive regular training to keep them updated about changing conditions and threats.
