# Camera

Video surveillance captures the most information of any the standard detection methods, allowing network administrators to see events as they happen and record them for archiving. Unlike many detection methods that rely solely on numerical data, such as access auditing, cameras allow administrators to see that something was not just done, but who did it.

Early video surveillance systems used closed circuit television (CCTV) cameras connected to a central recorder using coaxial cables. These were dedicated systems, separate from any other network. Today, video surveillance cameras typically connect to networks using Ethernet cables or Wi-Fi and save their information as digital video. This arrangement is not only less expensive than CCTV, since it can use existing equipment, but it can also take advantage of the network's own infrastructure. For, Power over Ethernet (PoE) can provide the cameras with electricity over the network cable, and administrators can place the cameras' switchports into their own VLAN, keeping their traffic separate from the rest of the network.

# Motion Detection

Motion detection equipment is described as any device capable of sensing the passage of a human through a specific area. The motion detectors that most people use are small boxes affixed to the wall containing passive infrared (PIR) sensors. Humans generate heat, and heat shows up on infrared, these devices can sense changes in the infrared pattern of a room and generate a signal that triggers an alert or an alarm. There are also devices intended for larger areas that use microwaves instead of infrared

In addition to infrared and microwave sensors, there are many other types of devices that can be considered motion detectors, including pressure pads under carpeting that detects footsteps, electric eye beams that signal an alarm when interrupted, and sound sensors that react to excessive noise. Unlike cameras, motion detectors can tell when someone enters a protected area, but they can't identify who that is.

# Asset Tags

Asset tagging is the process of assigning unique identifiers to company-owned hardware assets to facilitate the tracking of each device, both in its physical location and in its life cycle. Simple put, asset tagging is a solution to the problem of how to identify one particular computer when the company has purchased hundreds of them.

Originally, asset tagging consisted of affixing a unique number to each device and using that on all of its documentation, as trouble tickets and service contracts. The products created to facilitate asset tagging have evolved over the years. The first solution was probably an indelible ink marker, but this soon become impractical. Later, it became possible to purchase sets of adhesive metal plates with serialized asset numbers, which could be placed outside or inside the device's case. Later still, products with barcodes appeared.
