# Interference

A source of interference on a wireless network can be anything that affects the successful transmission of a signal. Interference can cause a client device connection to slow down, drop the connection intermittently, or not at all. Assuming that the problem client was functioning properly, a sudden increase in interference can be caused by new equipment installed nearby, such as corded phones or a microwave oven, or intervening walls or furniture that was not before, or any number of sources.

There are two main ways to address excessive interference on a wireless network: either remove the source of the interference or move the problem. Moving the client device away from the source of the interference should work, as moving the client closer to the access point.

# Channel Overlap

As explained in Chapter 12, the channels in the 2.4 GHz band overlap with each other, meaning that a device using one channel can interfere with another device of the adjacent channel. Mathematically, there are only three channels a 2.4 GHz wireless network can use avoid channel overlap entirely: channels 1, 6, and 11. Many administrators configure their devices to only use these channels for reason.

The problem with this strategy is that it only applies to the 2.4 GHz band and only on the 802.11 standards. 802.1g network, for example, the nonoverlapping channels are 5, 9, and 13.

The other issue that affects channel overlap is the ability to bond channels together to create a single, larger channel. Creating wider bonded means that there are fewer channels available. A 5 GHz 802.11ac network, for example, can support the bonding of up to 20 MHz channels, splitting the 5 GHz band into only two 160 MHz channels. Wider channels complicate the overlap situation as Troubleshoot should be conscious of the channel bonding strategy using the wireless network and consider channel overlap as a possible cause of interference.

# Antenna Cable Attenuation/Signal Loss

External antennas are typically connected to wireless access points using coaxial cables, and it is important to minimize the signal loss between the antenna and the WAP. The antenna connection is susceptible to attenuation as any other network medium, especially in the higher frequencies. Choose an antenna cable with low signal loss and install it properly, properly crimped connectors and a bend radius.

# RF Attenuation/Signal Loss

Radio frequency (RF) signals are susceptible to attenuation as any other signals. The farther from the access point a client device is located, the greater the signal loss and the weaker the signals will be. As a result of the weakened signals, the 802.11 standards call for a reduction in the connection. If the signals grow too, the client will be unable to connect to the network at all.
