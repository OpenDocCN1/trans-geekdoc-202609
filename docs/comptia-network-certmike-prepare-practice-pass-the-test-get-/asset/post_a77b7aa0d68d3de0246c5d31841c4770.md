
# CABLE CONSIDERATIONS

While there are standards-based specifications for various network types that administrators should adhere to when installing cables, there are also specific environmental concerns that can dictate the type of cable they use. Sources of interference and installations within air ducts or other air channels are both concerns that can affect the type of cable needed for the installation.

## Shielded and Unshielded

Twisted pair cables are available in shielded and unshielded varieties. Most twisted pair network installations use the unshielded twisted pair (UTP) version, in which individual pairs of wires are twisted together and enclosed in a sheath. The shielded twisted pair (STP) versions—which are all more expensive than UTP—are intended for environments where there is a great deal of electromagnetic or other types of interference presence. This can include locations with industrial equipment or sources of EMI.

Depending on the cable type, the shielding, which can be made of a metal foil or braided wire, can be wrapped around the entire bundle of wires, around the individual wire pairs, or both. In addition to preventing EMI from reaching the copper wires, the shielding is a conductor that can be attached to a ground.

## Plenum and Riser-Rated

Standard UTP cables are sometimes called riser-rated because they are intended to run between floors without traversing an air space. The air circulation spaces inside a building, whether inside of ductwork or not, are called plenums. There are special plenum-rated cables that are designed to run through the air spaces.

The difference between riser-rated and plenum cables is that the plenum variety is encased in a sheath of material that does not emit toxic vapors when it burns. Riser-rated cables have a polyvinyl (PVC) sheath that, if it burns in an air space, produces fumes.

Plenum cables are more expensive than riser-rated ones, and they might be more difficult to install. The plenum cable's fire-retardant sheath is less flexible than that of the standard PVC sheath which means the plenum cable has a larger radius that prevents it from stretching around corners easily.

## CABLE APPLICATION

Most UTP cables are wired "straight through," meaning that each pin in the connector at one end is wired to the same pin at the other. These cables typically take one of two forms. They are either permanently installed cables in the walls or ceilings that connect wall plates to a patch panel in a server closet or data center, or they are patch cords that connect computers and other devices to wall plates and patch panel ports to switches.

- There are, however, other applications for specialized types of cables, as described in this section.

## Rollover Cable/Console Cable

Routers typically have a command-line interface (CLI) for configuring the device, but they do not have screens or keyboards. However, they traditionally have a console port into which an administrator can plug a terminal or laptop and access the CLI. Modern routers can have a variety of interfaces for the console port, but earlier models used the RS-232 serial interface, with a DB-9 D-shell connector. Before USB, this was the interface people used to connect an external medium to their computers. Current routers might have an RJ-45 console port supporting the rollover wiring configuration, a standard Ethernet RJ-45 port, or even a mini-USB port.

A rollover cable, also known as a console cable, is a variation on the standard twisted pair design that eliminates both the twists and the pairs. A rollover cable is typically flat, not round like a UTP cable, and colored a light blue. At one end is a standard RJ-45 connector and at the other is either a DB-9 connector, as shown in Figure 22.1, or another RJ-45 and an EIA/TIA adapter that converts it to a DB-9.

## Crossover Cable

For communication between network devices to occur, the signals a device sends out through the transmit pairs of the cable must arrive at the receive pairs of the destination device. Standard Ethernet cables are wired straight through, so the crossover circuit that connects the transmit to the receive pairs is located in the network switch.

When a user wants to connect two computers directly to each other with an Ethernet cable, however, there is no switch to provide the crossover circuit, the user must connect the devices with a special crossover cable. In a crossover cable, the transmit pairs at each end are wired to the receiver pairs at each end. Crossover cables can also connect two switches or two routers, if needed, but most of the devices made today have some means of toggling the crossover circuit in the device.

## Power over Ethernet

Devices such as video cameras, VoIP telephones, and wireless access points can all benefit from being connected to a cached network. One of the problems with all three of these devices is the need to supply them with power when they are often located in places with no convenient outlets.

Power over Ethernet (PoE) is an IEEE 802.3 standard to weaken by which standard and Ethernet cables carry power, as well as data, to connected devices. As shown in Table 22.1, the original 2003 PoE standard was capable of supplying 15.4 watts of DC power, but the standard has matured to the point at which it now supply up to 71.3 watts.

## COMMON ISSUES

Troubleshooting cable connectivity issues can be complicated by the large number of things that can conceivably go wrong with cables. Some of the common conditions that a troubleshooter might encounter are described in this section.

## Attenuation

Attenuation is the tendency of a signal as it travels through a network medium. This is one of the reasons all possible physical layer specifications for networks have a maximum allowable cable length, which installers should not exceed. If a cable is too long, the signals can degrade excessively. All types of networks are subject to attenuation to different degrees.

As a general rule, signals attenuate faster on copper than on fiber, so fiber-optic cable can run for miles.

If a cable is too long, nothing can be done except to pull a new cable that measure the length of segment. The standard distance limitation for most UTP networks is 100 meters.

## Interference

In networking, the term interference can refer to electromagnetic interference (EMI) or radio frequency interference (RFI), both of which can be disturbing influences on network communication. EMI and RFI are typically caused by electrical or electronic equipment in proximity to the network cables. There are natural sources of interference, as well as solar flares.

Measuring the levels of interference on a cable run be part of the network installation. The only solutions for interference are to move the cable farther apart or replace the cable with shielded twisted pair (STP).

## Decibel (dB) Loss

The strength of a cable is measured in decibels (dB). Decibel loss is a value representing the ratio of a signal's strength to its attenuation at its destination. As noted, all cable types suffer from attenuation to some degree; the decibel loss measurement is a means of quantifying that effect. Decibel values are logarithmic, meaning that the strength decreases exponentially as the decibel level increases. If a 10 dB loss means the strength at the destination is one-tenth of its original strength, then a 20 dB loss means the signal is one-hundredth of its original strength.

## Incorrect Pinout

Inside a twisted pair are eight or more wires, grouped in pairs, with each pair consisting of a signal and a ground wire. At each end of the cable is a connector that plugs into the computer, wall outlet, or patch panel. The connector connects each of the eight wires in the cable to a copper pin, according to one of the pinout defined in the ANSI/TIA-568-D standard, as discussed in Chapter 3, "Cables and Connectors."

Inside a UTP cable, eight pins are connected to the cable's eight wires, and for a proper circuit to be established, each pin at one end of the cable must be connected to its corresponding pin at the other end.

An incorrect pinout, in which the individual wires are mixed up inside the connector, can manifest as a variety of errors, including open circuits, short circuits, and crosstalk.
