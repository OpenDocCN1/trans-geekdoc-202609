
Several defenses against gradient inversion attacks have been proposed. These include perturbing gradients [\[43\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0215), [\[51\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0255) and using transformation for training data that clients can apply on the fly [\[23\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0115), [\[46\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0230). More traditional cryptographic ideas including secure aggregation [\[7\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0035) or homomorphic encryption [\[38\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0190) for the gradients can also be used and presumably stop any eavesdropping attacks completely. They will not be studied here due to their special setups and overhead.

First, we draw attention to two strong assumptions that a current gradient inversion attack [\[18\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0090) implicitly makes. We show that by nullifying these assumptions, the performance of the attack drops significantly and can only work for low-resolution images. The findings are explored in Section [6.3](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0020) and already imply some more secure configurations in federated learning (Section [6.6](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0105)).

Second, we summarize various defenses (Section [6.4](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0060)) and systematically evaluate (Section [6.5](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0080)) some of their performance of defending against a state-of-the-art gradient inversion attack, and present their data utility and privacy leakage trade-offs. We estimate the computation cost of end-to-end recovery of a single image under each evaluated defense. We also experimentally demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of combined defenses. Our findings are summarized as strategies to further improve federated learning's security against gradient inversion attacks (Section [6.6](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0105)). Finally, we discuss emerging topics and future directions for gradient inversion attacks (Section [6.7](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0110)).

## 6.2 Gradient inversion attacks

Previous studies have shown the feasibility of recovering input from gradient (i.e., gradient inversion) for image classification tasks, by formulating it as an optimization problem: given a neural network with parameters `θ`, and the gradient `∇[θ]ℒ[θ](x^(*), y^(*))` computed with a private data batch `(x^(*), y^(*)) ∈ ℝ^(b×d) × ℝ^b` (`b`, `d` being the batch and image sizes, respectively), the attacker tries to recover `x ∈ ℝ^(b×d)`, an approximation of `x^(*)`, as

```
\arg\min\limits_{x}\mathcal{L}_{grad}(x;\theta,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(x^{*},y^{*})) + \alpha\mathcal{R}_{aux}(x).
```

(6.1)

The optimization goal consists of two parts: the term `ℒ[grad](x; θ, ∇[θ]ℒ[θ](x^(*), y^(*)))` enforces matching of the gradient of recovered batch `x` with the provided gradients `ℒ[θ](x^(*), y^(*))`, while the term `ℛ[aux](x)` further improves reconstructions by regularizing the loss using known image priors. Notably, the attack becomes more difficult as the dimensionality of the private batch `x` grows, namely, when `x` is a large training batch, or when `x` consists of high-dimension inputs (e.g., high-resolution images). Additionally, the model architecture and size further impact the quality of reconstruction.

Phong et al. [\[37\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0185) bring theoretical insights on this task by proving that such a reconstruction is possible with a single-layer neural network. Zhu et al. [\[51\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0255) are the first to show that accurate pixel-level reconstruction is practical for a maximum batch size of 8, using the `ℓ[2]`-distance as `ℒ[grad](⋅, ⋅)`, and not including a regularization term `ℛ[aux](x)`. The approach is successful for low-resolution CIFAR datasets [\[29\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0145) with simple neural networks with sigmoid activations, but cannot scale up to high-resolution images, or larger models with ReLU activations. Follow-up work [\[49\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0245) proposes a simple approach that can partially extract the ground-truth labels from the gradient, further strengthening the attack by relaxing the need to reconstruct labels `y^(*)` during gradient inversion (see Section [6.3.2](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0030) for more detail). More recent approaches improve recovery by adjusting the choice of `ℒ[grad]` and adding a regularization term `ℛ[aux](x)`. Geiping et al. [\[18\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0090) substantially improve the attack and enable recovery of low-resolution images from a maximum batch size of 100 from gradients by choosing cosine distance for `ℒ[grad]`, and total variation for `ℛ[aux](x)` (described in more detail in the following section). Moreover, the approach is able to reconstruct a single high-resolution image from ImageNet [\[11\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0055). Wei et al. [\[43\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0215) provide an analysis of how different configurations in the training may affect the attack by Geiping et al.

A more recent work [\[45\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0225) further improves the attack on high-resolution images, by adding an additional regularization term based on batch normalization [\[25\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0125) statistics and incorporating a regularization term that enforces consistency across multiple attack trials.

An orthogonal line of work [\[50\]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0250) proposes to formulate the gradient inversion attack as a closed-form recursive procedure, instead of an optimization problem. However, this approach can recover only low-resolution images when the training batch size is 1.
