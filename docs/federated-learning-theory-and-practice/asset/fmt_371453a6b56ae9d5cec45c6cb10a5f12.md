
$$\\arg\\min\\limits\_{x} 1 - \\frac{\\left\\langle \\nabla\_{\\theta}\\mathcal{L}\_{\\theta}(x,y),\\nabla\_{\\theta}\\mathcal{L}\_{\\theta}\\left( x^{\\ast},y^{\\ast} \\right) \\right\\rangle}{\\left\\| \\nabla\_{\\theta}\\mathcal{L}\_{\\theta}(x,y)\\right\\| \\left\\| \\nabla\_{\\theta}\\mathcal{L}\_{\\theta}\\left( x^{\\ast},y^{\\ast} \\right) \\right\\|} + \\alpha\_{TV}\\mathcal{R}\_{TV}(x)$$

where $\\langle\\cdot,\\cdot\\rangle$ is the inner-product between vectors, and $\\mathcal{R}\_{TV}(\\cdot)$ is the total variation of images.

We notice that Geiping et al. have made two strong assumptions (Section [6.3.2](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0030)). Changing setups to invalidate those assumptions will substantially weaken the attacks (Section [6.3.3](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0045)). We also summarize whether other attacks have made similar assumptions in [Table 6.1](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#t0010).

### 6.3.2 Strong assumptions

We now describe two different assumptions made by previous attacks which could weaken their strength in a more realistic scenario. Note that the attack from [[18]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0090) makes both strong assumptions.

#### Assumption 1: Knowledge of BatchNorm statistics

Batch normalization (BatchNorm) [[25]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0125) is a technique for training neural networks that normalizes the inputs to a layer for every mini-batch. Notably, batch normalization behaves differently during training and evaluation. Assume the model has $L$ batch normalization layers. Given $x^{(\\ast)}$, a batch of input images, we use $x^{[l]}_{(\\ast)}$ to denote the input features to the $l$-th BatchNorm layer, where $l \\in [L]$. During training, the $l$-th BatchNorm layer normalizes $x^{[l]}_{(\\ast)}$ based on the batch's mean $\\text{mean}(x^{[l]}_{(\\ast)})$ and variance $\\text{var}(x^{[l]}_{(\\ast)})$, and keeps a running estimate of mean and variance of all training data points, denoted by $\\mu^{[l]}$ and $\\sigma^{[l]2}$ respectively. During inference, $\\{\\mu^{[l]}\\}\_{l=1}^{L}$ and $\\{\\sigma^{[l]2}\\}\_{l=1}^{L}$ are used to normalize test images. For brevity, we use $\\mu, \\sigma^2$ to denote $\\{\\mu^{[l]}\\}\_{l=1}^{L}$, $\\{\\sigma^{[l]2}\\}\_{l=1}^{L}$, and $\\text{mean}(x^{(\\ast)})$, $\\text{var}(x^{(\\ast)})$ to denote $\\{\\text{mean}(x^{[l]}_{(\\ast)})\\}\_{l=1}^{L}$, $\\{\\text{var}(x^{[l]}_{(\\ast)})\\}\_{l=1}^{L}$ in the following sections.

We notice that Geiping et al.'s [[18]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0090) implementation assumes the attacker has access to the exact BatchNorm statistics of the private batch (i.e., $\\text{mean}(x^{(\\ast)})$ and $\\text{var}(x^{(\\ast)})$). This is a strong assumption because in standard federated learning frameworks (e.g., [[33]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0165)), clients keep running estimates of mean and variance locally and only share gradients. The aggregated statistics $\\mu, \\sigma^2$ are typically released only at the end of training, not per batch.

Note that this assumption may still be valid in some scenarios, e.g., if 1) the neural network is shallow, thus does not require using BatchNorm layers, or if 2) the neural network is deep, but adapts approaches that normalize batch inputs with a fixed mean and variance (as an alternative to BatchNorm), e.g., fixup initialization [[47]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0235).

#### Assumption 2: Knowing or being able to infer private labels

Private labels are not intended to be shared in federated learning, but knowing them would improve the attack. Zhao et al. [[49]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0245) find that label information of a *single* private image can be inferred from the gradient (see Section [6.3.3](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#s0045) for details). Based on this, the authors of [[18]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0090) assume the attacker knows private labels (see remark at the end of Section 4 in their paper). However, this assumption may not hold true when multiple images in a batch share the same label, as we will show in the next section.

### 6.3.3 Re-evaluation under relaxed assumptions

We re-evaluate the performance of the gradient inversion attack in settings where the two assumptions above are relaxed. For each relaxation, we re-design the attack (if needed) based on the knowledge that the attacker has.

#### Relaxation 1: Not knowing BatchNorm statistics

We refer to the previous threat model as $\\text{BN}\_{\\text{exact}}$, where the attacker knows the exact BatchNorm statistics of the private batch. We consider a realistic threat model where these statistics are not exposed and re-design the attack based on it.

**Threat model.** In each training step, the client normalizes its private batch $x^{(\\ast)}$ using the batch's mean $\\text{mean}(x^{(\\ast)})$ and variance $\\text{var}(x^{(\\ast)})$, keeps the running estimate of mean and variance *locally* as in [[33]](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#br0165), and shares the gradient. The client releases the final aggregated mean $\\mu$, and aggregated variance $\\sigma^2$ of all training data points at the end of training. Same as before, the attacker has access to the model and the gradient during training.

**Re-design A:** $\\text{BN}\_{\\text{proxy}}$, attacker naively uses $\\mu$ and $\\sigma^2$. A simple idea is that the attacker uses $(\\mu,\\sigma^2)$ as the proxy for $(\\text{mean}(x^{(\\ast)}),\\text{var}(x^{(\\ast)}))$, and uses them to normalize $x$, his guesses of the private batch. Other operations of the gradient inversion attack remain the same as before. However, [Figs. 6.1.d and 6.1.h](#Chp006_105-122_B9780443190377000144.xhtml#f0010) show poor-quality reconstruction with this re-design.
