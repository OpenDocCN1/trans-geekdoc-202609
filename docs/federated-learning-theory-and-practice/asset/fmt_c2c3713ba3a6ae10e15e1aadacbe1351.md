
Recently, many groups around the world have been working on federated QML to advance privacy-preserving capabilities [[12](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0060),[19](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0095),[27](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0135),[40](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0200),[46](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0230),[47](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0235),[49](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0245),[88](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0440)–[90](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0450),[92](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#br0460)], and most share some common features, e.g., the underlying circuits. The next section introduces the variational quantum circuit, or VQC, the building block of QML.

## 17.3 Variational quantum circuits

Quantum FL should be based on existing QML methods. In the near term, quantum computers have a limited number of qubits and can only execute shallow circuits. This means it is not practical to perform sophisticated machine learning algorithms purely on existing quantum devices. Instead, a *hybrid quantum-classical computing paradigm* is designed to leverage today's noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices ([Fig. 17.3](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#f0020)). The main concept behind such a paradigm involves splitting the computational task into two parts. One part that can benefit from using a quantum computer will be deployed on the device, while the other part, which is more suited to being solved classically, will be run on a classical computer. As a starting point, [Fig. 17.4](#Chp017_311-328_B9780443190377000272.xhtml#f0025) depicts the simplest parameterized quantum circuit. This single-qubit system initialized at state `|0〉` then goes through the `encoding gate`, which is parameterized by a parameter `θ`. The parameter `θ` depends on what a user wants to encode (e.g., pixel values from an image). Then, the quantum state will go through the `parameterized gate` – also called a `variational gate` – that is parameterized by `ϕ`. The `ϕ` parameter is the element to be trained or learned. After passing through the `R[y](θ)` and `R[z](ϕ)` gates, the quantum state should now in the form

$$
\begin{aligned} 
& {\begin{bmatrix} {e^{- i\phi/2}\quad} & 0 \\ {0\quad} & e^{i\phi/2} \\ \end{bmatrix}\left\lbrack \begin{array}{lc} {\cos(\phi/2)\quad} & {- \sin(\phi/2)} \\ {\sin(\phi/2)\quad} & {\cos(\phi/2)} \\ \end{array} \right\rbrack\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}} \\ 
& {= \begin{bmatrix} {e^{- i\phi/2}\cos(\theta/2)} \\ {e^{i\phi/2}\sin(\theta/2)} \\ \end{bmatrix} = e^{- i\phi/2}\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi/2}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle,} \\ 
\end{aligned}
$$

(17.1)

where $R_{y}(\phi) = e^{- i\phi\sigma_{y}/2} = \begin{bmatrix} {\cos(\phi/2)\quad} & {- \sin(\phi/2)} \\ {\sin(\phi/2)\quad} & {\cos(\phi/2)} \\ \end{bmatrix}$ and $R_{z}(\phi) = e^{- i\phi\sigma_{z}/2} = \begin{bmatrix} {e^{- i\phi/2}\quad} & 0 \\ {0\quad} & e^{i\phi/2} \\ \end{bmatrix}$.
