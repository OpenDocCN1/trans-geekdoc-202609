# No. of Folds: Most of the time K = 10 and we train a model new fold and test it on the last remaining fold.

No. of iterations: Since we have 10 folds, we can make 10 different combinations of 9+1 fold to train the model. The model can be trained in 10 iterations for each combination of 10-fold.

Once we train the model and test all on ten combinations (10 iterations) of training and test sets. We get 10 different accuracies.

We take an average of 10 different accuracies to model-performance-evaluations and also compute the standard deviation to have a look at 10 variant. This will give us a better idea of our model's performance.

+   - Large accuracy and a high variance - lower right

+   - Small accuracy (high bias) & variance - upper left

K-fold cross-validation approach divides the input dataset into K groups of samples of equal size. These samples are called folds. For each learning set, the prediction function uses k-folds, and the rest of the folds are used for the test set. This approach is a popular CV approach because it is easy to understand, and the output is less biased than other methods.

The steps for k-fold cross-validation are:

+   [1]. Split the input dataset into K groups

+   [2]. For each group:

+   - Take one group as the reserve or test data set.

+   - Use remaining groups as the training dataset

+   - Fit the model on the training set and evaluate the performance of the model using the test set.

Let's take an example of 5-fold cross-validation, so the dataset is grouped into 5 folds. On 1st iteration, the first fold is reserved for the test model, and are used to train the model. On 2nd iteration, the second fold is used to test the model, and are used to train the model. This process will continue until each fold is not used for the test fold. Consider the below diagram:
