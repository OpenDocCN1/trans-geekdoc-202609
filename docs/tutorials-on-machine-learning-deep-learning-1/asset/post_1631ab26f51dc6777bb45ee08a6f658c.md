```
**Input:** $(V_1, \dots, V_m, H_1, \dots, H_n)$, training batch $S$
**Output:** gradient approximation $\Delta w_{ij}, \Delta b_j, \Delta c_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$,
1 $j = 1, \dots, m$
2 $\Delta w_{ij} \leftarrow \Delta b_j \leftarrow \Delta c_i = 0$ for $i = 1, \dots, n, j = 1, \dots, m$
3 for $v \leftarrow v$
4 for $t = 0, \dots, k-1$
5 for $i = 1, \dots, n$ sample $h_i^{(t)} \leftarrow p(h_i|v^{(t)})$
6 for $j = 1, \dots, m$ sample $v_j^{(t+1)} \leftarrow p(v_j|h^{(t)})$
7 for $i = 1, \dots, n, j = 1, \dots, m$
8 $\Delta w_{ij} \leftarrow \Delta w_{ij} + p(h_i=1|v^{(0)}) \cdot v_j^{(0)} - p(h_i=1|v^{(k)}) \cdot v_j^{(k)}$
9 $\Delta b_j \leftarrow \Delta b_j + \dots$
10 $\Delta c_i \leftarrow \Delta c_i + p(h_i=1|v^{(0)}) - p(h_i=1|v^{(k)})$
```

Since $v^{(k)}$ is not a sample from the stationary model approximation (34) is biased. Obviously, the bias vanishes as $k \to \infty$. That CD is a biased-approximation also comes close by realizing that it does not maximize the likelihood of the data under the model but the difference of two KL-divergences [15].

> $KL(v|p) - KL(p|p)$ (35)

where $q$ is the empirical distribution and $p_k$ is the distribution of the variables after $k$ steps of the Markov chain. If the chain already reached stationarity it holds $p_k = p$ and thus $KL(p|p) = 0$ and the approximation error of CD vanishes.

The theoretical results from [3] give a good understanding of the CD approximation and by showing that the log-likelihood gradient and the corresponding bias based on a Markov chain — can be expressed as a sum of terms containing the k-th sample:

# **Theorem 1** (Bengio and Delalleau [3]). For a converging Gibbs chain

> $v^{(0)} \Rightarrow h^{(0)} \Rightarrow v^{(1)} \Rightarrow h^{(1)} \dots$

starting at data point $v^{(0)}$, the log-likelihood gradient can be written as

> $\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ln p(v^{(0)}) = -\sum_h p(h|v^{(0)}) \frac{\partial E(v^{(0)},h)}{\partial \theta} + E_{p(v^{(k)}|v^{(0)})} \left[ \sum_h p(h|v^{(k)}) \frac{\partial E(v^{(k)},h)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial \ln p(v^{(k)})}{\partial \theta} \right] (36)$

and the final term converges to zero as $k$ goes to infinity.

The first two terms in equation (36) just correspond to the expectation of the CD approximation (under $p_k$) and the bias is by the final term.

The approximation error does not only depend on the value of $k$ but also on the rate of convergence and the mixing rate of the Gibbs chain. The rate describes how fast the Markov chain approaches the stationary distribution. The mixing rate of the Gibbs chain of an RBM is up to the magnitude of the model parameters [15].

An upper bound on the expectation of the CD approximation error under the empirical distribution is given by the following theorem [12]:

**Theorem 2 (Fischer and Igel [12]).** Let $p$ denote the marginal distribution of the visible units of an RBM and $q$ be the empirical distribution defined by a set of samples $v_1,\dots,v_n$. Then an upper bound on the expectation of the CD-$k$ error of the log-likelihood derivative w.r.t some RBM parameter $\theta_a$ is given

```
$E_{p(v^{(0)})} \left[ E_{p(v^{(k)}|v^{(0)})} \left( \frac{\partial \log q(v^{(k)})}{\partial \theta_a} \right)^2 \right] \leq \frac{1}{2} \left\| q - p \right\|^2 \left(1 - e^{-(m+n)\Delta} \right)^k \quad (37)$
```

with

```
$\Delta = \max \left\{ \max_{i=1,\dots,n} \theta_i, \max_{j=1,\dots,m} \xi_j \right\}$
```

where

```
$\theta_l = \max \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n l_{\{w_{ij}>0\}}w_{ij} + b_l, \ \sum_{j=1}^m l_{\{w_{ij}<0\}}w_{ij} + b_l \right\}$
```

and

```
$\xi_l = \max \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n l_{\{w_{ij}>0\}}w_{ij} + c_l, \sum_{j=1}^m l_{\{w_{ij}<0\}}w_{ij} + c_l \right\}$
```

The (bound) also depends on the absolute values of the RBM parameters, on the size of the RBM (the number of variables in the graph), and on the distance variance between the modeled distribution and the start-distribution of the Gibbs chain.

As a consequence of the approximation error CD-learning does not lead to a maximum likelihood estimate of the model parameters. Yuille [42] specifies conditions under which CD learning is guaranteed to converge to the maximum likelihood solution, which need not for RBM training in general. Examples of energy functions and Markov chains for which CD-1 learning does not converge are given in [27]. The empirical comparisons of the CD-approximation and the true gradient for RBMs small enough that the gradient is tractable conducted in [7] and [3] shows that the bias can lead to a convergence to parameters that do not reach the maximum likelihood.

The bias, however, can also lead to a distortion of the learning process: After some iterations the likelihood can start to diverge (see figure 2) in the sense that the model systematically gets worse if k is not large [11]. This is especially bad because the log-likelihood is not tractable in reasonable sized RNNs, and so the misbehavior can not be displayed and used as a stopping criterion. Because the effect depends on the weights, weight decay can help to prevent it. However, the weight decay parameter $\lambda$, see equation (6), is difficult to tune. If it is too small, weight decay has no effect. If it is too large, the learning converges to models with low likelihood [11].

## 5.2 Persistent Divergence

The idea of persistent contrastive divergence (PCD) is described in [36]. The PCD approximation is obtained from CD (34) by replacing the sample $v^{(k)}$ with a sample from a Markov chain. The fundamental ideas underlying PCD is that one could assume the chains stay close to the stationary distribution if the learning rate is sufficiently small. The number of persistent chains used for sampling (9)) is a hyper parameter of the algorithm. In the canonical, there exists one Markov chain per training.

The PCD was refined in a variant called fast persistent divergence (FPCD), [37]. FPCD tries to reach faster mixing of the Gibbs chain by introducing fast parameters. Neither nor FPCD seems to enhance the mixing rate.
