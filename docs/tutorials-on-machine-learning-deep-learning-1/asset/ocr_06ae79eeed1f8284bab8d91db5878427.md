In a thermodynamic system, we're looking the system at a given temperature, for instance a gas. The equation says that, the probability of your system being in a certain state is inversely related to the energy of your system in that state.

![](bbox=[0.153, 0.07375, 0.893, 0.19625])

To explain **Boltzmann distribution** consider we've got a room, and we have a **gas**, for instance **air**.

+   - Now the question is how the gas is **distributed** across the whole room?
- Why is the gas, not in all one corner?

The answer is: They could be anywhere. *Statistically*, they could end up in **one corner**, in any room, at any given point in time. This is one of the possible states of this system.

+   - But the Boltzmann distribution is saying that the **probability of that state "all gas in one corner" occurring is **very low**.
- Because the molecules in state are **very close** to each other, making a lot of *chaos* and they would be moving *very quickly*. Given the constant temperature of the room.
- What we normally observe is gas (air molecules are equally spaced apart) are distributed over the room. Because it is the **lowest energy state for system**.

It applies to everything. For instance, if you dropped ink into water, it will start **spreading evenly** in all directions. It will form a star, because that's **not the lowest energy state**.
On the other hand, if you, a drop of oil into water, it won't start spreading, because that is the lowest energy state for that system.

+   - **Boltzmann machines** are constructed with a *similar concept*. The **energy** is defined in BMs with the **weights** of synapses.
- Once the system is trained up, the **weights** are **set**, the **system**, based on those **weights**, will always try to find the **lowest energy state for itself**.
- It has lots of differences, the weights will dictate what is the lowest energy state for the system, and it will constantly try to get to the lowest energy state possible.

That's why they called **Energy-Based Models**.
