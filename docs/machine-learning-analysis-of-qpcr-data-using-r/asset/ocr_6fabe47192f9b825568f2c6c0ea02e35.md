Despite its improvements, even blotting might suffer from sensibility issues. There are two solutions to this shortcoming: either amplify the signal or the target.

Let’s imagine a detection system with a sensitivity of one million arbitrary light units, and a probe that can emit one light unit when bound to the target. Let’s also imagine that there are only 10 targets in a specimen. As is, the binding of the probes will produce only 10 light units, thus the assay will give a negative result. To get a positive signal, either the signal or target itself should, somehow, be multiplied.

One method to achieve signal amplification is that of annealing a linker molecule, containing a binding site to the ROI and several other moieties that are recognized by the probes. For instance, if a linker can bind 10⁵ probes, the hypothetical 10 targets — each with one binding site for the linker — will produce one million light units. One of the best-known methods using this approach is branched DNA (bDNA) and it is used, for instance, in viral diagnostic (Wilber, 1997; Tsongalis, 2006). The bDNA assay is run more-or-less like an ELISA and has a sensitivity comparable to that of PCR (Collins et al., 1997).

Other methods to amplify the signal are based on the direct polymerization of probes (Yan et al., 2014) (Table 1). In this case, each target will be the template for the synthesis of several probes, allowing to quickly reach the threshold for a positive result. These enzymatic methods are carried out at the same temperature and are, therefore, classified as isothermal processes. One of the most popular is the nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA) also known as self-sustained sequence replication (3SR) (Deiman et al., 2002). Like PCR, NASBA uses primers. One contains a tail with a promoter sequence recognized by the DNA-dependent RNA-polymerase (DdRp) isolated from the virus T7. The primer is extended by the RNA-dependent DNA-polymerase (RdDp, or reverse transcriptase) isolated from the avian myeloblastosis virus (AMV). The nascent hybrid DNA/RNA is degraded by E. coli RNase H, which removes the original ssRNA and leaves the newly synthesized ssDNA. The latter anneals with a second primer, whose extension by the reverse transcriptase generates a dsDNA fragment with a complete T7-promoter sequence.
