The fact that the end-point PCR does not allow for the quantification of the targets is a major drawback of the assay, for determining the presence or absence of a target is an important achievement but, for modern purposes, limited in its applications. For instance, knowledge of a sample’s virus load is used to determine the effectiveness of an antiviral therapy. One could stop the PCR well before reaching the saturation phase, let’s say after 20 cycles in the example, but:

- The EF is a labor-intensive step that slows down the assay.
- EF introduces risks of contamination and manual errors. The opening of a PCR tube containing amplified DNA is associated with a risk of dispersing drops of amplicons inside the laboratory. If the aerosol of amplicons ends up in new PCR tubes, it will induce false-positive results. This risk of contamination is a major issue for the PCR laboratories.
- The results are still semi-quantitative because the quantification is obtained by comparing the fluorescence of the target against that of special DNA ladders where each band contains a predetermined amount of DNA. However, the bands are few and contain a limited range of DNA concentrations, hence the quantification is very rough.

The breakthrough in PCR was the introduction of signal detection during the course of the reaction itself. Such a ‘trick’ dispensed with the use of the EF step, simplifying and speeding up the reaction. A research group composed of Pamela Holland and David Gelfand at Cetus (Holland et al., 1991) found that the 5′→3′ exonuclease activity of the DNA-pol could degrade an oligonucleotide hybridized in front of the enzyme. By binding a fluorescent dye and a quencher at the extremities of the oligonucleotide, such a molecule (known as probe) would not radiate light because the photons emitted by the fluorescent molecule will be sequestered by the quencher. But, upon degradation of the probe by the DNA-pol during the extension time of the PCR, the fluorescent dye will be freed from the quencher activity and release photons in the reaction vessel that can be collected by an optical apparatus. At about the same time, the group formed by Russell Higuchi, Robert Griffith and colleagues at Roche reported that some intercalators (that is, molecules that bind with high affinity to the DNA) could increase their fluorescence when bound to the dsDNA, so that each amplicon could release a certain amount of light at each cycle (Higuchi et al., 1992). Furthermore, Kenneth Livak, Karin Deetz and colleagues at Perkin-Elmer/Applied Biosystems found that two probes marked with a particular set of fluorescent dyes and hybridized in close proximity could emit light (Heid et al., 1996). These approaches are now known, respectively, as hydrolysis probes chemistry (commercialized as TaqMan™), intercalator chemistry, and hybridization probes chemistry (or FRET, fluorescence resonance energy transfer).

The first intercalator to be used was ethidium bromide but, being carcinogenic, it has been substituted by other harmless dyes such as SYBR Green™, RedSafe™, and EvaGreen™.

The intercalators are cheaper than the probes and, although they are prone to attach to specific sequences, the sequences they bind to are common enough on the DNA strand to confer effective coverage of any amplicon (Bailly and Chaires, 1998). More importantly, intercalators allow melting analysis to be performed. By gradually increasing the temperature in the reaction vessel after the end of the PCR itself, the fluorescence emitted by the amplicons decreases because the intercalators are displaced from the DNA. When the temperature reaches the melting point (Tₘ) of the amplicon, the duplex is dissolved very fast and the fluorescence drops suddenly. The plot of the fluorescence against the temperature is that of a decreasing line that, at the Tₘ, suddenly drops to zero. By taking the first derivative of this curve, the graph is converted into a bell-shaped graph whose peak is Tₘ. In the presence of primer dimers or other contaminants, the graph will show more than one peak. The melting analysis, therefore, is used to confirm the specificity of the PCR.

On the other hand, probes allow the simultaneous detection of multiple targets (an approach known as multiplexing), which further expanded the PCR’s applications. This improved PCR assay was first named ‘real-time’ because the results could be read while the PCR was still ongoing rather than afterwards. Currently it is better known as ‘quantitative’ (qPCR) because it allows the quantification of the initial load of a sample. The next chapter will describe the quantification procedure in more detail.
