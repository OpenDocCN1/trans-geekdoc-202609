The PCR will then proceed into three reiterated stages (Figure 1.1A). The first stage will rise the reaction’s temperature to about 95°C, melting the double stranded DNA (dsDNA) and making it available to further use. The second stage will cool the reaction mixture to about 50°C, permitting the preferential annealing of short synthetic oligonucleotides to the ROI. A short period (about 10 s) will instead favor the annealing of shorts stretches of DNA. The annealing process (that is, the opposite of melting) is regulated by a constant rate *k* defined as (Wetmur, 1991): *k* = *k*<sub>N</sub>*L*<sub>S</sub><sup>−1/2</sup>*N*<sup>−1</sup>, where *L*<sub>S</sub> is the shortest length of the chains, *N* is the complexity of the sequence (a measure of the absence of repetitive stretches of nucleotides) an *k*<sub>N</sub> is the nucleation rate. From the equation, it can be seen that long chains take longer to anneal than short nucleotides. For instance, let’s continue in the hypothetical analysis of *E. coli*, which has a genome of 4.6 million bases (Mb) (Blattner *et al.*, 1997). Let’s assume to have a primer of 20 nucleotides. Since *k*<sub>N</sub> and *N* are the same for both the primer and the genome, it is possible to simplify the annealing rate equation using the constant α = *k*<sub>N</sub>*N*<sup>−1</sup>, so that the kinetics of annealing time for the whole genome will be α√4 639 221 = α2153.9, whereas that of the primer will be α√20 = α4.5. In other words, the primer will anneal 482 times faster than the whole genome.

Raising again the temperature to about 60°C will optimize the processivity (defined as the probability of continuing the extension of the primer without detaching from the template (Pavlov *et al.*, 2004)) of the DNA-pol. Each DNA-pol will have a specific optimal temperature. The result is that, with these three steps, the DNA-pol would have duplicated the ROI, hence the importance of the ‘powers of two’. When the polymerase works with 100% processivity (100% efficiency), each copy of ROI is amplified at each cycle and the kinetics of PCR follows the rule 2ⁿ, where *n* is the number of cycles the procedure has been run for. At the end of the procedure, there will be a mix of NA products each containing a ROI (Figure 1-1B). Since the PCR extends the primers until the end of the template’s strand, the newly synthesized strands will be shorter than the original segment. The most abundant NA species will be that extending from the forward to the reverse primers: this will have an exact length defined by the primer design and goes under the name of ‘amplicon’.
