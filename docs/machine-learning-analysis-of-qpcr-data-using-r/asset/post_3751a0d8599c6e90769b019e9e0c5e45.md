
Sequences are not simply duplicated at each cycle, but they can generate several other molecules. Also, the amplification does not happen once each cycle (that is, once every minute or so), but it is continuous and the primers do not represent the limiting factor of the reaction as in PCR. The combination of these two factors implies that NASBA amplifies its target faster and at higher levels than PCR. Transcription-mediated amplification (TMA) is similar to NASBA but uses a proprietary enzyme that combines reverse-transcriptase and RNase H activities (Zhao *et al.*, 2015).

Another popular isothermal method is loop-mediated amplification (LAMP) (Notomi *et al.*, 2015). This method requires the knowledge of six loci on the target DNA. Let’s indicate these loci with the letters A to F. A-B-C and D-E-F are upstream and downstream, respectively, of the ROI; the complementary sequences can be represented by Ā-F̄. A first primer (forward inner primer, FIP) bears the sequences 5′-C̄B-3′ and hybridizes on B by strand invasion; thus, there is no need to melt the DNA as a preliminary step as in PCR. This primer is extended by a DNA-pol generating a fragment (C̄B̄C-ROI-D̄ĒF). The newly generated strand is displaced by the addition of an outer primer with the sequence A. The C and C̄ loci of the displaced strand hybridize together forming a 5′ loop. The same process occurs at the loci D-F with the backward internal (BIP, bearing ĒD) and backward external (bearing F) primers. The end-result of this process is the so-called LAMP dumbbell structure, a single-stranded filament with the loops C̄B̄C and DĒD̄ that hybridizes with both FIP and BIP. The operator will define an ROI and use its presence as a surrogate for the actual isolation, growth and characterization of this bacterium. The PCR will then proceed into three reiterated stages (Figure 1.1A). The first stage will raise the reaction’s temperature to about 95°C, melting the double stranded DNA (dsDNA) and making it available to further use. The second stage will cool the reaction mixture to about 50°C, permitting the preferential annealing of short synthetic oligonucleotides to the ROI. A short period (about 10 s) will instead favor the annealing of short stretches of DNA. The annealing process (that is, the opposite of melting) is regulated by a constant rate *k* defined as (Wetmur, 1991): *k* = *k*<sub>N</sub>*L*<sub>S</sub><sup>−1/2</sup>*N*<sup>−1</sup>, where *L*<sub>S</sub> is the shortest length of the chains, *N* is the complexity of the sequence (a measure of the absence of repetitive stretches of nucleotides) and *k*<sub>N</sub> is the nucleation rate. From the equation, it can be seen that long chains take longer to anneal than short nucleotides. For instance, let’s continue in the hypothetical analysis of *E. coli*, which has a genome of 4.6 million bases (Mb) (Blattner *et al.*, 1997). Let’s assume to have a primer of 20 nucleotides. Since *k*<sub>N</sub> and *N* are the same for both the primer and the genome, it is possible to simplify the annealing rate equation using the constant α = *k*<sub>N</sub>*N*<sup>−1</sup>, so that the kinetics of annealing time for the whole genome will be α√4,639,221 = α2153.9, whereas that of the primer will be α√20 = α4.5. In other words, the primer will anneal 482 times faster than the whole genome.

Raising again the temperature to about 60°C will optimize the processivity (defined as the probability of continuing the extension of the primer without detaching from the template (Pavlov *et al.*, 2004)) of the DNA-pol. Each DNA-pol will have a specific optimal temperature. The result is that, with these three steps, the DNA-pol would have duplicated the ROI, hence the importance of the ‘powers of two’. When the polymerase works with 100% processivity (100% efficiency), each copy of ROI is amplified at each cycle and the kinetics of PCR follows the rule 2ⁿ, where *n* is the number of cycles the procedure has been run for. At the end of the procedure, there will be a mix of NA products each containing an ROI (Figure 1.1B). Since the PCR extends the primers until the end of the template’s strand, the newly synthesized strands will be shorter than the original segment. The most abundant NA species will be that extending from the forward to the reverse primers: this will have an exact length defined by the primer design and goes under the name of ‘amplicon’.

### Figure 1.1. Overview of the amplification reaction.

The PCR selectively amplifies a region of interest (ROI) of a nucleic acid. A. The PCR cycling conditions. A double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) is denatured by increasing the reaction temperature to about 95°C (melting). The thermal-cycler then reduces the reaction’s temperature to about 56°C allowing synthetic oligonucleotides (primers) to anneal to a selected locus within the ROI (annealing). The actual annealing temperature depends on the characteristics of the primers at hand. The temperature is raised again to about 60°C (elongation). This temperature matches the optimum for the DNA-dependent DNA-polymerase (DNA-pol for short) present in the reaction vessel and depends on the chosen enzyme. B. The PCR produces several types of DNA fragments with different abundances. The initial ROI, a dsDNA, is depicted at the top of the panel. During the first cycle of amplification, the two strands of the ROI are separated and duplicated. The newly synthesized strands extend from the 5′-end of the forward primer to the 3′-end of ROI (unless ROI is so long that the DNA-pol cannot cover the whole segment by the time allocated in the elongation phase). The result is a pair of molecules with heterogeneous strand lengths (c and d). In the second cycle of the PCR, the full-length strands of c and d serve as a template for c and d molecules. Instead, the short strands of c and d will generate fragments extending from the 5′-end of the reverse primer to the 3′-end of ROI (b and e), which are still of different lengths but shorter than c and d. In the third PCR cycle, the short strand of b and e will produce fragments extending from the 5′-end of the forward primer to the 3′-end of the reverse primer (a). The a molecule will be a homogeneous dsDNA fragment whose length is known in advance due to the design of the primers and goes under the name of ‘amplicon’. Since the DNA-pol can perfectly duplicate a within the time allocated for the amplification phase, a will multiply under the 2ⁿ kinetics and quickly become the most prevalent molecule in the reaction vessel. In contrast, the other fragments will duplicate linearly under the 2n kinetics.
