Also, the amplification does not happen once each cycle (that is, once every minute or so), but it is continuous and the primers do not represent the limiting factor of the reaction as in PCR. The combination of these two factors implies that NASBA amplifies its target faster and at higher levels than PCR. Transcription mediated amplification (TMA) is similar to NASBA but uses a proprietary enzyme that combines reverse-transcriptase and RNase H activities (Zhao *et al.*, 2015).

Another popular isothermal method is loop-mediated amplification (LAMP) (Notomi *et al.*, 2015). This method requires the knowledge of six loci on the target DNA. Let’s indicate these loci with the letters A to F. A-B-C and D-E-F are upstream and downstream, respectively, of the ROI; the complementary sequences can be represented by A-F. A first primer (forward inner primer, FIP) bears the sequences 5′-C̄B-3′ and hybridizes on B by strand invasion; thus, there is no need to melt the DNA as a preliminary step as in PCR. This primer is extended by a DNA-pol generating a fragment (C̄B̄C-ROI-D̄ĒF). The newly generated strand is displaced by the addition of an outer primer with the sequence A. The C and C̄ loci of the displaced strand hybridize together forming a 5′ loop. The same process occurs at the loci D-F with the backwards internal (BIP, bearing ĒD) and backwards external (bearing F) primers. The end-result of this process is the so-called LAMP dumbbell structure, a single-stranded filament with the loops C̄B̄C and DĒD̄ that hybridizes with both FIP and BIP.
