
actually meaningful. Visual inspection of the qPCR data is required alongside some precautions during the execution of the assay (Adams, 2006). The following is a quick list:

- *Do not trust published sets.* Check sequence and melting temperature of published primer/probe sets as there might be printing mistakes in the articles at hand.
- *Customize.* Optimize published PCR conditions to the equipment and chemistry at hand.
- *Linearize plasmids.* It is customary to use plasmids as standards but, due to supercoiling, not all the molecules quantified will be available for PCR. This will produce a bias in the quantification.
- *Calculate the number of molecules of the standard template.* The initial copy number of the standard, N, can be calculated with the formula: N = MA/WL, where M is the mass in gram of the target, obtained for instance by spectrophotometry (e.g., with NanoDrop™ instrument); A is the Avogadro number (6.023 × 10²³ molecules/mole); W is the average weight of either dsDNA (660 g/mole/base) or ssDNA (330 g/mole/base); L the length of the ROI in bases. Once N is known, serial dilutions can be made to generate the standard curve.
- *Include NTC.* Add a non-template control (NTC) to the qPCR run, preferably scattered around the plate. The NTC allows checking for possible spillage of material that could generate false-positive results. When quantifying RNA, controls without reverse transcriptase should be also added.
- *Include a positive control.* The standard curve is already a positive control but the control should be as similar as possible to the actual samples being analyzed to take into account possible inhibitors present in the sample’s matrix. For instance, if the samples are obtained from serum or stools, the control should be obtained by spiking a known amount of standard into PCR-negative serum or stools.
- *Check that the right light emission filters had been selected.* This test might seem a silly point, but many fluorescent dyes overlap in their emission spectra. If the filter is not correctly set, there could be an overlooked loss of amplification signal resulting in unnoticed qPCR inhibition.
- *Set the baseline.* The baseline refers to the initial cycles of amplification that might generate a signal higher than a threshold. There is no fixed rule for defining the baseline, but it usually lies in the first ten cycles. As a rule of thumb, it should be three cycles before the lowest Cq. Usually, baselining is performed separately on each sample.
- *Set the threshold.* The threshold refers to the light cut-off above which a signal is considered positive. Thermal-cyclers’ software automatically determines a threshold, usually based on a multiple of the standard deviation in the background light signal. Even in this case, there is no established rule for setting the threshold. It is accepted to set the threshold as low as possible above the background signal, or manually adjust it in order to obtain the highest coefficient of determination in the standard curve or use inflection points of the second derivatives of the amplification signal. Nevertheless, the threshold should lie in the exponential phase of amplification and the same level should be used for all reactions — at least within the same run.
- *Visually observe the amplification curve.* If the difference between null and peak amplification is less than 0.5 units in the background-subtracted signal (ΔRn), then the amplification is only apparent. Also, amplification shapes that are not sigmoid indicate problems with the PCR that underlie non-reactive reactions. There might be instances where the passive dye is degraded whereas the reported dye remains constant (*drop*) or, conversely, where the reporter dye increases in a non-exponential fashion (*creep*).
- *Check the intercept.* The ideal intercept should be between 33 and 37 cycles. If the intercept is outside this range, the template might have been degraded.
- *Check the efficiency of the reaction.* Since this is linked to the slope (Equation 5), the slope should be between −3.2 and −3.5 for 100% efficiency of amplification.
- *Check the fit of the data used for the standard curve.* The coefficient of determination (*r*²) should be above 98%.

It is also useful to monitor the behavior of the qPCR over time. A plot of the efficiencies versus time should indicate that each run lies between one or two standard deviation from the mean. The following will provide an example.

As for any diagnostic assay, there is also the need to evaluate the effectiveness of qPCR using a series of performance parameters (Fardy, 2009; Kralik and Ricchi, 2017). The limit of detection (or analytical sensitivity) is defined as the minimal concentration of sample that provides a success rate of 95%. For instance, in the setup of a qPCR assay targeting HPV-16 (Marongiu et al., 2014a), a standard dilution (range 10⁰-10⁵ c/µL) was tested 37 times and all reactions with an initial input above 10 c/µL gave a reactive result. Conversely, only 32 samples with an initial concentration of 10⁰ c/µL were positive. By using logistic regression, it was found that the limit of detection (LOD) was 1.7 c/µL (Marongiu et al., 2014b). The following listing will replicate such an instance (Figure 2.4).

While the LOD is valid for both end-point PCR and qPCR, the latter should also be associated with a limit of quantification (LOQ), which is defined as the lowest concentration that gives a CV below 25%. Other parameters include the definition of false negative (FN), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and true positive (TP) reactions, based on the comparison between qPCR and a gold standard that gives a trusted result. The diagnostic sensitivity provides the probability that the qPCR will correctly classify as reactive the positive samples and can be calculated as TP/(TP+FN). Conversely, the diagnostic specificity provides the probability that the qPCR will correctly classify as non-reactive the negative samples, and can be calculated as TN/(TN+FP). The diagnostic sensitivity and specificity should not be confused with the analytical sensitivity (LOD) and specificity (the capability of an assay to react only with the target of choice). Moreover, the accuracy of the qPCR is defined as the probability of correctly classifying the samples: (TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN).

The precision of the qPCR is based upon repeatability and reproducibility; the former is further subdivided into intra-assay and inter-assay repeatability. The intra-assay repeatability indicates the rate of variation between replicates within the same qPCR plate, and the inter-assay repeatability indicates the rate of variation between several qPCR plates. The repeatability is defined as the CV of each dilution and at least 15 replicates should be run. The reproducibility is similar to repeatability but it is performed on different laboratories, often even with different thermal-cyclers and chemistry — in which case it also indicates the robustness of the assay.
