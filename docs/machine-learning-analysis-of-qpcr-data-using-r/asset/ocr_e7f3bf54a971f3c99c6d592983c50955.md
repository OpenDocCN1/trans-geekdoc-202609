In other words, reactions with low efficiency of amplification will give strongly positive result but reactions with too high efficiency of amplification will produce negative results. The implications of having an HIV result either positive or negative need no further explanation. Quantification of low template sample is not trivial, and it has been shown that samples with less than 100 copies are randomly amplified, in a process that is modeled by the Monte Carlo theory, resulting in large fluctuations in the quantification (Karrer et al., 1995). In general, of course, the more accurate the quantification the better. Hence, anything that can affect a proper quantification should be avoided.

A plethora of analytical methods have been developed to overcome the assumptions of the fit-point method, for instance second derivative maximum (Luu-The et al., 2005), sigmoid curve fitting (Liu and Saint, 2002; Rutledge, 2004; Goll et al., 2006; Guescini et al., 2008, 2013) and linear regression (Ramakers et al., 2003; Tichopad et al., 2003). All these proved to be somehow superior to the fit-point method. For instance, the second derivative provided more accurate quantification of low template samples, albeit with a higher intercept, than the fit-point (Luu-The et al., 2005). Even more importantly, these alternative methods dispense the need to set the threshold and baseline values, implementing a user-independent method (Luu-The et al., 2005).

Moreover, qPCR is changing fast due, for instance, to improvements in the processivity of the DNA-pol, the miniaturization of the thermal-cyclers, and the throughput of the assay (Csako, 2006). In particular, the throughput of qPCR has risen from a single tube to 96 wells per plate and, currently, to a few hundred reactions per plate (Kodani et al., 2011; Pripuzova et al., 2012). Plates allocating thousands of reactions are already available (Morrison et al., 2006; Schlesinger et al., 2010). The operators responsible for issuing the qPCR results are, therefore, facing an increased labor burden detrimental to other laboratory duties and that rises the risk of releasing wrong results. In other words, modern qPCR analysis does not only need to be as accurate as possible but also automated to screen thousands of reactions in the shortest time possible. In this context, qPCR data analysis can only be machine-assisted; hence, the analytical methods should be capable of integrating with some machine learning algorithms. The single value (Cq) produced by the fit-point method — but, for instance, also the second derivative method — does not allow for such integration because a single value carries very little information. More advanced methods are needed.
