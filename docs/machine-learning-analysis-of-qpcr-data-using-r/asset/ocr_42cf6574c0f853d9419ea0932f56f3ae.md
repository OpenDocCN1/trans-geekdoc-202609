The breakthrough in PCR was the introduction of signal detection during the course of the reaction itself. Such a ‘trick’ dispensed the used of the EF step, simplifying and speeding up the reaction. A research group composed by Pamela Holland and David Gelfand at Cetus (Holland et al., 1991) found that the 5′→3′ exonuclease activity of the DNA-pol could degrade an oligonucleotide hybridized in front of the enzyme. By binding a fluorescent dye and a quencher at the extremities of the oligonucleotide, such molecule (known as probe) would not radiate light because the photons emitted by the fluorescent molecule will be sequestered by the quencher. But, upon degradation of the probe by the DNA-pol during the extension time of the PCR, the fluorescent dye will be freed from the quencher activity and release photons in the reaction vessel that can be collected by an optical apparatus. At about the same time, the group formed by Russell Higuchi, Robert Griffith and colleagues at Roche reported that some intercalators (that is molecules that bind with high affinity with the DNA) could increase their fluorescence when bound to the dsDNA, so that each amplicon could release a certain amount of light at each cycle (Higuchi et al., 1992). Furthermore, Kenneth Livak, Karin Deetz and colleagues at Perkin-Elmer/Applied Biosystems found that two probes marked with a particular set of fluorescent dyes and hybridized in close proximity could emit light (Heid et al., 1996).
