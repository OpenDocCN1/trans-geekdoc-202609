According to the protocol, several approaches could be used in combination. Once outside of the cell, NAs are easily degraded by nucleases, especially RNA. Thus, the extraction environment usually contains key elements to protect the genetic material. Cations (e.g., Na⁺) stabilize NAs by binding to their negative charges. Chelating agents, such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), remove ions (like Mg²⁺) that are the co-factors of nucleases. Reducing compounds such as β-mercaptoethanol (βMe) alter the disulfide bonds of enzymes (namely nucleases) inhibiting their activity. Proteases (e.g., protease K) are added to remove proteic structures that could sequester NAs.

- *Cleansing.* The cellular debris generated during the disruption of the cells hampers the recovery of NAs. Since the different cellular components have different densities, centrifugation offers the easiest way to separate NAs from other organelles. Density gradient centrifugation (DGC) was established in the 1950s (Meselson et al., 1957). With this method, a low molecular weight (MW) substance (most commonly cesium chloride, CsCl) is spun at high speed inside a test tube, resulting in its increasingly higher density across the length of the tube. Macro-molecules spun together with CsCl will distribute within this gradient in small regions where the centrifugal force neutralizes the density of the medium. The result is that a mixture of macro-molecules such as a cell lysate can be separated into a series of bands, each corresponding to a particular organelle. Coupling DGC with ethidium bromide (EtBr), a molecule that, after specifically binding to the DNA, emits fluorescent light allowing the specific identification of the DNA bands and their recovery by a syringe. DGC-EtBr had been a common laboratory procedure for decades. Although still in use, DGC-EtBr has been replaced by less laborious techniques that rely more on the next two steps of the extraction procedure.

The hybridization between the target NA and a probe is an essential tool for both research and diagnostic. Hybridization can be carried out directly within cells and, if the probe has been linked to a fluorophore, the procedure is known as fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH). FISH is generally assessed by microscopy, which is a low-throughput procedure, but its greatest problem is sensitivity for each cell must contain a large amount of target to produce a visible fluorescence. A solution to the ISH’s shortcoming is electrophoresis (Kroczek, 1993). With electrophoresis, the genomes from a set of cells are fractionated using either physical or chemical approaches. The fragments then migrate when exposed to an electric field, usually on polyacrylamide matrices (but agarose is also popular) to achieve high resolution of the fragments. This version of electrophoresis goes under the name of PAGE. Urea or formamide are used to denature the NAs’ three-dimensional structures, facilitating their migration (Summer et al., 2009; Petrov et al., 2013). The fragments are then stained with intercalator dyes such as EtBr. PAGE provides a series of bands that act as a sort of bar code (fingerprint) that can be used to specifically identify species. PAGE is good for small genomes such as bacterial ones, but it is cumbersome for human genomes. To reduce the number of bands, probes can be used. In this way, only the portions of the target genome that bind to the probe can be visualized. To improve the annealing of the probe with the target NA, the fragments are transferred to a nylon membrane. The probes are marked with enzymes to display the hybrids, and the procedure is known as either Southern (for DNA targets) or Northern (for RNA) blotting (Kroczek, 1993; Southern, 2000).

Since virtually each organism has a portion of its genome that is polymorphic (that is, peculiar to a given species), the amplification of such regions allows to provide the sensitivity needed to run the digestion assay. These polymorphic regions are usually formed by repeat regions whose number is species-specific (van Belkum, 1994). For instance, bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium possess the repetitive extragenic palindrome (REP) and enterobacterial repetitive intergenic consensus (ERIC), whereas humans carry the variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) (Jeffreys et al., 1985), and microsatellite sequences (short stretches of nucleotides repeated thousands of times) (de la Chapelle, 2003) that allow to differentiate one person from another with a tremendous benefit for forensic sciences. Despite its improvements, even blotting might suffer from sensitivity issues. There are two solutions to this shortcoming: either amplify the signal or the target.

Let’s imagine a detection system with a sensitivity of one million arbitrary light units, and a probe that can emit one light unit when bound to the target. Let’s also imagine that there are only 10 targets in a specimen. As is, the binding of the probes will produce only 10 light units, thus the assay will give a negative result. To get a positive signal, either the signal or target itself should, somehow, be multiplied.

One method to achieve signal amplification is that of annealing a linker molecule, containing a binding site to the ROI and several other moieties that are recognized by the probes. For instance, if a linker can bind 10⁵ probes, the hypothetical 10 targets — each with one binding site for the linker — will produce one million light units. One of the best-known methods using this approach is branched DNA (bDNA) and it is used, for instance, in viral diagnostic (Wilber, 1997; Tsongalis, 2006). The bDNA assay is run more-or-less like an ELISA and has a sensitivity comparable to that of PCR (Collins et al., 1997).

Other methods to amplify the signal are based on the direct polymerization of probes (Yan et al., 2014) (Table 1). In this case, each target will be the template for the synthesis of several probes, allowing to quickly reach the threshold for a positive result. These enzymatic methods are carried out at the same temperature and are, therefore, classified as isothermal processes. One of the most popular is the nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA) also known as self-sustained sequence replication (3SR) (Deiman et al., 2002). Like PCR, NASBA uses primers. One contains a tail with a promoter sequence recognized by the DNA-dependent RNA-polymerase (DdRp) isolated from the virus T7. The primer is extended by the RNA-dependent DNA-polymerase (RdDp, or reverse transcriptase) isolated from the avian myeloblastosis virus (AMV). The nascent hybrid DNA/RNA is degraded by E. coli RNase H, which removes the original ssRNA and leaves the newly synthesized ssDNA. The latter anneals with a second primer, whose extension by the reverse transcriptase generates a dsDNA fragment with a complete T7-promoter sequence.
