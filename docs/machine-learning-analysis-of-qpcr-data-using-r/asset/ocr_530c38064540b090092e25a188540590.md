hybridization probes chemistry (or FRET, fluorescence resonance energy transfer). The first intercalator to be used was ethidium bromide but, being carcinogenic, it has been substituted by other harmless dyes such as SYBR Green™, RedSafe™, and EvaGreen™.

The intercalators are cheaper than the probes and, although they are prone to attach to specific sequences, the sequence they bind to are enough common on the DNA strand to confer effective coverage of any amplicon (Bailly and Chaires, 1998). More importantly, intercalators allow to perform melting analysis. By gradually increasing the temperature in the reaction vessel after the end of the PCR itself, the fluorescence emitted by the amplicons decreases because the intercalators are displaced from the DNA. When the temperature reaches the melting point (Tₘ) of the amplicon, the duplex is dissolved very fast and the temperature drops suddenly. The plot of the temperature against the fluorescence is that of a decreasing line that, at the Tₘ, suddenly drops to zero. By taking the first derivative of this curve, the graph is converted into a bell-shaped graph whose peak is Tₘ. In presence of primers dimers or other contaminants, the graph will show more than one peak. The melting analysis, therefore, is used to confirm the specificity of the PCR.

On the other hand, probes allow the simultaneous detection of multiple targets (an approach known as multiplexing) and that further expanded the PCR’s applications. This improved PCR assay was first named ‘real-time’ because the results could be read while the PCR was still ongoing rather than afterwards. Currently it is better known as ‘quantitative’ (qPCR) because it allows the quantification of the initial load of a sample. The next chapter will describe the quantification procedure more in detail.
