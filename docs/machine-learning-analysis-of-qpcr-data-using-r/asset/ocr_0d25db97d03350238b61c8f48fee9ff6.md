The Koch’s definition for molecular biology is particularly relevant because it focuses on elements (the genetic sequences) that are relatively easy to detect. As mentioned, Koch himself was aware that a large group of bacteria was difficult to grow and isolate (these are referred to as fastidious bacteria), and the subsequent discoveries in virology expanded such a concern. Conversely, genetic material can be isolated from a sample regardless of the viability of a micro-organism. Furthermore, the genetic material is common to all living organisms; thus, genetic diseases can be included in the quest for disease diagnosis. For instance, the diagnosis of pathological conditions ranging from cholesterolemia to atherosclerosis and cancer are based on the identification of specific genetic sequences associated with these diseases (Stehbens, 1991; Brown and Goldstein, 1992; Garcion et al., 2009; Hudecova and Chiu, 2017; Sulc et al., 2020). The fact that the genetic signature of each species and even each individual is unique, implies that it is possible to establish the presence of virtually any living organism on a sample relying on the detection of its genome. This was a major leap for the medical and microbiological fields alike because it dispensed the laborious and inefficient requirements to grow and characterize an organism metabolically, morphologically or serologically. All that is needed is to recover an organism’s nucleic acids and check if the recovery was successful.
