The identification of the line was done by visual inspection of the plot as follows. By observing that a line pointing at an intercept of 0.32 MR could split the data in two groups, it is possible to identify a possible slope. Since the difference in MR values passed from about 0.20 at FCN = 20 to 0.29 at FCN = 10, it was possible to estimate the slope of the separating line as Δy/Δx = −0.09/10 = −0.009. ![](img/7ea01e6937e09d043e45c73fff9eaed3_93_0.png)

Figure 3.3. Overview of the support vector machine procedure. The support vector machine (SVM) is a supervised learning model, which means that it must run on pre-classified groups. There were two groups in this case: A (●) and B (○). The algorithm (kernel) finds a separation line between the groups known as a hyperplane (thick line) that maximizes the allocation of samples within their original group. In this case, the hyperplane divides the plot into two parts (upper and lower), locating the most elements of group A in the upper part of the plot and the most elements of group B in the lower part of the plot. To reduce computing labor, the kernel uses only a subset of the elements known as support vectors (highlighted here by squares). Therefore, the procedure reduces to the minimization of the distances between the hyperplane and the support vectors (dotted lines), akin to regression analysis. The model also provides for some flexibility in the classification process, which means that the items within the margins (dashed lines) are ignored during the classification process. The end product of the SVM analysis is that the points above the hyperplane will be classified as belonging to group A. Conversely, those below will be placed into group B.

Another notable aspect of SVM is that the hyperplanes do not have to be straight lines; they can instead be curves and extend in three dimensions. Tridimensional clusters can be linearized using the so-called ‘kernel trick’, which also allow to distinguish nonlinearly separable groups. The most common kernels used by SVM are linear (used for the linearly separable cases), polynomial, sigmoid, and Gaussian. Moreover, SVM allows for the definition of more than one group. Looking again at Figure 3.3, it is evident that using a curve capable of ‘wrapping’ the positive samples will avoid classifying the negative samples at high FCN as reactive. Furthermore, a classification procedure that could identify the samples designated as ‘uncertain’ in Figure 3.1-3.2 would substantially simplify the validation process by allowing the operator to focus solely on unsatisfactory reactions.

An attempt to apply SVM to the analysis of qPCR data was carried out (Marongiu et al., 2021a). The re-analysis of the 15,744 previously classified by maxRatio plus EM filtering (Marongiu et al., 2016a) showed that the combination maxRatio/SVM increased the accuracy of detection of positive and negative reactions from 99.6% to 100%, and the PPV from 96.5% to 99.9% in comparison to the fit-point method. In addition, the combination maxRatio/SVM detected 35 samples as suboptimal, implying that only 0.2% of the whole data set required actual validation through visual inspection. With a PPV of nearly 100%, the remaining reactions could be validated without the operator’s involvement, drastically reducing the qPCR workload. The next session will reproduce such analytical approach with a novel set of reactions.
