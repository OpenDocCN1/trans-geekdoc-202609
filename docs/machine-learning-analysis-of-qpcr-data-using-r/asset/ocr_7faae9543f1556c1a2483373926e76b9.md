Moreover, micro-organism — especially RNA viruses — mutate at a fast rate, so that high specificity might hamper the sensitivity of the assay. PCR’s kinetics (2^n) in also simple, allowing to calculate the number of amplicons at each cycle n with relative ease. Whatever the reason, PCR is the method of choice for many laboratories around the world, both for diagnostic and research purposes. The improvement of the assay to provide a result in real time gave rise to a process that allows the exact quantification of the target molecule. This assay is known as quantitative PCR (qPCR).

# Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) Assay

The simplest way to determine whether a PCR has been successful is to separate the amplicons by electrophoresis (EF). This procedure has the advantage of showing directly whether the amplicons are of the expected size or if the primers have amplified an unwanted region. A first downside is that a subsequent step — that is: EF — is required after the PCR itself. A second downsize is that the PCR has a limited amount of components, thus, it cannot go on forever. Since the number of cycles is typically much more than those required to reach a visible signal, the result is that, at the end of the reaction, all samples will display virtually the same level of signal regardless of the initial template input. This principle is described in more in detail in the following paragraphs.

Liu and Saint (Liu and Saint, 2002a) used the following equation to relate the initial copies of a samples with the amplification cycle:

X_n = X0(1 + E)^n
(1)

where X_n is the amount of target’s copies at the amplification cycle n, X0 is the initial target’s load, and E the efficiency of reaction or, in other words, the rate of target duplication, which is assumed to be 100% — in other words, each template is replicated according to the 2^n kinetics.
