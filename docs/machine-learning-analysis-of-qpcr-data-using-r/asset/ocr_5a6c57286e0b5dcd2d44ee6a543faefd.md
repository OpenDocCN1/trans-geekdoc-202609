+   - Allelic replacement should lead to the restoration of the pathogenic phenotype.

This kind of approach even allowed to associate viral infection with several types of cancer (Pagano et al., 2004). A more modern definition in the microbiological domain takes into account the concept of dysbiosis, that is the modification of the prevalence of commensal species found in healthy individuals (Petersen and Round, 2014). In fact, the recent advancements brought forward by microbiome studies based on massively parallel sequencing (MPS) have shown that pathogens such as norovirus (referred to as pathobionts because they are simultaneously pathogens and symbionts) are already present in a patient but do not cause a disease under normal conditions (Teunis et al., 2015). It is the imbalance of the microbial community as a whole coupled with immunological disorders that trigger the progress towards disease. This view has been included into modified Koch’s postulates (Neville et al., 2018; Vonaesch et al., 2018):

+   - Similar dysbiotic conditions are observed in hosts with similar health conditions.
+   - The dysbiotic microorganisms can be retrieved from the affected hosts.
+   - The introduction of the retrieved microorganisms in germ-free animals, in combination with similar diet and environment should lead to the same health condition of the original hosts.
+   - The composition of the dysbiotic microorganisms remains fairly stable.

The introduction of bacterial species associated with intestinal chronic inflammation into the gut of healthy mice resulted in the development of colitis in these recipients (Schaubeck et al., 2016). Nevertheless, the recovery of a target (either microbial or genetic) in a patient’s sample indicates an association between the target itself and the patient’s disease, but alone does not prove causation.
