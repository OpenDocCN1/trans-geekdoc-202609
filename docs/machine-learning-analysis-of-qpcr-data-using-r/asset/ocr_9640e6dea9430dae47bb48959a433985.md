Figure 1.3. The concept of PCR threshold. The qPCR generates increasing levels of light and a threshold (cut-off) value can be defined to separate the background noise from the actual signals. The axes must be multiplied by 1000 (× 1000). A. The different PCR phases. Each dot represents the data collected at each amplification cycle. In this case, the ideal number of target molecules is considered. The PCR can be subdivided into three phases. In the initial step, the multiplication of target molecules is not enough to generate a clear signal. In the second phase, the amplicons (species a in Figure 1.1) are amplified exponentially and taken over as the reaction vessel’s main species. The light signal, therefore, increases rapidly. In the third phase, the depletion of reagents and the accumulation of inhibitors reduces the processivity of the DNA-pol, and the reaction reaches a plateau. B. The PCR profile is the same regardless of the initial template input. Using simulated data from samples with increasing amounts of the template shows that the number of templates increases until reaching a plateau value. At the end of the reaction, all samples will contain the same number of amplicons (arrow). The difference between samples is simply the time needed to reach the plateau: samples with higher template input will remain longer in the plateau phase than samples with low input. C. Blow up of panel B, showing how the different samples will reach a hypothetical threshold level (10⁶ molecules) at different times. Samples with high template input will reach the threshold faster than the samples with low input.
