- *Knowledge of the sequence of the region of interest.* The role of the synthetic primer is not just in starting the polymerization process but also to provide specificity: the proper selection of the primer can make the difference between a good and a bad assay. One of the most important characteristics of a good assay is that of specificity: the assay should give a positive result only if the target is present in a sample. Cross-reaction with a sequence similar (but not related) to the target’s will render the assay unreliable. The probability of identifying a stretch of n nucleotides in a genome is obtained as follows. First one determines the probability of identifying a binding site, K, with the formula (Nei and Li, 1979): K = (g/2)^{G+C} × 2^{-1}(1-g)^{A+T} where g is the relative frequency of G and C in the sequence, and G+C and A+T are the numbers of these nucleotides in the sequence. Then the actual probability n of finding the sequence in a given genome of length N is given by: n = DNK, where D is the diploidicity of the organism.

The design of primers needs to follow some rules:

- Their length should be about 20 nucleotides;
- They should not contain stable hairpin structures;
- They should not contain stretches of homopolymers;
- They should not contain more than 50% GC nucleotides;

- The melting temperature of the primers (Tₘ) should preferably be about 55°C; Tm can be roughly calculated by the Wallace rule (Green and Sambrook, 2019): Tₘ = 2(A+T) + 4(G+C), where A+T and G+C are the number of these nucleotides in the primer. More precise Tₘ can be obtained from specific calculators available online.
- The difference in the Tₘ of the two primers should not be more than 5°C;

- The 3′ of the primers should not be complementary to avoid formation of dimers;
- The 3′ of the primers should not contain too many cgs;
- The primers’ sequences should be unique for the target to avoid non-specific amplification.

## Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) Assay

The simplest way to determine whether a PCR has been successful is to separate the amplicons by electrophoresis (EF). This procedure has the advantage of showing directly whether the amplicons are of the expected size or if the primers have amplified an unwanted region. A first downside is that a subsequent step — that is: EF — is required after the PCR itself. A second downside is that the PCR has a limited amount of components, thus, it cannot go on forever. Since the number of cycles is typically much more than those required to reach a visible signal, the result is that, at the end of the reaction, all samples will display virtually the same level of signal regardless of the initial template input. This principle is described in more detail in the following paragraphs.

Liu and Saint (Liu and Saint, 2002a) used the following equation to relate the initial copies of a samples with the amplification cycle:

X_n = X0(1 + E)^n
(1)

where X_n is the amount of target’s copies at the amplification cycle n, X0 is the initial target’s load, and E the efficiency of reaction or, in other words, the rate of target duplication, which is assumed to be 100% — in other words, each template is replicated according to the 2^n kinetics.

It is possible to replicate this with the following script [next page]:

```
LiuSaint_model <- function(initCopy, efficiency) {
  A <- vector(initCopy)
  for (i in 1:44) {
    A[i+1] <- A[i]*(1+efficiency)
  }
  return(A)
}
A1_0 <- 10
A2_0 <- 100
A3_0 <- 1000
A4_0 <- 10000
E <- 1
A1 <- LiuSaint_model(A1_0, E)
A2 <- LiuSaint_model(A2_0, E)
A3 <- LiuSaint_model(A3_0, E)
A4 <- LiuSaint_model(A4_0, E)
plot(1:20, A1[1:20], xlab="Amplification cycle", ylab="Number of molecules", type = "l", lwd = 1)
points(1:20, A2[1:20], type = "l", lwd = 2)
points(1:20, A3[1:20], type = "l", lwd = 3)
points(1:20, A4[1:20], type = "l", lwd = 4)
legend("topleft", legend = c(10, 100, 1000, 10000), title = "Input", lwd = c(1,2,3,4))
```
