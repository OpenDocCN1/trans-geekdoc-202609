
Such proofs were designed for bacterial diseases but it became soon evident that not all infectious diseases could fulfill them. Koch himself had to rectify his statements (Koch, 1890):

We are therefore fully warranted in affirming that if even only the first two requirements of the proof are fulfilled, the causal connection between parasite and disease is validly proved. Starting from this basis, we must admit that a series of diseases in which the experimental infection of animals has hitherto failed or been only partially successful are, in spite of this, to be regarded as parasitic.

Not only are some pathogenic bacteria difficult to grow but, with the rise of the field of virology, researchers and physicians discovered that pathogenic microorganisms could not be isolated directly but needed supportive cells to be grown; the encounter with prions made the picture even more complicated (Rivers, 1937; Fredricks and Relman, 1996). The aforementioned rules were named ‘postulates’ in 1884 by one of the most renowned of Koch’s pupils, Friedrich Löffler, when presenting his results on the isolation of the etiological agent of diphtheria, Corynebacterium diphtheriæ (Walker et al., 2006; Gradmann, 2014). Koch's postulates can be summarized in the following points:

- The pathogenic micro-organism must be present in all diseased hosts.
- The pathogenic micro-organism must be isolated from the diseased host.
- The inoculation of the isolated pathogenic micro-organism in a healthy host must lead to the same disease of the donor.
- The pathogenic micro-organism must be re-isolated from the deceased new host.

These postulates have changed several times during the years to agree with the newest discoveries in microbiology (Evans, 1976). For instance, with the advent of molecular biology, their focus passed from whole bacteria to their genetic sequences (Falkow, 1988):

- A given phenotype should be associated with a pathology.
- The specific inactivation of the gene associated with the pathogenic phenotype should lead to a measurable loss of the pathogenicity.
- Allelic replacement should lead to the restoration of the pathogenic phenotype.

This kind of approach even allowed to associate viral infection with several types of cancer (Pagano et al., 2004). A more modern definition in the microbiological domain takes into account the concept of dysbiosis, that is the modification of the prevalence of commensal species found in healthy individuals (Petersen and Round, 2014). In fact, the recent advancements brought forward by microbiome studies based on massively parallel sequencing (MPS) have shown that pathogens such as norovirus (referred to as pathobionts because they are simultaneously pathogens and symbionts) are already present in a patient but do not cause a disease under normal conditions (Teunis et al., 2015). It is the imbalance of the microbial community as a whole coupled with immunological disorders that trigger the progress towards disease. This view has been included into modified Koch’s postulates (Neville et al., 2018; Vonaesch et al., 2018):

- Similar dysbiotic conditions are observed in hosts with similar health conditions.
- The dysbiotic microorganisms can be retrieved from the affected hosts.
- The introduction of the retrieved microorganisms in germ-free animals, in combination with similar diet and environment should lead to the same health condition of the original hosts.
- The composition of the dysbiotic microorganisms remains fairly stable.

The introduction of bacterial species associated with intestinal chronic inflammation into the gut of healthy mice resulted in the development of colitis in these recipients (Schaubeck et al., 2016). Nevertheless, the recovery of a target (either microbial or genetic) in a patient’s sample indicates an association between the target itself and the patient’s disease, but alone does not prove causation. In the 1960s, Austin Hill proposed a series of criteria that must be fulfilled to prove that a candidate pathogen causes a disease, quite extending Koch’s postulates (Hill, 1965):

- Strength. There must be a large body of circumstantial evidence (that is, epidemiological) demonstrating the presence of the target (that is, a factor) and the disease, just as stated by the Koch’s postulates.
- *Consistency.* The epidemiological evidence must be demonstrated by different people, in different regions and periods to avoid any bias. This is particularly important for rare diseases.
- *Specificity.* The factor must be associated to a specific disease rather than a plethora of conditions. However, some diseases might have multiple causes and a given factor might cause more than one disease.
- *Temporality.* The factor must precede the disease just like the cause precedes the effect. Hence, infection must occur before the disease, as stated by Koch.
- *Gradient.* A higher exposure to a factor should be related to higher frequency (or worsening of the outcomes) of the disease.
- *Plausibility.* The causality between exposure to a factor and the development of the disease must be explained by a logical model.
- *Coherence.* The model should account for all the occurrences associated with a factor, without excluding the data that does not fit it.
- *Experimentation.* The model should be tested in the laboratory to assure its reliability. This is particularly relevant because statistical analysis can provide a measurement of the strength of association but cannot prove the causality between factor and disease.
- *Analogy.* The mode of action of a factor is usually similar to another factor. For instance, both oncogenic papillomavirus and JC virus impair the onco-suppressor p53 and both can cause cancer. These viruses encode proteins that interact with p53 and, although the proteins are different, they induce analogous molecular pathways.

Despite the practical issues involved in the association of an etiological factor (either a micro-organism, a protein, or a gene) to a disease, once the link has been recognized, the goal of diagnostics is to identify the presence of this factor in people affected by a disease to begin an effective treatment. For instance, the diagnosis of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) is based on the laboratory confirmation of the presence of the viral genome in a specimen (Loeffelholz and Tang, 2020) and so does the diagnosis of hemorrhagic fever, malaria, anthrax and tuberculosis, to list just a few (Sweeney et al., 2011; Long, 2015; Kaushik et al., 2016; Mukkala et al., 2018).

Koch's definition for molecular biology is particularly relevant because it focuses on elements (the genetic sequences) that are relatively easy to detect. As mentioned, Koch himself was aware that a large group of bacteria was difficult to grow and isolate (these are referred to as fastidious bacteria), and the subsequent discoveries in virology expanded such a concern. Conversely, genetic material can be isolated from a sample regardless of the viability of a micro-organism. Furthermore, the genetic material is common to all living organisms; thus, genetic diseases can be included in the quest for disease diagnosis. For instance, the diagnosis of pathological conditions ranging from cholesterolemia to atherosclerosis and cancer are based on the identification of specific genetic sequences associated with these diseases (Stehbens, 1991; Brown and Goldstein, 1992; Garcion et al., 2009; Hudecova and Chiu, 2017; Sulc et al., 2020). The fact that the genetic signature of each species and even each individual is unique, implies that it is possible to establish the presence of virtually any living organism on a sample relying on the detection of its genome. This was a major leap for the medical and microbiological fields alike because it dispensed the laborious and inefficient requirements to grow and characterize an organism metabolically, morphologically or serologically. All that is needed is to recover an organism’s nucleic acids and check if the recovery was successful.

## Methods to Collect Genetic Material

Having settled that genetic material plays a central role in diagnostics, it follows that it needs to be identified in the specimens. Genetic material is essentially a synonym for nucleic acids (NAs), which are either deoxyribonucleic acids (DNA) or ribonucleic acids (RNA).
