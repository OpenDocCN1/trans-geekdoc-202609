According to the protocol, several approaches could be used in combination. Once outside of the cell, NAs are easily degraded by nucleases, especially RNA. Thus, the extraction environment usually contains key elements to protect the genetic material. Cations (e.g., Na⁺) stabilize NAs by binding to their negative charges. Chelating agents, such as ethylendiamine-tetra-acetic acid (EDTA), remove ions (like Mg⁺²) that are the co-factors of nucleases. Reducing compounds such as β-mercaptoethanol (βMe) alter the disulfide bonds of enzymes (namely nucleases) inhibiting their activity. Proteases (e.g., protease K) are added to remove proteic structures that could sequester NAs.

+   - *Binding.* NAs are adsorbed to solid structures such as polymers, beads or gels. The rest of the cellular debris will have less affinity with the solid structures and will be washed away. Structures containing poly-A moieties can specifically immobilize mRNAs.
+   - *Cleansing.* The cellular debris generated during the disruption of the cells hampers the recovery of NAs. Since the different cellular components have different densities, centrifugation offers the easiest way to separate NAs from other organelles. Density gradient centrifugation (DGC) was established in the 1950s (Meselson et al., 1957). With this method, a low molecular weight (MW) substance (most commonly cesium chloride, CsCl) is spun at high speed inside a test tube, resulting in its increasingly higher density across the length of the tube. Macro-molecules spun together with CsCl will distribute within this gradient in small regions where the centrifugal force neutralizes the density of the medium. The result is that a mixture of macro-molecules such as a cell lysate can be separated into a series of bands, each corresponding to a particular organelle. Coupling DGC with ethydium bromide (EtBr), a molecule that, after specifically binding to the DNA, emits fluorescent light allowing the specific identification of the DNA bands and their recovery by a syringe. DGC-EtBr had been a common laboratory procedure for decade. Although still in use, DGC-EtBr has been replaced by less laborious techniques that rely more on the next two step of the extraction procedure.
