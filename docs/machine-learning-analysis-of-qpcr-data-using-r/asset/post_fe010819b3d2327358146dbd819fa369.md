
To account for all the characteristics that define a given qPCR assay and allow a direct comparison among different assays, Minimum information for publication of quantitative real-time PCR experiments (MIQE) is required — although not yet widely adopted (Bustin et al., 2009). Such information includes not only the primers’ sequences but also many of the parameters listed in this section.

## The Issues of the Fit-Point Method

The fit-point is intuitive and easy to implement. Nevertheless, despite its wide application, the fit-point method suffers from some drawbacks (Yang and Rothman, 2004). It assumes, for instance, that the efficiency of amplification is the same between the different reactions and constant throughout the course of the assay (Liu and Saint, 2002b). Even small differences in amplification efficiency can lead to tremendous errors in quantification that are oblivious to the fit-point method (Rutledge, 2004). Furthermore, Equations 2.5 and 2.6 hold only when the efficiency is kept constant (Rutledge and Côté, 2003).

To give an idea of the error in measurement, let’s calculate the initial copy numbers of a certain ROI under different values of amplification efficiency. From Equation 4, the initial copy numbers are linked to the copy number at a certain amplification cycle in a way that is dependent on the efficiency of amplification: log(N0) = log(Nt) − log(E+1)Cq. Based on the preceding example, let’s assume that 10⁶ ROI copies were reached at Cq = 9.96, and let’s calculate N0 for efficiencies of 100, 99.9, 99, 90, and 110 percent:

```r
Nt <- 1000000

Cq <- 9.96

E <- c(1.000, 0.999, 0.990, 0.900, 1.100)

log_N0 <- -((log(E+1)*Cq)) + log(Nt)

N0 <- 10^log_N0

R0 <- N0/N0[1]

percent_increase <- round(100 * ((N0-N0[1])/abs(N0[1])), 3)
```

```text
log_N0
> [1] 6.911765 6.916746 6.961690 7.422646 6.425815

N0
> [1] 8161400 8255548 9155658 26463413 2665720

R0
> [1] 1.0000000 1.0115358 1.1218245 3.2425092 0.3266254

percent_increase
> [1] 0.000 1.154 12.182 224.251 -67.337
```

That is, a drop of 0.1% and 10% in the efficiency can lead to a copy number overestimation of 1 and 224 percent over full efficiency. Conversely, reactions where the efficiency of amplification is above 100% lead to underestimation of the actual copy numbers. Thus, an efficiency of 110% conveys 67% less copy numbers than by considering the reaction fully efficient. To put such shift in quantification in context, let’s consider the following example. The typical limit of detection of HIV VL is about 40 copies per millilitre (c/mL) of blood (Adams et al., 2019). Let’s assume a sample with 35 c/mL and efficiencies of amplification of 99 and 110 percent, respectively. Using the functions PCR and find_cycle previously defined, it is possible to estimate the expected copy numbers based on a threshold of 10^6 light units:

```r
d <- 2
K <- 10^13
A1_0 <- 35
A1 <- PCR(A1_0, d, K)
a1 <- which(A1 > 1000000)[1]
cq_1 <- find_cycle(A1, 1:45, a1)
cq_1
```

```text
> [1] 16.60976
```

Hence, the returned copy numbers are:

```r
E <- c(0.990, 1.100)
log_N0 = -((log(E+1)*cq_1))+log(Nt)
N0 <- 10^log_N0

```

```text
> [1] 243.08398 31.05318
```

In other words, reactions with low efficiency of amplification will give strongly positive result but reactions with too high efficiency of amplification will produce negative results. The implications of having an HIV result either positive or negative need no further explanation. Quantification of low template sample is not trivial, and it has been shown that samples with less than 100 copies are randomly amplified, in a process that is modeled by the Monte Carlo theory, resulting in large fluctuations in the quantification (Karrer et al., 1995). In general, of course, the more accurate the quantification the better. Hence, anything that can affect a proper quantification should be avoided.

A plethora of analytical methods have been developed to overcome the assumptions of the fit-point method, for instance second derivative maximum (Luu-The et al., 2005), sigmoid curve fitting (Liu and Saint, 2002; Rutledge, 2004; Goll et al., 2006; Guescini et al., 2008, 2013) and linear regression (Ramakers et al., 2003; Tichopad et al., 2003). All these proved to be somewhat superior to the fit-point method. For instance, the second derivative provided more accurate quantification of low template samples, albeit with a higher intercept, than the fit-point (Luu-The et al., 2005). Even more importantly, these alternative methods dispense with the need to set the threshold and baseline values, implementing a user-independent method (Luu-The et al., 2005).

Moreover, qPCR is changing fast due, for instance, to improvements in the processivity of the DNA-pol, the miniaturization of the thermal-cyclers, and the throughput of the assay (Csako, 2006). In particular, the throughput of qPCR has risen from a single tube to 96 wells per plate and, currently, to a few hundred reactions per plate (Kodani et al., 2011; Pripuzova et al., 2012). Plates allocating thousands of reactions are already available (Morrison et al., 2006; Schlesinger et al., 2010). The operators responsible for issuing the qPCR results are, therefore, facing an increased labor burden detrimental to other laboratory duties and that raises the risk of releasing wrong results. In other words, modern qPCR analysis does not only need to be as accurate as possible but also automated to screen thousands of reactions in the shortest time possible. In this context, qPCR data analysis can only be machine-assisted; hence, the analytical methods should be capable of integrating with some machine learning algorithms. The single value (Cq) produced by the fit-point method — but, for instance, also by the second derivative method — does not allow for such integration because a single value carries very little information. More advanced methods are needed.

## maxRatio

The *maxRatio* algorithm has been introduced as an alternative to the fit-point method to determine whether a reaction is truly reactive and to produce a more reliable quantification (Shain and Clemens, 2008). The *maxRatio* algorithm is currently included in the Abbott Molecular *m2000 RealTime System* to identify reactive results. The *maxRatio* algorithm is computationally simple, for it considers the ratio between the amplification signal *A* at the cycle *n* and *n-1*:

$$
R_n = \frac{A_n}{A_{n-1}} - 1.
$$

(2.7)

In the absence of amplification, the signal is constant; thus, $A_n=A_{n-1}$, $A_n/A_{n-1}=1$, and the reduction of one unit in Equation 2.7 forces $R$ to zero. $R$ is not only dimensionless but also self-normalized. In fact, by decomposing $A$ into a part related to the actual amplification ($a$), and a part accounting for noise or background ($b$), it is immediately observable that $(a'b)/(ab) = a'/a$, where $a'$ and $a$ are short for $a_n$ and $a_{n-1}$. The noise $b$ is equal in both the dividend and divisor because it comes from the same reaction. Unlike the fit-point method, therefore, *maxRatio* inherently accounts for individual amplification inhibition overcoming one of the major pitfalls of PCR quantification. $R$ can be computed on either baseline-corrected ($\Delta R_n$) or raw fluorescent signal; the latter allows for a better evaluation since it does not cover up possible inhibition in the reaction.

Whereas the qPCR amplification curve built using the fit-point method is sigmoid, the *maxRatio* transform is Gaussian. The following listing will prove it using data from a PCR targeting *Campylobacter jejuni* previously analyzed by *maxRatio* (Marongiu, 2016a, 2016b).

Listing 2.6

```r
maxRatio <- function(A) {
  n = A[2:length(A)]
  n_minusOne = A[1:length(A)-1]
  R <- (n/n_minusOne) - 1
  return(R)
}
```
