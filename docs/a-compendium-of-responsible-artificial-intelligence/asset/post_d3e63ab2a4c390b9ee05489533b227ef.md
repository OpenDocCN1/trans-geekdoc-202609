## Accountability and Responsibility

### Who Bears the Blame when AI-driven Financial Decisions Go Awry?

The issue of accountability and responsibility jumps out very clearly in the vague sphere of AI-generated finance, specifically when the outcome of decisions based on financial AI goes haywire. Most of the ethical debates surrounding the application of AI in finance center on the complexities involved when advanced technology is blamed for issues. These debates are complicated by the various facets of the algorithms, the data, and the people involved. AI enhancement of almost every aspect concerning financial activities, for instance, decision-making regarding fraud detection, withdrawal limits for credit cards, optimal stimulation of investor behavior and forms of credit application, has led to many sophisticated. The same issue introduces additional complications when identifying culpable parties if an error occurs. In the case of AI-based decision-making, the players' network includes data scientists, algorithm creators, manifolds of the realm of finance, and AI systems, as opposed to conventional decision-making methods in which accountability rests with the precise assignment of individuals or departments.

Attribution is one of the most daunting tasks in AI-driven finance, owing to the ‘black box’ feature, which is commonplace with several AI models. Most deep learning algorithms are called ‘black boxes’ due to their inherent sophistication, and even the most knowledgeable practitioner finds them counterintuitive. Many researchers focus on deep learning as a complicated phenomenon to define. This complexity is underlined in a study by [29], who explains the issues related to accountability in algorithmic transparency concerning such systems.

A further aspect that aggravates the practice of placing blame on finance is that AI systems are dynamic and evolving. As such, AI algorithms receive more information and get revised; especially those that use machine learning principles. Consequently, this implies that an algorithm that works effectively at a particular time can begin malfunctioning due to alteration of the conditions in the underlying data. The question arises: who gets to supervise and revise these systems? The work of [30] tackles the problem of accountability in such dynamic and automatic systems. It suggests that there is a need for oversight and verification all the time.

The legal environment, on the other hand, defines and assigns responsibility in AI-driven finance. Specific policies include the tectonic GDPR that incorporates notions such as the ‘right to explanation’, meaning that users deserve to understand any decisions made by AI systems that affect them. Thus, this regulation anticipates a change in the accountability burden for the specific corporations involved and those who produce and provide services based on AI systems. A paper by [31] tackles some of the aspects of such regulation, including AI accountability, justifying such regulation by the fact that law must adjust to change in technology. The question of responsibility in the case of the rogue efficiency of an AI in financial services posits an apparent reality: it cannot be an individualistic proposition. Rather, such a method entails coordination with and between the financial institutions that employ AI systems, the developers of those systems, the regulators with the systems, and the users of systems. Lastly, a framework for accountability in the AI-driven finance landscape encompasses precise modes of operation and expectations of consistency between AI systems and surveillance, traceability, reinforcement, and judicial context.

## The Role of Human Oversight in AI Systems

In the case of AI-based finance, especially, the question of who oversees AI applications is tied to the broader questions of accountability and responsibility. This is important because such institutions are increasingly turning to the use of AI for different processes within their operations, such as making evaluations concerning creditworthiness using a variety of measures and deciding on algorithmic trades. Such a need comes from both the understanding of the imperfection of the AI systems and the necessity of the human touch in ensuring that risks are contained, and ethical concerns observed. All the above are significant reasons why human oversight in finance utilizing artificial intelligence is intertwined among many activities. First, it alleviates the limitations and biases that cannot be entirely eradicated in AI systems. The learning algorithms in the field of artificial intelligence, and more so machine learning, can be trained to perform optimally only on the right data sets loaded for training. Mistakes may also arise in these AI systems due to biased or incomplete training, which puts such systems at risk of making biased and unreasonable decisions. Typically, such bias correction endeavors will require some level of human supervision. Angwin et al. (2016) note the importance of a human check on AI systems by looking into how bias operates in the algorithm that decides the fate of an offender who must be jailed. The third reason is that human presence or interaction is required to contextualize the complex data created by the AI systems. This is especially true about the usage of AI in applications involving deep learning where several AI models are just 'black boxes'. This opacity poses a significant problem in the finance industry, where such decisions should be communicated and 'internally' justified. Human interpretations aid such decisions to ensure they fit within ethical and legal frameworks. Burell et al. [32] discuss the problems of understanding 'black box' systems and the need for human beings in such processes.

Moreover, reviewing the processes is important for ensuring trust in AI-aided financial designs. In the finance industry, trust is essential, yet opaque AI solutions can undermine this trust. AI systems can be deployed safely and efficiently in finance by assuring clients, and other stakeholders that AI decisions are not being made in a vacuum and that human reviewers validate AI decisions. According to the report by the European Banking Authority [33] the human element remains crucial to the success of AI in providing financial services. Responsibility for adherence to regulatory provisions also lies with human involvement. There are many regulatory frameworks for financial institutions, so compliance with such laws using AI systems is a challenge. Professionals need to make sense of the set guidelines and ensure that such systems and all designs comply. Douglas W. Arner [34] assesses the problems of artificial intelligence regulation in Euromoney 2016 in the development of human monitoring mechanisms. In finance operated by AI, concern with governance is an imperative process in ensuring that the use of AI is ethical, equitable, and lawful. It is a mix of compliance, innovation, and subjective assessment. As advancements in AI occur and extend within the scope of financial services, the human aspect of regulation in achieving justice with AI practicability will be more relevant.
