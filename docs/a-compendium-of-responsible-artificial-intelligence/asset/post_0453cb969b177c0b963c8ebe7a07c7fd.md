## The Need for Interpretable Models in Finance

The importance of interpretable modelling has grown significantly for AI use applications in finance, especially for presenting results and understanding model complexity. Financial decision-making is deeply intricate and involves numerous factors, making it insufficient to comprehend why AI made specific choices; there are also legal and ethical frameworks to consider. The following review, grounded in contemporary academic literature, explores the role of time series forecasting in the finance sector, the reasons regulators demand interpretable models, and the challenges and best practices for adopting and implementing interpretability.

## The Imperative for Interpretability

However, this desire to provide interpretability to the AI models applied to finance is technical as opposed to trust, compliance, and ethical decision-making. One of the constraints preventing the everyday usage of deep networks in decision-making is the unexplainability of their “black box” models. This offers an innovative strategy for enhancing understandability and trust in deep learning architectures by combining interpretable modules and visualization techniques [24]. They use saliency maps, feature attribution, and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) as tools within optimal settings and point out that rendering a model more interpretable without deleterious effects on its performance is possible. This study emphasizes the need for efforts to create dependable and understandable AI systems, including spatial reasoning capabilities, especially in sensitive applications where understanding and trust in AI decisions are paramount.

## Challenges in Achieving Interpretability

It is well known that understanding AI models is not an easy task, especially within the fast-paced financial sector. One concept discussed in the [25] study is hybrid interpretable models, which address the need to handle models that can be both simple and complex. Their work focuses on such models’ theory, taxonomy, and methods and underlines performance versus transparency. To the best of our knowledge, this work provides a fresh PAC approach to strengthening our belief in hybrid models, suggesting that there should be a defined acceptable limit for the information a model provides, or that a hybrid model could be as effective as or better than a completely opaque model. In contrast, only some information about the model is provided, and more helpful information is withheld.

## Striking a Balance between Privacy and Customization

One of the most challenging moral issues resulting from the interaction of AI, finance, and general socio-cultural activities is ensuring privacy and customization. The very introduction of AI in financial services has made it possible to provide unprecedented levels of customization for end users of financial products and advisory services. However, gathering and processing huge personal data and making people quite tangled up about privacy issues is necessary to achieve this kind of personal effect. The lure of personalization in the financial services industry cannot be downplayed. Based on data insights, AI models can better comprehend and anticipate individual customer needs, make risk assessments, and provide the required financial services more oriented to a customer’s economic activities and aspirations. It isn’t just about improving CX; this level of personalization creates a competitive advantage and operational efficiency within the finance industry. Nevertheless, there is a clear tradeoff to personal privacy. To run such AIs, there is a need for massive data, including sensitive financial data, financial habits and behaviours, and personal information that can compromise privacy if abused.
