The apprehension regarding some of these issues has been somewhat served by legislation such as the GDPR. These laws focus on protecting the customer's data and require financial institutions to observe certain principles of data minimization, purpose limitation and transparency. Nevertheless, performing these tasks in the context of AI-based personalization is easier said than done. The nature of AI algorithms being dynamic and often obscured makes compliance and management of the users' confidence challenging.

In addition, the issue of informed consent on the part of the customer in the context of financial services is changing—the process of providing consent to consent in money transactions used to be very simple. But now, with artificial intelligence, where data is used beyond the initial purpose of completing a cash transaction, the definition of consent is changing. Clients need to be aware of what information would be gathered about them, the reasons that justify the need for such knowledge, and the possible effects, such as privacy invasions, the knowledge may prompt. This balancing act can and should be done in AI finance, where personalization and privacy must be sacrificed. Here, it will not be done as a hack but as an alternative approach. Investment into intelligible AI platforms by financial institutions where clients can identify how their data is harnessed will be paramount. This requires appropriate communication plans, structural and organizational measures, and tools to enhance information privacy. Additionally, there is a need for ongoing dialogue between regulators, financial institutions, and technology developers to guarantee that customization powered by AI is utilized to its full potential without violating people's right to privacy.

# Accountability and Responsibility

# Who Bears the Blame when AI-driven Financial Decisions Go Awry?

The issue of accountability and responsibility jumps out very clearly in the vague sphere of AI-generated finance, specifically when the outcome of decisions based on financial AI goes haywire. Most of the ethical debates surrounding the application of AI in finance center on the complexities involved when advanced technology is blamed for issues. These debates are complicated by the various facets of the algorithms, the data, and the people involved. AI enhancement of almost every aspect concerning financial activities, for instance, decision-making regarding fraud detection, withdrawal limits for credit cards, optimal stimulation of investor behavior and forms of credit application, has led to many sophisticated. The same issue introduces additional complications when identifying culpable parties if an error occurs. In the case of AI-based decision-making, the players' network includes data scientists, algorithm creators, manifolds of the realm of finance, and AI systems, as opposed to conventional decision-making methods in which accountability rests with the precise assignment of individuals or departments.
