## 1.1.1 Transistor Action

The nanoelectronic devices are the building blocks of modern electronics. The transistor is a device used to control the flow of electric current in a circuit. The most common type of transistor is the Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor (MOSFET). It is a three-terminal device that uses electric field to control current. The three terminals are the source, drain, and gate. The gate is used to control the current flowing between the source and drain. When a voltage is applied to the gate, it creates an electric field that affects the conductivity of the channel between the source and drain. This allows the transistor to act as a switch or an amplifier. The MOSFET is used in many electronic applications. There are two main types of MOSFETs: n-MOSFET and p-MOSFET. In an n-MOSFET, the majority carriers are electrons, while in a p-MOSFET, the majority carriers are holes. The operation of the MOSFET depends on the threshold voltage, which is the minimum gate voltage required to turn the device on. When the gate voltage is below the threshold voltage, the channel is not formed and no current flows. When the gate voltage is above the threshold voltage, the channel is formed and current flows between the source and drain.

## 1.1.2 Advent of CMOS

CMOS was first introduced in 1963 by Jean Hoerni and Robert Ng at Fairchild Semiconductor. It is a technology that uses both n-type and p-type MOSFETs to create logic gates. The main advantage of CMOS is its low power consumption. This is because CMOS circuits only consume significant power when they are switching. In the static state, the power consumption is very low. This has made CMOS the most popular technology for digital electronics, including microprocessors, memory chips, and integrated circuits.

## 1.1.3 Scaling of CMOS

Scaling refers to the process of reducing the size of transistors and other components. This has led to higher performance, lower power consumption, and lower cost. However, as transistors become smaller, several challenges arise, such as leakage current, heat, and reliability issues. To address these challenges, new materials and structures have been developed, such as high-k gates and FinFETs.

## 1.1.4 Moore's Law

Moore's Law was proposed by Gordon Moore in 1965. It states that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles approximately every two years. This has been the driving force for the semiconductor industry for decades. However, as we approach the physical limits of silicon, some believe that Moore's Law is ending.

## 1.1.5 Koomey's Law

Koomey's Law states that the energy efficiency of computing operations is doubling approximately every two years. This is similar to Moore's Law but focuses on energy efficiency rather than density. This law is important for the development of mobile and IoT devices.

## 1.2 Short Channel Effects

As transistors become smaller, the channel length decreases. This leads to short channel effects (SCE), which can affect the performance and reliability of the MOSFET. SCE include subthreshold leakage, drain-induced barrier lowering, and hot carriers.

## 1.2.1 Subthreshold Slope 'SS'

The subthreshold slope is a measure of how quickly the drain current changes with the gate voltage in the off-state. A lower subthreshold slope is better because it means the device can turn off more quickly.

## the 1.2.2 Drain Induced Barrier Lowering ‘DIBL’

DIBL is a phenomenon where the drain voltage reduces the potential barrier at the source-channel interface. This leads to an increased leakage current and a decrease in the threshold voltage.

## 1.2.3 Channel Length Modulation (CLM)

CLM occurs when the effective length of the channel decreases as the drain voltage increases. This leads to an increase in drain current in the saturation region.

## 1.2.4 Gate-Induced Drain Leakage (GIDL)

GIDL is a leakage current that flows from the gate to the drain. It is caused by the high electric field at the drain region.

## 1.2.5 Doping Gradient

Doping gradient refers to the variation in dopant concentration along the channel. This can be used to control short channel effects.

## 1.3 Junctionless Transistors

Junctionless transistors are a type of MOSFET that does not have a p-n junction. Instead, the channel is uniformly doped. This design helps to reduce some short channel effects.

## 1.3.1 Theory

The theory of junctionless transistors is based on the formation of a channel by the electric field of the gate. When the gate voltage is applied, the carriers are depleted from the channel, allowing current to flow.

## 1.3.2 JLFET

JLFET stands for Junctionless Field-Effect Transistor. It is a type of junctionless transistor.

## 1.3.3 JAMFET

JAMFET stands for Junctionless Alpine Metal FET. It is another type of junctionless transistor with a different structure.

## 1.3.4 Features

Features of junctionless transistors include lower manufacturing cost, better performance at high frequencies, and reduced leakage.

## 1.4 Dopeless Transistors
