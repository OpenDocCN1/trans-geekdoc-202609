### ### 3.1.4 Gate Dielectric Methods

### 3.1.4.1 High-k Dielectric-Material Gate Dielectric

The high-k dielectric constant material is used to significantly reduce the leakage current and gate tunneling with low gate oxide thickness. In most of the semiconductor fabrication industry, a substitute to the SiO2 will be replaced by a material with a high-k dielectric constant like HfO₂, with the value of k=21. The combination of low dielectric k with high dielectric k stacking is a famous method that easily scales the device without actually influencing the device performance. The equivalent oxide thickness (EOT) can be calculated by using equation 3.1. This will calculate the actual thickness of oxide for low dielectric ($t_{ot1}$) with high-k dielectric ($t_{ot2}$) [24].

```
EOT = t_{ot1} + \frac{\epsilon_{r1}}{\epsilon_{r2}}t_{ot2} (3.1)
```

Where $\epsilon_{r1}$ is absolute permittivity for SiO2 and $\epsilon_{r2}$ is absolute permittivity for another high-k-dielectric constant.

### 3.1.4.2 Metal Gate

High-k dielectric stacking is an efficient method of reducing the leakage current; loss of mobility takes place to a large degree with the use of a high-k dielectric. The metal gate is established for providing protection against mobility degradation. Work function variation occurs due to the inconsistency of different gate dielectric alignments in the band [25].

### 3.1.4.3 Multiple Gates

Multiple gates are an effective technique used for THE reduction of SCE for a small scale dimension. It provides good control over the channel, which prevents the device form SCE and improves the device's ability [26]. Among all structures, cylindrical structure that surround the channel will provide a great pathway from charge carriers. Due to introduction of the tri-gate MOSFETs, fabrication is now feasible up to 22 nm. It is surrounded by three sides to provide high controllability over the channel at very low dimensions. This will also improve the drain current to suppression of the leakage current.

Various researches have also evaluated new devices such as gate-all-around (GAA), SOI, and DG-MOSFETs as shown in Figure 3.5, to increase the degree of gate-over-channel control. All multi-gate systems, the system structure of gate-all-around (GAA) and nanowires (NWs) comes with the minimum channel length, which offers a better scaling device.

## 3.1.5 TFET (A Low Power Device)

A TFET works on the breakdown mechanism for reverse biased p–i–n junction semiconductor devices. This mechanism effectively works under a high electric field that induces quantum mechanical interband tunneling phenomena (IBT) at the tunnel junction, resulting in large current at a small voltage level [27–29], TFETs have been proposed as a suitable candidate for the sharp transition between OFF-and ON-states, have obtained the lowest OFF-state CMOS performance, and potentially will scale the supply voltage in a technology compatible with the existing CMOS technology platforms [30–31].

### 3.1.5.1 TFET Structure and Operation

TFET is a gated p-i structure that is based upon the working principle of quantum mechanical band-to-band tunneling (BTBT). This is made up of a solid-state semiconductor that is operated in a reverse bias gate control mechanism. The operating principle is clearly understood by device schematic and energy band diagram, as shown in Figure 3.6. In the reverse bias, the electron will flow according to the Zener tunneling [32] from the source valence band to the channel conduction band. In the OFF-state condition, the channel region is fully depleted due to high p+ doping and n+ doping. The depletion regions get reduced by applying the gate voltage biasing. This will turn ON the transistor mode due to the increase in the electric field. The minority charge carriers transmit from the source to channel and are regulated by the gate biasing voltage.

The main advantage of the tunneling phenomena is that the energy band cuts the 'tail' of the Boltzmann electrons in the p-type region [28–29]. Where A and B are material parameters. The p–i–n structured TFET device is reverse biased for the OFF-state region, and the leakage current is smaller than in MOSFET. Therefore, TFET has ability to avoid SCE, can achieve high $I_{ON}/I_{OFF}$ ratio, and is independent of geometrical scaling.

### 3.1.5.2 The WKB Approximation

The Wentzel–Kramer–Brillouin (WKB) approximation enables the tunneling current to be determined using arbitrary sharp barriers. A formal derivation is detailed in [35]. TFET will employ BTBT modes (lateral and vertical tunneling). The advantages of perpendicular vertical tunneling to the gate-dielectric interface makes it attractive compared to traditional MOSFET. Obtaining high ON current in TFET is a challenging task, as $I_{ON}$ is critically dependent on the transmission probability $T_{WKB}$. The barrier can be estimated by a triangular shape, so that $T_{WKB}$ can be measured using the WKB approximation.

```
$T_{WKB} \approx \exp\left(-\frac{4\lambda\sqrt{2m^*E_G}}{\Delta\phi}\right) \quad (3.3)$
```

Where bandgap is $E_G$ and effective mass is $m^*$. The spatial extent of transition region at the source–channel interface is defined by screening length ($\lambda$), shown in Figure 3.7. In a TFET, the gate voltage ($V_{GS}$) at constant drain voltage ($V_{DS}$) BTBT can be calculated by approximating the energy barrier width by triangular potential.

### 3.1.5.3 Subthreshold Swings

The characteristic of the device below threshold voltage ($V_{TH}$) is known as subthreshold slope ($SS$). It is defined as the gate voltage required to lower the subthreshold current by a factor of 10. It is defined as the minimum amount of gate voltage required for lowering subthreshold current by a factor of 10.

The subthreshold swing is defined as follows:

```
$SS = \frac{\partial\log I_D}{\partial\log V_G}^{-1} \qquad (3.4)$
```

The unit of subthreshold swing is defined as mV/decade. In the case of MOSFET, the slope is independent of gate biasing ($V_{GS}$) below the threshold voltage.

```
$SS = \frac{\partial\log V_G}{\partial\varphi_s} \frac{\partial\varphi_s}{\partial\log I_D} = \left( 1 + \frac{C_{DEF}}{C_{OX}} \frac{kT}{\ln10} \right) \qquad (3.5)$
```

Where

```
$\frac{kT}{q} \ln10 \geq 60\text{ mV / Decade}$
```

At $T=300\text{ K}$, $C_{DEF}$ capacitance at the depletion region and $C_{OX}$ is oxide capacitance, $\varphi_s$ is the surface potential. At the equilibrium condition, the Boltzmann mobile carriers' distribution at the source region explain that a MOSFET cannot switch very steeply from OFF-to-ON and vice versa.

The TFET has an electron-band tunneling process in which the charge carrier is injected from the source side, in contrast to the MOSFET which has thermionic emission, so that the TFET can achieve a sub-$60\text{ mV/decade}$ subthreshold swing. Wang, Hilsbeck et al. reported that through a theoretical analysis, the subthreshold swing of TFETs can be diminished to under $60\text{ mV/decade}$ [36].

Bhuwka et al. achieved the subthreshold swing with a value of $44\text{ mV/decade}$ in the vertical structure of TFET (Figure 3.8). On carbon nanotube (CNT) technology, Appenzel et al. implemented the tunneling device and novel method for making doping sharp (DS) junctions in the strained-Si nanowire TFETs [39]. Both n-type and p-type FETs demonstrated strong ON currents. For n-type FETs, a minimum of $30\text{ mV}$ was achieved at $300\text{ K}$.

In a recently published paper, a subthreshold slope has been reported less than $60\text{ mV/decade}$ [40]. However the TFET reported a very low SS value, the MOSFET had a better ON/OFF ratio. This thanks to a low ON current output from TFET characteristics. The low current is due to the disparity between the TFET and the MOSFET carrier injection mechanisms.
