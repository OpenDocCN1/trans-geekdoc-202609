## 14.3 MECHANISM OF PHOTODECTION

The photodetectors, also designated as photosensors, are the sensor of electromagnetic radiations or incident light. The operation of photodetectors is usually dependent on the material properties and thermal processes in photosensitive materials. The photoelectric effect governing the photodetector mechanism demonstrated in Figure 14.2 [9]. If the energy of the incoming photons $E_p = hf = hc/\lambda$ (where h stands Planck's constant; $f$ the optical frequency of incoming photons; $c$ denotes the velocity of light; and $\lambda$) exceeds the bandgap ($E_g$) of the material, one electron is promoted to the conduction band for each photon absorption. An empty hole is created in the valence band and is recognized as the photogeneration of the carrier (electron-hole pair), as illustrated in Figure 14a. In Figure 14.2, $E_F$ denotes Fermi-level energy; $E_v$ and $E_c$ signify valence and conduction band energies, respectively; $V$ corresponds to the applied bias; and $R_L$ represents the load resistance. By applying a bias, the photogenerated carriers are collected in an external circuit and optical energy is converted into electric energy, as shown in Figure 14.2b. Photogeneration and the separation of an electron-hole pair in the depletion region of the reverse-biased p–n junction are demonstrated in Figure 14d [9].

The depletion region width should be large to allow a large number of incident photons to be absorbed so that maximum carrier pair generation can be achieved. Meanwhile, large carrier drift times in depletion layer affect the operational speed of the photodetector; however, it is essential to the depletion region width. Therefore, a balance must be maintained over the response speed and number of absorbed photons [9].

The photoelectric effect of photodetection can be categorized as the photovoltaic effect (PVE) and the photoconductive effect based on different device architecture and material characteristics. The PVE is based on the separation of photogenerated carriers in opposite directions under the influence of the $E_{field}$ at the p–n junction (homojunction, heterojunction, and Schottky junction without external bias voltage (Figure 14.3a). As shown, the photovoltaic photodetectors work without dark current [34]. The net photocurrent shows the current-voltage characteristics, as depicted in Figure 14.3c. The built-in $E_{field}$ can be introduced by either the electrostatically split gates and doping or by using the function difference between the contacts and graphene [40]. A similar effect can be achieved by applying an electric field with a bias voltage that aligns the net electric field toward its direction, and hence photogenerated carriers are collected by the reverse-biased electrodes resulting in photocurrent to the external circuit, as shown in Figures 14.3b and 14.3d.

A photodetector operating in photoconductive (or photovoltaic) mode is designated as a current source with (or without) the bias voltage. The photovoltaic mode has only the advantage of the lowest dark current, which in turn improves specific density. Alternatively, the photoconductive mode offers the following advantages:

- i. Increased response speed due to the reduction in junction capacitance.
- ii. Weak signal detection due to the recombination probability of photogenerated carriers under reverse bias.
- iii. Increased quantum efficiency due to the depletion region width.

The recent approach also utilizes thermal effects to produce an electric field through electron diffusion into metal contacts. The thermal effects include thermoelectric and photovoltaic effects. These thermal effects play a dominant role in photocurrent generation in graphene-related 2D materials.

The photoelectric effect (PTE) is a thermal effect caused via the heating effect in the channel due to nonuniform incident light and is also known as Seebeck effect. When the spot of incident light is smaller than the channel length, a temperature gradient at the semiconducting channel leads to a temperature difference $\Delta T$ at the two ends, as demonstrated in Figure 14.4a and b. A photoelectric voltage $\Delta V_{PTE}$ proportional to $\Delta T$ generated by the PTE is given as $\Delta V_{PTE}=\Delta T(S_2-S_1)$, where $S_1$ and $S_2$ the Seebeck coefficients of the material in V/K with different doping [13].

In the other hand, in the photoelectric effect (PBE), the resistivity of temperature-sensitive material changes due to the light heating effect caused by the absorption of the photon, as depicted in 14.4b. Under the thermal radiation, the resistance of the material can be decreased or increased, resulting in an increased or decreased current across the device, as shown in 14.4d [13].

The major difference between PTE and PBE is that the photocurrent in PTE is self-driven, as in photovoltaic devices, whereas no photocurrent generates for PBE without an external bias, as shown in 14.4b. The photocurrent in PTE depends on Seebeck coefficients. However, in PBE, the photocurrent depends on the change of resistivity, which is a function of temperature [13].

## 14.4 TYPES OF SEMICONDUCTOR PHOTodetECTORS

A semiconductor photodetector is a device that absorbs the incident, that is incoming photons, and converts it into electrical signals. This conversion mechanism is outlined in previous sections. To understand the development of photodetectors, it is essential to elaborate upon the major types of semiconductor photodetectors.

### 14.4.1 The Semiconductor p–n Single-Junction and p–i–n Dual-Junction Photodetectors

The semiconductor p–n junction and p–i–n junction are the semiconductor photodetectors without an internal gain mechanism, where a single electron-hole-pair (EHP) is generated per absorbed photon.

Figure 14.5a shows a reverse-biased highly p-doped (p*) and lightly n-doped (n*) p*-n-single-junction photodetector with diffusion and depletion regions. The depletion layer is created by negatively charged ions in the p-type material and positively charged donor atoms in the n-type material when carriers are swept to their majority under the built-in $E_{field}$. Therefore the width of depletion region depends upon the doping levels for reverse bias voltage (i.e., the lower the doping, the wider depletion region [9]). As illustrated by the absorption region in 14.5a, photons are absorbed in both the diffusion and depletion regions. The position and width of absorption region per photodetector: During the weak absorption of photons and on fabricated material for the photodetector, the absorption region can be fully extended across the device. Thus, carriers are produced in both the diffusion and depletion regions. In the depletion region, the carriers are separated and drifted under the built-in $E_{field}$ while outside this region, the holes diffuse near the depletion region to be collected. Most of the incident photons are absorbed in the depletion region, the width of depletion region should be made as large as possible by reducing the doping in n-region.

The homojunction photodetector suffer from low quantum efficiency due to grading profile. For homojunction, the photodetectors with heterojunction are preferred, as they exhibit better quantum efficiency due to a more efficient $E_{field}$ [32], [34].

To allow the operation in a longer spectral regime where light enters deeper into the photosensitive material, a broader depletion region is required. To accomplish this, the semiconductor material is lightly doped (usually of n-type) so that it may be treated as intrinsic (i), and to create a low resistance highly n-doped (n*) thin layer is employed.
