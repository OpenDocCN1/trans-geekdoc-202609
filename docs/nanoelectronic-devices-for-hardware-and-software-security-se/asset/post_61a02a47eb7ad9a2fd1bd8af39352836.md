## 4.1 INTRODUCTION

In past decades, ubiquitous implementation of integrated circuits (IC) implies their importance and utility in day-to-day electronic product. These diverse applications aroused an imperative for multiple functionalities which resulted in the incessant miniaturization of the devices. Traditionally, field effect transistors (FET) are mostly comprised of bulk materials, mainly silicon, german, and other III-V semiconductors. Researchers and technologists have been able to successfully scale down the devices to sub-nanoscale dimensions over the last five decades, which was in good agreement with Moore’s famous prediction (popularly coined as Moore’s law) stating that the density of transistors on a chip will double every two years. Following this prediction, researchers and industries set up their goals and devised a roadmap termed International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors (ITRS). These strategies and predictions have recently started to falter as active lengths hitting the nanometer regime. This has been the case for the following reasons:

(a) At these dimensions, the transport of the carrier over the channel is not properly governed by statistical mechanics (typically known as Boltzmann equation).
(b) At these dimensions, the electrostatic control of the controlling terminal, usually as the gate, becomes inappropriate.
(c) At these dimensions, short channel effects in the devices become prominent, commonly referred to as current leakage and the resulting heat dissipation by an increase in power.
(d) At these dimensions, the significance of contacts and interconnects between devices becomes more important, as comparatively significant power is dissipated at the interface rather than the channel itself.

As a result, researchers are looking for possible solutions at every level of abstraction (viz. circuit and devices). To address these short channel effects, devices such as multiple gate transistors [8, 9], FinFETs [10], FETs [11–13], gate-all-around FETs [14], and ultrathin body transistors (UTB) [15, 16] are actively looked into. Further, various circuit techniques such as power gating [17] and use of multi-threshold transistors have also popularly used. These approaches are often chosen as top-down approaches to mitigate scaling issues. Since the famous talk by Richard Feynman – “There is plenty at the bottom” – the bottom-up view has always been considered one of the approaches to understand fundamental properties. Recently, with the advancements in computational and science techniques, the bottom-up approach is serving as a foundation for new-era nanoscale devices [19–21]. However, both the approaches, nearly top-to-bottom and bottom-up, have their advantages and limitations. For example, on the one hand, it is difficult to cultivate the complex quantum (bottom-up) view for a bigger circuit and system. Thus, the optimal solutions at each system, device, and material are consistently being proposed to achieve the required overall result. In recent decades, fundamental bottlenecks due to intrinsic materials properties such as electron mobility (due to scattering in bulk materials) and mechanical and thermal instability raised a severe motion for the research to look for promising new materials [22]. Various applications are the consequence of an unprecedented quest for the material community. Nanotechnology continues to generate the genre of materials where at least one of the dimensions is in the range of nanometers (nm). Unlike bulk materials, nanomaterials offer a lot of scope not to overcome existing issues but also to widen the applicability horizon. Among these nanomaterials, low dimensional nanomaterials such as C-based structures have special attention. These structures include 0D fullerenes (balls of carbon), 1D carbon nanotubes (CNT), graphene nanoribbons (GNR), and the 2D graphene which is monolayer of graphite [23]. The exotic properties of graphene are backed by the recent research in this area, which eventually reconsidered the overall interest in the field of layered materials possessing superior electronic and optical behavior [24]. Moreover, their excellent mechanical strength, optical transparency, and unique direct bandgap make them suitable for a wide variety of applications [25–33]. With device performance, it is also evident that for channels forming a 2D semiconductor, the carriers will remain focused in the atomically thin layers, ensuring better electrostatic control. Such an excellent gate control reduces leakage current and reduced short channel effects. Unlike the typical semiconducting applications, these materials also possess enhanced mobility of carriers, tunable electronic properties, and intrinsic spin orbit coupling (SOC), which widens their pool of applications [34].
