# 9.1 INTRODUCTION

Advancement in the nano optical device design to support higher data rate communication demands and higher bandwidth to prop up ever-increasing requirements of the 5G era. Recently, all-optical communications have attracted a large interest due to the feasibility of spectral efficiency and bandwidth. Success of emerging nano devices defines the intelligence and required information of the ultra-high speed communication channels, power efficient and portable super computing, ultra-fast switches, interconnects, and autonomous smart systems, and its size is restricted to a maximum of 100 nm. If semiconductor materials are fabricated with 1 to 100 nm, at least one in dimension, the device shares the properties of nano devices. Ever-increasing demand connectivity in the telecommunication industry requires ultra-fast data rates, which in turn lead to increased memory and smart computing capabilities of communication devices with less power consumption. The prototype of all-optical devices based on high bit rate optical gates are coming out from laboratories to avoid inefficient opto-electronic-optical (O-E-O) inversions conversations in the system. A variety of all-optical gates is available to execute all-optical signal processing using nonlinear waveguides [1], semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) [2–4], an erbium doped fiber amplifier [5], and photonic crystal fiber [6–8]. An SOA-based design is proposed because of its compact size, low power consumption, sensitivity, and integration capability. SOA-based reporting work such as a magnitude generator, polarization splitter, and adder [9–15] shows good performance. The nonlinearity of SOA [16] includes self-phase modulation (SPM), cross-gain modulation (XGM), cross-phase modulation (XPM), four wave mixing (FWM), and cross polarization (XPM). SOA gates can be designed with interferometric configurations such as nonlinear interferometer (NI), Sagnac interferometer (SI) gates, Michelson interferometer (MI) and Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI).

All-optical gates can be designed with UNI configuration [17], where the orthogonized clock pulse will be generated by passing across polarized fiber. All-optical logic gates implemented with Sagnac interferometers have a bulky structure but are comparatively faster [18–22]. The amplitude and phase modulated probe and pump signal from the SOA leads to destructive and constructive interference between the pump and probe signal, which generates a logic 0 and 1. The 2 x 2 coupler in the SI method joins input and output. The total phase shift of π/2 is produced by the Sagnac gate by applying the clock signal on the polarization maintained fiber. TWSOA is used to design all-optical AND and logic gate successfully at 20 Gbps. Then, based on the constructive and destructive superposition of the pump and probe signal, logic 0 and 1 are produced. The SOA-MI structure is used with symmetrical Fiber Bragg grating to produce logic output based on XGM of SOA. The MI arrangement is very simple, and with proper arrangements, the results will be accurate in the required logic. In MI, superposition principle between the probe and pump signal will return logic 0 and 1. Comparatively, the MI arrangement [24–26] requires much less power than MI. The DI configuration [27] requires less power, as it possesses only one SOA for a nonlinear operation to achieve a logic function.

In the all-optical gates designed using MZI methods, the arms of the interferometer. Phase modulation is realized when two SOAs can be changed to amplitude when the phase difference is introduced in the interferometer. Phase difference can be obtained by 2 x 2 coupler or using phase shifter. There are two types of MZI configuration, which includes co-propagating MZI and counter-propagating MZI. A co-propagating MZI uses filters to separate the probe and pump signal, while there is no need of a filter in counter-propagating MZI.

In this chapter, an SOA–MZI-based secured all-optical communication systems with all-optical XOR gates is proposed, and investigation of various modulation formats over the proposed system is carried out with an aim to support higher data rates of nanoscale communication devices. Section 9.2 presents the design of the SOA–MZI-based secured transmission link. Section 9.3 describes the impact of various modulation schemes over the proposed design, and Section 9.4 explains the results and calculated performance metrics. Section 9.5 concludes the chapter.

# 9.2 DESIGN OF SOA–MZI-BASED COMMUNICATION LINK

The block diagram of the proposed system is given in Figure 9.1. It consists of a transmitter, all-optical XOR gate, and receiver. An XOR operation is achieved with the XPM technique [28]. Choice of operating wavelength and source defines the wavelength of pump and the probe signal at 10 Gbps is 1552.52 nm and 1558 nm, respectively. Two data signals are generated by a pseudorandom generator, and 80 ps delay is used to generate other data input. Mach–Zehnder modulator is used to modulate the data and carrier signals. Modulated output is transmitted through an XOR gate to generate encrypted output. Message security is enhanced with encryption, which uses right key in both encryption and decryption process. In encryption, XOR logic is widely used because of its advantages such as computational speed, in particular, hardware feasibility, and commutative and associative property. An all-optical XOR gate is the basic block of the encryption process in the proposed system, and it is used for secure data transmission in support of data hiding when no particular security is required. To achieve all-optical XOR operations, SOA-MZI configuration is used by varying the carrier intensity of the pump signal. Optimized phase shift of π occurs when the lower probe signal and high frequency pulse signal travels along the upper and lower arms of the SOA-MZI configuration, which in turn leads to XOR operation.

The Boolean of XOR logic gives 1 at the output when nonuniform input data combines at SOA (combination of A=0, B=1, and A=1, B=0). On the other hand, when both inputs are the same, (combination of A=1, B=1, and A=0, B=0), logic output turns into 0; 1 logic is represented by the presence of optical pulses, and 0 logic is represented by an absence of optical pulse at the receiver output. Polarization of optical field is considered in the gain saturation process, and polarization dependence is negligible in the XGM and XPS process of SOA. The electric field propagation in the z-axis is given by the standard wave equation:

$$\frac{\partial^2 E(z,t)}{\partial z^2} + n^2\frac{\partial^2 E(z,t)}{\partial t^2} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 c^2}\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial t^2}$$ (9.1)

The relationship between polarization P and susceptibility $\chi$ is given as $P = \chi E$

$$\chi(N) = \frac{nc}{\omega}(\alpha - j)g(N)$$ (9.2)

Carrier dependent density $g(N)$ is calculated by:

$$\g(N) = \Gamma[\alpha(N-N_0)] - \alpha_p$$ (9.3)

Wave equation is given by:

$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial z} = -\frac{1+j\alpha}{2}g(z,t)E(z,t)$$ (9.4)

Integrating in z direction

$$\E(L,t) = E(0,t)\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_0^L g(z,t)dz\right]$$ (9.5)

Carrier density rate equation is given as:

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{I}{qV} - \frac{N}{\tau_s}\frac{g(n)}{\omega}|E(t,z)|^2$$ (9.6)
