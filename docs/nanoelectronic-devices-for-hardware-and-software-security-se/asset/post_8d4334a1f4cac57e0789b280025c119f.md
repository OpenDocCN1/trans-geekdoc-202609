
## 1 Emerging Nanoelectronic Devices

### 1.4.1 Electrostatic Doping

The concept of electrostatic doping can be a very suitable alternative to overcome the challenges associated with chemical doping. In electrostatic doping, the concentration of carriers in the body of the semiconductor is influenced by the electrode attached to it. This is mainly doping of an intrinsic semiconductor material by electrodes. When a semiconducting material is brought near a conducting material or metal electrode, band alignment near the interface occurs, which induces charge carriers. The carriers can be electrons or holes depending on the type, polarity, and work function of the electrode. The relative difference between the Fermi level and energy bands of semiconductor determine the concentration in the semiconducting material. On the metal-semiconductor interface it can be controlled by metal's work function and semiconductor properties as work function, electron affinity, and energy band gap, shown in Figure 1.9 [16]. Many devices electrostatic doping have been proposed such as diodes [17], tunnel field effect transistors (TFETs) [18], impact-ionization MOSFET (IMOS) [19] and dopless transistors [15].

### 1.4.3 Features

- Doping devices are free from impurity doping and impurity gradients, thereby making them dopeless and junctionless in their architecture. Therefore, they are free from complex fabrication processes. They can also relax thermal budgets required in annealing processes [20].
- Being dopeless, they are also more immune to random dopant fluctuations, making them a suitable alternative to technology nodes [14].
- DLTs are more resistant to short channel effects. This immunity is due to enlargement of depletion region outside the gate length, as the substrate is undoped. This increases the effective or actual gate length ($L_{eff}$) of the DLT. It also highlights the better scalability of the device [21].
- Due to the dopeless nature of the device, a DLT is less sensitive to parameter variations as gate oxide thickness, gate length, device thickness, and doping variations. This makes it more suitable to low-power logic technology applications [14].

### 1.4.4 Challenges in JLT and DLT

There are various issues in JLTs that need to be addressed. Some problems in JLTs, such as random fluctuations, mobility, and high source/drain resistances, can be caused by the use of dopeless transistors. The problem of gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL) is a common issue that exists for the nanoscale JLT and DLT [22][23]. As the gate lengths are fast approaching sub-10 nm scale, the problem of GIDL is a serious issue. All types of junctionless devices – JLTs, JAMFETs, and DLTs – suffer from this problem (Figure 1.12 (a,b,c)). The issue of GIDL is pronounced in JAMFET, owing to the existence of high-low junctions that reduce the effective gate length in JAMFET [24]. This GIDL affects the turn-off capability ($I_{ON}/I_{OFF}$) of the devices. It affects various analog performance metrics that have been addressed in this work.

### 1.4.5 Gate-Induced Drain Leakage in JLT, JAMFET, and DLT

As explained in 1.2.4, GIDL is a dominant phenomenon in short channel devices. The narrowing of tunneling barriers at low gate voltages ($V_{GS} \leq 0.2 V$) between the channel and drain regions aids the tunneling of electrons from the valence band of the channel to the conduction band of the drain. This BTBT current for short channel lengths is the predominant mechanism for GIDL in JLT [22]. The tunneling of electrons from the channel region to the drain region generates holes in the valence band of the channel region. The accumulation of holes in the channel region serves as the p region of the parasitic bipolar transistor, while drain leads to forward biasing of the emitter-base (source-channel) junction of the parasitic BJT which triggers the BJT (Figure 1.12d). The forward biased-emitter-base injection of the parasitic BJT leads to substantial collector current or leakage current in JLTs [22]. The collector current ($I_C = \beta_B I_B$) is the product of the parasitic BJT gain and $I_B$ of DLT. The tunneling current by JLTs serves as the parasitic BJT's base. This parasitic BJT formation severely hampers the OFF-performance. For a further decrease in gate voltage, the concentration of holes in the valence band increases the potential of the base or the floating body potential in parasitic BJT. This leads to saturation of the collector current in parasitic BJT. Thus, for any negative voltage junctionless transistors, the drain current increases marginally, signifies dependence of drain current ($I_D$) on negative gate voltages ($V_{GS}$) (Figure 1.12). There have been many solutions proposed for the problem of GIDL in JLT, JAMFET, and DLT. BTBT-induced GIDL in JLT was first reported by Gundapeni et al. [22]. The bulk planar JLT (BPJLT), which had a junction in vertical direction due to the presence of p-well, was proposed, which effectively swept the BTBT generated holes. But this reduced ON-state current and increased capacities owing to the presence of another junction. The extended back gate in double JLT widened the tunnel barrier at drain-channel interface and increased the barrier height at source-channel interface in the OFF-state [25]. For nanowire JLTs, many device architectures, such as core-shell nanowire, doping, and nanowire dot, were proposed to mitigate this GIDL by Sahay et al. [22]–29]. For nanowire JFET, the dual-gate material (work function) gate was proposed to widen the tunnel barrier at the drain channel interface in the OFF-state [24]. A dual metal stack gate was also proposed to counter the effect of GIDL in sub-10 nm nanowire JLT [30]. All these techniques either (a) widened the tunnel barrier, (b) lowered the electric field at the drain-channel interface, or (c) increased the source-channel barrier height in the OFF-state. This led to reduced BTBT, in turn suppressed GIDL. The gate spacer design guidelines and diameter difference in nanowire JLT and nanowire JFET for GIDL were also successfully explained in [31][32]. The proposal for raised source/drain extensions to reduce the effect of GIDL by inhibiting parasitic BJT action in DLT on buried oxide (BOX) was proposed by Sindhu et al. [23].

Therefore, the objectives presented in this chapter are listed here.

- One of the objectives behind this chapter is to highlight the problem of GIDL in these nanoscaled, junctionless, and dopeless devices.

- There have many techniques proposed for nanowire JFETs, but for a more robust technological perspective, we need less complex fabrication process. These ideas could be extended to nanowire architectures by suitable fabrication steps.

### 1.6 SIMULATION ENVIRONMENT OF JLT AND DLT

The 2D simulations were implemented by Silicon Atlas [33]. The quantum confinement model proposed by Hansch [34] is activated, the device thickness is less than 10 nm. To model the phenomenon of tunneling in OFF-state or low gate voltages, a nonlocal band-to-band tunneling (BTBT) model based on the Wentzel-Kramillouin method is activated. The nonlocal BTBT is sufficiently calibrated and is already used in the literature [35]. The tunneling masses 0.10 m, for electrons and 0.17 m, for holes were used according to [35] for JLT. Fermi-Dirac statistics were involved in the intrinsic carrier concentration needed for Shockley-Hall (SRH) series. The Lombardi model of mobility, and Auger recombination were incorporated into model mobility and lifetime phenomena. As the doping is higher in a JLT (~10⁹), a band gap narrowing (BGN) is also invoked to model high doping effects. The setup selection calibration was done by replacing the transfer functions of DG-JLT for $V_{DS}=0.02$ V, $T_{SI}=10$ nm, $w=12.5$ nm and work function difference ($\phi_{MS}$) of 1.12 eV.
