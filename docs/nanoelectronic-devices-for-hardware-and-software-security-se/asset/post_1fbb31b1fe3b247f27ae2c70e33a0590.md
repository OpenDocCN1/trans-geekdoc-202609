
$I_D \exp \left|-\frac{4\sqrt{2m^*}E_g^{3/2}}{3|e|(E_g + \Delta E)|} \frac{\varepsilon_{Si}}{\varepsilon_{ox}} t_{Si} t_{ox} \Delta\Phi\right| \quad (2.27)$

where $e$ designates the electron charge, the effective carrier mass is denoted by $m^*$, $E_g$ represents the bandgap of the semiconductor at the tunneling junction, the dielectric constant of the semiconductor and the gate oxide are $\varepsilon_{Si}$ and $\varepsilon_{ox}$, respectively, $t_{Si}$ and $t_{ox}$ denote the thickness of the device (which is for silicon material in this context) and the thickness of the gate oxide, respectively. The energy extends to $\Delta\Phi$, and $h$ denotes reduced Planck’s constant. It is worth noting that the WKB approximation for the drain current ($I_D$) in all cases may not be correct. For instance, in the case of direct bandgap semiconductor such as indium arsenide and heterojunction as silicon/germanium or quantum effects and Phonon-assisted tunneling turn out to be dominant [23]. From equation 2.27 it can be concluded that the tunneling current can be improved using the methods shown here:

- i. Reducing the gate insulator thickness ($t_{ox}$).
- ii. Using an material that has a high dielectric constant ($\varepsilon_{ox}$).
- iii. Using different materials that have an effective carrier mass ($m^*$) and energy bandgap ($E_g$) lower than silicon.

These methods are also given to improve the performance of the device, not only for enhancing $I_{ON}$ but also for reducing the leakage current, and for improvement in the subthreshold swing, which is a crucial characteristic in any transistor device. Apart the techniques mentioned, many researchers have been developing and exploring new strategies and techniques in terms of both modifying the structural architectures (TFET structure design engineering) as well as materials engineering. In a more extensive manner, several methods to enhance the device performance of the TFET can be categorized accordingly.

## 2.5.1 Gate Engineering

As the gate controls the band bending and the carrier injection density in TFETs, gate engineering is found to have a tremendous impact on the transistor ON-state current. This implies multiple gates, including using a double gate instead of the single gated TFET. The double gate enables the coupling of the gate and channel with the potential because tunneling takes place from two sides, underneath each of the gates at the source/channel junction [24]. For an ultra-thin double gate TFET (DG-TFET), the increment in $I_{ON}$ is more than twice that of a single gate TFET because of the impact of both the gates [25]. In addition to this, the gate around TFET (GAA TFET) is another method to control the channel and boost $I_{ON}$. Another technique is the dual-material gate (DMG) TFET, in which different work material gates are used and the existence of low work function gate material close to and high work function gate material near the channel/drain junction reduces the ambipolar current [27].

## 2.5.2 Materials Engineering

As silicon-based technologies have advanced and improved over recent decades, TFETs dependent on silicon materials are favorably attractive, as this enables TFETs to exploit the widely developed technologies of silicon processing and makes the implementation of TFETs in the latest CMOS process flow much simpler. However, as seen from the fundamental mechanics of tunneling in equation 2.27,

 semiconductor materials with a smaller bandgap are preferred for a greater tunnel probability and higher drive current. Owing to its wide bandgap and the indirect tunneling mechanism, silicon attributes to low tunneling current in TFETs. As the silicon-based TFETs fail to meet the required ON-current for advanced technologies, another method of improving tunneling in TFET is to use a smaller bandgap material. This include using germanium, III-V semiconductors, carbon nanomaterials, and [28–31]. Germanium (Ge) is chemically identical to silicon (Si) material and is compatible with the current CMOS processes to a significant degree. In recent decades, the higher visibility and carrier mobility of germanium in contrast to silicon has drawn the interest of researchers. German possesses a bandgap of 0.66 eV, which makes it a suitable candidate for improving the TFET performances. $I_{ON}$ can be increased–2,700 times by implementing germanium in the channel of TFET instead of silicon-based method [32]. A silicon-germanium (SiGe) alloy in which the mole fraction is carefully estimated is also, as the effective bandgap in SiGe can be smaller than that of Si. Another way is to implement a heterojunction TFET in which germanium is employed at the channel while silicon is used at the channel region. The electron affinity differs from each other (4.0 eV for germanium and 4.05 eV for silicon), they form a strained (type-II) heterojunction. ## 2.5.3 Doping Engineering

Another technique for enhancing the TFET in terms of $I_{ON}$ is focusing on the doping techniques (especially the source doping). Increasing the source doping in TFETs reduces the tunneling-barrier width due to the increase in the built-in electric field between the source and channel, owing to the bandgap narrowing. This increases the ON-state current substantially. It is noteworthy to mention that varying the source doping concentration in one way may not necessarily affect directly the OFF-state leak currents in TFETs. Without changing the semiconductor material, the ON-state current can also be enhanced using a technique called pocket doping, as depicted in Figure 2.5.

The pocket region depletes and leaves ionized positive charge. However, the pocket region does not alter the potential of the intrinsic region for a gate voltage as well as drain voltage. Large electric fields around the tunneling region tend to increase the generation rate, thereby enhancing the tunneling current. Concurrently, this extra field ensures that the subthreshold swing is lessened. Optimization of the pocket doping concentration, width, and activating of the dopants has been explored and established for the pocket TFET, where an average subthreshold slope of 66 mV/dec with ID$_{ON}$ = 1.4 µA/µm is attained [36].
