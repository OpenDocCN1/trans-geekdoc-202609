Graphene's high mobility can be enhanced up to 15,000 under room temperature conditions and high even with more carrier density ($>10^{12}$ cm$^{-2}$) for both chemically and electrically doped samples. Since extrinsic scattering is eliminated, its mobility will be increased due to weak electron-phonon interactions. In the graphene SymFET, the Fermi degeneracy is enhanced over a huge energy gap, then the critical temperature for the excitonic condensate has been considered to be more than the room temperature. A macroscopic tunneling will be provided by the excitonic condensate formation between the layers. This will be helpful to design the structure of the device for high-frequency applications. For designing the graphene SymFET structure, consider the list of device parameters given in Table 16.1 [8]. The tunneling insulator (BN) is fixed with the thickness of 0.33 nm (~1 atomic layer), 0.66 nm (~2 atomic layers), 0.99 nm (~3 atomic layers), and 1.32 nm (~4 atomic layers). Whenever the tunneling insulator is vary with a large variation of $\Delta E$, the resonance peak caused by the tunneling current leads to substantial broadening. The insulator thickness ($<0.66$ nm $\sim2$ atomic layers) and barrier height ($\sim0.5$ nm) are wide enough to achieve the electron relaxation time in graphene doping.

In graphene, the charge carriers (electrons or holes) can be shifted by the electric field or chemical doping. The doping effect of the electric field is frequently achieved in graphene-field-effect transistors (FETs); the charge carriers are attracted against the Si/SiO substrate [9,10]. The type and concentration of carriers can be tuned by varying the gate voltage, $V_G$. The sign of the gate voltage is opposite to the induced carrier; a negative gate voltage ($V_G$) encourages holes. Graphene interacts with the chemical species by chemical doping [11]. Chemical doping is of two types: one is surface transfer and another is substantial doping. In such cases, the effect of doping occurs once some of the carbon atoms in the graphene lattice are swapped by other atoms with a dissimilar number of valence electrons. For boron, $B$, and nitrogen, $N$, substitutional atoms lead to p- and n-type conductivity, respectively [12,13, 46–49]. However, the electronic structure of the graphene is modified by the integration of foreign atoms into the graphene lattice. Hence, it can be used for ultra-high frequency and TH applications in a high-security hardware device.
