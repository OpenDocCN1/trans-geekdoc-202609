# 2.1 INTRODUCTION

Since the first principle of the field-effect transistor (FET) was demonstrated in 1960 and the implementation of the complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) was realized in 1963, a tremendous progress in the field of CMOS technology has been perceived [1–3]. Following the well-known Moore’s law states that the number of transistors being used in the integrated circuits (ICs) should be doubled every 18 months by scaling the dimensions of transistors [4], perhaps the biggest test confronting the CMOS technology is way to produce the up-and-coming generation of superior, high efficiency, low powered devices while preserving its performance and circuit speed regardless of the cost per function, as well as its power consumption. It is noteworthy that regardless of many daunting technical difficulties, the tendency of miniaturization of the size of semiconductor devices and the subsequent exponential development and growth of the semiconductor industries have unprecedented undoubtedly for many decades. The challenges accompanying the upcoming miniaturization of transistors, which at first appeared insurmountable, were overcome using ingenious methods before

However, as transistors are being continuously downscaled to the sub-50 nm regime or lower, they face a genuine threat as an increase in the incapacity of transistors to switch from the OFF-state to the ON-state without improving in power dissipation, and they possess a high subthreshold swing (SS) [5], [6]. Here, the SS is described as the amount of voltage needed to lower the current by an order of magnitude and is limited to 2 KT/q, which is 60 mV per decade at room temperature for a metal-oxide-semiconductor field effect transistor (MOSFET). The physics relating to the main operating mechanism of the conventional CMOS transistor impose certain limitations that preclude further reduction in the scaling down of the transistors while maintaining a constant power supply voltage fails to improve the energy efficiencies in ICs that have been acquired by reducing the power supply voltage [8]. This requires investigating and developing transistors that are dependent on new working principles, better architectural design, and even maybe innovative non-silicon-based materials that could surround the shortcomings of the traditional transistors [6]. In this regard, tunnel field effect transistors (TFETs), which work based on the principle of quantum mechanical tunneling, have been shown to have a remarkable switching characteristic that goes beyond the theoretical abilities of the traditional transistors, such as the MOSFET, whose operating mechanism is due to the thermionic emission of carriers over a potential barrier [9–11]. TFETs can be implemented in ICs with low power supply voltage, which results in an exceptionally efficient circuit; this makes TFETs interesting for researchers and the semiconductor industries and has prompted extensive study in recent times in both theoretical and experimental fields [12], [13].
