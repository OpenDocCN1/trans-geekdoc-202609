
## 15.3.4 Three Terminal Junction (TTJ)

The YBJ/TTJ contains three terminals named left, right, and center terminals. In a Y-branch junction (YBJ), the angle between the left and right terminal is kept at 60° as shown in Figure 15.2 [66]. The left and right terminals are directed 180° from each other in the TTJ [57]. The defined configuration is also known as the T-branch junction (TBJ). The AC signal is in either push-fixed and push-pull device [56], [57], [79], [87]. The working principle and output of a YBJ junction is similar to that of TTJ. Both the configurations have been fabricated using Si [55], III-V group-based heterructure (GaAs/AlGaAs [62], InAlAs/InGaAs [78], [79]) and novel material such as graphene [81], [124]–[136]. The TTJ can be used to get the 0 and 1 binary logic in digital circuits as AND gate [84], [83], NAND gate [61], half-adder [59], SR latch [75], frequency mixer [73], and differential amplifier [84].

## 15.3.5 Former Ballistic Rectifier (FTBR)

The FTBR is a nonlinear electronic device that converts the AC signal into the DC using asymmetric device geometry. A triangular antidot at the front of the device directs the carriers in a particular direction for getting the DC response [101], [105], [107]. The ballistic rectifier works in the nonlinear regime; therefore, it can be characterized using the extended Landauer–Bütticker approach [90], [105]. The size of the input terminals can be less compared to the size of the output terminal for the deflection of the maximum number of electrons towards the lower terminal of the device. The FTBR is expected to generate a non-zero response when a small signal is applied between the input terminals. The working of ballistic rectifier is similar to that of a bridge rectifier without any requirement of doping junction or Schottky barrier, as shown in Figure 15.3 [98]. The active region of the device is smaller than or comparable to the mean free path of charge carriers, and as a result, the carrier moves like a billiard ball without encountering any impurity scattering [90]. Therefore, scattering takes place only at the boundary of the device structure. Being a planar device, it offers parasitic capacitance with a minimal impedance mismatch with external hardware circuitry that allows operating at several THz frequencies [98], [103]. The ballistic rectifier has been designed using III-V materials (GaAs-AlGaAs [101], InGaAs/InP [91], InAlAs/InGaSb [96], [102], InGaAs/InAlAs [78], [79], [85]) and graphene [25], [26], [137]. These devices can be used for frequency multiplication [103], THz detection [138], energy harvesting [137], etc.

### 15.3.6 Ballistic Deflection Transistor (BDT)

Recently, the ballistic rectifier geometry has been modified by BDT, which was based on ballistic deflection of electron and steering [112], [114], [121]. Two gate terminals (left gate and right gate) were introduced with respect to the source terminal to control the electron movement in an east or west direction by actuating it from the source direction [108], [114], [118]. The BDT can be used as a NAND gate [119], flip-flop [109], And gate [110], or Or gate [110].

### 15.3.7 Graphene Based Nano-Rectifiers

Due to extensive properties, graphene has been explored to design the futuristic devices that can be used for many potential applications. A graphene-based single transistor was demonstrated by Wang et al. in 2009 as a frequency multiplier by rectifying the input signal. It was demonstrated to be a high frequency operation with more efficiency when compared to conventional diode or field effect transistor (FET) based multipliers [139]. Theoretical work on graphenebased SSD demonstrated the higher carrier mobility and rectification ratio allow operation at higher frequency and room temperature [140]. The first graphene-based three terminal junction (G-TTJ) rectifier was reported by A. Jacobsen et al. in 2010, as shown in Figure 15.4a [136]. The rectification in this device exhibits the change in sign by reversing the gate voltage at room temperature [136].

## 15.4 BACKGROUND

a number nanoelectronic devices are available with dimensions smaller than the mean free path of charge carriers. The ballistic transport of charge carriers is dominant in the active region of the device. The ballistic transport governs the elastic travel of carriers within the channel region of the device without losing any energy. In contrast, scattering takes places only at the device boundary, which results in heat dissipation through the electrical contacts [141]. During the last two decades, ballistic transport has been well explored in nanoelectronic devices at room temperature and high frequency.

The phase coherence in a four terminal conductor was conducted by Büttiker in 1986 [142]. The electrochemical potential and transmission probability and resistance between leads of the conductor have been used to form the current in the output leads. In previous studies, which the voltage drop has been seen over a distance comparable to the mean free path of carriers without considering the phase coherence, [14], four terminal resistance has been developed to relate the symmetry between voltage and current at the input and output leads. Similarly, experiments have been performed on QPC to study the quantum phenomenon [30]. The QPC shows the conductance plateaus at lower temperature due to comparable mean free path, and with the increase of temperature and magnetic field, the quantized conductance behavior includes Hirayama. Hirayama and Tarucha in 1993 reported the room temperature ballistic transport in the four terminal device from AlGaAs/InGaAs/GaAs materials in Figure 15.5 [143]. The bending resistance of junction-junction has the negative characteristic obtained in response to the applied field at different temperatures ranging from 1.5 K to 290 K. The result clearly demonstrates higher carrier mobility at low temperature (6.5 × 10 cm²/Vs as compared to below 35 K) (7.8 × 10³ cm²/Vs vs above 70 K), which suggests that the mean free path is more abundant at lower temperatures.
