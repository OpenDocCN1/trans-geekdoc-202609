## 15.2 HIGH SPEED MATERIALS

The requirement of high speed electronic components and devices in communication, medicine, and security is increasing day by day, which results in the requirement of novel materials. The progress in material science and enabling the growth of novel materials with high carrier concentration and mobility, for III–V materials and graphene.

### 15.2.1 III–V Based Materials

High speed devices have been fabricated using III–V material quantum well heterostructure. The III–V material contains different compound materials such as InP, AlGaAs, InGaAs, and GaN [8], [9]. High mobility enables the heterost-based devices to operate at very high frequency, up to several GHz, and room temperature. These semiconductor structures can be grown with materials nearly the same lattice constant as that of a different bandgap.

The combination of different compound materials generates the two-dimensional electron (2DEG) in the channel where carriers are free to move only in two directions. As a result, charge charges provide better control to design the devices [10]. The fabrication process of heterostructure is similar than that of bulk materials, which consist of different layers as substrate, buffer, spacer, barrier, and cap layer [9]. These materials can be grown using molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) [11] and organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) [12] techniques.

### 15.2.2 Novel Materials

Novel materials including graphene have been exposed with very high carrier mobility. Graphene was first demonstrated at the University of Manchester, UK, in 2004 [13]. The graphene is a unit of graphite having two-dimensional (2D) honeycomb lattice structure with zero bandgap [14], [15]. Graphene has excellent properties such as high carrier mobility, thermal conductivity, mechanical strength, flexibility, transparency. Therefore, graphene has attracted many industries and academia to utilize it for various applications [15], [16]. High quality monolayer graphene and few layer graphene can be grown by mechanical exfoliation [13], [17]; however, other techniques such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [18], [19], chemical exfoliation [20], and ultrasonic exfoliation [22] can also be used to grow the multilayer graphene. The graphene mobility up to 20,000 cm²/Vs at room temperature [23]. The mean path for graphene has been estimated to be in the µm range, at at a mobility of 10,000 cm²/Vs and carrier concentration of $5 \times 10^{12}$ cm⁻² [13]. A large mean path allows the fabrication of electronics devices ballistic transport.

Several research groups are now concentrating on developing new classes of 2D materials such as dichalcogenides (MoS₂, and WS₂), 2D ZrO, h-BN, and phosphorus [16], [24]. These materials have structure similar to that of graphene. The nanofabrication provides creates to combine the graphene with other 2D materials such as a graphene/MoS₂, heterostructure to take the advantage of both the material properties. The graphene/h-BN-based heterostructure is expected to provide high carrier mobility up to 200,000 cm²/Vs [25], [26].

### 15.3 Quantum Point Contact (QPC)

The QPC is a narrow region with comparable wavelength to the charge carriers. The constriction can be formed by breaking the conductor into two parts so it can generate the 2DEG in the narrow region. The quantization of conductance can be realized in the QPC using GaAs/AlGaAs-based heterostructures [27], [31].

### 15.3.2 Single Electron Transistor (SET)

The behavior of conventional semiconductor devices is different from submicron devices. The conventional transistor becomes ON in response to electrons, in contrast to submicron/nanometer transistors, which are ON and OFF every time in response. This behavior of small transistors is due to charge quantization and coulomb interaction between charge carriers [32], [37]. The submicron-sized SET was first invented by Fulton et al. in 1987 [35]. The SET contains two gates, in comparison to conventional metal-oxide semiconductor field effect transistors (MOSFETs). When a positive voltage is applied to the upper gate terminal, keeping the bottom gate at a negative potential, the electrons are accumulated in the gap which is of the order of 700 Å, and 2DEG is formed in SET where electrons are confined in one direction and free to move in the other two directions [32], [37]. This electron gas separated from lead by tunnel or barrier junction results in conductance oscillation with respect to charge density. The potential applications of SET are at high frequency operation and multilevel logic [32].

### 15.3.3 Self Switching Diode (SSD)

The conventional diode is part of various electronic circuits for decades. The counterpart of this diode at high frequency is SSD [38]. The I–V characteristic of SSD is similar to a diode without any need of doping and/or potential barrier for handling the nonlinear effects. The channel becomes opened and closed in response to applied voltage between trenches. A wide variety of turn-voltage can be used in the SSD by changing the width and length of the channel [43], [44], [46]. The SSDs can be designed using III-V heterostructures [38] and graphene [123]. These have also been demonstrated at 1.5 TH and room temperature [41], [48]. The rectifier circuit and digital logic can also be realized using multiple SSDs [38], [42], [51].

The classical-based mathematical models are unable to explain the working of novel devices [57], [101]. Here, semiclassical-based models are being used for estimating the nonlinear ballistic transport. Such devices include the TTJ [52]–[79], [82]–[87] and FTBR [77]–[79], [88]–[99].
