## 14.4.2 Avalanche Photodetector

Another major type of photodetector with an internal gain mechanism is an avalanche photodetector (APD), as shown in Figure 14.6a, and has a more refined architecture than the p–i–n photodetector to produce an extremely high electric field region (~10 V/cm). Therefore, in the depletion region where the photons are absorbed and the carrier pairs generated, a high electric field region exists where electrons and holes can attain enough energy to newly generated carriers. This phenomenon is recognized as impact ionization or carrier multiplication (CM) and is the process that results in avalanche breakdown in reverse-biased diodes. Normally, this needs a higher reverse bias voltage so that the newly generated carriers via impact ionization can produce more carriers with similar phenomenon, as shown in Figure 14.6b [9]. More than 10⁴ of multiplication can be achieved in APDs. Thus, APDs internally multiply the primary photocurrent before entering the input circuit of the amplifier. As the photocopy is multiplied before facing the thermal noise associated with the receiver circuit, the sensitivity of the receiver increases.

A commonly used architecture for attaining carrier multiplication with slight excess noise is composed of a high resistive, that is, likely p+-type doped (p*) material deposited on a p* substrate. A p*-type ion implant is then made in the high resistive material, followed by n*-layer. The i-region is intrinsic material that is made with p-type doping. This configuration is termed p*–i–n* reach through the avalanche photodetector (RAPD) and is shown in Figure 14.6a. The term reach-through arises from the photodetector operation. The high electric field region is also known as the avalanche multiplication gain region, which is comparatively narrow and centered on the p-n' junction.

**FIGURE 14.6** (a) Reach-to-avalanche photodetector and the high electric field (gain) in the avalanche and depletion region. As the bias voltage increases, the depletion region extends to the p-region till it "reaches through" to the almost i-n region [6].

The photons are incident on the device through the p'-region and are absorbed in the i-region, acting as the collection region for the photogenerated carriers. After being absorbed, the photons give up their energy, thus generating EHP, and are separated by high E$_{field}$ in the i-region. The photogenerated carriers drift through the i-region in the p-n' junction. In the high field, inner carrier multiplication takes place [6].

## 14.4.3 Metal-Semiconductor-Metal Photodetector

The metal-semiconductor-metal (MSM) photodetector is regarded as an interdigitated photoconductor with the metal-semiconductor and semiconductor-metal junctions similar to Schottky barriers. The MSM photodetector is monolithic integration and is fabricated using processing steps similar to those needed for the construction of field-effect transistors (FETs) [43]. Normally, the MSM photodetector can be realized either back-to-back ohmic contacts during photoconduct operation or back-to-back Schottky contacts during photovoltaic operation. However, neither of these device architectures can work under self-powered mode, which is 0 V bias [44].

A typical device scheme of the MSM photodetector is depicted in Figure 14.7 [27] that comprises two metal electrodes on the top of the semiconductor absorption layer that absorbs a significant amount of incident photon over the desired spectral regime [9]. The low-resistance contacts are created by the conducting layer with the interdigitated electrodes with a finite width (1–7 $\mu$m) and finger spacing (1.5–5 $\mu$m), as demonstrated in Figure 14.7 [9], [27], [45], [46]. This MSM structure resulted in a planar architecture with an inherently low parasitic capacitance that allowed high-speed operation (up to 300 GHz) MSM photodetectors.

Also, the interdigitated metal finger contacts are made to maximize the coupling of light into the absorption layer by reducing their obstruction of the active area. This will reduce the distance that photogenerated carriers have to travel before being collected. The quantum efficiency of the MSM photector is reduced due to the shadowing effect caused by the interdigitated electrodes if the photon is injected from the electrode side. The quantum efficiency can be enhanced with the use of an antirelection coating to the surface. The problem can also be avoided by back illumination if the substrate is transparent to the incident photon [47].

## 14.4.4 Quantum Wells and Dots Photodetector

The miniaturized physical sizes of nanomaterials provide various nanostructuredodetectors such as quantum wells (QWs) and quantum dots (QDs) with unique photodetection properties [48–50]. The photodetection efficiency of the photodetector can be enhanced by simply changing the corresponding material properties [34].

The QWs- and QDs-based photodetectors are the alternative solutions to replace the Hgₓ,CdTe photodetectors in the IR and TH spectral regime with the high-temperature operation and high-density chip integration [34]. The quantum wells and dots are usually fabricated by inserting a well or dot layer in energy barriers via epitaxial molecular beam epitaxy (MBE). The well barriers isolate layers resulting in increased material absorption in the active area, reducing tunneling effects, and reduced dark current [9]. The quantum efficiency of such structure is generally achieved through the materials with mismatched valence or conduction band. The scheme of the band alignment in QW and QD photodetectors is shown in Figure 14.8. The potential barrier at the interface due to the energy band match creates confinement of the free carriers in the well (or dot) layer. Upon light, the photoexcited electrons from the confined conduction band (well or dot) layer are swept out to the electrodes. The intraband absorption in QWs and QDs permits IR photetection beyond the intrinsic banding of materials. To further enhance the absorption capacity, multilayers of QWs (or QDs) are employed in the active region of the photodetector. The resultant photodetector device is denoted as the dots-in-well (DWELL), as shown in Figure 14.8b [9], [34].

For the efficient coupling of light to the active region, an optical coupling layer (corrugated, gratings layers, etc.) is included in the photodetector device. The inclusion of such a layer is important in QWs-based photodetectors, 2D QD do not absorb the incident photons directly from normal incidence. However, no such limitation exists for the zero-dimensional (0D) QDs due to their better three-dimensional quantum confinements, allowing the QDs based IR photodetectors in device integration [34].
