## 14.2 SEMICONDUCTOR PHOTodetECTORS

To detect photons, as optical radiation in a particular spectral regime, the internal and external photoemission effects of electrons are employed. Although external photoemission devices such as vacuum photodiodes and photomultiplier tubes meet certain performance standards, they are very heavy and need high voltages for their operation. Conversely, internal photoemission devices such as semiconductor photodetectors or without internal gain offer better compatibility and performance. These photodetectors are designed with a growing number of semiconductor materials, for example, silicon (Si) [9], germanium (Ge) [10], III-V alloys [11], II-VI alloys [12], and numerous novel two-dimensional (2D) materials [13], all of which many photodetection utilize in numerous ways. They have significant applications in all the major existing fiber optics communication systems.

The internal photoemission effect can occur in both the intrinsic and extrinsic regime of semiconductors. In intrinsic absorption, the incident photons excite electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. Moreover, for efficient absorption with rapid response, the intrinsic absorption phenomenon is impurity created in the material. Generally, all photodetectors utilize intrinsic p–n junction for optical fiber communications.

A basic photodiode is a reverse-biased p–n optoelectronic device with a junction exposed to light. Photodetectors absorb most of the requirements and hence are widely utilized as photodetectors. A photodetector absorbs the incident optical radiation and converts it into the electrical signal, called photocurrent, which is amid before possible processing. Thus, when taking into account signal attenuation associated with the link, the performance of the system is estimated at the photodetector. The enhancement of the photodetector performance enables the installation of fewer repeater stations and reduces the operating costs and capital investment. Under equilibrium conditions, a potential barrier exists across the depleted areas on either side of the p–n junction. Generally, the photodetectors are sufficiently reverse-biased, meaning that there is no current flow without incident light; the intrinsic area has been depleted by carriers. ### 14.2.1 Basic Requirements for Photodetector

A photodetector must meet severe requirements in performance and compatibility with optical communication systems when converting an optical signal to an electrical signal. The optical signal weakened and distorted after emerging from the fiber end, so the photodetector must satisfy the following stringent performance requirements [9], [15]:

- i. Smaller size, that is, the photodetector must be well-matched with the physical sizes of the optical fiber.
- ii. Better sensitivity (responsivity) in the desired spectral regime, that is, wavelength selectivity.
- iii. High fidelity, that is, the photodetector response should be linear for a wide range of the optical signal.
- iv. Efficient conversion of photons to electrons, that is, high external quantum efficiency.
- v. Low noise (i.e., low dark currents, leakage currents, and shunt conductance) and high gain.
- vi. Fast response time, that is, large bandwidth.
- vii. Insensitive to the temperature variations.
- viii. Low voltages, that is, the photodetector should not require excessive currents or bias voltages.
- ix. Long operating life and reasonable cost compared to other system components.
- x. High reliability, that is, the photodetector must perform continuous room temperature operation for several years.
- xi. High photetection speed.

Among photonic devices, photodetector holds a critical position and found a variety of consumer and scientific applications. The major issue of a photodetector is its compatibility with CMOS technology to provide low power dissipation, good noise immunity, high performance, and better reliability. To attain high-performance and CMOS-compatible technology, a suitable semiconductor material must be selected. The semiconductor materials used in a photodetector define many of its critical properties. Both the light wavelength to which it responds and the noise level are crucial parameters that depend on the material used in the photodetector. The wavelength sensitivity of various materials happens when only photons having enough energy to excite an electron across the energy bandgap of the semiconductor material will generate significant energy in producing the current from the photodetector.

Si-based photodetectors [9], [16–18] show their better performance in the ultraviolet (UV) and visible spectral regime with sufficient speed, low dark current, moderate conductivity, and long-term stability. The use of a Si-based photodetector is commercially available and is restricted to the first-generation spectrum due to an indirect energy bandgap of Si (−1.12 eV), which gives a response above 1,100 nm. Therefore, in the spectral regime, 1,100 to 1,600 nm (second-generation systems) research is dedicated to semiconductor materials with narrow bandgaps. This motivates the integration of Si with Ge [10], III–V [11], [16], [19], and II–VI [12] compounds that provide a better response in the wider spectral regime. The performance of such devices has been further enhanced in recent years, and a selection of photodetectors is now commercially available.
