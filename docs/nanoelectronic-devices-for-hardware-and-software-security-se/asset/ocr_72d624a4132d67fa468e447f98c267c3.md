# 15.2 HIGH SPEED MATERIALS

The requirement of high speed electronic components and devices in communication, medicine, and security is increasing day by day, which results in the requirement of novel materials. The progress in material science and enabling the growth of novel materials with high carrier concentration and mobility, for III–V materials and graphene.

# 15.2.1 III–V Based Materials

High speed devices have been fabricated using III–V material quantum well heterostructure. The III–V material contains different compound materials such as InP, AlGaAs, InGaAs, and GaN [8], [9]. High mobility enables the heterost-based devices to operate at very high frequency, up to several GHz, and room temperature. These semiconductor structures can be grown with materials nearly the same lattice constant as that of a different bandgap.

The combination of different compound materials generates the two-dimensional electron (2DEG) in the channel where carriers are free to move only in two directions. As a result, charge charges provide better control to design the devices [10]. The fabrication process of heterostructure is similar than that of bulk materials, which consist of different layers as substrate, buffer, spacer, barrier, and cap layer [9]. These materials can be grown using molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) [11] and organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) [12] techniques.

# 15.2.2 Novel Materials

Novel materials including graphene have been exposed with very high carrier mobility. Graphene was first demonstrated at the University of Manchester, UK, in 2004 [13]. The graphene is a unit of graphite having two-dimensional (2D) honeycomb lattice structure with zero bandgap [14], [15]. Graphene has excellent properties such as high carrier mobility, thermal conductivity, mechanical strength, flexibility, transparency. Therefore, graphene has attracted many industries and academia to utilize it for various applications [15], [16]. High quality monolayer graphene and few layer graphene can be grown by mechanical exfoliation [13], [17]; however, other techniques such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [18], [19], chemical exfoliation [20], and ultrasonic exfoliation [22] can also be used to grow the multilayer graphene. The graphene mobility up to 20,000 cm²/Vs at room temperature [23]. The mean path for graphene has been estimated to be in the µm range, at at a mobility of 10,000 cm²/Vs and carrier concentration of $5 	imes 10^{12}$ cm⁻² [13]. A large mean path allows the fabrication of electronics devices ballistic transport.

Several research groups are now concentrating on developing new classes of 2D materials such as dichalcogenides (MoS₂, and WS₂), 2D ZrO, h-BN, and phosphorus [16], [24]. These materials have structure similar to that of graphene. The nanofabrication provides creates to combine the graphene with other 2D materials such as a graphene/MoS₂, heterostructure to take the advantage of both the material properties. The graphene/h-BN-based heterostructure is expected to provide high carrier mobility up to 200,000 cm²/Vs [25], [26].
