An insulator is employed to separate the gate from the channel region. A TFET is termed as n- or p-type according to the channel's dominant carrier, which is electrons for an n-type TFET or holes for p-type TFET, respectively. The chapter focuses only on the n-type TFET as the principle of operation is similar for both types of TFET (p-type injection into the source is holes whereas it is electrons for n-type). However, it should be noted that regardless of the type (n- or p-type), TFETs are ambipolar devices in comparison to MOSFETs. For instance, in an n-type TFET, if the gate is highly negatively biased, electrons being the main contribution in the current, transport may show p-type=like behavior.

For an n-type TFET, a positive source voltage ($V_{gs}$) is applied to the gate, which induces a band bending at the channel, resulting in tunneling of electrons from $E_s$ of the source to $E_c$ of the channel region. The electrons that tunneled the channel region are swept to the drain terminals due to the positive biasing at the drain. At this time, the transistor is considered to be in the ON-state, when the gate is biased and no tunneling occurs from the source to the channel region, it is considered as OFF-state, as depicted in Figure 2.2. An in-depth discussion on the tunneling phenomenon is demonstrated in section 2.3. One benefit among the BTBT is the lack of the thermal (KT) dependency which makes for sharper than 60 mV/decade ON characteristic at $T = 300$ K. The resultant band to band tunneling current is mathematically defined as (detailed equations in section 2.3) [14]:

```
$I_{BTBT} \simeq A^2 \exp\left[\frac{B}{\varepsilon}\right]$$ (2.1)
```

where $A$ and $B$ are parameters dependent on the semiconductor material. Since the p–i–n structure of the TFET is in reversed bias in the OFF-state, diode leakage currents are the main contribution to the off current ($I_{OFF}$), and are substantially lower than the OFF-state current of the MOSFET [15].
