## 14.4.5 Barrier Photodetectors

Among different device architecture and design concepts, barrier photodetectors have been most extensively used, which was first developed by White [51]. Normally, an n-type dual-heterojunction bipolar photodetector comprises a narrow bandgap contact layer, a wide bandgap barrier layer (B), and a narrow bandgap active (or absorber) layer, resulting in an nBn photodetector as shown in Figure 14.9a. The major carriers, that is, electrons are blocked in such devices due to the absence of valence band discontinuity, which allows the passage of photogenerated minority carriers, that is, holes. Due to a large conduction band offset and a nearly zero valence band offset, the major carrier current between two electrodes is blocked, as illustrated in Figure 14.9b. This unipolar photodetector operates as a minority carrier photogenerator. An nBn photodetector suppresses the noise level and dark-current by reducing surface-level leakage current and depletion-level thermal generation recombination current, resulting in better uniformity with an ordinary p–n–p junction photodiode [52], [53]. The nBn photodetectors have been effectively developed using Hg,CdTe for IR detection, InAsSb/Sb-based IR photodetector [57], and InGaAsSb/GaSb [58].

Compared to the III-V semiconductor heterostructures, Hg,CdTe does not show a near-zero valence band offset, requiring high bias voltage to control the generated carriers [49]. To solve this issue, a p-doped barrier is used to increase the conduction band offset and reduce the offset in the valence band. Moreover, due to existence of the barrier, the n-type contact layer in nBn device can be replaced with the p-type contact layer, resulting in a pBn photodetector, as shown in Figure 14.9a. Therefore, a p–n junction is located between the barrier and the active.

A wide variety of barrier photodetectors have been developed as CB,n and CB,p photodetectors [59], [60]. In the first category, both the barrier and active layers are of n-type,

## 14.5 CHARACTERISTICS PARAMETERS OF THE PHOTODECTORS

To compare and quantify the photodetector performance, a set of fundamental characteristic parameters or figures-of-merit need to be considered. This section briefly explains the characteristics parameters of photodetectors.

### 14.5.1 Electrical Parameters

#### 14.5.1.1 Current Density and Resistance Area Product

In photodetectors, the electrical characteristics depend on many parameters, such as doping profiles, device length, material type, and defects. The dark current density ($J_{dark}$) is an important characteristic parameter of photodetectors for estimation of operating temperature and bias voltage. The dark current density $J_{dark}$ is a combination of diffusion current density ($J_{diff}$), drift current density ($J_{drift}$), and tunneling current density ($J_{TUN}$). $J_{TUN}$ comprises trap-assisted tunneling (TAT) and band-to-band (BTB) tunneling [64]. The high reverse bias leads to bending in the energy band to tunnel electrons from the valence to the conduction band. The effective resonance area product $(RA)_{NET}$ arises due to different current densities, and can be written as:

$$\frac{1}{(RA)_{NET}} = \frac{1}{(RA)_{diff}} + \frac{1}{(RA)_{drift}} + \frac{1}{(RA)_{TAT}} + \frac{1}{(RA)_{BTB}}$$

where $(RA) = [d/dV]^{-1}$. When the device is illuminated with incident photocurrent ($P_{in}$), carriers are produced in the junction. Such photogenerated carriers get separated or drifted away due to the presence of built-in $E_{field}$ and contribute to photogenerated current through an external circuit.

#### 14.5.1.2 Photosensitivity and Linear Range

The photosensitivity is defined as the ratio of photocurrent density $J_{light}$ at a desired wavelength to the dark density $J_{dark}$. The generated $J_{light}$ and photosensitivity of the device must be larger to achieve high-performance photodetector. Linear dynamic range (LDR) defines the input illumination range within which the photocurrent of a photodetector varies linearly with intensity and is approximated in logarithmic scale as:

$$LDR(dB) = 20\log\left(\frac{J_{light}}{J_{dark}}\right)$$

A high value of LDR is preferable to detect both weak and strong light.

#### 14.5.1.3 Photoswitch Time

The photoswitch time measures the response speed of a photodetector and is characterized by the rise time and fall time. The rise time (or response time, $\tau_r$) and fall time (or recovery time, $\tau_f$) are measured at 10% and 90% of maximum $J_{light}$ and are strongly influenced by charge transport and collection mechanism. A rapid photoswitch response, that is, smaller values of rise and fall times, is desirable for the high-performance photodetector.

### 14.5.2 Optical Parameters

#### 14.5.2.1 Spectral Photoresponse

For any photodetectors, they can only respond to a specific wavelength range. When designing photodetector, the first consideration is choosing the proper photosensitive material that can respond to the photo signal. To detect the photo signal, the bandgap of the selected material should be smaller or equal to the photon energy.

#### 14.5.2.2 Quantum Efficiency

Quantum efficiency ($QE$) determines how efficiently the semiconductor material converts light into current, that is, it measures the sensitivity of the photodetector. It is the percentage of photogenerated charge that contributes to the photocurrent and, mathematically, is the ratio of electron-hole pairs ($N_e$) generated to the number of incident photons ($N_p$), i.e.,

$$QE = \frac{N_e}{N_p}$$

The QE of the device is wavelength-dependent due to the semiconductor energy gap and wavelength-dependency of optical absorption. The QE may decrease due to reflection, absorption, and electrical properties of the semiconductor device. The two types of quantum efficiencies, that is, external $QE_{ext}$ and internal ($QE_{int}$), take into account a photodetector. $QE_{ext}$ is a measure of the number of photogenerated carriers collected by the electrodes by excitation by incident photons. $QE_{int}$ of internal photoemission (IPE) process is the number of carriers emitted to semiconductor material per absorbed photon. If the lifetime of photogenerated carriers exceeds the transit time, a $QE$ of more than 100% is in photodetectors.

#### 14.5.2.3 Photocurrent Responsivity

The photocurrent responsivity ($R_i$) is a ratio of photocurrent to the incident light power and is given by

$$R_i = \frac{I_{light}}{P_{in}} / W$$

The $QE_{int}$ which gives the internal photocurrent responsivity ($R_{int}^{int}$), and the $QE_{ext}$, which determines the external photocurrent responsivity ($R_{ext}^{ext}$), are calculated using the following relations [66–69]:

$$R_{int}^{int} = QE_{int} \left( \frac{\lambda}{hc} \right) A / W$$

$$R_{ext}^{ext} = QE_{ext} \left( \frac{\lambda}{1.24} \right) A/W$$

#### 14.5.2.4 Specific Detectivity

The specific detectivity ($D^*$) determines the smallest signal that a photodetector can detect and is calculated using the following relation

$$D^* = \frac{R_{ext}}{2} \sqrt{\frac{(R_{\lambda})_{NET}}{kT}}$$

where $(R_{\lambda})_{NET} = \left( \frac{dI}{dV} \right)_{V=0}$ is the resistance area product at zero biased, that is, under the self-powered mode. The high value of $(R_{\lambda})_{NET}$ is desirable to result in high specific detectivity.
