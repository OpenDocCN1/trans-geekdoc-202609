## 15 A Deep Learning Strategy for Sign Language Classification and Recognition for Hearing-Impaired People

Souvagya Ganguli, Sumit Anand, Supri ti Das, and G Rohith

DOI: 10.1201/9781003465775-15

### 15.1 INTRODUCTION

Sign languages are forms of communication that convey meaning using manual gestures or visual cues. Sign language, often known as SL, is the principal mode of communication utilized by persons who are deaf or hearing impaired when attempting to convey their thoughts or emotions to hearing people in everyday life [1]. A collection of gestures, hand motions, facial expressions, or head movements can be used to represent letters or symbols in SL, and each letter of the alphabet has a corresponding sign. There are between 138 and 300 distinct forms of sign language used throughout the world now due to the fact that there are more varieties of sign language being used around the world and that these variations are being improved as a result of various groups of people connecting with each other [2]. The visual means of communication utilized by the hand-talk community and ordinary people to transmit the meaning include hand gestures, signals, body movements, facial expressions, and lip motions; we know this language as a sign language. The process of sign language recognition (SLR) is difficult and intricate, and the current state of the art in artificial intelligence makes possible a wide variety of study prospects. According to the figures compiled by the World Health Organization, around 5% of the global population is affected by hearing loss. The United Nations has made the projection that there will be 900 million people who are hard of hearing in the world by the year 2050 [3]. As a result, SLR is garnering a lot of attention at the moment. SLR has the potential to eradicate the language barrier that exists within the hand-talk community (deaf-mute).

The following are some more ways that SLR contributes to the improvement of communication:

- It lessens the level of frustration felt by members of the hand-talk community.
- The elimination of the communication barrier caused by SLR results in improved communication.

The two modalities of the SLR are as follows:

- Sensor based: Sensors placed in close proximity to the signer can record the signer’s head and finger movements as well as their overall motion. Gloves equipped with sensors can follow the signer’s movements and interpret what they are trying to say. Sensor-based SLR, as opposed to vision-based SLR, yields effective results.
- Vision based: The vision- or image-based method is used to identify the gestures taken by many cameras (or a webcam). It analyzes the image or video and draws out elements related to the hand’s palm, fingers, and motion. Classification is accomplished by using these gleaned properties. Misclassification occurs because of insufficient lighting, a distracting background, or blurred images. Though vision-based SLR may work in real time, special care has to be taken with preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification.

A significant amount of research [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11] was done in an effort to build high-performance SLR. Despite this, it is difficult, and it is one of the relatively new study disciplines that has a huge research scope. Multiple hand movements and facial expressions add complexity and difficulty to SLR. Many hand signals appear to be of the same stance, but lip shapes and eyebrow locations help differentiate them. In any case, these may be distinguished from one another through the use of facial expression and lip movement. Hand gestures, palm positions, finger motions, facial expressions, and body language are all essential components of sign language. The efficiency of SLR is profoundly affected by these factors. There are still several issues that need to be resolved, even if the overall trend of the suggested models shows a considerable improvement in recognition accuracy in sign language. In addition to analyzing applications, datasets, hybrid models, complexity, and next research directions in the field, the study proposes a taxonomy to classify the suggested models for isolated and continuous sign language recognition [12]. People with speech and hearing impairments have benefited greatly from the various scientific research and technical advancements that have been undertaken in recent decades. Two of the most common methods used to aid this cause are deep learning and computer vision. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are efficient architectures for natural language processing (NLP) tasks such as language translation, question and answer functions, image captioning, and video captioning. Although simple or “vanilla” RNNs can take as many time steps as needed, in order to remember dependencies over a longer period of time – say, dozens or even hundreds of time steps – a special sort of RNN called long short-term memory (LSTM) can be used. Gated recurrent units (GRUs), a mild variant of the LSTM, have fewer gates and no memory cells.
