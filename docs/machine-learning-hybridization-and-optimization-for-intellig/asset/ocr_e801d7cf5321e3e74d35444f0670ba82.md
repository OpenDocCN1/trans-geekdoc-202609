Ayush et al. [7] used recurrent neural networks (RNN) for the prediction of cases in the future as various models have been developed to forecast future cases. They explain the limitations of data availability and also consider various assumptions in terms of its reliability. They present various types of models such as LSTM, Bi-Directional LSTM, and encoder-decoder LSTM, experimented with univariate and multivariate data. They use the models for the prediction of future cases in India and also a few other states which has more COVID cases in the country. They predict the cases for two months into the future, and the results have been promising as the accuracy obtained from the models shows beyond doubt that the available data is insufficient.

The focus of Saurav et al. [8] was on controlling the spread of COVID-19 across India with the implication of lockdown, and they designed a model that predicts the total number of days the entire country needs to be in lockdown in order to lead to the isolation of the virus and to remove the last possible case occurring due to COVID-19. The model was based on a formula obtained by arithmetic progression, with parameters such as the total number of infected people, the rate of spread of the virus from each person, and the recovery rate of the infected; however, it does not take into account the asymptomatic cases due to which the rate of spread factor would vary from its true value. The results discuss the amount of days lockdown has to be established for various rates of spread.

Petropoulos et al. [9] suggested an exponential smoothing technique which was used for forecasting cases of COVID-19 cases for 10 days into the future. The model predicts over various chunks of intervals because of the uncertainty of the predicted value over the actual data. This occurs because of the limited amount of data available for COVID-19 and the exponential growth in cases across the globe in certain intervals of time, since the data available from the initial months of the COVID-19 model was trained only upon the data obtained from China, but the uncertainty occurred when cases started emerging in other countries; this led to an exponential growth, thereby leading to the poor performance of the model, which is why the chapter focused on predicting cases for a 30% interval, followed by 50% and 75%, to provide better accuracy.
