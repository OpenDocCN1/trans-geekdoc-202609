## 12.3 EVOLUTION OF THE PATH PLANNING PROBLEM

For humans, getting from one location to the next is a simple undertaking. One makes a decision about how to proceed. Such a simple and straightforward operation is a significant difficulty for robots. Path planning is a crucial challenge in robotic applications [30]. Finding a sensible route for a robot to travel from starting location to a destination position, whether it be a cleaning robot, a mechanical arm, or a mysterious drone, is indeed a standard challenge. Determining a way from an initial place to a desired location is the issue, taking into account the modeling framework, the sort of robots, the purpose, and so on. This problem was approached in many ways in this study. A path optimization method that is productive is necessary for safe and reliable mobile robot navigation because the integrity [31] of the successful approach has a significant impact on industrial applications. As it affects other parameters like computation time and resource usage, decreasing the distance traveled is usually the primary goal of the mapping process. In this survey, we have studied research articles from the past four decades to interpret the evolution of route planning algorithms. Initially, researchers started to work with classical and hybrid approaches. Path planning issues are frequently addressed with approaches from the conventional category, including cell decomposition (CD), potential field (PF), road map, and subgoal network. These techniques are understood to be computationally costly and are shown to be less effective in managing unfamiliar, imperfectly known, or dynamic settings in their fundamental articulation [32]. With regard to building a viable route among the source and destination places, they are also frequently found to rely on thorough previous information about the environment. These problems can be resolved by meta-heuristic methods since they are adept at managing unknowable or imperfectly known settings. Each repetition of these procedures generates a series of interim paths that get them one algorithmic step closer to the final location. In contrast to conventional methods, which create an optimal path based on previous data and then implement it, meta-heuristic techniques produce a subpopulation of potential movements in each loop. Following that, the optimal path will be selected and carried out based on their fitness. Some researchers viewed meta-heuristic-based techniques for path planning as being based on techniques like genetic algorithms (GA), neural networks (NN), particle swarm optimization (PSO), and ant colony optimization (ACO) [33]. To improve mobile robots’ skills in dealing with stationary and moving obstacles, several experts combined neural network models and reinforcement learning. These problems can be resolved by meta-heuristic methods simply because they are adept at managing unknowable or imperfectly known settings. Each repetition of these procedures generates a series of provisional paths that get them one algorithmic step closer to the final position.

Potential field (PF), Dijkstra’s algorithm (DA), rapidly exploring random tree (RRT), and probabilistic road map (PRM) are the traditional techniques for path planning that were considered during the research. These four strategies were picked because they have a solid track record in the field and are frequently and productively used to solve path planning issues. These approaches are acknowledged to have mediocre overall performances in global path planning issues. Additionally used in local path planning solutions are PF, RRT, and PRM [34].

### 12.4.1 PARTICLE SWARM OPTIMIZATION

Particle swarm optimization (PSO) is a direct approach to finding the optimal solution to a given problem. Unlike other optimization strategies, PSO only requires the fitness value and does not rely on the direction or any derivative of the objective. The solution space of the problem or function is populated with a multitude of small, simple entities called particles, each evaluating the fitness function at its current location [36]. Subsequently, each particle determines its movement through the solution space by combining a historical component of its current and best-fit coordinates with the coordinates of one or more members of the swarm, along with some random perturbations. After every particle has been updated, the process moves on to the next iteration. Ultimately, the swarm converges in the vicinity of the maximum of the fitness function, resembling a flock of birds searching for food together.
