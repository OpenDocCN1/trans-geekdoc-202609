
In [20], Albaro et al. put forward a centralized decision-making algorithm for a collaborative hunt in which all the UAVs maintain and combine data related to the search region. The robots partake in their detector compliances with each other. These compliances help to modernize the locally available chart related to the area. The data collected by each of the UAVs comprises the count of compliances done in each subarea of the terrain. The robots can share the information among themselves and they partake in the terrain data. The centralized base station allocates the robots to a different area without any discovery crimes [17] or a detector with only mis-findings [16]. Some approaches also focus on limitations, for example, dimension noise [22] and the discovery of crimes of false admonitions and mis-findings [21]. In [21], the authors use theoretic detector operation to decide the efficient paths of robots in a well-distributed manner. The detector operation redirects the path of a robot to such a location where anticipated information gain gets optimized due to unborn detector compliances. Though the stated algorithms minimize queries related to the hunting region, the methods do not take into account communication limitations.

## 2.2.6 DISTRIBUTED INFORMATION MERGING AND DISTRIBUTED DECISION-MAKING (DIMDD)

The UAVs can become largely independent when they are enabled to both incorporate the information and make decisions on their own. The completely distributed multi-robot collaborative hunt approaches [23, 24, 25] enable UAVs to retain seeing, communication, processing, and memory capabilities [26]. Each UAV has the ability to sense the terrain, modify its hunt chart, merge and exchange information, and update its path as per requirements in order to efficiently search the terrain. In some of these approaches, seeing and communication limitations are not considered [23, 24, 25]. A subset of approaches of this class takes into account limitations like seeing or communication limitations, but the approaches cannot work if both limitations are present simultaneously. In [27], the authors propose an analogous approach in which limitations related to communication range have been considered. A unique species of this algorithm is that the robots can decide about using a collective decision-making methodology. Though the system assumes communication limitations, it does not come up with a realistic detector model which considers seeing the limitations. An approach with completely distributed cooperation has been put forward in [17] in which the UAVs match in terms of participating double detector compliances. Here, the system considers a realistic detector model for both types of crimes; however, limitations in dispatches are not included.

In [18], Hu et al. devised a multi-UAV collaborative approach which uses both communication and seeing limitations. This approach also focuses on agreement among the UAVs in order to maintain analogous charts about each robot with a limited number of compliances.

## 2.3 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

We have discussed various types of decision-making algorithms in this chapter, like centralized decision making, distributed decision making, and their subcategories. We have also found the role of big data processing technologies to be very crucial in managing the information which gets generated by a network of UAVs every moment. Simple centralized decision-making can be used with two UAVs searching for a mobile target. Improved versions of centralized decision-making, like centralized information merging and distributed information merging, can be used to solve even complex problems. In simple distributed decision-making, the UAVs are unable to communicate with each other; hence we have the problem of data redundancy. This also results in overlapping of the paths of the UAVs, thereby reducing the efficiency of the system. However, this problem has been solved in centralized decision-making and distributed decision-making, where information is stored centrally, but the UAVs are free to decide their own paths. Hence, to conclude, depending on the complexity of the environment, we need to choose the appropriate algorithm accordingly.
