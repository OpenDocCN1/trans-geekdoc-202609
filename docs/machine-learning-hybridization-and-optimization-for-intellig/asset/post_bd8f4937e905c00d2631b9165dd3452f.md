### 12.1 INTRODUCTION

Robotics is frequently described as an inertial navigation system which comprises a set of computer devices working together to complete a task. At the beginning, robotics was restricted to a few engineering and heavy-industry applications. Eventually, when robotics gained widespread acceptance across a variety of industries, numerous robot forms were used to complete different tasks [1], which increased the need for route planning procedures. The procedure of detecting the best way from a particular location to another in a search space is called path planning. Path planning improves the way by figuring out the easiest route connecting sending and target endpoints. Another definition of path planning includes the technique of decomposing a significant path transition into a series of repeated steps to produce precise moves to optimize particular objects. Robots that are independent in nature and employ [2] route-finding algorithms to establish passage endpoints across space might be considered as objects. A mobile robot is one that fits this description and can be utilized to resolve the difficulties in this domain. Path planning issues are significantly influenced by environmental conditions [3]. Path planning can be classified into dynamic and static classes depending on the description of the surrounding environment. In the first case, information about a search space with known geometry stationary barriers is provided, and an approach is then used to find the path. Then, the robot employs sensing devices or legitimate data and gathering tools [4] to locate obstacles that are continually moving throughout the whole workplace. The strategies used for path planning have a significant history. Beginning with a heuristic method to address the path planning challenge and progressing to adaptive and composite techniques to identify the best route from cell A to cell B, the robot will typically move across the solution space.

Most jobs that can be completed simply can be handled by one robot. When numerous goals must be achieved concurrently, a multi-robot approach is necessary, which complicates the situation. Numerous robots have been employed in the past for applications such as flexible route planning, cooperative construction, multiple job allocation, mapping environmental variables, and many others [5]. Multi-robot technology is employed to solve challenging tasks. To work on it, you need an altogether different architecture. Each robot is given a specific objective to achieve, and each robot must travel the best route possible to get from where they are to where they need to go. It becomes more dynamic and sophisticated when numerous computers are managed at once in a shared workspace – dynamic in the sense that, in addition to the static impediments that currently exist in the workspace, each robot acts as an obstruction to every other robot. Because the environment is always changing, any standard path planning technique should act differently to calculate the best route for every individual robot. The requirement to incorporate a coordination mechanism into the working design to control all the robots operating in that workspace adds another layer of complexity [6]. As a result, it is necessary to address two concerns at once while addressing the multi-robot system issue. The first concern is the path planning algorithm, which seeks to determine the best possible route for individual robots, as well as the best possible optimized path including all the robots occupying the same workspace. The subsequent problem to solve is how to effectively coordinate robot movement so that all of them follow their ideal paths and do so without colliding [7].

There isn’t an extensive review that systematically incorporates path planning in mobile robots, despite the fact that numerous surveys are accessible that do so in various ways. The rest of this chapter is organized as follows: In Section 12.2, we go through the previous research works that are relevant to this domain. Section 12.3 explores the development of path planning methods over time, while Section 12.4 delves into the analysis of these algorithms, further addressing environmental inspection. Finally, Section 12.5 concludes and discusses future research related to this work.

### 12.2 RELATED WORK ANALYSIS

Al-Jarrah et al. [8] presented the path planning of multi-robot and collision-free coordination using a probabilistic neuro fuzzy network. The work employs subducted clustering along with least square calculation, which helps in creating fuzzy rules to understand the correlation between input and output data. The authors incorporate a leader-follower approach. A neural network was implemented to train membership function attributes, and ANFIS was used to tune fuzzy rules. Mathew et al. [9] adopted a traveling salesman problem approach with two variations including enumeration and reduction in several static warehouses. The objective of this research is to overcome the limitations of the heterogeneous delivery problem and formulate it as an optimal path planning concern. With this method, we can observe a 50% reduction in distance traveled by the truck and fuel consumption. In their study, Yang et al. resolved the collaborative path planning [10] issue using ant colony optimization (ACO). They considered the stationary obstacles in an unknown environment for simulation purposes. To enhance the confluent speed of the proposed method and smoothness of the initial path, they incorporated a multifactor heuristic function. Nasrollahy et al. addressed the problem of route planning with variable barriers in a dynamic environment. This strategy is straightforward and effective in both dynamic and static contexts and reduces overall costs [11]. This approach can be used to overcome any type of obstacle, independent of its size and shape, even if in this research, we consider the obstacles to have a circular form. In the study by Yu et al. [12], a polynomial-time algorithm called "split and group" is employed for path planning of multiple robots in well-connected environments. This approach addresses grid-based challenges and consistently produces a globally optimal schedule for all instances of the problem. In other words, SaG approximates results with a sublinear computation time and is an ideal case of the O(1)-approximation procedure. Kala et al. suggested a coevolutionary genetic programming approach for path planning. By combining centralized and decentralized planning [13], this model applies the concepts of collaborative progression to produce pathways which are best in terms of timing, and they also outlined the paradigm and problems associated with multispeed, map-based route planning for numerous robots. This involves the application of the evolutionary approach's wait for robot feature.

Duchoň et al. [14] worked with the mobile robot in a grid map. The necessary presumption for this is having SLAM and functional reactive movement. The A* technique is improved upon, and numerous adjustments (Basic Theta*, Phi*) are introduced. The changes are particularly concerned with computational efficiency and path maximization. Sharma et al. [15] tackled the problem of cooperative path planning for multiple robots in a warehouse setting. However, in such a multi-robot environment, these two issues are interconnected and must be addressed simultaneously to execute any operation successfully. It is crucial to manage each parameter carefully while optimizing the others. Gurji et al. [16] compared various path-finding techniques and also suggested the time-efficient A* algorithm. For both algorithms (A* and Modified A*), the percentage increase in path distance and the percentage decrease in computation are detected and measured. In this study, it is found that the updated A* approach decreases processing time by at least 65% with a maximum path length rise of 3.4%. Arora et al. [17] employed genetic algorithms for optimal path planning. The initial population consists of 20 random points. Among these, the points that fall within any barrier are identified. These points are assigned a very high destination length, rendering them unsuitable for selection. The final choice is based on the stored solutions with the lowest values. Chen et al. [18] discussed the issue of multi-robot path planning and outlined the fundamental components of a smart warehouse and also provided a graphical representation of the entire system. The genuine warehouse scenario involves consistent robot driving, so it is suggested that the architecture of the allocation column in the WHCA* method be adjusted. The persistent time of each location is reserved, and this information is used as storage.
