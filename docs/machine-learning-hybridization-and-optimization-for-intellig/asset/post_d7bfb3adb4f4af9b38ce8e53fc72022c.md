Joint decision-making capability in a network of UAVs allows them to manage their limited resources like battery, processing power, and so on and to improve the area of coverage. It also enables a user to control multiple robots at the same time. Joint decision-making assures the action of each UAV proves to be useful for the entire network of UAVs. Any uncoordinated behavior will result in a waste of resources and redundant coverage of the environment.

In cooperative multi-UAV searching, UAVs are typically coordinated to reduce the time to search for the location(s) of one or more fixed points. Exchanging the regional charts among each other assists the robots in understanding their surroundings and planning their routes in a better and more effective manner. Data is generated in a continuous manner and big data technologies are required to process the huge amount of generated data [3].

Cooperative searching is expressed by three parameters: (i) capturing the environment and making updates on the chart by each robot, (ii) communicating about regional data, and (iii) making collaborative judgments regarding search activities based on whatever information is available. Hence, the task of designing a network of UAVs requires intensive study and is still an open research problem.

## 2.2 LITERATURE REVIEW ON DECISION-MAKING FOR UAVS

There are various algorithms for decision-making for UAVs, which help to increase the efficiency of the system. While choosing an algorithm, we need to keep in mind the environment and other parameters, as different algorithms perform differently in different scenarios.

### 2.2.1 CENTRALIZED DECISION-MAKING (CD)

The centralized decision-making approach uses various methods to centrally decide and plan the paths for the UAVs. No information related to the environment is used here. Authors in [4] have proposed a CD approach for optimizing the routes and depicting exposures in the case of two robots hunting for a moving point. The terrain is modeled through streamlining of the graphs where the vertices symbolize the waypoints of the UAVs while the edges represent the connection among the waypoints. Retreating-horizon optimization is used to decide the routes in such a way as to reduce the time needed to search for a target. Daniel et al. in [5] have examined the centralized mobility algorithms in the case of multiple robots covering a given region. They also avoided spare compliances of similar regions. However, their work does not discuss tasks related to information-gathering. Rather, it concentrates on channel-apprehensive mobility.

### 2.2.2 DISTRIBUTED DECISION-MAKING

The distributed decision-making (DD) approach uses various technologies to distribute the decision-making process to plan the routes of UAVs. Information related to the area of search of the targets is not used. In [6, 7, 8], the authors proposed a distributive collaboration of some decision-making algorithms for UAVs which do not require a hunt chart. The authors induced excellent results for temporal and spatial information about the hunt region. In [6, 8], the authors put forward several configurations for UAVs which are connected by a network in order to completely explore an assigned region for previously unknown locales of targets. Their algorithm primarily focuses on adding or reducing the required number of robots for exploring the area. However, the authors do not concentrate on the process of modeling the detector crimes for participating in the information related to the search area. A force vector, which in turn helps in guiding the robots in the proper routes, is induced by the UAVs. This algorithm also ensures that the UAVs do not collide with each other and at the same time maximizes the area of terrain for the successful discovery of the target. However, since there is less global information, their algorithm sometimes suffers from information redundancy.

### 2.2.3 CENTRALIZED INFORMATION MERGING AND CENTRALIZED DECISION-MAKING (CIMCD)

In [9], the authors proposed an algorithm in which a central base station accounts for coupling the data and deciding the path for a group of homogeneous UAVs. In this case, each UAV collects some information during its flight. The UAVs are unfit for communicating the information with other UAVs or the ground station during their flight. After the robots reach the base station, the collected data is shared with the base station. The robots also receive information from the base station at this moment. Hence, the base station is responsible for merging the partial and delayed data from the various UAVs with the help of a consolidated hunt chart. The base station is also responsible for assigning new paths as per the K-shortest path algorithm [10]. This process is repeated again and again unless the query in the region to be explored has dropped below a certain level. In [11], Lum et al. use various robots with varied abilities to explore a particular terrain and search for the position of various fixed targets. It is assumed that there is perfect communication among the UAVs and the ground station during their flight. Hence, the complexity of information lagging is avoided. The base station combines the information from all the UAVs and stores it in a residency-grounded hunt chart. It generates immediate routes for all the robots which restrict them to different Voronoi partitions [12]. The system guarantees a total hunt of the terrain. In [12] Mirzaei et al. put forward an analogous approach that discusses a centralized assignment of miscellaneous UAVs to different Voronoi partitions. This approach uses a restricted look-ahead dynamic programming method to decide the best path for robots in order to maximize the amount of information collected by a group of UAVs. In this approach, two types of UAVs are used. The first type of UAVs spread over the terrain in order to cover the terrain in an optimal manner. The second type of UAV modifies its configuration iteratively. This process ameliorates the delicacy of the collaborative hunt.

### 2.2.4 CENTRALIZED INFORMATION MERGING AND DISTRIBUTED DECISION-MAKING (CIMDD)

In a multi-UAV collaborative hunt, the majority of the research depends upon consolidated hunt maps for the incorporation of data and distributed DM for the robots [13, 14]. The UAVs can piece together centrally stored information which comprises data related to the terrain and other UAVs. Depending on the availability of the centrally stored information, the UAVs are capable of planning the paths in a distributed manner. The algorithms in this domain incorporate artificial implicit fields [15], machine learning (ML) algorithms [16], group dissipation patterns [15], mixed integer direct programming [18], and evolutionary algorithms [19] as collaboration procedures in order to minimize the implicit imbrications in the routes of UAVs. In these methods, it is concluded that the reduction of imbrications in the path of UAVs can ameliorate the effectiveness of collaborative hunting. No sophisticated information is required in these approaches. They only need a system for modernizing the centrally stored information grounded on their detector compliances and previous information. Their approaches model either a perfect detector which does not have any discovery crimes [17] or a detector that only has mis-findings. In these approaches, it is assumed that there is no noise during the process of communication between the base station and the robots.
