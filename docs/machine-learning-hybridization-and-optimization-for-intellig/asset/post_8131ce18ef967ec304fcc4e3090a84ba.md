## 3.2.3 Literature Survey

The section presents a literature survey of various literatures which researched COVID-19; most of them would be new, as the problem of COVID-19 itself was new, but the ideas or models used in the literature might be the ones that can frequently be used. We take a few models which have been used in these chapters to explore the possibility of forecasting COVID-19 cases in India and do an elaborate comparison of the models that have been used.

Furquan et al. [6] analyzed how various ML models were used to predict the future number of cases that might occur due to COVID-19; in particular, they used models like linear regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), support vector machines (SVM), and exponential smoothing. The results were obtained by comparing the performance of the models in the prediction of upcoming infected cases, deaths, and patient recovery rates for the upcoming 10 days. Out of all the models, exponential smoothing performed the best in prediction followed by linear regression and LASSO regression, with SVM performing poorly in all the scenarios.

Ayush et al. [7] used recurrent neural networks (RNN) for the prediction of cases in the future as various models have been developed to forecast future cases. They explain the limitations of data availability and also consider various assumptions in terms of its reliability. They present various types of models such as LSTM, Bi-Directional LSTM, and encoder-decoder LSTM, experimented with univariate and multivariate data. They use the models for the prediction of future cases in India and also a few other states which has more COVID cases in the country. They predict the cases for two months into the future, and the results have been promising as the accuracy obtained from the models shows beyond doubt that the available data is insufficient.

The focus of Saurav et al. [8] was on controlling the spread of COVID-19 across India with the implication of lockdown, and they designed a model that predicts the total number of days the entire country needs to be in lockdown in order to lead to the isolation of the virus and to remove the last possible case occurring due to COVID-19. The model was based on a formula obtained by arithmetic progression, with parameters such as the total number of infected people, the rate of spread of the virus from each person, and the recovery rate of the infected; however, it does not take into account the asymptomatic cases due to which the rate of spread factor would vary from its true value. The results discuss the amount of days lockdown has to be established for various rates of spread.

Petropoulos et al. [9] suggested an exponential smoothing technique which was used for forecasting cases of COVID-19 cases for 10 days into the future. The model predicts over various chunks of intervals because of the uncertainty of the predicted value over the actual data. This occurs because of the limited amount of data available for COVID-19 and the exponential growth in cases across the globe in certain intervals of time, since the data available from the initial months of the COVID-19 model was trained only upon the data obtained from China, but the uncertainty occurred when cases started emerging in other countries; this led to an exponential growth, thereby leading to the poor performance of the model, which is why the chapter focused on predicting cases for a 30% interval, followed by 50% and 75%, to provide better accuracy.

Ian Cooper et al. [10] used a SIR model to find the infection rate of COVID-19 disease across the globe. The SIR model is a very common model used for finding the rate of infection and predicting the total number of people who would be infected at a particular period. SIR stands for susceptible, infected, and removed. Generally in this model, the total number of susceptible was constant; initially the entire susceptible population has a chance of being infected, and from that infected population, people either die or recover and are hence removed. So the number of susceptible people monotonically reduces to zero and the number of infected people rises to its peak and then reduces to zero, which ultimately get added to the recovered population.

Apurbala et al. [11] used piece-wise regression for the prediction of upcoming cases, deaths, and recovery. The initial method of using simple linear regression was providing huge error values because in various parts of India the growth in the number of cases was exponential at certain points, and henceforth, they used doubling time. Doubling time is the time taken for the current period for the infections to double. Doubling time is inversely proportional to the infection increase rate, because when the doubling time is large, then the increase in cases has been linear until that point, after which it doubles. The point in the graph where there was an increase that had doubled its previous value was considered as a breakpoint; with the help of breakpoints, piece-wise regression occurs. The chapters find breakpoints based on the difference between the current and previous points. Finally, with the help of piece-wise regression with linear regression, they were able to produce accurate values matching those of real data.

Muhammad et al. [12] used various supervised machine learning models for the forecasting of cases in various countries and suggested the best model out of them. The models used are decision trees, support vector machines, the naive Bayes model, and logistic regression. On running various models, it was discovered that the decision tree model provided the best accuracy, the SVM model had the highest sensitivity, and naive Bayes provided the highest specificity.

Massonnaud et al. [13] introduced a model for forecasting future cases as susceptible, exposed, infected, removed (SEIR), in which the varying rates of infection spread start from the best case (1.5) to the worst (3), based on which they calculate the total number of cases that might occur soon and conclude whether the beds available in various regions would be sufficient for the intake of patients that might occur due to the virus.

Malavika et al. [14] used logistic regression for forecasting cases for the short-term interval, which provided results with 95% accuracy where the model was trained in states with high daily cases. The SIR model was used for the prediction of cases in the worst-case scenario so that the necessary steps could be taken.

Anastassopoulou et al. [15] used the SIRD model (susceptible, infected, recovered, dead) and tried to find the reproduction rate of the virus in two different scenarios. The first one obtained the reproduction rate based on the official count provided, but since there are high chances of cases that are asymptomatic, the work has increased the count of infected by 20 and the recovered by 40 and calculated the reproduction rate of the virus. The average of the reproduction rate for both scenarios was taken as the final reproduction rate based on which the forecasting of the possible total number of infected cases in China was carried out for an interval of the upcoming 30 days.

AlJame et al. [16] used a combination of various models to predict if a person is COVID-19 positive based on the blood test provided. The ensemble model provided in the chapter is ERLX which comprises logistic regression, diverse classifiers, and extra trees, the outputs of which were combined by the XGBoost classifier. The pre-processing was done by KNNImputer with isolation forest for outlier detection in the model with synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) for balanced data distribution. The above-proposed model provided far better accuracy (99.8%) compared to various state-of-the-art models; due to the high accuracy obtained from the model, it was considered to be one of the most robust and reliable models for diagnosis of COVID-19 based on blood samples.
