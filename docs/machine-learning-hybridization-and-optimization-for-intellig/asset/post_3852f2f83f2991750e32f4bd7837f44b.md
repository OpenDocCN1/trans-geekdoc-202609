## 15.2 PROPOSED WORK

Problem statement: The preceding studies [15, 16, 17, 18] have shown, among other things, that methods that depend their accuracy on the user’s behavior are worse when it comes to recognizing signs in a language other than English. This is due to the high cost and complexity of the necessary hardware installations [15]. For this reason, most studies have focused on vision-based models. The creation of sign language recognition models is evidently still a challenging subject of research, despite the many studies that have been undertaken in this area.

In order to solve continuous sign issues that are independent of the signer, an appropriate model has to be developed [16]. Since each signer’s duration, pace, and context will be unique, it is difficult to create a model that is both highly accurate and consistent among all models [17]. Several academics have made suggestions on where CNN-related model research could go next. The dependency of models with regard to the optimization of tuning parameters adds another layer of difficulty to the task of selecting the most appropriate model to adopt and enhance [18]. The preceding analysis of the literature shows that most of the existing CNN models have been created for various sign languages, including those used in India, the Middle East, and China. However, advancements in British sign language detection using the CNN and LSTM models have received little attention. No research has compared the accuracy of LSTM and CNN models when used to recognize British sign language. As a result, there is a pressing need for research into improving future models’ accuracy by regulating the relevant parameters and establishing a trustworthy assessment technique for selecting the best model. Therefore, the goal of this study is to create a system for recognizing British sign language that employs CNN and LSTM while simultaneously maximizing accuracy by fine-tuning the relevant parameters. However, an issue arises when two people who speak different languages cannot communicate with one another without the physical assistance of a translator, which may not always be convenient to arrange while using LSTM. A similar issue also arises when a normal person and a person who is deaf or speech impaired interact with one another. The proposed model makes use of deep learning to create a low-priced sign language recognition system. The system is implemented in Python using the OpenCV and Keras libraries. The project evolved via three distinct stages as follows.

### 15.2.1 PREPARATION OF A DATASET WITH EXISTING STATE-OF-THE-ART DATA

The dataset is produced using the video camera’s live feed. Every frame that recognizes a hand in the defined region of interest (ROI) will be saved in a directory called “gesture directory,” which has two folders called “train” and “test,” each of which has 10 folders for the numbers 1–10 containing pictures taken with the create_gesture_data.py. The live cam feed is taken using OpenCV, and an ROI is created.

The background and foreground (ROI) are differentiated. The threshold values are calculated for every frame, and contours (for converting the image obtained to black and white such that the background is black and the objects detected in ROI are white) are determined by which we can know if there is any foreground object being detected in ROI (i.e., a hand).

Inspired by [21] and [22], who have presented the novel histogram equalization techniques, we impart adaptive histogram equalization which tries to predict the actual range of bins and try to exhaustively utilize the range between 0 and 255. Histogram equalization is performed by converting the image into HSV color space, and the algorithm is applied to luminance without changing the hue and saturation values of the image. The new intensity values try to expand the number of regions and the dynamic range, which helps to highlight the sign language more clearly and looks like edge-detected images as seen in Figure 15.2 (a–d). Figure 15.2 (a–d) shows the samples used for training after applying a histogram at a hue and saturation level. From Figure 15.2 (a–d), it can be seen that, while stretching the histogram, the sign numbers are much more visible and are indicated as white patterns on a black background. The previously created model was loaded, and some of the necessary variables were set, such as the background variable and the dimensions of the ROI. Similar to when preprocessing the dataset, a bounding box is defined, and then the cumulative average to identify the object in ROI is calculated. Since a contour may be obtained whenever a hand is detected, the ROI threshold can be used as a test image when trying to find the maximum contour.
