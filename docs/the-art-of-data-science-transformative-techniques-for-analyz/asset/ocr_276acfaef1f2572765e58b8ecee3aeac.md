A crucial statistic in hypothesis testing, the p-value measures the likelihood of getting the desired outcomes—or something even more extreme—if the null hypothesis is correct. More substantial evidence against the null hypothesis is shown by a decreased p-value, which leads to its rejection. Traditionally, a significance level (α), commonly set at 0.05, is chosen as the threshold. If the p-value is less than α, the results are deemed statistically significant, allowing researchers to reject the null hypothesis in favor of the alternative. However, it is essential to interpret statistical significance cautiously, recognizing that it does not imply the practical value or importance of the observed effect.

The concept of Type I and Type II errors adds a layer of nuance to hypothesis testing. A Type I error occurs when the null hypothesis is incorrectly rejected, falsely indicating the presence of an effect or difference that does not exist. Conversely, a Type II error arises when the null hypothesis is poorly retained, overlooking a genuine impact or difference. Balancing these errors is a delicate task, and the choice of significance level influences the trade-off between them. Researchers must weigh the potential consequences of making each type of error based on the specific context and implications of the analysis.
