Soon after this the Director collaborated with Walter Maner and organized “the National Conference on Computing and Values (NCCV). This was funded by America’s National Science Foundation. The aim was to foster cooperation among various professionals like computer scientists, philosophers, public leaders, law-makers, entrepreneurs, sociologists, psychologists, journalists, and others.

Another objective was to analyze and make progress in some of the important sub-fields of information and computer ethics. These areas included computer privacy, ownership of intellectual property, educating about computer ethics and helping persons with disabilities. The NCCV which was held in August 1991 witnessed the participation of various disciplines along with the efforts of Bynum and Maner.

The NCCV was attended by over four hundred people from all states in America and other countries as well. The conference produced monographs, video programs, and an extensive bibliography and other computer ethics materials. This was distributed amongst several colleges and universities in the following years.

Various professional ethics advocates came forward which included among others Donald Gotterbarn, Keith Miller and Dianne Martin. Professional organizations also became involved. These included organizations like Computer Professionals for Social Responsibility, the Electronic Frontier Foundation, and the Special Interest Group on Computing and Society (SIGCAS) of the ACM. They led projects that centered on developing the professional responsibility of computer practitioners.

Information and computer ethics turned into an integral module of undergraduate computer science programs that Computer Sciences Accreditation Board accredited. In 1991, “Computers, Freedom, and Privacy” conferences started to be organized annually. In 1992, the ACM also adopted an upgraded version Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct.

In 1995, Europe was also engulfed by the swift advancement made in the field of information and computer ethics. Maner joined hands with Simon Rogerson – a lecturer in De Montfort University in England. They jointly developed the Centre for Computing and Social Responsibility and organized the first computer ethics conference in Europe which was called ETHICOMP95. This conference was a huge success and attracted attendees from various countries in Europe. This conference was hugely responsible for giving birth to computer ethics scholars in Europe. The ETHICOMP traveled to many countries in Europe post-1995. It was held every 18 months and even moved to countries beyond Europe. Some of the countries to host this event were Poland, Greece, France, Spain, Portugal, China, and Japan. Encouraged by this far-reaching response, the Australian scholars John Weckert and Christopher Simpson with the backing of Bynum and Rogerson, established the Australian Institute of Computer Ethics in 1999. They also organized AICEC99 in Melbourne which was the first international computer ethics conference in that part of the globe. This has been followed by numerous AICE conferences.

Simon Rogerson was a prominent person in Europe in the rapid growth of information and computer ethics. His achievements include setting up the Centre for Computing and Social Responsibility at De Montfort University and co-heading the influential ETHICOMP conferences.

His contribution was also:

- He contributed towards adding computer ethics to De Montfort University’s curriculum;
- He also created a graduate program with advanced computer ethics degrees which comprised PhDs; and
- Was responsible for co-founding and co-editing two computer ethics journals with Ben Fairweather. Those two journals were: The Journal of Information, Communication, and Ethics in Society in 2003 and the electronic journal The ETHICOMP Journal in 2004.

Rogerson was also part of the IT Committee of the British Parliament. He went on to participate in various computer ethics projects alongside agencies based in the European Union.

During the late 1990s and early 2000s, various computer ethics were developed in Europe. Some of the prominent examples of those developments are:

- In the 1990s, Luciano Floridi founded the IE Research Group at Oxford University.
- In 1997, Jeroen van den Hoven created the Computer Ethics: Philosophical Enquiry series of conferences (CEPE), which was held alternately in America and Europe.
- In 1999 Van den Hoven developed the journal Ethics and IT.
- In the same year, Rafael Capurro established the ICIE.
- In 2004 Rafael Capurro developed International Review of IE, a journal.
- In 2005, Bernd Carsten Stahl created The International Journal of Technology and Human Interaction.

In conclusion, it can be said that computer ethics development has multiplied exponentially. New researches, new establishments, new newsletters, new research centers, study materials, websites, university programs, college degree courses, and illustrious professorships have contributed to this growth. As further advancement is made in information and computer ethics, additional subjects and topics continue to surface.

Various new topics have emerged in this field of ethics which include robot's ethics, cyborg ethics, “open source movement,” electronic government, global IE, IT and genetics, computing for developing countries, computing, and terrorism, ethics, and nanotechnology. The above-mentioned sub-fields are only a few among the various disciplines that have developed.

The field of computer ethics is relatively new in comparison to other scholarly disciplines. It came into existence in the late 1940s when Norbert Wiener created it. The progress made was very slow since Wiener's perceptions, and intuitions were far ahead of his time. Since 1985 information and computer ethics has advanced exponentially. America was the torchbearer in this respect followed by Europe and then the rest of the world.

## 1.9. CONCLUSION

IE has made significant advancement over the years. Other disciplines have also embraced this field in the last few decades.

The United States was the pioneer in establishing this field. The works of various scholars and researchers have addressed several issues in their work including privacy policies, right to information, the fair use of information, and ethical dilemmas among others. However, there were no courses that were specifically dedicated to the ethical issues in this field. When such courses were first offered in the U.S., IE broadened its horizon to embrace IT, computer ethics, and moral dilemmas in the management of information systems and information in society.

Unfortunately, even today most schools of library and information science do not offer courses specifically pertaining to ethical issues, and these issues are presented in the context of other issues. It can be summarized that IE is a complex and evolving field that has been influenced immensely by other disciplines, customs, and perspectives.
