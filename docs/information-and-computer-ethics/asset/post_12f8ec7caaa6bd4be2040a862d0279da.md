In the latter half of the 1960s, a computer scientist at MIT named Joseph Weizenbaum created ELIZA. This was a basic computer program that he called ELIZA. His intention was to offer a rough imitation of “a Rogerian psychotherapist engaged in an initial interview with a patient.”

The reaction of people to his modest program startled and dismayed Weizenbaum. For example, some computer scientists at MIT, who even though were aware of how the ELIZA program worked, became emotionally involved with it. They even shared their personal views and opinions with it. Some psychiatrists deliberated that ELIZA is proof that computers soon would perform automated psychotherapy.

The reactions to his invention made Weizenbaum anxious that an “information-processing model” of human beings was reinforcing an already growing tendency among scientists, and even the public, to see humans as “mere machines.” Consequently, he wrote the book Computer Power and Human Reason in 1976 in which he expressed his anxiety. Many scholars were influenced by this book and some speeches he gave in the various parts of the country. They began to contemplate about the impact that computing would have on society and morals.

### 1.5.1. Walter Maner and His Views on Computer Ethics

Walter Maner who was associated with Old Dominion University in Virginia coined the term “computer ethics” to refer to that field of inquiry dealing with ethical problems aggravated, transformed or created that can be attributed to computers. Maner even offered a course on this subject at his university.

In the latter part of the 70s and the mid–80s the courses offered by Maner in the university gained a lot of popularity. He also offered a large number of courses and workshops aimed at educating people about computer ethics in the philosophy and computer science conferences all over America. In the year 1978, he launched his Starter Kit in Computer Ethics independently. Through this kit, he intended to assist the university teachers to plan courses on computer ethics.

The Starter Kit also contained suggestions for university prospectus, a rational explanation for including this course in the curriculum of the university, a list of the purposes of the course, some handy instruction on the teaching approach to be adopted, some topics that could be included like cyber-crime, privacy, and confidentiality, professional code of ethics.

The course offered by Maner in combination with the Starter Kit and the conferences he conducted influenced the students of America in the field of computer ethics. Several scholars began to concentrate on this new field, and there was also an increase in the number of university courses that were offered on this subject.

Maner also generated a Starter Kit on Teaching Computer Ethics, basing it on his teaching experiences and research in the planned new field. He circulated copies of the kit to the attendees of the workshop he ran and speeches that he gave at philosophy conferences and computing conferences in America. The starter kit was published as a monograph in 1980, by the Helvetia Press and the National Information and Resource Center on Teaching Philosophy, headed by the present author.

## 1.6. PROFESSIONAL AND COMPUTER ETHICS

Donald Gotterbarn, in the early 1990s, supported a different emphasis within computer ethics. He was of the view that computer ethics should be perceived as professional ethics. It should be dedicated to the progress and improvement of standards of good practice and codes of conduct for computing professionals. In 1991 Gotterbarn published an article “Computer Ethics: Responsibility Regained,” in which he quoted:

> “There is little attention paid to the domain of professional ethics – the values that guide the day-to-day activities of computing professionals in their role as professionals. By computing professional, I mean anyone involved in the design and development of computer artifacts. The ethical decisions made during the development of these artifacts have a direct relationship to many of the issues discussed under the broader concept of computer ethics.”

Gotterbarn wanted to contribute to the field of professional ethics, hence collaborated with other supporters of professional ethics like Simon Rogerson, Keith Miller, Dianne Martin and Chuck Huff in a large number of projects to enforce professional ethics in computer practitioners. Gotterbarn was already a part of ACM to generate the next version of its “Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct.” This was adopted by ACM in 1992.

Gotterbarn along with his co-workers in ACM and the computer society of Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), established licensing standards for software engineers. He also spearheaded a joint task force of these two institutes to develop the “Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice” which were adopted by them in 1999. Gotterbarn also created the Software Engineering Ethics Research Institute (SEERI) at East Tennessee State University in the late 1990s. In the early 2000s, he co-created a computer software with Simon Rogerson called Software Development Impact Statements (SoDIS). The purpose was to support individuals and organizations to devise ethical “stakeholder analyses.” The analysis would help in ascertaining the moral impact of software development projects.

The efforts of Gotterbarn and many other projects emphasized on professional responsibility and facilitated the professionalization and ethical maturation of computing practitioners.

In 1995, Krystyna Górniak-Kocikowska in her ETHICOMP95 presentation “The Computer Revolution and the Problem of Global Ethics,” made a staggering prophecy. She predicted that computer ethics will gradually penetrate into all cultures worldwide and will emerge as global ethics. According to this “Gorniak hypothesis,” regional ethical theories like Europe’s Benthamite and Kantian systems, as well as the diverse ethical systems embedded in other cultures of the world, all derive from “local” histories and customs and are unlikely to be applicable worldwide. Gorniak opined that computer and IE have the possibility to deliver a global ethic that can cater to the new Information Age.

The computer revolution is expected to give birth to a new ethical theory that will emerge from computer ethics. This evolving field is expected to be even more significant than even its creators and supporters believed.

The nature of the Computer Revolution is unique. This suggests that ethics that emerge in the future will be universally accepted. It will be multidimensional in nature and truly global. It can also be considered global since it considers human actions and relations in its totality.

Computer networks are not affected by borders, and hence it can be said that computers have a universal presence. Computer ethics to a large extent can mean evolving global ethics.

It is crucial that regulations with respect to computers are drafted effectively. However, if these rules are not well accepted widely by most of the computer users, its implementation will be unsuccessful. Computer ethics will gain global character when it evolves into a global ethic.

The “Gorniak hypothesis” intended to cause a strong response, and it made a noteworthy contribution to the “uniqueness debate.” Maner had asserted in ETHICOMP95 conference in his keynote address that IT “forces us to discover new moral values, formulate new moral principles, develop new policies, and find new ways to think about the issues presented to us.”

Gorniak’s hypothesis reiterated the same. However, Gorniak did not foresee that IE would pave the way for concepts that would be applicable globally. She projected that a global ethics theory would develop with time because the internet is universal and consequently the ethics conversation centering around it also has a global character.

Gorniak was accurate in her prophecy. Global network, especially the internet, has been fundamental in connecting human beings worldwide. Computer ethics appears to be progressing into an expansive and even more significant field today. It might be rightly termed as “global IE.” For the first time in history, steps are being taken on a global level, to formulate mutually agreed standards of conduct. Also, the need for securing human values and advancing them is being felt worldwide.
