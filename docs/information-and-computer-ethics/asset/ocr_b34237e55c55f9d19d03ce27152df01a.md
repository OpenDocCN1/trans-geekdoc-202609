They cannot be termed as severe as the impairment is negligible. Also, to measure the impact is a challenge since their effects are not immediate or direct. They do not involve any illegitimate methods. The ease with which it can be performed makes them low on responsibility.

Most web defacements violate computer crime statutes. Schmitt’s criteria can be used to assess web defacements. This would yield the result that it is high on four criteria- directness, presumptive legitimacy, measurability, and directness. It is easy to restore websites from back up; hence it is not that severe. But the damage could be high in cases like where a visitor makes use of incorrect medical information, or the security of online bank transaction is compromised, or the reputation of the enterprise owning the website is lost. Intrusiveness and responsibility can be ranked as moderate and low respectively. There are very few nations that claim to be responsible when they employ these methods. Web defacement cannot be performed by everyone; however, there is no dearth of hackers who can.

It can be deduced that, apparently, defacements are even less legitimate that web sit-ins and are certainly despised by many hacktivists.

Other types of hacktivism can also be scrutinized through Schmitt’s criteria. Generally, actions that breach computer crime statutes are ranked moderate to high. Example of such cyber-attacks are actions to deter a child pornography site, attacks that aim to pint out and rectify lapses in security systems. Even though the end objective may be ethical, the methods employed may be disputed.

Any act that can be classified as cyberterrorism would have a very serious outcome even in the best-case scenario. Cyber-attacks that do not breach crime laws are ranked low as per Schmitt’s criteria. For instance, developing new software and employing them in letter writing campaigns or innovating software for obtaining censored information by breaching its firewalls.

These activities cannot be considered illegal at least in the US and cannot be compared to the use of force. Cyber-attacks that are classified in upper ranges of Schmitt’s criteria may require more justification but cannot be termed immoral. One factor to be considered is whether a possibility exists to achieve the activist’s goals by actions which are within the ambit of law.
