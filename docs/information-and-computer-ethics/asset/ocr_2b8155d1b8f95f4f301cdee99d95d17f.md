Attackers may not always abide by the laws of war in order to gain privileged combatant status. They may not be encouraged to because it is implausible that they might fall into the enemy hands. This holds true since the victims are unaware of the status of the combatant who initiated the attack. The situation can be compared to attacks using weapons that can be used from a great distance such as unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) and intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBM) since in the case of the latter too it is difficult to trace the attacks. However, these weapons are not that discrete and can reveal the identity of the nation instigating the attack. But in case of cyber weapons anonymity is almost certain.

Even if the non-state actor to whom the attack has been outsourced can be traced back to the instigator state, the connection does not make the attacker a combatant as per legal status. We can take the cyber-conflict between Russia and Georgia as an example. In these attacks that were carried out in August 2008, evidence pointed towards the website “StopGeorgia.ru” which carried instructions against Georgia to Kremlin.

Russian intelligence services (GRU) and “Nashi” a youth association were the mediums used for giving the commands. This led to the suspicion that the Russian government outsourced the task to non-state “hacktivist” to accomplish its goals. Even if we take into account that individual hacktivist may be supported by a nation-state, they cannot be granted the legal status of combatant. They would still be considered as cybercriminals.

Taking all things in consideration, this conflict between Russia and Georgia displayed a practical model for conducting low impact cyberattacks. The government could achieve at least a part of its tactical goals without the conceding proof of the attack being state-sponsored. This was possible because the instigating-state employed the services of patriot hackers or cyber-militias, through informal routes appealing to nationalist fervor.

The actual character of cyberwarfare and the status of actors participating in these activities is ambiguous and has been heavily guarded by employing abstruse terms in media and literature. Politicians also sensationalize these terms and leave a lingering idea that these concepts are unique in their nature. Cyberwarfare lacks solid empirical datasets and requires advancement in technologies that can be applied to the art of war.

It is important to acknowledge the presence of various non-state actors in cyberspace and their role in state-sponsored cyber-attacks. There may be no clear-cut distinction between these actors. It will be difficult to differentiate between script kiddies, cybercriminals, and cyberspionage
