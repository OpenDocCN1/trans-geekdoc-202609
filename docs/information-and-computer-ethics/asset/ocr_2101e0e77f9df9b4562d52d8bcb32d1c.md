Gotterbarn also created the Software Engineering Ethics Research Institute (SEERI) at East Tennessee State University in the late 1990s. In the early 2000s, he co-created a computer software with Simon Rogerson called Software Development Impact Statements (SoDIS). The purpose was to support individuals and organizations to devise ethical “stakeholder analyses.” The analysis would help in ascertaining the moral impact of software development projects.

The efforts of Gotterbarn and many other projects emphasized on professional responsibility and facilitated the professionalization and ethical maturation of computing practitioners.

In 1995, Krystyna Górniak-Kocikowska in her ETHICOMP95 presentation “The Computer Revolution and the Problem of Global Ethics,” made a staggering prophecy. She predicted that computer ethics will gradually penetrate into all cultures worldwide and will emerge as global ethics. According to this “Gorniak hypothesis,” regional ethical theories like Europe’s Benthamite and Kantian systems, as well as the diverse ethical systems embedded in other cultures of the world, all derive from “local” histories and customs and are unlikely to be applicable worldwide. Gorniak opined that computer and IE have the possibility to deliver a global ethic that can cater to the new Information Age.

The computer revolution is expected to give birth to a new ethical theory that will emerge from computer ethics. This evolving field is expected to be even more significant than even its creators and supporters believed.

The nature of the Computer Revolution is unique. This suggests that ethics that emerge in the future will be universally accepted. It will be multidimensional in nature and truly global. It can also be considered global since it considers human actions and relations in its totality.

Computer networks are not affected by borders, and hence it can be said that computers have a universal presence. Computer ethics to a large extent can mean evolving global ethics.

It is crucial that regulations with respect to computers are drafted effectively. However, if these rules are not well accepted widely by most of the computer users, its implementation will be unsuccessful. Computer ethics will gain global character when it evolves into a global ethic.
