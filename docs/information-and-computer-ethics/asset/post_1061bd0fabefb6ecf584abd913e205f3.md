## 6.4. UN SECURITY COUNCIL ACTIONS AND CYBER-CONFLICTS

There are certain set of exceptions which every country has to adhere to during the cyberconflicts. The actions that are authorized by the United Nations Security Council is the foremost exception to the common exclusion on the use of forces.

In order to restore and maintain global peace and security, the Security Council can make use of the article 42 of the United Nations Charter which are primarily to be used through the military forces of a country. However, unless the Security Council fulfills the conditions of articles 39, 41, and 42, the Security Council is not entrusted the power to use the military forces under the United Nations Charter.

Article 39 must be examined first before the Security Council can sanction the use of force. Article 39 requires the existence of “threat to the peace, breach of the peace, or act of aggression” which is to be determined by the Security Council. It is only when the above criteria are fulfilled, the Security Council has got the power to initiate in restoring the global peace and security which should be in line with the Articles 41 and 42 of the United Nations Charter.

Article 41 that provides guidance on the use of nonmilitary measures is Charter’s favored method to restore international peace and security. Under this article, the Security Council has the power to administer a country for the usage of nonmilitary measures to coerce an offending country into ceasing its aggression. The members of the United Nations are responsible in implementing the nonmilitary measures, which includes the “complete or partial interruption of economic relations, and other means of communication, and the severance of diplomatic relations.”

When the measures under Article 41 have not been effective or is not expected to be successful, military measures under Article 42 may be sanctioned by the Security Council. Although Article 41 authorizes the Security Council to impose the use of non-military forces, but a state cannot be forced to take military action invoking Article 42, it can only authorize the state to do so.

As discussed, the use of force is prohibited under normal circumstances. However, another exception to this restriction is when force is required to be used for self-defense. The Article 51 of the UN Charter confers this right. This article asserts that “nothing in the present Charter shall impair the inherent right of [states to engage in] individual or collective self-defense” in response to an “armed attack.” Self-defense was considered a righteous act even before the UN Charter, and this fact has been implied in Article 51 as well.

The UN Charter also endorsed this right under the customary international law. The fundamental principle of self-defense is the right to survive is cardinal to all states and states may employ force to safeguard themselves and their citizens.

This right is independent of the UN Charter. Hence self-defense analysis borrows from the principles customary international law and the provisions of Article 51.

The essence of the principle of self-defense is that it may be resorted to when faced with a threat of armed attack. Although this principle is accepted and acknowledged by the international law, the question of what constitutes an armed attack is yet to be settled. This lack of clarity has led to the deliberation over when a state has the right to retaliate under self-defense.

It is vital that self-defense is invoked at the right time, hence the dispute of what can be termed as armed attack, needs to be settled. In case of cyber-attacks, there can be more than one interpretation, unlike traditional warfare weapons. Hence the debate on what can be classified as armed attack has become more prominent. Self-defense analysis is further complicated because of competing theories among legal scholars on the interplay between the UN Charter and customary international law. Some scholars favor UN Charter. Their argument is that Article 51 places a restriction on self-defense since it allows the same only to respond against an actual armed act. While others emphasize on customary international law.

Their argument is that the states have every right to be proactive and retaliate against impending armed attacks as well. Imminent armed attacks have been deliberated later. The important point here is although different philosophies have come up with various descriptions of an armed attack, there is a common agreement over the fact that once a state is attacked with armed forces by another state, the victim states and its allies have the legal authority to use force against the perpetrator.

Methods employed for self-defense must obey the international law. An armed attack does not automatically empower the victim state to start unrestricted war against the perpetrator. Self-defense should be resorted to only when it is necessary and when it cannot be avoided because alternative peaceful methods have been deemed ineffective in the prevailing circumstances. Self-defense should be conducted in proportion to the armed attack. Self-defense actions should be restricted to the amount of force deemed necessary to thwart any advances or to overthrow an ongoing attack.

This principle in no way states that the magnitude and extent of defense should be comparable to those of the attack. In self-defense, a state may employ force of greater magnitude if it is required to deter the attacker. The chief consideration here is to determine the amount of force, to either tackle the present hostile situation or to prevent future attacks from happening. The above-mentioned principles set out the boundaries within which the responses must conform.
