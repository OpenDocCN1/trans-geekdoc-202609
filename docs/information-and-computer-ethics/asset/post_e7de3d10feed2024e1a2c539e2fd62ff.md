### 4.11.2. Accuracy

Deception has a method for messing up individuals’ lives, particularly when those with incorrect data have leverage in power and authority. Consider the situation of Louis Marches. He, an outsider, was a hardworking man who, with his wife Eileen, finally saved enough money to buy a home in Los Angeles in the 1950s.

They took out a long-term loan from Crocker National Bank. Every month Louis Marches would go to his local bank, with his loan coupon book in hand, to make his payment of $196.53. He carefully checked to make sure the teller had marked “paid” in his book on the correct line, that is, only on the line for the month in which the payment was due. Moreover, he continued doing this long after the bank had switched to its computerized credit processing system.

One September a few years earlier, the bank informed Marches that he had failed to make his current house payment. Marches grabbed his coupon book, went to the bank, and, in broken English that hinted at his national origin, tried to explain to the teller that this dunning notice was incorrect. He insisted he had made his payment.

The stamp on his coupon book showed that he had paid. The teller entered Marches’ account number on the keyboard and reviewed the resulting screen. Unfortunately, she could not confirm Marches’ claim, nor could the head teller, nor the branch manager. When confronted with a computer-generated screen that clearly showed his account was delinquent, this hierarchy of bank officials simply ignored the entries recorded in his coupon book and his continued protests. Confused, Marches left the bank in disgust.

In October, however, Marches obediently went to the bank to make his next payment. He was informed that he could not make his October payment because he was several months financially past due. He again showed the teller his stamped coupon book. She refused to accept it, and he stormed out of the bank. In November he returned as scheduled, as he had done for more than 20 years, and tried to make his payment again, only to be told that he was now two months financially past due. And so it went until inevitably the bank dispossessed him. Eileen learned of the foreclosure from an enthusiastic bank debt collector while she was sleeping, recovering from a heart attack.

She fell after hearing the news and suffered a near-fatal stroke that incapacitated her right side. At some point during this ordeal, Marches, who until then had handled his own legal work, was introduced to a lawyer who agreed to represent him. They sued the bank. Eventually, after quite a while of anguish, the Marches received a settlement of $268,000. All the bank officials who testified could say was, “Computers make mistakes. Banks make mistakes, too.”

Today the general public is generating such a large amount of data about so many individuals and their activities that exposure to errors is enormous. Moreover, this growth in data raises another issue: Who is responsible for all of this?

### 4.11.3. Property

One of the most complex issues the general public faces as a society is the topic of intellectual property rights. There are significant financial and ethical concerns surrounding these rights; concerns revolving around the unique nature of data itself and the means by which it is transmitted. Any individual item of data can be extremely expensive to create in the first instance.

However, once it is produced, that data has the illusory nature of being easy to copy and share with others. Moreover, this replication can occur without destroying the original. This makes data difficult to protect because, unlike tangible property, it becomes transferable and difficult to keep to oneself. It is even hard to secure proper compensation when someone else uses individuals’ data.

We currently have several flawed institutions that attempt to protect intellectual property rights. Copyrights, patents, encryption, privacy promises, and such traditional values as trust and reliability are the most commonly used defenders of intellectual property. Problems, however, nevertheless abound in this area. Let us focus on just one aspect: artificial intelligence and its growing subfield, expert systems.

To fully appreciate the ethical dilemma regarding expert systems, it is important to turn back the clock somewhat, around two hundred years, to the beginnings of another society: the steam industrial society. From this vantage point, the public may foresee some of the issues of the information society.

Nowhere is the potential threat to human dignity more serious than in the age of information technology, particularly in the field of artificial intelligence. Practitioners of artificial intelligence proceed by extracting knowledge from specialists, workers, and professionals, and then embedding it into computer software where it becomes capital in the economic sense.

This process of “disembodying” knowledge from an individual, and accordingly “embodying” it into machines, transfers control of the property to those who own the hardware and software. Is this transfer of property justified? Consider some of the best commercial artificial intelligence systems of the day.

Who owns, for instance, the chemical information contained in DENDRAL, the medical knowledge contained in MYCIN, or the land information contained in PROSPECTOR? How is the provider of his knowledge to be compensated? These are among the issues the public must address as increasingly intelligent information systems are created.

Concern over intellectual property rights relates to the content of data. There are some equally pressing property rights issues surrounding the channels through which data passes. Bandwidth, the capacity to convey data, is a scarce and ultimately fixed commodity. It is a “commons.” A commons resembles an empty vessel into which drops of water can be freely and easily added until it fills and overflows. Then its capacity is gone. As a resource, it is limited.

How will the limited resource of bandwidth be allocated? Who will have access? This leads us to the fourth issue.

### 4.11.4. Access

The main path to data is through literacy. Literacy, since around 1500 A.D. when the Syrians first invented a consonant alphabet, has been a requirement for full participation in society. Every advancement in data handling, from the invention of paper to modern digital platforms, has placed new demands on achieving literacy. In an information society, a person must have at least three things to be literate: One must have the intellectual skills to manage data. These include skills such as reading, writing, thinking, and calculating. This is a task for education.

One must have access to the data technologies that store, transmit, and process data. This includes libraries, radios, TVs, phones, and increasingly, PCs or terminals connected via networks to mainframe computers. This is a socioeconomic issue.

Finally, one must have access to the data itself. This requirement returns to the issue of property and is also a socioeconomic issue.

These requirements for education are a function of both the knowledge level and the financial dimension of the individual. Unfortunately, for some people in the world today, both of these dimensions are currently eroding.

Consider for a moment the social impacts of electronically stored databases. Before their emergence, vast amounts of information about publications, news events, financial and social statistics, and scientific discoveries have been available in printed, microfilm, or microfiche format at relatively low cost. For most of us, access to this information has been largely free.

We just went to the public or school library. The library, in turn, paid a few hundred dollars for the service and made it available to whoever requested it. Today, however, much of this data is being converted to automated databases, and the cost to access these databases can run into thousands of dollars.

Usually, access to databases is gained only by obtaining a terminal or PC. For example, if one wants access to the New York Times Index through the Mead Corporation service, one must first have access to a terminal and communication line, and then pay additional connection and access charges to obtain the data.

This implies that those who wish to use this service must meet several conditions. First, they must know that the database exists and how to use it. Second, they must have acquired the necessary technology to access it. Third, they must be able to pay the fees for the data. Therefore, the educational and financial barrier is extremely high to play the modern information game.

Many individuals cannot or choose not to pay it and are therefore excluded from participating fully in social issues. As a result, they become data “dropouts” and over time will become the source of numerous social problems.
