Wiener was of the opinion that human life should be based on the values and impartiality. This led to the “great principles of justice” which according to Wiener should be the basis for forming a society. Wiener strongly believed that these principles would enable humans to thrive through various flexible actions. Wiener’s great principles can be referred to as “The Principle of Freedom,” “The Principle of Equality,” and “The Principle of Benevolence.” Wiener did not coin the names for his principles; he only stated them. The following list of principles can be derived from his work (Figure 1.1).

- **The Principle of Freedom:** In order to establish justice, “the liberty of each human being to develop in his freedom the full measure of the human possibilities embodied in him.”
- **The Principle of Equality:** Justice necessitates “the equality by which what is just for A and B remains just when the positions of A and B are interchanged.”
- **The Principle of Benevolence:** Justice entails “a goodwill between man and man that knows no limits short of those of humanity itself.”

Wiener’s works are based on human nature and an ideal society. From his works, we can derive that different cultures that are characterized by various practices, languages, traditions, and religions can provide the required platform for supporting a decent human life. Wiener supported the view that “variety and possibility belong to the very structure of the human organism,” hence he could welcome the existence of a broad diversity of cultures in the world to maximize the possibility of choice and creative action.

According to Wiener, a society should support an enabling environment where a man can maximize their potential as sophisticated beings that can process information, make choices and be responsible for their own lives. To establish such a social order, freedom, equality, and human compassion should be there in the society. Ethical relativists opine that there can be no universal foundation for ethics since diverse laws, ethics, and norms exist in the worldwide.

Wiener’s response was that as per his work on human life, humans can adopt various cultures and traditions while still observing “the great principles of justice.” These principles lay the foundation for ethics across diverse cultures.

### 1.4.2. Methodology for Information Ethics

An evaluation of Wiener’s approach to resolving IE issues it can be observed that he attempts to assimilate new cases by applying already existing, ethically acceptable laws, rules, and practices. This is particularly true in the case of his book “The Human Use of Human Beings.” All societies have principles, customs, rules, and regulations that collectively mold human conduct in that society.

According to Moor, these policies constitute a “received policy cluster.” In a reasonably unbiased society, they can form the basis for resolving issues related to IE. Wiener combined the “received policy cluster” with his account of human nature, his “great principles of justice,” and his critical skills in clarifying vague or ambiguous language. This way he was able to establish an efficient technique for evaluating the following IE issues:

- Address an ethical question or case pertaining to the infusion of IT into the society. Generally speaking, this would focus on the opportunities arising from technologies that have an impact on life, contentment, happiness, health, liberty, knowledge, prospects, and human principles.
- Provide explanations for any ambiguous or unclear notions and philosophies that may be pertinent to a case or any issue.
- Check the possibility for applying laws, rules, customs, and principles that already exist in the society and uphold moral values and guide humans to tread on the ethical path.
- If customs, practices, and policies are not providing the required guidance to deal with an issue or resolve a situation, the great principles of justice should be used to find a fitting solution that will conform to the moral traditions of the civilization.

If the traditions, precedents, and policies that one starts with are embedded within a reasonably just society, then this method of analyzing and resolving IE issues will likely provide just solutions that can be assimilated into that society.

In the late 1940s and early 1950s, Wiener expressed unambiguously that introducing the newly invented computing and IT would usher in a new era in the society. This new “automatic age” will be the second industrial revolution. Continuous, relentless, and multidimensional efforts would be required, and this would bring about a change in every aspect of life.

According to Wiener, IT has dramatically altered human life and positioned them “in the presence of another social potentiality of unheard-of importance for good and for evil.” (Wiener, 1954) Wiener predicted decades ago that an “information age” will emerge. He also predicted that the metaphysical and scientific basis that he envisaged can act as a guiding force for resolving and interpreting the ethical issues that information technologies may stimulate.

### 1.4.3. A Cybernetic View of Human Nature

Wiener emphasized on the physical anatomy of humans and also the immense potential that they hold for developing themselves and innovate new processes. Wiener often compared humans to lesser evolved organisms to explain his view that humans are capable of remarkable achievements.

Cybernetics takes the view that the configuration of the machine or of the organism is an indicator of the competence in the execution of tasks that can be expected from it. The fact that the mechanical rigidity of the insect is such as to limit its intelligence while the mechanical fluidity of the human being provides for his almost indefinite intellectual expansion is highly relevant to the point of view of this book. Humans have a physiological advantage and intellectual capability over the rest of nature and can adapt themselves to the fundamental alterations in his living conditions. The human race can continue to be dominant only if they make use of their inherent, adaptive, learning faculties that they derive from their physiological structure (Wiener, 1954, pp. 57–58, italics in the original)

The physiology of humans empowers them to absorb a wide range of information from their environment, take in information about conditions and events within themselves. Human anatomy not just enables access to all the information but allows to reason, analyze, reflect, determine, and engage intellectually with the information obtained. Wiener inferred from all his research that the objective of human life was to thrive as an information-processing organism since they have the inherent ability to do so.

It is evident that humans possess the ability to absorb massive amounts of information, gather knowledge on an ongoing basis. Almost half of human life could be dedicated to learning and studies, since they are physically capable to do so unlike insects like ants. The two intrinsic characteristics of human sensorium are variety and possibility. This is because these two characteristics are a part of the structure of the human organism (Wiener, 1954, pp. 51–52).

## 1.5. DEVELOPMENT OF COMPUTER ETHICS

In the 50s and 60s, Norbert Wiener contributed profoundly to the field of IE, and this was wider than the more specific field of computer ethics. Wiener’s principles and notions about IE is not restricted to computer ethics alone but can also be extended to other fields like Agent ethics, Internet ethics, and the ethics of nanotechnology, the ethics of bioengineering, even journalism ethics, and library ethics.

In the 1960s and early 1970s, even before Walter Maner came up with the term “computer ethics” and offered a definition in the field, progress was made in the exact field of computer ethics. For example, Donn Parker, who was a computer scientist at SRI International, had started to take notice of the illegal doings of computer professionals and began to study them. This was during the mid-1960s. The article “Rules of Ethics in Information Processing,” was published by Parker in Communications of the ACM. This contained information about cases of computer crimes.
