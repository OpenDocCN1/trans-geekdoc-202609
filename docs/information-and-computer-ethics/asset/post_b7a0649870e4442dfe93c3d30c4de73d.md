
## 6.3. CYBERWARFARE

Instances of major cyber-attacks around the world have often prompted the various governments to shift the blame on other governments. Often there have been speculations of cyberwarfare breaking out. As defined by the Oxford English Dictionary “The use of computer technology to disrupt the activities of a state or organization, especially the deliberate attacking of information systems for strategic or military purposes.”

The significance of the above definition is that the aim of cyberwarfare is not in attacking the neighboring countries with the use of computer-technologies, but it means that intra-national banks or any other actions which uses the computer system as a mode to launch a threat.

The biggest challenge that cyberwarfare poses is the difficulty to find out who could be the perpetrator of the attack. Some latest technologies and intelligence can expose the state or group that may have been responsible, but with intelligent use of technology, it is easy to cover up the identity, cover up the tracks from where hacks have sourced as well as mask the malware infections and other methods employed.

Many freelance groups and hackers are recruited by the intelligence agencies and military operations to help them in attacking the target on their behalf. It becomes more challenging for the victims to identify the perpetrator to give them appropriate punishments for their criminal offenses (Figure 6.2).

### 6.3.1. Tools to Avoid Cyberwarfare

The weapons used to tackle cyberwarfare are identical to the ones primarily used in criminal attacks everywhere. So, there are DDoS botnets to serve up denial of service attacks which can interrupt if not actually take strategic servers out of play. DDoS is usually used as a resource in various data crimes in order to direct smokescreen for other activities on the network. Social engineering and spear phishing attacks are also weaponized to introduce an attacker into the system of an adversary. The insider threat is a very important weapon in the cyberwarfare armory, and so does having a mole to leak information.

Cybersecurity issues and challenges can be highlighted better with illustration to explain the subject in hand. However, it is not easy in case of state-sponsored attacks as the attack cannot be pinpointed to a specific person as explained above. There are several other illustrations that could be classified as cyberwarfare details and illustrates the kinds of attacks which shall be studied below.

The most obvious cyberwarfare is the Stuxnet, which was initially discovered in 2010. The Stuxnet cyberwarfare primarily involved Israel and the United States using a sophisticated worm to destroy nuclear centrifuges in Iran, so as to delay the progress of its nuclear weapons development program.

What happened in Russia can be taken as an example. The country has been suspected of carrying out numerous state-level cyber-attacks. It is facing the accusation of increasing cyber-attacks against Ukraine. Allegedly, it has used BlackEnergy attack that caused blackout to 70,000 houses in the country in 2015 and Not Petya malware, which was actually designed purely to damage the systems it infected but was impersonating a ransomware.

The most sought after topic which has been on headlines is the North Korea's nuclear posturing and turbulent diplomatic relationship with the US that also includes the active participation in cyberspace. According to researches, “the North Korean state has been linked to the prolific and dangerous hacking organization codenamed HIDDEN COBRA, also known as the Lazarus Group. Both the Sony hack of 2014 and the hack of a Bangladeshi bank in 2016 were pinned on these hackers.”

### 6.3.2. Jus ad Bellum and Cyberconflict

What is a subject matter of contention is when a state can take steps to counter cyber-attacks in self-defense? The laws of war are well defined and comprise of widely accepted principles. However, the application of those principles is a challenge. Most of the law of war was developed to counter traditional war methods; hence their principles cannot apply to cyber-attacks in all scenarios.

In the paradigm of examining the armed attacks, the scope of an attack and the identification of an attacker has been easier when compared to other cyber-attacks. Generally, it becomes difficult for a state to assess the nature of cyber-attack when they are in process and becomes more challenging to identify the person responsible for the attack which has made states unwilling to react to those attacks in self-defense for fear of violating the law of war. This negligence of non-reactive nature to the attacks have turned cyberwarfare into one of the key topics in international law.

The crux of this chapter is to discover the unique challenges which the cyber-attacks pose to the law of war and provide the readers an analytical framework for dealing with them. Once the present state of the law of war are fully explored, this chapter will demonstrate that states have a right under international law to:

- Treat cyber-attacks as acts of war and not merely as criminal matters.

- Use defensive mechanism against computer networks, both active and passive, against states found guilty of initiating cyber-attacks. Also, states have a responsibility to counter attacks within their borders and will be held guilty even if there was passive involvement.

The key purpose of this chapter is to provide a deep understanding of the law of war. This chapter primarily deals in addressing the technical aspects of cyberwarfare also showcases the presence of legal body for every state which acts as a self-defense to these cyber-attacks.

The following are the seven principles of law of war which the U.S. Department of Defense summarizes; (DoD OGC)

- **Distinction of Combatants from Noncombatants:** The exclusive right to use force lies with the nation’s armed force, and they must not mask their identity and hide behind civilians or in civilian properties;

- **Military Necessity:** The targets of the cyber-attacks should make a direct contribution to the war effort or produce a military advantage;

- **Proportionality:** During an attack on a lawfully military target, if collateral damage to civilians and civilian properties is inevitable, the probable military advantage should be proportionate to the damage;

- **Indiscriminate Weapons:** Avoidance of using weapons which cannot be directed with any precision. An example for an indiscriminate weapon is the bacteriological weapon;

- **Superfluous Injury:** Abstain from using weapons that are likely to cause irrevocable losses and fatal injuries;

- **Perfidy:** Neither the symbols that are protected are not meant to be utilized to immunize the military targets from attacks, nor they are to be fake surrendered or issued false reports of cease-fires; and

- **Neutrality:** Countries are entitled to immunity from attack if they do not assist either side; else, they become legitimate targets.

Out of the above seven principles, the first three principles deal with the wars which are to only be performed by the military forces, and the primary focus of that attack should of military targets rather than civilian ones irrespective of the nature of attacks whether cyber or kinetic.

The consent to cyber-attack crucial infrastructures such as financial systems, civilian energy distribution, transportation, and telecommunication are permitted only if the attack do not cause disproportionate and redundant collateral damage to civilian property and noncombatants.

### 6.3.3. Proposals and Defenses

Active defenses are crucial to make the state cyberdefense measures effective. Also, it acts as a warning and a deterrent to cyber-attacks since the assailant would not want to deal with counterattack. For the purpose of using active defenses, the country should ensure a legal authority prior to performing a defense attack, which flows from the longstanding duty which mentions that the state is in a plight to prevent nonstate actors from using their territory to commit cross-border attacks.

Conventionally, the duty of the state is to deter illegal activities that the state has prior knowledge of. However, this duty has evolved in the background of international terrorism, and now states are also bound to act against groups with past records of illegal acts.
