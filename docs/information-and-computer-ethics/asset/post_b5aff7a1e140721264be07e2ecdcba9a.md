
## Table 6.2: Benefits and Drawbacks of Using Non-State Actors in Cyberspace Operations

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Gaining the initiative – element of surprise | No direct control of non-state actors |
| Plausible deniability | Risk of unintended collateral damage |
| Can choose target and attack vector | Targeting of own resources |
| Determinate scale and duration of attack | Escalation to conventional war |
| Exploit legal uncertainties | Labeling as sponsor of terrorism |
| Possibility of rapid attacking-by-proxy | Backlashes (blackmailing etc.) |

Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310827486_Non-State_Actors_in_Cyberspace_Operations/download

The above table gives a keynote of some of the significant “benefits and drawbacks” of performing a cyberwarfare, and how the employment of non-state actors is associated in the cyberspace operations.

## Benefits of Using Non-State Actors:

- The primary advantage for an attacker is that the attacks can be performed in a much-secured way which can result in huge surprises or high possibility of uncertainty. As the attacker is in a position of initiating the attack, the options left with the defender are only to respond to those attacks, which are mostly predicted by the attacker.

- The attacker has the advantage of precision of time, and the ability to choose the specific target as per their own picking, using attack methods that are most suitable. For an attacker to perform an attack, not many processes or systems are needed other than a computer system. On the other hand, a victim would require several protections or defense systems in order to overcome a cyber-attack. The defense materials that are required to shield the victim’s computer systems are exorbitantly expensive.

- The attacker can decide how to attack and the scale and duration of attack to have the desired impact. The responsibility of the attack can also be entrusted to a third party. This way they can amplify the impact of the act and also not risk direct attribution.

- Even if the accused is identified by the victim and the attack can be attributed to the perpetrator, the absence of relevant international laws applicable is a loophole that can be successfully used to create a shield of legal ambiguity.

- It is not only that an attacker should perform an attack. There are chances that the attack could be outsourced to the organized cybercriminals, mercenary hackers or the cyber militias. This recruitment of third parties or the non-state actors tend to increase the doubt in the global community; however, the absence of proofs will shield the attacker from political ramifications. Thus, making it challenging for the victims to perform a counterstrike on the attackers.

- It becomes less expensive for an employer, if a non-state actor is recruited who have had previously identified Internet forums and social networks, technically competent force, suitably motivated, and rapid mobilization of a considerable to perform these attacks.

## **Drawbacks of Using Non-State Actors:**

- The attacker may not be in total control of non-state actors in cyberspace operations, even though he commands what targets and methods should be employed during the cyber-attack, as there is little control over undesirable behavior, and it is difficult to end ongoing attacks.

- Attacks may extend beyond the desired scope and size. The attacker may accidentally hit targets that were not planned and cause undesired collateral damage. Excessively keen associates of cyber cities, not limited by the limitations that govern military organizations, could opt to target civilian targets without any thought of possible consequences.

- Common infrastructures like Internet backbone connections, may get overloaded which would adversely impact the attacker’s network and resources if attack non-state actors are engaged.

- In the case where an attack is diverted against the systems of the civilians, the attacker and their nation could be accused of sponsoring for cyberterrorism or could be accused of committing war crimes, becoming pariah as far as international relations are concerned.

- If attacks are attributed to the initiating nation-state, the political impact would be disastrous, even though the laws of war are ambiguous regarding cyberspace. If matters are escalated, the attacker state may have to face warfare through traditional means.

- Although the immediate attacks that are performed by the non-state actor tends to be successful, in the long run, these attackers are not tend to be reliable. These attackers, over a period of time, might blackmail a public administration, forcing the government officials to expose the complex details relating to the nation and contracted cyber espionage agents might defect to the opposing nation if offered political asylum.

### 6.6.3. Legal Issues Related to Non-State Actors in Cyberworld

The cyberwarfare has to deal with many legal challenges especially those pertaining to frameworks within which the present state-to-state operate. The main emphasis of this chapter is not on in-depth analysis of quirks and twists of international law. However, the current ambiguity in the prevailing legislation and international conventions do deserve mention, and to discern the impact of this uncertainty on employment of non-state actors in state-sponsored cyberconflict.

If cyber-attacks is resorted to, it will be against the spirit of the Law of Armed Conflict, even if it cannot be considered as a breach of the precept of war. Even other damaging cyber actions of less gravity may also be classified as acts of war by the UN Charter. Consequently, the UN Charter may authorize legitimate use of force in self-defense.

An important point to remember is that for an act to be categorized as cyber-attack, it should include violence or the possibility of the act becoming violent. This ambiguity prevents the classification of cyberspace as a combat zone. Consequently, it would serve the interest of many nations if the laws of warfare are not applied to cyberwarfare.

Another point to note is that cyber-attacks cannot be carried out while conforming to laws and regulations. It is challenging to carry out the attacks within the ambit of the legal framework.

However, if the revised protocol on cyberwarfare is endorsed worldwide, nations may consider carrying out cyber-attacks through cyber-militias. This can be a feasible option since cyber-militias are known to undertake outsourced jobs of this nature.

Unfortunately, international laws have not been amended yet, and they remain ambiguous about the status of cyberwarfare. The attacker seems to have the upper hand, and the victims remain at the receiving end with very little means of retaliating.

Another pertinent question arises regarding the status of the individual who carries out a cyber attack and whether he can earn the distinguished status of a combatant. The Third Geneva Convention confers two types of status on a combatant namely privileged and unprivileged.

Privileged combatants are considered a part of the armed forces who are a party to the conflict. They enjoy the following privileges,

- (I) they follow the orders of a superior who is responsible for people under him;
- (ii) Carry distinguishing identification that can be recognized even from a distance;
- (iii) They are permitted to carry arms with no attempt to conceal them;
- (iv) They have to carry out operations within the dictate of laws of war.

Most non-state actors like hackers, criminals, and terrorists evidently, do not strictly adhere to this purview. One argument is that when a state sponsors a cyber-attacker or cyberspionage agents, these agents obey the orders of a superior who is responsible for their actions. However, it is very clear that these groups do not wear a fixed distinct sign that can be identified nor, do they carry arms blatantly. Besides, many of their activities can be classified as a breach of the laws and customs of war.

The requirements set out in the Geneva Convention may not be met, even by the regular military cyber force. Also, being a part of cyberwarfare could be detrimental to them, since the privileged combatant status awarded to them could be revoked. Members of the armed forces may carry out cyber-attacks in uniforms, but it will not be visible to the victims. There is very less possibility of carrying arms blatantly as most cyber-attacks cannot be traced to their original source even if they are exposed.

Combatants should not breach laws of war or engage in actions that are in violation of the same. Actions like causing harm to civilians will rip off the special combatant status of the attacker. This inhibits the use of commercial infrastructure for the purpose of cyber-attacks, on paper at least. While privileged combatants deserve the same treatment as a prisoner of war, unprivileged combatants may be tried under the civilian laws of the nation that has detained them and may be punished under the same.
