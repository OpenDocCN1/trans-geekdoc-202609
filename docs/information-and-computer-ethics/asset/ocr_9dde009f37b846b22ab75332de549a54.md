Some scholars favor UN Charter. Their argument is that Article 51 places a restriction on self-defense since it allows the same only to respond against an actual armed act. While others emphasize on customary international law.

Their argument is that the states have every right to be proactive and retaliate against impending armed attacks as well. Imminent armed attacks have been deliberated later. The important point here is although different philosophies have come up with various descriptions of an armed attack, there is a common agreement over the fact that once a state is attacked with armed forces by another state, the victim states and its allies have the legal authority to use force against the perpetrator.

Methods employed for self-defense must obey the international law. An armed attack does not automatically empower the victim state to start unrestricted war against the perpetrator. Self-defense should be resorted to only when it is necessary and when it cannot be avoided because alternative peaceful methods have been deemed ineffective in the prevailing circumstances. Self-defense should be conducted in proportion to the armed attack. Self -defense actions should be restricted to the amount of force deemed necessary to thwart any advances or to overthrow an ongoing attack.

This principle in no way states that the magnitude and extent of defense should be comparable to those of the attack. In self-defense, a state may employ force of greater magnitude if it is required to deter the attacker. The chief consideration here is to determine the amount of force, to either tackle the present hostile situation or to prevent future attacks from happening. The above-mentioned principles set out the boundaries within which the responses must confirm.

The UN Charter lays down the condition that a state may only use force against another state when it is compelled to do so to defend itself or under the auspices of the Security Council (Article 39). The Charter has been successful in laying down a moral principle of a justified reason when a state can attack another state, and this has been accepted universally. States are only empowered to safeguard themselves from threats of violence or actual aggression.
