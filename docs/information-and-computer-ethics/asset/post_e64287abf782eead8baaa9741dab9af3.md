This holds true since the victims are unaware of the status of the combatant who initiated the attack. The situation can be compared to attacks using weapons that can be used from a great distance such as unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) and intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBM) since in the case of the latter too it is difficult to trace the attacks. However, these weapons are not that discreet and can reveal the identity of the nation instigating the attack. But in case of cyber weapons anonymity is almost certain.

Even if the non-state actor to whom the attack has been outsourced can be traced back to the instigator state, the connection does not make the attacker a combatant as per legal status. We can take the cyber-conflict between Russia and Georgia as an example. In these attacks that were carried out in August 2008, evidence pointed towards the website “StopGeorgia.ru” which carried instructions against Georgia to Kremlin.

Russian intelligence services (GRU) and “Nashi” a youth association were the mediums used for giving the commands. This led to the suspicion that the Russian government outsourced the task to non-state “hacktivists” to accomplish its goals. Even if we take into account that individual hacktivists may be supported by a nation-state, they cannot be granted the legal status of combatant. They would still be considered as cybercriminals.

Taking all things in consideration, this conflict between Russia and Georgia displayed a practical model for conducting low impact cyberattacks. The government could achieve at least a part of its tactical goals without the conceding proof of the attack being state-sponsored. This was possible because the instigating-state employed the services of patriot hackers or cyber-militias, through informal routes appealing to nationalist fervor.

The actual character of cyberwarfare and the status of actors participating in these activities is ambiguous and has been heavily guarded by employing abstruse terms in media and literature. Politicians also sensationalize these terms and leave a lingering idea that these concepts are unique in their nature. Cyberwarfare lacks solid empirical datasets and requires advancement in technologies that can be applied to the art of war.

It is important to acknowledge the presence of various non-state actors in cyberspace and their role in state-sponsored cyber-attacks. There may be no clear-cut distinction between these actors. It will be difficult to differentiate between script kiddies, cybercriminals, and cyberspionage agents or between cyber militias and patriot hackers as the distinction is indistinct. Likewise, multiple engagements can be undertaken by individual actors. Nevertheless, it is important to distinguish between the actors for the purpose of analysis.

There is a significant chance that this arena will have gained more prominence in the coming years, as internet becomes ubiquitous throughout the developing countries of Asia and Africa, due to these asymmetric advantages that may be leveraged in cyberspace. The crucial infrastructures of these countries will also evolve in the process. Cyber-attacks that are carried out with a political motive will be much more intense both in scale and number of incidences. Further, these attacks may not be linked to the perpetrator because the internet can guard identity.

The upsurge in the number of Internet users globally, problematic cyber activities engaged by state actors like cyber scammers and script kiddies will also grow. These users can create a lot of annoyance due to the following reasons:

- Firstly, the number of users in this group that are keen on misusing the cyberspace resources for personal motive is high.

## 6.7. RESPONDING TO INTERNATIONAL CYBERATTACKS AS ACTS OF WAR: PROPORTIONALITY

A state that is engaged in war has to distinguish between combatants and noncombatants as per the principle of distinction. This principle can be perceived as two interrelated duties. The first principle is that the civilian population should be clearly demarcated from their forces. Secondly, before the onset of the attack, the superiors should not keep clear demarcation between civilian or civilian objectives and military objectives.

The Article 57.1 of the Protocol Additional to the Geneva Conventions of 12 August 1949 also enforces certain restrictions on certain persons “those who plan or decide upon an attack.” In this chapter, they have been referred to as commanders.

- The commander’s first duty is to “do everything feasible to verify that the objectives to be attacked are neither civilians nor civilian objects.”
- The commander also has the duty to “take all feasible precautions in the choice of means and methods of attack with a view to avoiding, and in any event to minimizing, incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians and damage to civilian objects.”
- The commander should not instigate any attack “which may be expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, damage to civilian objects, or a combination thereof, which would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.”

It is important to note that, the concept of proportionality does not in any way place a constraint on commanders from causing any civilian death or injuries or destruction to civilian property. Proportionality states that loss to life and property should not exceed when compared to the “concrete and direct military advantage contemplated.”

The principle of proportionality prohibits an attack if it is anticipated that expected damage to civilian lives and destruction of civilian property would outweigh the military gain contemplated from such an attack. In order to analyze proportionality, the direct military advantage should be evaluated against the destruction of civilian life and property.

## 6.8. CAUSES OF HACKTIVISM

Jus ad bellum lays down the operations that states are prohibited from initiating against each other in the normal course of events, i.e., operations in which force is employed. Similarly, it is in the domain of domestic laws to place restrictions on operations that non-state actors can conduct.

The United States has laws governing cyber-attacks are contained in Title 18, Section 1030 of the U.S. Code (at the federal level) and in state computer crime laws.

These laws place a restriction on most of the cyber-attacks like web defacements, use of viruses, network intrusion, etc. Jus ad bellum permits states to engage in operations that employ force in order to retaliate, that may otherwise have been termed as illegitimate. Domestic legal doctrine includes a concept of self-defense that permits the use of force by victims that in other circumstances would be considered illegal.

Cyber-attacks can also be used as a self-defense mechanism, and this aspect has been discussed later. There are some ethical grounds on which cyber-attackers can engage for political or social causes. Hacktivism may be ethically acceptable in cases like civil disobedience where laws and demands of the government are defied but through peaceful means. Civil disobedience is a means to raise objection, show dissent and highlight those laws, policies, and practices that are considered immoral or unfair.

The methods employed for civil disobedience include demonstrations, barriers, trespass, etc. It involves defying law but is not necessarily accompanied by unethical actions. All civil disobedience movements cannot be automatically termed as moral but, in some cases, they may cross that boundary. For instance, the blocking of the entry point of an emergency room in a hospital to raise voice against the government’s health care policy.

We can take the example of the Electronic Disturbance Theater (EDT). EDT promoted the application of electronic civil disobedience, mainly through “web sit-ins,” which were viewed as virtual forms of physical sit-ins and blockades. Each sit-in would choose one or more websites as targets at a particular time and date and was updated in the public-forum in advance.

The activist has to go to a website to join the movement and select a target. Flood Net which is a Java applet would get downloaded on their computers so that the targeted website would get some traffic. A single participants’ traffic would have insignificant impact on the website that is targeted; however, the collective traffic of thousands of participants, if they participate, could create havoc in the target website.

EDT had primarily used their web sit-ins to protest against the adverse policies of Mexican and U.S. government that had an impact on the Chiapas. Initially EDT was used to express solidarity with the Mexican Zapatistas, but later they also fought for numerous other causes. This idea also found acceptance among other groups like the U.K.-based Electro Hippies.

With web sit-ins gaining popularity, the software tools employed became more sophisticated. Some of the software used included ones that could be loaded into the target’s system in advance and later be operated from participants’ system, software that would be activated by active participation of the person involved.
