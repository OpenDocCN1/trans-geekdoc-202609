## 2.4.4. The Technological Transparency Paradox

One may think why the services of social media tend to be of free of use, but nonetheless, usually make great benefits for the private organizations that provide these kinds of services. It is not a deep secret that the way these organizations make benefit is by selling of the information that the social networking sites users are uploading to the system.

More users imply to more information that has been provided by them, and the greater the worth that is inculcating that respective information becomes.

The social value of owning this is quite doubtful, but the economic value is undeniable. When this was written, Mark Zuckerberg has constantly been grouped in the top ten billionaires by the Forbes Magazine.

A success built on offering a free service to the world. What companies like Facebook do charge for our services, like focused advertising, which let third-party organizations to use information that users have offered to the social media applications?

The outcome is that advertisements bought on an application like Facebook are most probable to be seen as beneficial for workers who are likely to go for these products just by clicking. The personal and more detailed information shared, the more beneficial it will be to the organization that it is shared with.

The thorough explicitness of connecting greatly and sharing their personal information with the various companies has been encouraged. All the people who tend to make the utilization of those networking technologies, generally receives great worth, in terms of the evidence by the great growth of the pertaining technology.

Shannon Vallor, a philosopher, accounts the trend of transparency as a form of what she calls the “Technological Transparency Paradox”. She further notes that those in favor of developing technologies to encourage radically transparent societies, do so under the principle that this directness will raise accountability as well as democratic ideals.

But the irony is that the craze of such explicitness usually tends to acquire just the exact opposite along with the organizations that are greatly accountable, and also, which have not been selected democratically, possessing the information which can be utilized to enfeeble the democratic societies.

Indeed, the user is radically transparent and open to the organization. The algorithms that are used to gather the data and the third parties that this information is shared with is not subjected to the accountability and is opaque. The users of these technologies are required to be very transparent, but the organizations profiting off the information are not needed to be equally transparent.
