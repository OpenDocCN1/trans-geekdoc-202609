Article 41 that provides guidance on the use of nonmilitary measures is Charter’s favored method to restore international peace and security. Under this article, the Security Council has the power to administer a country for the usage of nonmilitary measures to coerce an offending country into ceasing its aggression. The members of the United Nations are responsible in implementing the nonmilitary measures, which includes the “complete or partial interruption of economic relations, and other means of communication, and the severance of diplomatic relations.”

When the measures under Article 41 have not been effective or is not expected to be successful, military measures under Article 42 may be sanctioned by the Security Council. Although Article 41 authorizes the Security Control to impose the use of non-military forces, but a state cannot be forced to take military action invoking Article 42, it can only authorize the state to do so.

As discussed, the use of force is prohibited under normal circumstances. However, another exception to this restriction is when force is required to be used for self-defense. The Article 51 of the UN Charter confers this right. This article asserts that “nothing in the present Charter shall impair the inherent right of [states to engage in] individual or collective self-defense” in response to an “armed attack.” Self-defense was considered a righteous act even before the UN Charter, and this fact has been implied in Article 51 as well.

The UN Charter also endorsed this right under the customary international law. The fundamental principle of self-defense is the right to survive is cardinal to all states and states may employ force to safeguard themselves and their citizens.

This right is independent of the UN Charter. Hence self-defense analysis borrows from the principles customary international law and the provisions of Article 51.

The essence of the principle of self-defense is that it may be resorted to when faced with a threat of armed attack. Although this principle is accepted and acknowledged by the international law, the question of what constitutes an armed attack is yet to be settled. This lack of clarity has led to the deliberation over when a state has the right to retaliate under self-defense.

It is vital that self-defense is invoked at the right time, hence the dispute of what can be termed as armed attack, needs to be settled.
