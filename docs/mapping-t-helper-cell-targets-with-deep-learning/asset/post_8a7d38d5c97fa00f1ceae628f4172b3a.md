## Mapping T helper cell targets with deep learning

Yuan Liu & Leng Han

By integrating multi-dimensional data with deep learning, a new method known as ImmunoScope predicts both histocompatibility class II (MHC-II) presentation and T helper cell immunogenicity. ImmunoScope shows potential to accelerate de novo discovery and vaccine design.

T cells scan the body for protein fragments known as peptides that appear on cell surfaces. A subset known as CD4 T cells recognizes these peptides and releases signals to other cells. To understand how T cells recognize peptides, it is important to consider the role of antigen-presenting cells. These cells break down proteins from tumour mutations, or during normal cell turnover into short peptides. These peptides must bind to MHC-II molecules. Once bound, the MHC-II complex on the cell surface presents the peptide to T helper cells. Traditionally, in single-variant experiments, cells are engineered to express a single MHC-II allele so that every received peptide can be unassignedly, but only a few alleles are covered. In contrast, multi-variant experiments, natural antigen-presenting cells with several alleles produce data that cannot assign each peptide to a specific allele. Assays measure how well affinity is conserved over all pairs. This approach has strengths and limitations, most computational methods focus on one approach such as binding affinity, multi-variant motifs, or T cell activation, and other perform [tasks] which peptides often relies on mass spectrometry analysis. In a study in this issue of Nature Machine Intelligence, et. al. present ImmunoScope, which circumvents this requirement by using deep learning to predict the peptides that are displayed to T helper cells, enabling analysis of which displayed peptides trigger and activate immune response. By merging diverse sources of data, ImmunoScope offers rapid mapping of antigen-presenting T helper cell targets, with clear implications for personalized immunotherapy.

(1) 246,000 and 2.4 million peptide-allele pairs from single-variant mass spectrometry data across 142 alleles; (2) about 432,000 and 4.4 million examples from multi-variant mass spectrometry data covering the same alleles; and (3) roughly 129,000 binding affinity measurements spanning 80 classes. ImmunoScope uses multiple-instance learning to treat each mutant as a candidate of peptide-allele pairs and de novo predictions through iterations. A poly-anchor triplet lost clusters confirmed binders and non-binders models in the internal representation. This approach enables ImmunoScope to generalize to unseen peptides and alleles, achieving an area under the recall curve (AUC-PR) of 0.856 and an area under the operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.825, outperforming tools in nearly 90% of cases.

ImmunoScope, which predicts which peptides will trigger T helper cell activation, uses learned embeddings trained on over 75,000 peptide-allele pairs with measured release of interferon-γ in T helper cells. New peptides predicted by them show no activation. Ambiguous records are resolved and roughly 17,900 examples are revised for evaluation. ImmunoScope reflects the prediction embeddings using cross-entropy loss, a loss to address class imbalance, and a triplet loss to separate epitopes from data. ImmunoScope achieves an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.909, outperforming tools that score over 0.885 and 0.875.

To demonstrate real-world utility, the authors applied ImmunoScope to the discovery of melanoma neantigens and SARS-CoV-2 immune escape. In 42 patients with melanoma, ImmunoScope predictions show strong correlation with mass spectrometry data from tumor samples. In SARS-CoV-2, ImmunoScope forecasts spike protein fragments from the original strain and the variant identified by common mutations, showing a 68% reduction in T helper cell activation for mutant peptides, matching observed reductions in CD4+ T cells.
