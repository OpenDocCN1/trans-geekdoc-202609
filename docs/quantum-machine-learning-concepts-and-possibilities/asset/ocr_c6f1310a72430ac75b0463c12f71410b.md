Mini-batching:

+   - Smoothes out per-sample gradient fluctuations,
- Is compatible with shot noise averaging, and
- Allows parallelization across quantum circuits or devices.

However, if $f(x_i)$ must be measured separately, mini-batching increases the circuit evaluation count. Thus, batch size and shot count must be jointly tuned to balance runtime and statistical robustness.

# 6.2.9.5 Practical tips

+   - Start with low counts (e.g. 100–500), then increase adaptively as training progresses
- Monitor gradient norms and loss plateaus to detect shot-limited training.
- Apply gradient clipping to prevent noisy updates from destabilizing learning.
- Use momentum-based optimizers (e.g., Adam) that average out short-term fluctuations.

# 6.2.10 Loss landscapes and barren plateaus

One of the fundamental challenges in training quantum circuits is the barren phenomenon. Barren plateaus refer to regions in the parameter space where the gradient of the loss function becomes exponentially small, making training via gradient descent ineffective. Understanding when and why these flat landscapes are crucial for designing effective and scalable quantum learning models.

# 6.2.11 Intuition behind barren plateaus

In classical optimization, flat regions in the loss landscape slow down training because gradient magnitudes are too small to guide meaningful updates. In quantum circuits, this can be much more severe due to the exponential size of the Hilbert space [1].

When a variational circuit becomes too expressive, it approximates a 2-design, meaning that it 'scrambles' inputs uniformly across Hilbert space. As a result:

+   - The expected gradient of any observable becomes zero.
- The variance of the gradient shrinks exponentially with the number of qubits.

Mathematically, this results in

$$\left[rac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_0}\right] = 0, \quad \text{Var}\left[rac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_0}\right] \in \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\text{poly}(n)}\right) \quad (6.38)$

or worse, in $\mathcal{O}(e^{-n})$, depending on the ansatz and cost function.
