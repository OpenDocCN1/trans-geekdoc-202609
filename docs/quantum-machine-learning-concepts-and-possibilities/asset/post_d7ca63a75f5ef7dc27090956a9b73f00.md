## 6.1.2 Parameterized quantum circuits as learning models

As introduced in the previous chapter, a parameterized quantum circuit (PQC) or variational quantum circuit (VQC) consists of an arbitrary set of quantum gates, which are parameterized by a set of controls that determine the outcome of the circuit. A specific type of PQC is so-called quantum neural networks (QNNs), where the suitable set of parameters for a task is learned on a given set of data, resembling the training of a classical neural network [9–11, 13, 37].

Classical neural networks provide the architectural and conceptual blueprint for variational quantum models. Understanding classical neural networks sets the stage for appreciating their quantum counterparts. Quantum circuits, like neural networks, consist of sequential transformations applied to inputs, followed by a nonlinear output process (in this case, measurement). These gates may be fixed (e.g. Hadamard, CNOT) or parameterized (e.g. $R_X(\theta), R_Y(\theta), R_Z(\theta)$), and their structure defines the *ansatz*, the expressive capacity of the model. The general workflow of a VQC classifier is as follows:

1. **State preparation** (*data encoding*): Classical input data $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are encoded into a quantum state $|\psi(x)\rangle$ using an *encoding circuit* $U_{enc}(x)$. This circuit maps the data into the Hilbert space of $n$ qubits.
2. **Parameterized quantum transformation:** A parameterized quantum circuit $U(\theta)$, composed of gates with tunable parameters $\theta = (\theta_0, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_l)$, is applied to the final state. This is the quantum analog of learnable weights in classical neural networks.
3. **Measurement and output:** After applying the variational circuit, a measurement is performed in a chosen basis (the computational basis). The classifier output is derived from the *expectation value* of a measurement operator $M$ on the final state,

```
$f(x; \theta) = \langle\psi(x; \theta)|M|\psi(x; \theta)\rangle, \quad (6.2)$
```

or explicitly in terms of circuit units:

```
$f(x; \theta) = |0\rangle U^{\dagger}_{enc}(x) U^{\dagger}(\theta) U(\theta) U_{enc}(x)|0\rangle. \quad (6.3)$
```

The result of a measurement can be interpreted in different ways, depending on the classification task:

- *Binary classification*: A single-bit observable (e.g. Z) is measured

The training loop resembles classical training, but with quantum evaluations at the forward step and specialized techniques (e.g. parameter-shift rule) for computing gradients. The final state before measurement

```
$|\psi(x; \theta)\rangle = U(\theta) U_{enc}(x)|0\rangle^{\otimes n}. \quad (6.4)$
```

This hybrid structure *classical input* $\rightarrow$ *quantum embedding* $\rightarrow$ *parameterized quantum evolution* $\rightarrow$ *classical output* forms the backbone of quantum classification models.
Like neural networks, VQCs can be stacked, extended with data re-uploading layers, and in conjunction with classical layers. However, their training dynamics and representational capacity differ significantly, motivating new theoretical and empirical analysis.

## 6.2 Backpropagation and gradient estimation in quantum models

Training variational quantum classifiers requires differentiating expectation values of **observables with respect to circuit parameters**. Unlike classical neural networks, where backpropagation flows through a graph of differential primitives, quantum hardware only returns **samples from measurement outcomes**, so gradients must be estimated through **hardware-compatible rules**. In hybrid models, these quantum gradients are then stitched into a larger *end-to-end* auto-differentiation pipeline. Below we review the main techniques, their assumptions, costs, and when to use them.

### 6.2.1 Automatic differentiation on hybrid computational graphs

In modern machine learning, automatic differentiation (autodiff) [14] is the backbone of efficient gradient-based training. Whether in PyTorch [15], TensorFlow [16], or JAX [17], autodiff enables seamless computation of derivatives by traversing a **computational graph**. In the context of quantum machine learning, the introduction of hybrid models—where quantum circuits are embedded within classical learning architectures—raises a crucial question: how do we propagate gradients through a model that contains a quantum circuit?

The answer lies in hybrid computational graphs, which extend the autodiff paradigm to quantum nodes. These nodes encapsulate quantum operations (state preparation, variational evolution, and measurement) and expose *custom gradient rules* so that classical optimizers can update parameters across the quantum-classical boundary.

#### Hybrid quantum graph structure

A typical model consists of:

- **Classical nodes**: feature pre-processing, dense layers, and loss functions.
- **Quantum nodes**: parameterized quantum circuits acting on input-encoded states, returning expectation values or sample statistics.

These nodes are linked in a computation graph, where data flow *forward* during evaluation, and gradients flow *backward* during training. The overall architecture resembles a deep learning pipeline, but with variational circuits taking the role of nonlinear transformations.

Formally, let $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be a $d$-dimensional classical feature vector, and let $\psi(x; \theta)$ be prepared by the quantum circuit parameterized by the vector of angles $\theta$. The output of the quantum circuit is $f(x; \theta)$—example, if the output is an expectation value over $M$:

```
$f(x; \theta) = \langle\psi(x; \theta)|M|\psi(x; \theta)\rangle. \quad (6.5)$
```

The output is a scalar value bounded between $[-1, 1]$ from a label $\hat{f}(x; \theta)$ which is extracted. Examples of label extraction include using a classical bias parameter as binary labels, or re-scaling values from $[-1, 1]$ to $[0, 1]$. A classical loss $L^C(y, \hat{f})$ is then evaluated using ground truth label $y$. The gradient with respect to the parameters is

```
$\nabla_{\theta} L(f(x; \theta), y) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial f} \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta} \quad (6.6)$
```

Here, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}$ is computed classically, while must be estimated using quantum-compatible methods, such as the parameter-shift rule.
