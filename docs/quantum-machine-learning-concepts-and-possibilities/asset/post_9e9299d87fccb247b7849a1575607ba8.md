# 4.1.3 Kernel methods

Instead of explicitly computing $\phi(x)$, *kernel methods* evaluate inner products in the feature space using a *kernel function*

$k(x, x') = \langle\phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle.$

This trick—known as the *kernel*—allows efficient computation in high-dimensional space. A popular example is the radial basis function (RBF) kernel,

$k(x, x') = \exp\left(\frac{|x - x'|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),$

which corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature space.

Kernel methods such as the SVM use kernel functions to construct classifiers with strong generalization properties. The dual formulation of the SVM optimization problem depends only on kernel evaluations, making it possible to learn complex decision boundaries without explicitly constructing the feature-vectors.

# 4.1.4 Theoretical guarantees

The use of kernel methods is grounded in the *representer theorem*, which ensures that solutions to a wide class of regularized empirical risk minimization problems lie in the span of the kernel evaluations on the training set. Furthermore, *Mercer’s* theorem guarantees that any continuous, symmetric, positive-definite kernel corresponds to an inner product in some Hilbert space.

### The kernel trick
Given a map $\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathcal{F}$, the kernel trick allows us to compute inner products in $\mathcal{F}$ without explicitly constructing $\phi(x)$. That is,

$$k(x, x') = \langle\phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle \quad (4.7)$$

can be computed directly in the input space. This leads to powerful algorithms that operate in high-dimensional or even infinite-dimensional feature spaces with computational cost depending only on the size of the dataset.

These classical techniques motivate quantum counterparts. By replacing classical feature maps and inner products with quantum states and overlap amplitudes, one can define **quantum kernels** and **quantum-enhanced inference models**. The next sections will explore how quantum computing implements these ideas, with the potential for exponential expressivity and computational advantages.

# 4.2 Quantum feature maps

One of the most promising avenues for leveraging quantum computing in supervised learning is the use of **quantum feature maps**. These maps embed classical data into high-dimensional quantum states, opening the possibility of using quantum hardware to implicitly access large Hilbert spaces that are intractable to simulate classically. The inner products between quantum states serve as the foundation for **quantum kernel methods** and other quantum-enhanced inference algorithms.

# 4.2.1 Quantum states as features

Let us recall the concept of a classical feature map $\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathcal{F}$, where $\mathcal{F}$ is a high-dimensional feature space. In the quantum setting, the role of $\phi(x)$ is played by a quantum state $|\phi(x)\rangle$, prepared by a quantum circuit that encodes the classical data $x$.

Formally, a *quantum feature map* is a mapping

```
$x \rightarrow |\phi(x)\rangle \in \mathcal{H}, \quad (4.8)$
```

where $\mathcal{H}$ is the Hilbert space associated with an $n$-qubit quantum system, i.e., $\mathcal{H} \simeq (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n}$. The quantum circuit that prepares $|\phi(x)\rangle$ is often denoted by $U_{\phi}(x)$, such that

```
$|\phi(x)\rangle = U_{\phi}(x)|0\rangle^{\otimes n}. \quad (4.9)$
```

The encoding circuit $U_{\phi}(x)$ may include data-dependent gates such as $R_y(x)$, and the choice of encoding affects the performance of quantum algorithms.

# 4.2.2 Data encodings for feature maps

Several encodings were introduced for. Here, we briefly recall their relevance in quantum methods:

- **Basic encoding**: Encodes strings directly into feature space.
- **Amplitude encoding**: Represents $x$ as quantum state:
```
$|\phi(x)\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{2^n} x_i |i\rangle \quad (4.10)$
```
- **Angle encoding**: Maps $x$ to rotation:
```
$|\phi(x)\rangle = \bigotimes_{j=1}^n e^{i x_j Z_j} |+\rangle. \quad (4.11)$
```
- **Hamiltonian encoding**: Applies unitary evolution $e^{-iHt}$ data.

# 4.2.3 Properties of quantum feature maps

Quantum feature maps are designed to:

- **Expressive**: Capture complex relationships in Hilbert space.
- **Hard to simulate classically**: Due to the exponential size of the Hilbert space.
- **Implicitly high-dimensional**: In the number of qubits $n$.

**Table 4.1.** Comparison of classical and quantum feature maps.

| Property | Classical feature map $\phi(x)$ | Quantum feature map $|\phi(x)|$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Domain | $\mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathcal{F}$ | $\mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n}$ |
| Representation | Explicit vector | Quantum state prepared by circuit |
| Dimensionality | Often infinite (e.g., RBF) | Exponential in number of qubits |
| Inner product | $\langle\phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle$ | $|\langle\phi(x)|\phi(x')\rangle|^2$ |
| Potential benefit | Improved separability | Compact and expressive embeddings |

These properties make quantum feature maps central to machine learning. In table 4.1, we present a comparison to highlight similarities. In the next section, we will see how quantum feature maps induce quantum kernels.

# 4.3 Quantum kernels and kernel methods

The concept of a kernel is central to many inference algorithms, particularly for SVMs and Gaussian processes. When data are encoded into quantum states via a quantum feature map $x \mapsto |\phi(x)|$, the inner product between these states defines a *quantum kernel*. Quantum kernels offer a natural extension of classical methods into the quantum regime, with the potential to represent highly expressive similarities that are difficult to compute classically.

# 4.3.1 Quantum kernel definition

Given a quantum feature map $|\phi(x)|$, the associated quantum kernel is defined as

```
$\kappa(x, x') = |\langle\phi(x)|\phi(x')\rangle|^2 \quad (4.12)$
```
