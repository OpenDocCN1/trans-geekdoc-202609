
but we can only access it through **shot-based sampling** on a quantum device. If $M$ has eigenvalues in $[-1, 1]$, then the **standard deviation** of the sample mean over $N$ shots is

$$\text{Std}[\hat{\gamma}] = \frac{\sqrt{\text{Var}(M)}}{\sqrt{N}} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$$
(6.36)

Thus, to reduce the error in the estimate by a factor of 10, we must increase the number of shots by a factor of 100. This statistical noise is referred to as **shot noise**, and it affects:

- **Forward evaluations** of the model output,
- **Gradient estimates**, in particular when using the parameter-shift rule, and
- **Loss values**, which are often nonlinear functions of noisy outputs.

## 6.2.9.2 Gradient variance and signal-to-noise ratio

Let $\hat{g}_i$ be the estimated gradient of parameter $\theta_i$. The variance of this estimate scales with:

- The number of shots,
- The functional sensitivity of the circuit, and
- The parameterization of the gates.

When gradients are small (e.g., near flat regions or in barren plateaus), **shot noise can dominate**, making the signal indistinguishable from noise. This leads to:

- Unstable optimization steps
- Poor convergence or stagnation, and
- Bias in gradient direction due to measurement error.

Quantifying the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** of gradients is essential in practice. The **Fisher information** or **gradient norm** serves as proxy for when to adapt shot counts or learning rates.

## 6.2.9.3 Adaptive shot allocation

To mitigate the impact of shot noise:

- **adaptive shot strategies** [29, 30]: allocate more shots to parameters or data points with high gradient variance.
- **Estimate the variance per parameter** during training and dynamically adjust measurement budgets.
- **Variance-reduced estimators**, e.g., averaging over symmetric circuits, parameter mirroring, or antithetic sampling.
- These techniques can reduce the total number of shots while maintaining effective convergence, in particular hardware-constrained settings.

## 6.2.9.4 Mini-batching over data

If the training dataset $\{(x_i, y_j)\}$ is large, one can further reduce variance by averaging gradients over **mini-batches** of size $B$:

$$\mathcal{L}^B = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \nabla_0 L(x_i; \theta, y_i). \quad (6.37)$$

Mini-batching:

- Smoothes out per-sample gradient fluctuations,
- Is compatible with shot noise averaging, and
- Allows parallelization across quantum circuits or devices.

However, if $f(x_i)$ must be measured separately, mini-batching increases the circuit evaluation count. Thus, batch size and shot count must be jointly tuned to balance runtime and statistical robustness.

## 6.2.9.5 Practical tips

- Start with low counts (e.g., 100–500), then increase adaptively as training progresses
- Monitor gradient norms and loss plateaus to detect shot-limited training.
- Apply gradient clipping to prevent noisy updates from destabilizing learning.
- Use momentum-based optimizers (e.g., Adam) that average out short-term fluctuations.

## 6.2.10 Loss landscapes and barren plateaus

One of the fundamental challenges in training quantum circuits is the barren phenomenon. Barren plateaus refer to regions in the parameter space where the gradient of the loss function becomes exponentially small, making training via gradient descent ineffective. Understanding when and why these flat landscapes are crucial for designing effective and scalable quantum learning models.

## 6.2.11 Intuition behind barren plateaus

In classical optimization, flat regions in the loss landscape slow down training because gradient magnitudes are too small to guide meaningful updates. In quantum circuits, this can be much more severe due to the exponential size of the Hilbert space [1].

When a variational circuit becomes too expressive, it approximates a 2-design, meaning that it 'scrambles' inputs uniformly across Hilbert space. As a result:

- The expected gradient of any observable becomes zero.
- The variance of the gradient shrinks exponentially with the number of qubits.

Mathematically, this results in

$$\left[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_0}\right] = 0, \quad \text{Var}\left[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_0}\right] \in \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\text{poly}(n)}\right) \quad (6.38)$$

or worse, in $\mathcal{O}(e^{-n})$, depending on the ansatz and cost function.

## 6.2.11.1 When do barren plateaus occur?

Barren plateaus are not universal, they depend on specific circuit choices and cost functions. Key findings include:

- **Global cost functions** (e.g., fidelity between full states) are more susceptible.
- **Deep random circuits** (or those approximating 2-designs) show exponential gradient decay.
- **Unstructured ansatze** with too much expressivity suffer worse than task-specific circuits.
- **Problem-invariant initializations** (e.g., all-zero angles) often land directly in flat regions.

Recent research has formalized these results and identified several conditions under which barren plateaus arise.

## 6.2.11.2 Avoiding barren plateaus

Several strategies have been developed to mitigate this phenomenon:

- **Local cost functions**. Observables that depend on a small number of qubits (e.g., local Hamiltonian terms)
- **Layerwise training**. Gradually grow the circuit depth during training. This keeps the parameter landscape smoother in early stages [33].
- **Problem-inspired ansatze**. Architectures developed from the structure of the physical system (e.g., hardware efficiency or QAOA circuits) [34, 35].
- **Parameter initialization schemes**. Break symmetry using random initializations or schemes as identity blocks or shallow pretraining [36].
- **Quantum gradient methods**. Incorporate curvature information to traverse plateaus more effectively.

$$\mathcal{L} = \langle Z_{i} Z_{i+1} \rangle.$$

This prevents averaging over the full state and preserves meaningful gradients [32]. 

These insights help to co-design circuits and training routines to avoid untrainable regimes, an especially important consideration for scaling to larger quantum systems.

## 6.3 Architectural variants: QCNNs and quantum graph-based models

Variational quantum classifiers can be realized through a variety of architectures, each imposing different biases and capabilities. Two prominent classes of models that extend beyond simple ansatze are **quantum convolutional networks (QCNNs)** [37] and **quantum graph-based models**, such as **quantum graph networks (QGNNs)** [38].

### 6.3.1 Quantum convolutional networks

QNNs are variational architectures inspired by classical convolutional networks (CNNs), which have proven remarkably effective in processing structured data. QNNs translate this principle into the quantum domain, where quantum circuits extract hierarchical features using local operations and pooling.

A QCNN consists of a circuit comprising three key components: Quantum layers: These layers apply parameterized, local operations across subsets of qubits. The arrangement is translation-invariant, analogous to the application of a filter kernel across an image. Typically, the quantum layer includes:
- Two or three-qubit entangling gates (e.g., CZ, CNOT, CRY).
- Followed by single-qubit rotations (e.g., RY, RZ).
These operations are repeated across the entire register using shared parameters, capturing local correlations and symmetry features.

Pooling layers (quantum downsampling): To reduce the number of active qubits and aggregate information, QNNs implement pooling either through:
- Measurement and discard: Certain qubits are measured, and their outcomes are used to conditionally update the rest (non-unitary, requires classical feedback).
- Unitary pooling: Learned units disentangle and isolate a subset of qubits, which are then traced out or ignored.
Pooling reduces the circuit width, enabling logarithmic depth scaling, which is favorable for implementation on NISQ hardware.

Fully connected or readout layer: At the final stage, the remaining few qubits are used to compute the model's output. A final measurement (typically in the Z-basis) of a designated qubit produces the classification result.
