# IMPORTANCE OF ELECTROGASTOGRAM (EGG) AND ELECTROMYOGRAM (EMG)

Electromyography (EMG) is a diagnostic method which evaluates the health condition of muscles and the nerve cells (neurons) that control them. Motor neurons transmit signals that cause muscles to contract and relax. These signals are translated into graph form using EMG. The results of EMG help to identify disorders of muscle, nerve and disorders affecting the neuro-muscular connection [4]. Some symptoms that require EMG include: tingling, numbness, weakness, cramp or pain, involuntary muscle twitching. The results of EMG help to determine the underlying cause for the symptoms which possibly include: muscular dystrophy (group of conditions that weakens or damages the muscle over time), myasthenia gravis (weakness in the voluntary muscles), radioulnar pinched nerve (carpal tunnel syndrome), amyloid lateral sclerosis.

EMG electrodes are utilized to detect the biochemical activity within a person's muscle. There are two primary types of EMG electrodes, namely surface electrodes (also known as skin electrodes) and inserted electrodes. The latter can be further classified into two types: needle electrodes and wire electrodes.

The objective of this EMG system is to assist individuals who are equal or easily to assess their muscle strength level and obtain beneficial muscle signals for rehabilitation purposes [3].

As earlier, EEG is a non-invasive method used for recording the gastric myoelectric contractions. EEG has a wide range of applications in electrophysiological studies and clinical practice. It is commonly used to assess the efficacy of interventions and therapies aimed at improving gastric motility in patients with digestive disorders. By measuring the electrical signals generated by the smooth muscle cells of the stomach, EEG can provide objective data on the effectiveness of the interventions. In addition, EEG is a valuable tool for detecting wave abnormalities in patients with suspected or confirmed gastric disorders. By analysing the frequency and amplitude of electrical signals, healthcare providers can identify patterns associated with gastric dysmotility and develop personalized treatment plans for patients. Overall, EEG has the potential to improve the accuracy of gastric motility disorder diagnosis and inform effective treatment strategies. [1]

# 2.1 Physiology of stomach

The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag in the frontal plane. The process of gastric emptying, which refers to the movement of food from the stomach into the small intestine, is regulated by a complex interplay of neural, hormonal, and mechanical factors. The rate of gastric emptying can affect nutrient absorption and blood glucose levels, and can also play a role in conditions such as gastric motility and dumping syndrome. The study of gastric motility and gastric emptying is important in understanding the physiology of the digestive system and treating disorders. The volume of stomach is determined by the amount of stored food and ranges between 25 ml and 1500 ml in the fed state. After a typical meal of ~1000 mL, the stomach measures around 30 cm along its length curve and exhibits a maximum width of 10 cm. Peristaltic muscular waves, which are ring-like contraction that contract the stomach circumferentially, running from the corpus through the antrum to the pylorus, are mixing and grinding the food in the distal part of the stomach. Temporal coordination of these so-called antral contraction waves (ACWs) with the aperture of the pylorus controls releases the digesta into the duodenum [5].

The function of stomach include:

- Acts as a reservoir for ingested food
- Mixes food with gastric secretions
- Empt gastric contents into the duodenum [6].

Endoscopy involves the insertion of a long, flexible tube with a camera and light source through the mouth or rectum to view the inside of the digestive system. It can help diagnose various gastrointestinal disorders such as ulcers, inflammation, tumors, and polyps.

According to the Nyquist criterion, the sampling rate must be at least twice the maximum frequency (fs > 2fmax), where fs represents the sampling rate and fmax represents the highest frequency in the signal. If a series is aliased, it becomes impossible to retrieve the lost information using any processing technique.

The principle underlying the Nyquist criterion for temporal sampling is also relevant for spatial sampling. The highest detectable spatial frequency without aliasing is determined by the density and measurement area of the electrodes. The electrode acts as an analog filter removing spatial frequencies with wavelengths shorter than its actual measurement diameter. This is because the electrode changes the potentials within an area in contact with the gel or measurement area. For instance, an array of electrodes with uniform-to-center spacing of d and electrode diameter of D [7], applying the Nyquist criterion to the edge-to-edge distance between neighbouring electrodes yields the following limitation:

```
d - D < λmin / 2
```

where λmin is the shortest wavelength of the signal.

The normal frequency of gastric electrical activity, or normogastria, is typically between 2.25–3.75 cps. Frequencies that are lower or higher than this range are referred to as arrhythmias, with abnormally low frequencies known as Bradycardia (1.00–2.25 cps) and abnormally high frequencies known as Tachycardia (3.75–9.00 cps).
