Andrew Holland, COO at the American Security Project, stated: "The Arctic is the only theatre of operations where the U.S. Navy is outclassed; Russian surface warships have demonstrated the ability to carry out complex operations in the High North, meanwhile the American Navy maintains a policy that only allows submarines to operate above the Bering Strait [61]. This attitude towards activity in the Arctic has been described as "American reluctance" and is thought to be caused by the economic importance of America's territories around and above Alaska is less substantial than that of Canada, Norway and Russia. While the U.S. has Silicon Valley and numerous large cities as economic powerhouses, Russia lacks this and views the Arctic as their strategic resource base.

If Russia can prove that its continental shelf extends to the Lomonosov and Mendeleyev ridges, they would control 60% of the hydrocarbons detected in the region – an incentive for expansion. Russia supports UNCLOS border decisions but also accepts that disputes may lead to conflict. Economic prosperity and sustainable commercialization in the Arctic are the main priorities for Russia, however, competition to make conflict inevitable. Russia has a willingness, if necessary, to use military force in order to protect their interests in its sovereign waters [62]. Russia also have 38 ice-breakers, some being nuclear powered. In comparison, the combined number by ten other countries (USA, Canada, Sweden, Norway, Finland, China, France, Germany, Japan and South Korea) is only [22].

The ignition for increased militarization of the area can be traced back to 2007 when the extent of sea-ice hit record lows, and Russia used this as an incentive to ‘make moves’ in the Arctic, planting a titanium flag 14,000 feet below the surface of the North Pole. Since then Russia has doubled the number of operational bases within the Arctic Circle, now maintaining twenty-seven bases [64]. Furthermore, a number of strategic bombers patrol the Arctic, based in Engels Base in Southern Russia. An additional 16 deep-water ports are located along the Northern Sea Route, alongside a further 10 Search and Rescue (SAR) bases. In comparison, the United States maintains only one; Thule Air Base, in Greenland.

Admiral Paul Zukunft, Commandant of the U.S. Coast Guard stated, "They’ve [Russia] got all their pieces on the board now, and we’ve got a pawn and maybe a rook. If you look at this Arctic game of chess, they’ve got in checkmate rate at the very beginning [65].

Most analysts believe there is little that sovereignty issues will lead directly to conflict [66] [67]; indent current security concerns associated with the pre-2030 Arctic are directed towards SAR efforts, for which the US Coast Guard has primary responsibility. The main concern for the US Military will be to plan and adapt to the human response to changing conditions in the Arctic [68]. An alternative for the US is increased commercialization, with fears that it may lead to an ecological threat from unsustainable activity. This activity may prevent eco terror, and there is fear that radical terrorists may identify the Arctic as a new venue [69]; For the US, the concern is not "what are the security risks" but, "How will security risks as the geopolitical and economic expansion play out?"
