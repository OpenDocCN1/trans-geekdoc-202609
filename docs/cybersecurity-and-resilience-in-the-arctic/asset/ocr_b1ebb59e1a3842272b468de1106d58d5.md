In another study of Aghan youths (Eggerman & Panter-Brick, 2010), we find a poignant example of adolescent hope. The report includes two figures drawn by a 14-year-old boy. One illustrates his present plight, barefoot and pushing a wheelbarrow also does jobs to earn small amounts of money for the family; the second, a future (adult) self, becoming an “excellent” doctor, now well-dressed and carrying a medical bag.

Wellbeing is more closely tied to everyday experiences, including structural violence, in Afghanistan, than memories of wars; and suffering from one generation to the next family members. “Resilience and fortitude resort upon hope” and the “belief that adversity can be overcome and a process of meaning-making gives coherence to past, present, and future experiences” (Eggerman & Panter-Brick, 2010, p. 81).

Unfortunately, there is a dearth of which has empirically tested the role of longitudinal data and focused on experiential histories of youth (Stoddard, et al., 2011). However, because positive experiences have potential to generate hope at later time during adolescence, a hopeful sense for the future may actually emerge from a calamitous situation to lessen the effects of hopelessness. At the group level, the objectives of these interventions must be to “develop hope, cohesion-social norms, and collective efficacy, focusing on the ability of youth to act effectively in their communities; and and developing a sense of hope, meaning and purpose” (Stoddard, et al., 2011, p. 292). Eggerman & Panter-Brick (2010) assert that data collected in their research indicate that “interventions focusing on everyday ecology – strengthening family and wider social networks – need to go in hand with interventions focusing on everyday material ecology” (p. 82) Study also indicate that the quality of education provision must be improved to further goals of psychosocial wellbeing in the lives of a new generation. Given the current disruption to social fabric in places like the Arctic, it is important that we understand ways in which we might mitigate hopelessness, and encourage hope in our youth, in order to pave pathways towards greater social-ecological resilience.

Man’s primary motivational force is the “will to mean” (Frankl, 1992, p.98). Meaning is the core of being human; a motivating, vitalizing force that drives action in individuals. Identifying future goals, striving for them by creating, tasks that are meaningful, achieving a greater goal (Baumeister, 1991; Hunter, Dik, & Bannin, 2010); having the fortitude to endure, upon believing in one’s life has meaning. Frankl (1992), in his book, reported to a public opinion poll in France where 89 percent of respondents indicated the need for something something or someone for whom they would die. Another survey of US college students indicated 16 percent aside to “making a lot of money,” while 78 percent prioritized “finding a purpose and meaning to their life” (p. 99).
