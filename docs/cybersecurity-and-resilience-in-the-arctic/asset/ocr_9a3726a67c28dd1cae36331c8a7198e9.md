The Arctic Council’s ability to foster trust and confidence between states on soft policy issues has a key factor in enabling cooperation to address societal vulnerabilities in the region.

The Arctic Council is a unique intergovernmental organization including Indigenous Peoples as Permanent Participants on the Council; states with the Arctic International Association (AIA), Arctic Athabaskan Council (AAC), Gwich International Council (ICI), Inuit Circumpolar Council (ICC), Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North (RAIPON) and Sami Council (SC). Permanent Participants are consulted in Arctic negotiations but do not have voting rights (Arctic Council, 2015). Indigenous peoples constitute a significant percentage of the Arctic population. For example, in Canada, Indigenous make up 53% of the total population across the three Northern territories (StatsCan, 2016). The inclusion of Arctic peoples in the decision-making process at the Arctic Council builds societal resilience resilience concerns. Salminen and Hussain argue that empowered peoples “are capable of avoiding risks and improving the system of protection” which transforms them from “passive receivers of security to active security producers” (2018). The Arctic Council empowers Indigenous peoples to exercise agency and actively contribute to the development of a secure and resilient Arctic

The Arctic Council produced a report in 2016 on the resilience of socio-ecological systems in the Arctic, building on collaboration with Arctic countries, Indigenous peoples, and Arctic scientific organizations. The report summarized the role of the Arctic Council as a platform for facilitating the alignment of interests between Arctic societies, communities, and individuals. The Council contributes to the development and preservation of sustainable communities and ecosystems by coordinating international responses to threats in the region (Arctic Resilience Assessment Co-Chairs, 2016).

Arctic societies are “able to come together to identify and respond to challenges” (Arctic Council, 2016), including security and cyber security challenges.

Under the Arctic Council, three legally-binding agreements were negotiated: on search and rescue, oil-spill preparedness, and scientific cooperation. The Agreement on Aeronautical and Maritime Search and Rescue in the Arctic and the Agreement on Cooperation on Marine Oil Pollution Preparedness and Response in the Arctic demonstrate an expansion of the role of the Arctic Council through the deployment of the military and paramilitary assets of states to address vulnerabilities in the region (Greaves and Sommers, 2017).
