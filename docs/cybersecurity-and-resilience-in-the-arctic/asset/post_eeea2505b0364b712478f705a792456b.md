Governance can be understood as "the reflexive self-organization of independent actors involved in complex relations of reciprocal interdependence" (Jessop 2003). As such, it encompasses all processes of governance taking place within the modalities of command (by hierarchical government), dialogue (with networks), and exchange (as a basis of market and dictated by market forces). While the governmental authority is vested with responsibility and accountability, from perspective of governance it is limited or transformed in the modern world. This is precisely because of the existence of various actors and the operation of markets, including autonomous rules through which these markets operate. Only totalitarian authoritarian dictatorships claim the capability of limiting all governance to the processes of governmental command.

The aforementioned complexity of Arctic governance landscape and changes taking place in the region mean today a number of different actors are engaged in the governance of the Arctic. Not all of these actors are actually from the Arctic, nor in line with the aforementioned multidimensional character of governance – are all of those with interest in governing the Arctic nations and states. From the governance perspective, the national governments are powerful actors, but joined in governance by private sector actors and the global market forces as well as diverse non-actors engaged in networks of interdependence. In addition, networks between different components of state bureaucracies have helped building further foundations for long-term international cooperation. This is not surprising, given the growing interest in the region and the importance of international cooperation for the Arctic. Indeed, the Arctic has long since been for peaceful cooperation. International cooperation is characterized by a palpable sense of exceptionalism insofar particular between the West and the Russian Federation – often play a reduced role in the Arctic. The notion of Arctic exceptionalism builds upon the existence of networks of interdependence among various Arctic state and non-common interests, major conflicts of interest as well as the appreciation of economic value of a peaceful and well-regulated region (Byers 2017). However, as some authors point out (Käylä and Mikkola 2015), the Arctic cannot be viewed as a closed system and thus the perceived exceptionalism may be superficial in character. In a way, cooperation in the extreme environment of the Arctic remains cooperation that takes precedence over local considerations. In both areas, the risk that domestic politics in Arctic states is not to be ignored. The 2019 ministerial meeting of the Arctic Council – the key forum for international governance and cooperation in the Arctic – took place in Rovaniemi, Finland, was a good example of the potential for outside dynamics to influence Arctic cooperation. The Secretary voiced rightly concerns about the presence of China in the Arctic and the inclusion of climate change language into the document – undermining the Arctic stance. In addition to nation states, the indigenous peoples play an active role in shaping the future of the Arctic. This is visible in the Arctic Council where indigenous organizations hold a unique status – permanent participants, as well as is the Arctic Fisheries Agreement, of increasing interest in the Arctic from outside.

The multitude of actors corresponds with a range of tools which are at the disposal of governments. While governments might focus on governance on national, sub-national, and international levels, non-state actors might find the possibility of providing input into the creation of soft law or technical standards to be often binding through UNCLOS (cf. IMO 2014). This chapter meant to provide an introduction to the governance of the Arctic in this time of rapid change and multiple challenges, as well as tools available to governments. To this, we will look at institutions from the perspective of Arctic governance. We will focus on existing international cooperation. The chapter focuses on the international level of Arctic governance. However, Arctic realities and developments are primarily shaped by national actions and within the Arctic states. In addition to the primary focus on international governance, we therefore discuss the dynamics playing out at other levels and in particular their linkages with policymaking.

## 2 Governance challenges in the Arctic

### 2.1. Effectiveness of law in the Arctic

Sheeban (2018) highlighted that “[the multitude] of challenges facing Arctic populations relate not only to military forces, as realistic political theory would assume, to a broader range of economic, environmental and social insecurities.” The same applies in principle to the other parts of the Arctic as well, and it is these aspects that are at the focus of this chapter’s discussion on governance effectiveness. Addressing these needs requires international cooperation and Arctic governance, in particular the governance of Arctic spaces through the use of international treaty law. Law as a regulatory tool faces a number of challenges. To begin, in democratic society, law reacts to the demands of society and is reactive in nature where democratic societies may be seen tool for governments to maintain control of society. Not all of the states which are located in the Arctic or have a significant interest in the Arctic are democracies that are committed to the rule of law and for human rights not only on paper but in practice. Different levels of respect for the rule of law and the human rights of those affected by regulation, or even the complete absence of enshrined human rights or the rule of law, also show that the same tool might not be equally appropriate in all circumstances. As international treaties require consensus, they are too rarely utilized. While the precautionary approach an increasingly relevant role in the Arctic in the context of (international) environmental law, the precautionary dimension of treaty-based international cooperation in the Arctic is otherwise limited. This also means that the capacity of international treaty law to mitigate risks in the Arctic is limited. As the experience of international maritime safety has shown, international legal developments are often triggered by specific events which have negative effects become visible; these events highlight the need for further (legal binding) regulation. In addition, the use of (international) law to govern risk-related issues requires infusions of technical expertise into the process of creating norms.

### 2.2. Decision-makers' knowledge of Arctic developments

The question of expertise highlights the need for interdisciplinary cooperation and uninterrupted flows of information. This applies in particular to the exchange of information between different areas of expertise and different knowledge systems (traditional, lay, and scientific knowledge). Such cooperation can contribute to raising awareness and to the understanding of environmental, social, economic, cultural, and other aspects of the challenges which international law is expected to address. The awareness of both problems and of one's field of view is particularly relevant in the Arctic as many governance decisions are far from the places and people affected by the decisions. The distance between urban centers and remote settlements is not only geographical but also cultural. Arctic regions, for instance, livelihoods and lands with which decision-makers are unfamiliar. Arctic conditions may present different challenges and for different practices than other parts of the world, other marine areas, and even those areas of the Arctic. Decisions made in Washington, Moscow, Stockholm, or Brussels (see 2012 and Stepien/Kivooura, 2017: pp. 16 et seq.) dramatically affects the lives of people who live in the periphery, thousands of miles away. The lack of professional and Arctic experience among policymakers located in the centers of power needs to be compensated for input provided by Arctic experts and residents. Ideally, engaging with Arctic rights holders, stakeholders, and experts will allow policymakers to better understand and consider their decisions in the diversity of the Arctic, the multiple definitions of the Arctic, and the challenges that specific regions or urban locations face. Doing so allows for reason among decision-makers that there is not one single definition of the Arctic and within the Arctic, urban settings raise different questions than remote settlements. Different regulatory approaches are used to respond to these factual challenges, with examples regarding the involvement of private actors in the creation of norms and standards, different levels of centralization among the different Arctic States, and so forth. Even definitions of governance might differ a primary aim in a capital city, Arctic communities will often be forced to focus on issues such as resilience building, which can require increased investments, for example to add a layer of redundancy in the construction of infrastructures.

### 2.3. Responding to climate change

Another challenge to international Arctic governance is the divergence among Arctic States concerning evaluation of the issue facing the Arctic today. Climate change is not limited to the melting sea but also affects the biosphere and permafrost and is a threat to traditional livelihoods (AACA, 2017). Some governments, however, perceive the increasing accessibility of the Arctic as an economic opportunity, in particular with regard to extractive industries (cf. Byers, 2010: para. 38). The increasing interest in the Arctic has been described as “21st century gold rushes, [which are] challenging the political will and governance capacities of national governments who, for decades, have largely ignored the Arctic” (Byers, 2010: para. 38).
