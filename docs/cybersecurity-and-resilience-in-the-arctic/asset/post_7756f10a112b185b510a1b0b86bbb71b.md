## 1.5.2. Tourism

Tourism in the Arctic is not a new phenomenon. Traveling in the footsteps of explorers and adventurers has lured people toward the North & South Poles for many years. Indeed, tourism along the Norwegian coastline and to the northern parts of the Scandinavian mountain has taken place over 100 years [48].

With changing Arctic conditions, the region has become heavily inundated by tourism in recent years, with Nordic countries and Greenland leading the way. In 2016, half a million people visited Iceland alone, and in 2017 Russia saw a 20% increase in visitors [49]. Also saw a surge in cruise ships setting sail for the Arctic, with Crystal Cruises’13 Serenity traversing the Northwest Passage for the first time. “Last-chance tourism” is on the rise – global warming and climate change threaten to permanently alter the Arctic environment, and those who have previously thought about wanting to go before it's too late.

While tourism is often assumed to be environmentally damaging, the assumption that it necessarily brings economic benefits is also challenged. Adams [50] found that in a number of coastal communities the magnitude and intensity of passenger visitors raises social cost, causing economic regimes to shift that rapid socioeconomic restructuring. Managing and developing tourism connections between remote communities and their residents has to be a challenge across all Arctic regions [51][52]. These obstacles make these areas difficult to develop, fostering a tension between local economic development and cultural preservation [53].

Furthermore, widespread standards of regulation are sparse. A range of permits are required for cruise operations across different arctic regions, the rules ruling what is and isn't acceptable are challenging to enforce. Monitoring every cruise ship would be a large task, and they are expected to follow the rules. The Polar Code provides guidance on discarding food waste and sewage, yet catching ships breaking these rules is hard, and enforcement of these is up to the IMO’s 172 member states, with no pre-specified permits [54].

## 2. Military and Security in the Arctic

There are two views of public discourse regarding military operations and security threats in the Arctic, each opposing a contrasting of the North as it has emerged in importance and value throughout the 1900s. The mainstream discourse highlights stability and peacefulness and the absence of armed conflicts and the low likelihood of them occurring [55]. The industrialized and militarized Arctic of the Cold Beg to dissipate in the late 1980s and was transformed from a state of confrontation to one of international cooperation. The alternative discourse, a largely majority view, sees the Arctic and its oceans having the capacity to generate armed conflict, directly as a result of a “race for resources”, particularly those related to energy.

The region is greatly characterized by an increasing pressure for these natural resources, including mineral resources and fish stocks. Maritime border disputes, conflicting Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZ) and incoherence in regard to the concinental shelves of Arctic states pose threats and shape the parameters of northern geopolitics. The climate crisis, however, has been intensifying military build-up in the Arctic. Regional powers attempt to secure and protect their borders which previously were reinforced by a continental-sized division of ice. While the vast expanses of ice, industrial emissions and global warming are now heating this region of the planet as fast as the global average, decreasing not only the extent of sea ice but also its thickness, and the period in which sea ice is at its peak [56]. It is estimated by Wang and Oxford [57] that as as 2037, Arctic summers could come to entirely ice-free Arctic.

With a rapidly changing environment, the modern geopolitical and strategic importance of the Arctic cannot be understated. As a location between three continents, with an abundance of valuable resources, including shipping lanes and rapidly increasing accessibility, the Arctic has the potential to become valuable to those able to effectively lay claims to it. This means that the Arctic attracts attention not only from Arctic states but also from Europe and Asia (namely China, Japan, South Korea, Singapore and India). These states see the benefits of engaging activity through the Arctic and each differently positioned within the collective decision making of Arctic politics. Each possesses, either directly and/or indirectly, an ability to influence the long-term direction of the informal regime, not least if interests coalesce. The deposits of natural resources will have economic importance for those able to obtain them, claiming and performing sovereignty over these regions is subsequently of vital importance to many of the Arctic states including the US and Canada, who currently have disputes over borders in the Beaufort Sea.
