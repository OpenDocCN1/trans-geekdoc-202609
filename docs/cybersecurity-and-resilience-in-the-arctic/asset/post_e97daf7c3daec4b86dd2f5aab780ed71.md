## 2.4 Arctic weather and climate

The climate of the Arctic is unlike experienced anywhere else in the world. The region currently feels the warming effects of climate change at the pace of the rest of the world (Sand et al., 2015), increasing the rate at which glaciers and ice sheets melt, along with the decreasing sea-ice extent in the Arctic ocean. These changes have facilitated an increase in activity amid northern latitudes, for shorter shipping routes between Asia and Europe (Melia et al., 2016; Lassere, 2014), and an ingress to some of the world's most vast natural resources stores, including minerals, oil and gas (Ember and Zemetakis, 2009). These changes, have not only facilitated more activity but have also increased unpredictability in weather conditions, and increased risk is more vulnerable in the Arctic.

Moreover, the increase in activity is not limited solely to enterprise; governments are also becoming more aware, as militaries begin to bolster their capabilities and presence in these sectors (Watts, 2019). Typical issues associated with these sectors experienced in moderate climates may have more implications and consequences in Arctic temperatures; a power outage leading to the loss of heating and electricity may merely be a temporary nuisance, but in sub-freezing temperatures a scenario could put people into fight against freezing conditions, putting them at risk of developing hypothermia and frostbite, whilst increasing the likelihood of cardiac arrest (Hassi et al., 2005). Prolonged exposure to these temperatures would be fatal.

Harsh and rapidly changing Arctic conditions are in part influenced by natural variations, the strength in the trends of recent decades point to another cause – the increased emission of greenhouse gases, primarily as a result of human activity warming fossil fuels (The Royal Society & National Academy of Sciences, 2014). marked the second year in a row where global temperature was more than 1°C above industrial levels, and atmospheric concentration of CO2 has risen by twice 45% (UK Met Office, 2017). Future temperature rise is predicted to be as much as 4.8°C in a worst case scenario (IPCC, 2023).

Climate change and the transformation of the Arctic is likely to have significant regional and global impacts. Atmospheric and oceanic circulation patterns will change; sea level rise will threaten low-lying coastal regions, agriculture and food security. Widening variations in precipitation will lead to droughts in some regions and increases in downfall in others. There will be significant, measurable impacts on coastal communities world-wide, ecosystems, water resources, human health and well-being will be affected across several continents (Incopera, 2015).

A rapidly changing environment has implications not just on people and ecosystems dependent on the Arctic, but also on the existing and transient infrastructure developments within the circumpolar region. Understanding the risks associated with a changing Arctic is necessary to evaluate and develop operationalizing resilience.
