# 1. Introduction

Life the circumpolar north is no stranger to change and ambiguity. From season to season, landscapes across the Arctic; see ice grows and retreats, temperatures fall and rise, and conditions abruptly change. Yet, in the 21st century, the rate at which these change are increasing [1]. These changes fundamentally impact individuals, communities, economic ventures, and the environment - both in the circumpolar north and throughout the rest of the world. The cause of this rapid increase in the rate of change is attributable to human activity - primarily to burning fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas), but also by the clearing of land [2] and large-scale pastoral farming practices [3].

The impacts of climate change are amplified in the Arctic [4], and threaten significant impacts on the encircled and surrounding region of Arctic, ranging from an increase in global sea levels; impacting the frequency and severity of extreme weather events (EWEs), to exacerbating extreme droughts in some areas widening precipitation variation in others [5]. Moreover, these changes in the regulation of global climate systems are likely to threaten food, water, and economic security [6]; the effects which will be disproportionate within and between nations, amplifying inequalities. Political turmoil is likely to have a significant cascade effect, as tensions rise, and mass migrations and refugees couldvert stability, increasing the possibility of conflicts between nations.

The prospective aspect of climate change on the Arctic requires us to look at our options. With the threat of such negative consequences, the Arctic is in need of a modern framework to guide public and private interests. Agreements outlining mitigation strategies at reducing our production of greenhouse gases (GHGs) have already begun to take form in such as the Paris Agreement on climate change, the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, and the UN Sustainable Goals. The next step is to look at future options in order to evaluate and improve their resilience, accepting that not all threats can be prevented, but that our response to them can be enhanced.
