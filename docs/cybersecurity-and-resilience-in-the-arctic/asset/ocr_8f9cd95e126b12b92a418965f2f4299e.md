Additionally, to maintain infrastructure's utility in the changing Arctic, Arctic people must have empowerment writ-large (e.g., legal, financial, cultural) to manage developments [21] [10]. This will require a deeper understanding of how infrastructure hinders or enables the resilience of indigenous peoples to stressors of the changing Arctic.

Our co-authors have heard from Elders and other Indigenous Knowledge holders from Huslia Village, Alaska, that development is seen by the Indigenous peoples as sources of both risk and resilience. Roads and mines were identified as sources of both economic opportunity and social value. They also described how increased transportation-based modify the social cohesion and wellbeing of communities through greater access to services and rewards, exposure to noxious pollutants, and economic legacy if industrial or commercial projects cease operations or offer ephemeral economic benefit (also see [22]) [23].

Research exists on risk, resilience, and adaptation for Arctic infrastructure (e.g., [24] [18]). However, there are few attempts to acquaint Arctic resilience concepts toward actionable outcomes guided by local communities, including as they rely to cybersecurity. There are fewer efforts, if any, that contain multiple ways of knowing the system and explicitly engage with Indigenous Knowledge to improve assessments of infrastructure and cyber security. Investigators' capacity to identify and analyze drivers of acute and chronic risks to infrastructure and security is directly limited to our ability to protect life at the local, regional, and national scale. To accomplish this, investigators can assess qualitative changes in subsystems in function of time (i.e., differing stages of resilience) and domain area (i.e., social, environmental, climatological, Indigenous Knowledge, and others) [20]. This approach can accommodate the complexity of compounded infrastructure challenges in the Arctic, with a resilience matrix identifying areas of converging and cascading disruption that could yield considerable negative consequences to various elements of society, economy, and environment. This approach is nested within the broader tiered approach and likely falls under activities of Tier 2.

When including aspects of Far North development and the built environment in a tiered approach, activities can include:

2) co-developing with Indigenous Partners and/or other local communities and projects investigates a resilience matrix for comparative analysis of the resilience and risk profiles of current and pending Arctic installations.

# Domain 3: Health and well-being

Historically, Indigenous communities have been expected to adapt to Western norms of health and well-being. In the contemporary Far North, however, Indigenous research tells us they are redefining what means to be resilient and healthy. During our 2018 workshop, Elders from Huslia and Nulato in Interior Alaska told us that their top concerns include individual's health, access to jobs, hunting, maintaining culture, and food. In this context, resilience is not accepting historical injustice and pain, accepting structural inequality, and accumulating stress tension.
