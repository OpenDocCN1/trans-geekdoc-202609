# ## Introduction

The Arctic has quickly become a focal point for the impacts of climate and environmental change; the region is more than just an area of cold temperatures, snow, and ice. Environmental changes are affecting agricultural practices and, in many cases, habitability conditions among traditional Arctic societies. Melting ice packs are opening access – in particular waterways -- to new routes and mineral resources; tourism and immigration are bringing new energy, water, food, and waste management demands; and governments and international agencies are becoming increasingly involved. In the High North raise important questions about sovereignty and jurisdiction have begun to create challenges of resource expansion and infrastructural development in problematic conditions.

Proposed solutions to many of these challenges have involved advanced information technologies, bringing with them large server installations, network infrastructure, and personal services. Communication over vast distances allowed population-regions to connect with the rest of the world, modernizing communities and their operations. Many have since looked to the Arctic as a place for profitable growth, as conditions become more viable and previously frozen expanses open up in response to warming temperatures, enabling an ultimate collective action problem. Across the globe, governments, transnational corporations, and billions of dollars are entangled in a resource-based economy, where pursuit of one often comes at the cost of others. Desire to be an “early bird” often results in a lack of sustainability in policies and applications, yet in an environment as fragile as the Arctic mistakes can lead to abrupt and irreversible global catastrophes. The new challenge is not simply to create a survival pathway for indigenous peoples, but to maintain sustainable development and ensure protection of infrastructure, operations, and resources in the Arctic.
