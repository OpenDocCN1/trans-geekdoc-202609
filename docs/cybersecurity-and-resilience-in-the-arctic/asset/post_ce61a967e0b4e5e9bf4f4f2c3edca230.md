
## 1.2.1. Transit Traffic

The purpose of transit traffic is for ships to navigate the Arctic for the sole effort of linking Southern markets with one another. Increases in operations costs by the complexities of Arctic routes porte the potential to offset the advantages that would otherwise be gained from the raw savings in distance. Bulk shipping – that of ores, grains, and petroleum products loaded into cargo holds and tanks – is based on the demand of a specific amount from point A to point B. These shipments are managed as on-off operations, making them more suitable for seasonal shipments [16]. Liner shipping, in contrast, would likely be impacted by the scale of changes in the Arctic, alongside uncertainty transit times, disrupting just-in-time supply-chains which might require greater degrees of reliability.

## 1.2.2. Resources-Based Traffic

Resources-Based traffic is traffic bound specifically in this context for the Arctic in order to load or unload cargo and from the region. The Arctic contains vast of mineral resources, including lead and nickel. Both lead and zinc have been shipped every summer from the mid-70s to the 2000s, nickel has been shipped year-round [17]. For these shipments, no alternative routing is available; the cargo must be shipped from Arctic locations to demanding it across the rest of the world. Hence, large technical requirements are needed to decide what routes are most viable. The rapidly changing climate and environment of the Arctic are having a direct impact on costs; ice-free summer windows have allowed for non-ice-strengthened ships to be used, requiring no ice-breaker fleets either. Furthermore, the number of resources that can be shipped from the vast expanses in the Arctic are currently limited by the number of ships that can traverse through Arctic conditions; changing conditions are likely to only increase the number of ships that can be used to transport these resources but also increase the length of the shipping season, allowing for more profitability.

## 1.2.3. Re-supply traffic

The need for re-supply traffic is derived from that of local communities. Food and lighter goods can be brought by aircraft, but building materials, vehicles and heavier equipment are only be transported via ships. This traffic is also mitigated by the availability of local ports, and furthermore the overall demand for these resources, which will likely be shaped more by the socio-economic development of Arctic communities under changing climatic conditions.

A key question surrounding the Arctic and its large resource stores is whether development in the Arctic will be driven by oil and gas production. ## 1.3.1. Who's drilling?

The world's first oil field, Chibisukoye, was discovered in the Republic of Komi, in 1930. Development to retrieve this oil started in the same year, and two years later in 1932 the large Yarga oil field was discovered [18]. Drilling in the Arctic has hence been around for a less than a century, yet onshore areas in the region have already been explored, with some 40 billion barrels of oil (BBO), over 1,000 cubic feet (TCF) of natural gas, and 8 billion barrels of gas liquids have been developed (as of 2009), primarily in the Western Siberian Basin of Russia and on the North Slope of Alaska [19].

In the last decade, drilling in the Arctic has been slowing due to the shale-oil and gas revolution, which has largely reduced the immediate impact of Arctic fuels. Russian-owned "Gazprom" abandoned the Shtokman field in the Barents Sea in August 2012, one of the largest offshore Arctic projects, due to spiraling costs.

Drilling into the Arctic sea-bed is more expensive when compared to the same activity in lower latitudes; specialized equipment is necessary for operating in freezing temperatures, large supplies of parts are required to ensure greater reliability and furthermore require higher salaries [20]. Some economists estimate that oil would need to be selling for more than $70 per barrel for companies to begin even when Arctic drilling [21]. Private companies such as Shell have paused exploratory drilling in the Arctic, more notably Chukchi Sea, since suffering major setbacks in 2012. In September 2015, tests showed disappointing results and abandoned Arctic drilling and have not conducted operations in the Arctic [22]. Other companies continue to explore oil resources in the Arctic, including Chevron, ExxonMobil, Cairn Energy, BP, Statoil, Caelus Energy Alaska, Rosneft, Eni S.P.A., and ConocoPhillips Alaska [23][24][25].

The extraction of resources from the Arctic will be largely dependent on the economic landscape of the future, and whether the Arctic presents a favorable alternative will depend on energy prices from different sources. Canada, America and Denmark have no active exploration sites [27]. However, there is some activity in Norwegian and Russian waters. Russia is still actively drilling for oil, yet the impacts of economic sanctions imposed by the west restricted access to capital and offshore technology that more efficiently enabled these efforts. Sanctions combined with a declining price of oil globally has made Arctic drilling largely unprofitable, with several projects in the Russian Arctic expected to be delayed for the foreseeable future [28].

## 1.3.2. Demand, development and activity

Predicting future oil and gas production levels of the Arctic is likely to always be difficult. These predictions are based on a complex set of variables, each of which is often unpredictable themselves.

Firstly, the environment has proved to be extremely difficult to operate in. With the nature of the Arctic, changes in its environment can trigger feedback loops, impacting the rate at which they occur. For example, rising levels of CO2 enhance the greenhouse effect, this rise in temperature then thaws layers of permafrost which had previously trapped CO2 and Methane, releasing more into the atmosphere and further enhancing the greenhouse effect, in continuing the increase in temperatures [29]. ## 1.4. Infrastructure

The development of infrastructure in the Arctic including roads, ports, railways, airports and telecommunications has been driven by resource development, although it should be noted that not infrastructure also has a role in providing and creating nautical charts, and electronic aids to navigation. Today, it is the socio-economic development of Arctic communities, combined with the needs of the state and the increasing feasibility of business activity in the Arctic that will the key drivers for investments in the Arctic. The recent growth in infrastructure development are no small or minor trend, with $300 billion in projects completed, in motion or proposed in Russia above [32]. It is also here that the Russian Federation emerges as the clear leader in Arctic infrastructure, followed by Finland, Norway, Canada and the United States. Each has proposed a significant infrastructure investment within their own Arctic territories.
