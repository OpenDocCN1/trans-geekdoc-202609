There is sparse research concerning cybersecurity issues in the Arctic. Cybercrime and espionage are on the rise, targeting both state and non-state actors. and terrorism threaten international peace and security as well as the economic well-being of nations down to targeted attacks. Industries related to critical infrastructure (CI) are particularly at risk for cyberattacks.[114]. However, the definition and classification of critical infrastructure varies globally. [115], [116] For example, in the United States, critical infrastructure is divided into 16 sectors: chemical, commercial facilities, communication, manufacturing, defense industrial bases, emergency services, financial services, food and agriculture, government facilities, healthcare and public health, information technology, nuclear, transportation, and waste systems.[117] Broadly, critical infrastructure assets are so valued to a nation that their destruction would incapacitate national security, economic, public health, and overall safety. Energy systems (i.e., energy grids) are adopting frequent cyberattacks that have devastating consequences.[118]–[120] In the Arctic, rapid, and no-analogue changes in the climate amplify the consequences of attacks or disruptions to critical infrastructure located in remote regions.[121] Critical infrastructure is instrumental to industries such as oil, natural gas, and mining.[122] Critical infrastructure in the high north is not necessarily exceptionally critical infrastructure conditions (ECIC).[122] Arctic may have unique characteristics: cascading effects,[123]–[126] ambiguous links and hazy institutional accountability,[127], [128] and life or death consequences Serious changes can fundamentally alter the Arctic ecosystem, causing a system shift or shift.[131] Recovery times are longer due to the isolated, remote nature of the Arctic. The weather, ecosystem, and threats rapidly change, making it difficult to predict outcomes and consequences. Moreover, this uncertainty and unpredictability make it hard to establish adequate legal and governance instruments to ensure proper mitigation and recovery from a system disruption. Currently, risk-based approaches are effectively used to protect and exceptionally critical infrastructure. Hybrid threats come with unknowns and uncertainties that make it difficult for traditional risk-based approaches to identify and quantify. Cyberattacks are easily carried out from any part of the world and difficult to detect and predict. In the Arctic critical infrastructure is controlled by state and non-state actors.
