# Quantitative Modeling of Supply Chain Resilience: Literature Review

Avi Chaim MERSKY a, Maureen GOLAN b, and Igor LINKOVA c,1
a US Army Engineer Researcher and Development Center
b US Army Corps of Engineers, Contractor
c1Corresponding author, Igor.Linkov@usace.army.mil

**Abstract.** Ensuring a reliable and efficient supply chain is important to an organization's mission. Disruptions to the supply chain can harm organizational performance, and improving resilience can minimize such harm. This paper conducts a bibliometric and thematic review of supply chain resilience focused on modeling; supply chains where they operate. Resilience is defined based on system evolution (plan, absorb, adapt) in response to changes, effects on components of the supply chain sub-systems (production, transportation, command and control). The results show that most papers investigated either all phases of resilience for incomplete supply chains or components of resilience for whole chains, but not both. This paper argues that improving supply chain resilience requires looking more than a set of locations and spots in production, but also requires a system representation of the multiple networks and decision tools that determine how goods travels between locations.

## 1. Introduction

As globalization increases, the performance of supply chains becomes more critical to businesses, organizations, and communication routes opening in the Arctic and sub-Arctic further. New trade catalyze globalization, allowing organizations to increase the distance between suppliers and the number of suppliers for the goods and services they require to function. This turns up the supply chain to additional vulnerabilities. Within Arctic latitudes, such disruptions can include, among others, environmental and climatological disruption, cyberattack, and infrastructure strain and collapse (Verny 2015; Milaković 2014), the use of Arctic and sub-Arctic adds complexity to the existing surrounding supply chain management and development, forcing planners and decision makers to proactively address threats and vulnerabilities to their systems.

A risk-based approach to protecting supply chains is no longer effective. It is commonly understood that a resilience-based approach is necessary to ensure supply chain management best practices. Unfortunately, this report shows that there are significant gaps in the literature regarding supply chain resilience, mostly with respect to a failure to apply a systems-based approach to quantifying, analyzing, and improving supply-chain resilience.

This paper will give a brief overview of the terms and definitions requisite for this discussion before embarking on an in-depth review. This review aims to frame and categorize the current state of the field of supply chain resilience, as well as to illustrate cases in the literature which would benefit from additional research. This study should be helpful to both scholars of supply chain resilience and to managers and organizations looking to improve the resilience of their supply chain.

It is important to start by defining one of the key operational concepts, the supply chain. According to the definition as set forth by Beamon, the supply chain is an "integrated process wherein a number of businesses work together in an effort to: (1) acquire raw materials, (2) convert these raw materials into specified products, and (3) deliver these final products to retailers" (Beamon 1998). A supply chain can alternatively be defined as "the network of companies involved in the upstream and downstream flows of products, services, finances, and information from the initial supply to the customer" (Petti et al. 2010; 1992; Lambert et al. 2005; Mentzer et al. 2001). Both definitions emphasize the complex and layered structure of supply chain networks and how a supply chain can be constructed merely by the direct connections of one on the process or relation chain.

Increasing complex supply chains may face a diverse array of disruptions, which have the potential to harm performance, especially in large-scale infrastructure projects. Global supply chains have faced disruptions from natural disasters, water, and terrorism, as well as business and economic shocks (Peck and Christopher 2004; Knemeyer et al. 2009; Goldsby and Rao 2009; Petti et al. 2010; Dolgui et al. 2018). As discussed above, one approach to managing these disruptions is to minimize the likelihood that they occur and harden the system to make it less vulnerable, i.e., the risk-management based approach. Historically, supply chain management using highly deterministic optimization, discrete scenarios analysis, and stochastic optimization, on various visiting location, location, and cetera (Klose and Drex 2005; Klib and Martel 2012; Kouvelis and Yu 1997). This approach can lead to supply chains that are strong against frequent and predictable events, but often leads to supply chains that are brittle against low probability and high consequence disasters (Klib et al. 2010; J. Wang et al. 2016). Risk management strategies can fail when faced with unpredictable or unpredictable scenarios (Hochstein et al. 2015; Klib et al. 2010).

An alternative is a resilience-based approach, which views some disruptions as inevitable and unpredictable (Merky & Trump 2020). The National Academy of Sciences defines resilience, for systems as "the ability to prepare, recover, and successfully adapt to adverse events" (National Research Council 2012). This four-stage definition is increasingly used by organizations and governments (National Research Council 2012; Larkin et al. 2015; Linkov and Oliveira 2017).

A complete supply chain is rarely within a single location nor controlled by a single decision maker. Decisions about how to run a supply chain, how to produce, and how to move goods are made by a network of the people who run the companies and organizations involved, called a Command and Control (C2) network. This includes the network of individuals making decisions and authorizing actions within a single company, from executives to line managers, but also those making decisions for that company's suppliers, shippers, et cetera. The C2 describes the informational flows allowing decision making and actions.

Overall, a system of networks, rather than just one, is necessary to model a supply chain. The supply chain's resilience can be assessed as a function of multiple interdependent networks, as shown in Figure 1. For example, transportation networks are generally run separately from the supply chain, with companies using public roads, rivers, and rail networks, maintained and used by actors outside the supply chain with their own competing interests. The organization controlling the supply chain may have limited influence on how the transportation network, and its components, respond to disruptive events, limiting the supply chain's ability to recover.

Unfortunately, as discussed in the Results and Discussion section, most of the recent literature fails to consider the above methods and findings in examining supply chain resilience. Particularly critical is the systems-view of supply chains, i.e., that the supply chain consists of a system of networks.
