In the Arctic, indigenous knowledge, which had been acquired through the sharing of personal coping and adaptive strategies, reinforced traditional methods of survivability and strengthened adaptive behaviors among the broader socio-ecological group. In this world, individual-specific resilience measures became aligned with place-based adaptive practices for reflectivity to empower collective resilience (Goldstein, 2012).

The human quality of conscious self-transcendence—the shaping and re-shaping of self—as an outcome of independent or collective action (Frankl, 1992), supported by feedback which is both conscious and unconscious, leads to tacit knowing within a given SES (Archer, 2003). In the Arctic areas, it is vital for vulnerable people to have the opportunity to nurture their adaptive capacity (Berkes & Ross, 2013; Davidson, 2010). Even in more connected circumstances, history reflects situations in which people with relevant and important issues but low levels of agency are out of analysis and planning processes, which further marginalizes that. As individuals become victims of the externally-imposed community-based adaptation projects, the result is lost opportunities for them to become agents of day adaptive practices and to control realise efforts (Bruijn, van Dijk, & Foeken, 2001; Soeters, 2016). The inherent gaps in power Arctic stakeholder groups normally inhibits collective agency and especially opportunities to cultivate shared meaning. This reinforces the importance of collaborative efforts based on some level of shared meaning, promoting collective action in Arctic communities.

Research indicates that decisions and behaviors that greatly impact capacity depend upon the phenomenon of shared-making, and such reinforcing factors as individual agency and the dynamics of voice. The practices of meaning-making serve as the discourse or interactions which define the salient aspects of setting and context, resulting in shared understanding individual beliefs and attitudes related to common norms (Stahl, 2003). The strength of the collaborative group relies relies on every member having a voice. Additionally, the level of honest communication and trust one perceives within that group, will determine the type and effectiveness of the actions, and whether collective agency can be achieved (Donnellon, Gray, & Bougon, 1986; Salvia & Quanta, 2017).
