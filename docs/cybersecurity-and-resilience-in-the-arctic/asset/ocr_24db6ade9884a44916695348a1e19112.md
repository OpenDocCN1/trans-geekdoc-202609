Permanent Participants (PPs) that six indigenous organizations hold in the Arctic Council. The status ensures that the indigenous representatives take part in virtually all discussions taking place in the Council, although the decisions are made through the consensus of the eight Arctic states. The role of Indigenous Peoples in other venues of Arctic cooperation – such as the Bears Euro-Arctic Council -- while not matching the PP status, also stands in a global competitive context.

However, it is at the national and local level, in several Arctic states, where Indigenous Peoples are especially important actors within governance frameworks. In North America, land-claims agreements have led to the creation of elaborate governance structures for co-management of lands, waters, and resources. Involving indigenous actors in market mechanisms has been a significant element of these processes, through the creation of native charters and drawing clear ownership parchways. In Greenland, Finland, Norway, and Sweden, the Sami are represented by directly elected Saami parliaments. The parliaments are primarily consultative bodies for national and local decision-making but are also equipped with limited decision-making powers, especially in the areas of education, language, and culture.

Business actors are involved in Arctic cooperation over various sets of industry concerns (e.g. oil and gas producers), and more recently through the Arctic Economic Council (AEC). The AEC was established as a fully Arctic Council of businesses from Arctic states. The AEC has been carrying out various assessments (related to broadband connectivity or investment needs), providing space for networking, and bringing the private sector’s voice more strongly into the discussions taking place within the Arctic Council. Independence of the AEC, businesses operating in the Arctic operate best practices. The Arctic Investment Platform drafted under the auspices of the World Economic Forum is here a case in point.

The specific rules adopted by some form a vital part of Arctic governance and the normative framework. This is not surprising considering the high level of responsibility that lies on businesses operating in remote locations characterized by elevated risk levels, as well as the fact that regulations are often slow to follow the expansion of activities. The self-regulation of the Arctic industry is a good example of these dynamics. Market forces relate to insurance companies’ requirements and reputational considerations constitute important pseudo-sanction mechanisms. Often, companies prefer self-regulation over governmental or international action. However, industry self-regulation is often criticized regarding its level of ambition and effectiveness.

# 4 Conclusions and outlook

International cooperation has long been the default position for Arctic governance. This continues to be the case as interest in the region grows and new actors enter the stage. The emergence of new actors and political conflicts could upset the Arctic in the future threaten to strain the constraint of Arctic exceptionalism, i.e., the willingness to cooperate across political divides. For the time being, though, the Arctic remains a space of cooperation rather than one of confrontation. Clear rules and their acceptance by all actors with a stake in the Arctic contribute to this spirit of cooperation.
