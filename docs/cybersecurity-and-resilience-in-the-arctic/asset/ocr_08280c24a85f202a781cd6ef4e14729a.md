# 2.2.1. Threats to Ship Cyber Systems

The capability to disrupt or destroy key USN and NATO cyber systems already exists; it is just a matter of time before a successful attack is carried out. An example from the private sector illustrates the ease with which a major ship system can be disrupted. In 2013, a group of researchers from U.T. Austin successfullyed the GPS of an $80 million yacht with a custom-made device, spoofing the 213-foot ship hundreds of meters off course with the crew or the ship's hundreds of meters off course [71].

Imagine a bad actor -- whether terrorist group, lone wolf, or foreign government -- using a similar strategy. Military ships could be easily hijacked without detection, resulting in catastrophic effects. The U.S. Navy uses encrypted systems on its battle force ships and maritime vessels, which makes them harder to spoof GPS systems on civilian vessels [72]. However, even this encrypted GPS can be spoofed by sophisticated actors given inherent vulnerabilities.

Furthermore, as over 90 percent of DoD deployment and distribution transactions are handled on unclassified systems [73], it can be assumed that the same percentage holds for the Navy in particular. The DoD Arctic Strategy charges the Navy's 2nd Fleet with 'responsibility for ensuring the readiness of, and dynamically employing, maritime forces in the Arctic, and the ability to fight across multiple domains, thereby defeating aggression' [16]. This is a major element of the Navy's and DoD's -- strategy (including incorporation of the new Multi-Domain Operations doctrine, to become increasingly important to the Joint Force [74], [75]). Spoofed GPS systems could severely impair the Navy's ability to transport troops and material, to efficiently and dynamically employ forces, and to fight across multiple domains. At best, spoofed by GPS systems could be routed off-course and significantly delayed, costing valuable time and money. At worst, they could be tricked into crashing with other ships or natural hazards (such as ice floes) or entering enemy territory.

In recent years, the accelerating rate of technological development has dramatically altered the ways in which ships communicate and operate. Modern ships are equipped with "zens of microprocessors running over 100 million parameters of control software," and ship infrastructure is now "a mixture of electromechanical components and highly integrated hardware and software forming a set of computer networks" [76], [77].
