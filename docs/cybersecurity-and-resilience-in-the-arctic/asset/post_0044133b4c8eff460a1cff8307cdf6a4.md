
# Article 115 of UNCLOS highlights the extent of the flag state in specific activities which can cause damage to cables; flag states have the duty of ensuring that the owners of cables provide compensation for ships which suffer damage in an attempt to prevent damage to a cable [6] [8]. If, for example, the master of the aforementioned vessel Real had chosen not to cut the cable but instead to abandon the ship or otherwise risk damage to the ship for the purpose of preventing damage to the cable, the flag state of the ship which laid the cable would have been under an obligation to provide compensation for the damage suffered by the Real. In light of the significant economic importance of cables, this might provide an additional level of economic safety for cases in which harm to a cable can be prevented at the cost of some damage to a vessel but without endangering the vessel and the health of those on board. For flag states of cables laying vessels this however may lead to an additional administrative burden, in cases in which the owner is of a different nationality than the flag state of the cable-laying vessel and does not have assets in the flag state, which in turns enhances the importance of the maritime industry for cases. The duty contained in UNCLOS Article 115 can be traced back to Article 29 of the High Seas Convention [29] and Article 7 (1) of the 1884 Convention for the Protection of Submarine Cables [30] [8]. It is to be noted, however, that it will be difficult in practice to provide sufficient evidence to support a claim against the owner of the cable.

Individuals who are affected by an intentional interference with subsea cables will seek redress in domestic legal systems: UNCLOS Article 113 [8] obliges states to create norms in domestic criminal law [30] which aim at protecting cables. The occurrence of damage in the absence of domestic legislative measures are which by Article 113 would not have legal consequences for the offending vessel or the owner of the damaged cable [30]; however the failure to enact the required domestic legislation constitutes a violation of public international law by the state in [30]. UNCLOS does not provide a direct basis for compensation for non-state cable owners, nor for individual users. The practical question will be whether national courts are able to provide justice, in particular in cases of intentional interference by foreign agents as part of a campaign of hybrid warfare.

## 4 Conclusions and outlook

When it comes to subsea cables, international law is still stuck in the old, state-centered paradigm. But the Westphalian approach to international law remains at the core of 21st century international law requires new solutions which are not adequately provided by the lex lata – at least on the international level. It appears surprisingly that especially large corporations, which operate globally and have the technical need, competence, and financial ability to engage in the laying of intercontinental subsea cables, should be content with their Westphalian-era approach to the nation state in order to protect their rights internationally. While it must be noted there are non-public discussions between corporations and states in this regard, the problem of intentional or unintentional damages to subsea cables is hardly new.

What is the need to corporations, states and individual users depend on the functioning of subsea cables and the significance a disruption can have only in economic terms but also in terms of interpersonal relationships and public health. Especially in remote and sparsely settled parts of the world, such as the Arctic, connectivity is an essential good. The connectedness of devices and the reliance on electric communication to provide medical services are just some examples which illustrate the dependence of Arctic populations on a functioning internet, including intercontinental subsea cables.

Effective protection of subsea cables is therefore in the interest of states, corporations and individual users. One explanation for the relative silence of global corporations involved in cable-laying is the possibility that the likelihood of actual interference is thought to be low. This might be explained by the relative invisibility of subsea cables. However, the technology to interfere with cables which are located even far from the coast has been around for decades and could be deployed, at least by the armed forces of a few nations, and short international. International law as it is today is limited in its ability to prevent interference and to provide adequate legal protection for cable owners and end users. The lack of concerted efforts indicates this understanding of the existing technical possibilities and the possibility of attacks on subsea cables as of a hybrid conflict. As interest in subsea cables is growing in the Arctic, this discrepancy between an already existing risk and international legal standards which were created long before the internet gained its practical significance has for billions of people around the world, including the residents of the Arctic, has to be taken into account by Arctic nations.
