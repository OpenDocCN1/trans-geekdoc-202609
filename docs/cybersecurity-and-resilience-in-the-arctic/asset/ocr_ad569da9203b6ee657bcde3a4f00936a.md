This Agreement was signed on October 30, 2013, as a proactive treaty and potentially a motor for a large-scale commercial fishing in the Central Arctic Ocean for a duration that scientific evidence on the fish stocks is not readily available. The treaty offers a regulatory model for the governance of sector-specific regimes in the Arctic in an area beyond national jurisdiction in the light of high level of scientific certainty. The treaty is applicable to those of the signing countries whose fishes are likely to move further North for the exercise of commercial fishing in the central Arctic Ocean in the near future.

While it is in the marine Arctic where international law plays a particularly important role, there are numerous instruments that are relevant for the Arctic terrestrial environment and its peoples. The UN Framework Convention for Climate changes climate in the Arctic. The role of the Arctic region in the global climate regime has become further pronounced following the Paris Agreement due to a greater focus on adaptation, including the requirement for national reporting adaptation actions. Arctic research is an important element of assessment carried out by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. Recently, the increasing international attention to black carbon and other short-term climate pollutants manifested by activities within the Arctic Council. This focus on the problem of black carbon, which is a significant climate forcer in the Arctic, also resulted in the emergence of global networks dedicated to this, the most well-known which is the Climate and Clean Air Coalition.

Of visible relevance for Arctic governance are global instruments such as the Convention on Biological Diversity, the RAMSAR Convention on Wetlands, and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES). International agreements dealing with long-range pollution, while having limited influence on activities taking place within the Arctic itself, are of critical importance for the Arctic environment as many of these chemicals are transported by wind and ocean patterns into the Arctic. Relevant instruments include the 2001 Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants, the 2014 Minamata Convention on Mercury and the protocols under the UNECE Convention on Long-Range Transport of Air Pollutants.

The international instruments codifying Indigenous Peoples’ rights are also an important part of the global yet Arctic-relevant legal framework. Two central documents are the 1989 ILO Convention 169 on the Rights of Indigenous and Tribal Peoples as well as the 2007 UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.
