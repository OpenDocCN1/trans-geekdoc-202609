## 3.2. Cyber-resilience

Resilience as a descriptor in a plethora of fields. Colloquially, resilience has multiple meanings from elasticity to toughness. Resilience is both a philosophy and a methodological practice. The National Academy of Sciences defines resilience as the ability to prepare for, absorb, recover from, and adapt to events. While modern applications of resilience focus on analyzing how systems bounce back from disruptions. The emphasis is on the role of recovery after a disruption to a system, as well as absorption of a threat to ensure survival. Not all threats are preventable. Most are mitigable. Despite comprehensive preparation and planning, it is impossible to prevent all threats. An unexpected disruption will always occur. Therefore, resilience is a way to handle systems that are unknown or have uncertainties. Resilience can complement traditional risk-based approaches. Methodologically, it can assess how a system performs and functions in various scenarios regardless of type of threat.[97] Resilience analysis is built upon traditional risk assessment but minimizes the long-term damage of external shocks to system efficiency and functionality.[97] Moreover, resilience allows actors to manage system, cascading effects where one disruption can trigger connected systems, as well as, probability threats with catastrophic consequences.[97, 159] Resilience tries to provide the foundation for a ‘soft landing’ in order to reduce both harshly and quickly efficiently improve system rebound to full functionality.[97] Thus, system resilience is an adaptive process where system functions are constantly adjusting to deal with threats.

These same theories and methodological approaches can be applied to the cyberspace. As national security and critical infrastructure grows more dependent on information systems and technologies, their vulnerability to cyber threats increases.[99] Cyber threats are often unpredictable and can have ruinous consequences. governance of cyber-security is not trivial. The easiest comparison is to environmentalism as both cyber space and the environment fall under commons,[100] Regrettably, the commons is difficult to enforce and subject to overuse. While environmental overuse is visible overfishing, massive deforestation, cyberspace is difficult to see i.e. information pollution, stranded bandwidth),[100], [101] Yet, cyberspace is more than simple information or computer networks.[102], [103] Cyberspace is directly connected to people, infrastructure, financial systems, transportation, information, and even healthcare. Cyber-resilience refers to the ability to continuously deliver the intended outcome adverse cyber events.[104] When approaching cybersecurity risks, a multi-scale perspective is necessary to take into account the complex nature of cyber threats.[105] Decision-makers may need the ability to plan for cyber threats in order to absorb, recover; physical, information, cognitive, and social—in order to adapt them.[106] Strong cyber-resilience accounts for four keys—to establish robust cybersecurity systems.[107] Linkov et al (2013) developed a cyber resilience matrix that accounts for the stages of resilience (plan and prepare, absorb, recover, adapt) as the previously mentioned system domains (physical, information, cognitive, social).[106] Resilience is a network property of the system. But few existing laws and policies account for systems as multi-domain networks do not establish a robust governance framework for cyber-resilience.

## 3.3. Unique Arctic Challenges

While may of the cybersecurity issues discussed above remain a concern, cybersecurity is different in the Arctic in critical ways. The consequences of failures can be more severe, and with cascading effects. The physical environment is unique—harsh, isolated, and unpredictable. There are more direct and political reliance on digital services and technology among those who rely stable connectivity within and across the region. Access to broadband and high-speed internet is growing, especially in the U.S. and European High North ([16]). For example, many reindeer herders rely on GPS to help navigate and hide. Additionally, Arctic inhabitants rely on social media for their “social” interactions with others due to the remoteness of their cities. Social media is also used to strengthen communities by enabling the political empowerment of marginalized communities and spread knowledge. Indigenous textile and design artists have created strong online networks through blogs and forums, sharing and claiming ownership of their traditional culture with each other and the rest of the world. Digitization of the Arctic appears invitable, with an increasing number of new and emergent vulnerabilities for cyber security.[109], [110] Unfortunately, the effects of new technology on cybersecurity are often uncertain. It is difficult to maintain local expertise in rural areas to solve or fix problems if an issue arise, putting rural communities increasingly at risk as they become reliant on technology.
