Stevens (2015) views cyber security through the various responses to perceived risks and threats of information and communication technologies and the achievement of freedom from risks and threats to the infrastructures which societies and technologies rely on. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU), the United Nations agency for information and communication technologies, have a development center (ITU-D) which fosters international cooperation and solidarity in the delivery of technical assistance and in the creation, development, and improvement of telecommunication and ICT equipment and networks in developing countries (ITU 2019). One of the areas of action of the ITU-D is cyber security. In the context of this paper, cyber security is defined as “the collection of tools, policies, guidelines, risk management approaches, actions, trainings, best practices, assurance and technologies that can be used to protect the availability, integrity and confidentiality of assets in the connected infrastructures pertaining to government, private organizations and citizens; these assets include computing devices, personnel, infrastructure, applications, telecommunications systems, and data in the “cyber-environment” (ITU et al. 2018).

Cyber security is a broad concept and it is useful to put it in the context of this Arctic critical infrastructures. There is no universally recognized definition of critical infrastructures. However, some descriptions and definitions can be found in literature. The European Union set up the Programme for Critical Infrastructure Protection (EPCIP), which aims at improving the protection of critical infrastructures in the EU (European Commission 2006). The EPCIP defines critical infrastructure as an asset or system which is essential for the maintenance of vital societal functions. The EU to developing a National Cybersecurity Strategy describe critical infrastructure as assets that are essential to functioning, security, and economy in nations (ITU et al. 2018). Dunn (2007) describe critical infrastructures as vital points “fundamental to the national security and the essential functioning of industrialized societies as a whole, and not necessarily to the military in particular.” Which sectors vary between countries; governments need to consider which entities and services to include based on national risk assessments. A few non-exhaustive examples are energy (electricity, oil, and gas), finance and banking (credit institutions, trading venues, and central counterparties), and healthcare (healthcare organizations, hospitals and private clinics).

The security of critical infrastructures has been part of national security for decades (Klitou 2014). With an increasingly smart and connected world where critical infrastructure is connected and often dependent on the internet, the protection of critical infrastructure becomes a priority for many countries in recent years (Klitou 2014). Cyber security measures and procedures are thus one of the significant means to protect critical infrastructures. The European Union Agency for Network and Information Security (ENISA) stated in one of its latest publications (ENISA 2018): “cybersecurity must be a shared responsibility and all the actors should facilitate a secure and resilient digital world. However, to achieve that, it is essential to have a thorough understanding of related risks and threats, as well as secure and protect against them.” Clark and Hakim (2017) note the increase in both number and sophistication of cyber attacks against critical infrastructure as infrastructure becomes more reliant on internet and software technology.

Characterizing cyber threats has been a subject of research. Jouini et al. (2014) propose a classification for security threats in systems. They consider criteria: source of threat, agents (human, environmental), technological, motivation, intention, impacts. The impacts are further classified into different levels of destruction, corruption, or disclosure of information, then denial of service, elevation of privilege, or illegal usage. However, in a later publication (Jouini and Arfa Rabai 2016) the same authors note that this classification is limited, especially when attacks against multiple systems are coordinated and synchronized, such as physical attack against infrastructure coupled with hacking and spread of misinformation. These types of threats have been described as hybrid threats (The European Centre of Excellence for Countering Hybrid Threats 2017; Linkov et al., 2019).

Resilience, in an engineering context in particular, has been defined as the ability of a system to "plan and prepare for, absorb, respond to, and recover from disasters and adapt to new conditions" (National Research Council 2012). Many other definitions have been proposed, but most agree on resilient systems having the qualities of reacting and adapting to disruptions (Linkov and Trung 2019; Haimes 2009; Park et al. 2013; Ayyub 2014; Thorisson et al. 2017). This has been contrasted with traditional risk analysis approaches, focus on hardening the system against disruptions which lack the component of recovery and adaptation. Thiele (2016) emphasizes the need for resilient systems against hybrid threats, including cyber threats. The diversity of hybrid threats, including attacks by militaries, parallels, criminal enterprises, and various hackers can be found in Linkov et al., (2020), which argue that the nature of 21st century cyber warfare must be coupled with a resilience-oriented strategy to optimize rapid identification of cyber threats as well as expeditious recovery to reduce potential losses associated with digital system disruption. Decisionmaking across multiple sectors and hierarchies must cross-link to the exploration of diverse vulnerabilities (Merad and Trump 2020). The authors identify four areas to enhance resilience: identification of key vulnerabilities, synchronization of cross-governmental decision making, military sustainability and civil preparedness, and balancing resource allocations.

Modeling of interdependencies of critical infrastructure has received considerable attention in the literature. Wu et al. (2016) include physical and geographic interdependencies to model cascading failures in an infrastructure system under terrorist attacks. Ouyang (2016) identifies vulnerabilities and critical locations in a power and gas system that is attacked at defined nodes. Heraceous et al. (2017) provide an overview of several methods, including input-output models, agent-based modeling, and network models, in addition to proposing an approach using hybrid automata that can combine discrete events and continuous time. The network model seems to be the most common approach and has been used to evaluate the impacts of attacks on critical infrastructure. Nan and Sansavini (2017) quantify system resilience and introduce a hybrid modeling approach for decomposing the infrastructure network into components and defining their interactions.

## 3. Perspectives of Cyber Security Challenges

The security of Arctic critical infrastructure needs to be addressed through technological, organizational, and human and societal advancements. This section discusses challenges and potential pathways to increase system resilience.

### 3.1. Technological Challenges

Traditional infrastructure in the Arctic regions faces challenges due to degrading permafrost, coastal erosion, flooding, and other factors (Hjort et al. 2018; Melvin al. 2017; Doré, Niu, and Brooks 2016; Ford, Bell, and St-Hilaire-Gravel 2016). Yet the rapidly changing conditions provide new opportunities in information and communication technology, such as data centers, and transportation. Due to low population density and vast distances between various populations and civilian installations, remote sensing can create efficiencies. Environmental factors such as fog, rain, and extended periods of darkness during winter can create challenges against these technologies. Proximity to the magnetic pole makes GPS more sensitive. Databases on sea ice (Walsh et al. 2017) are important for the navigation of commercial, military, and tourist vessels; those have been developed to assess operational limitations different scenarios (Stollard et al. 2018). Remote sensing plays an important role in monitoring and understanding human activities in the Arctic (Eksen and Olsen 2018). Innovative methods to overcome the technological limitations of satellite, radar, and other technologies include citizen science and data collection, in particular contributions from inhabitants of Arctic (Raymond-Yakoubian 2018), as well as integration of open source data using search engines (Li 2018); Li, Wang, and Bhatia 2016).

In order to find and define threat and patterns for various layers of infrastructure, the characteristic of the current infrastructure in Arctic region be viewed realistically: where people live, how type of buildings are used in the region, where the cities are located, which kind of communications systems are used, what transportation options are available, etc. (Hummelholm 2019). There is also a need to go through cases when defining requirements for future infrastructures and information and communication systems. From the use cases, system requirements can be defined, and further dependencies can be identified by system components that can be targets or vulnerable to cyber threats. With these cases, the different types of system architectures required for different services can be defined. These architectures include an Arctic with infrastructure architectures, integrations architectures, security and cybersecurity architectures, and smart device architectures. Once the Arctic architectures are defined, dependencies, risks, vulnerabilities, and cyber threats can be explored. In addition, different types of threats such as natural events, accidents, terrorists, and cyber attacks must be taken into account.

In order to provide services to the right place and at the right time over such a large area, it is useful to group the Arctic regions' services in the different segments where the current structures and services can be located. Future Arctic regions' structures can be divided into different service sectors: infrastructure, energy, buildings and mobility, communications, public and private services (Hummelholm 2018).
