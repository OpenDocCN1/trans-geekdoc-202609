Container shipping is traditionally reliant "just-in-time" supply chains which require precise schedules, and the Arctic poses many challenges to these. A more suitable fit for these conditions is bulk cargo, where tight schedules are less of an issue. The NEP has typically been utilized for this type of transit, with the reduced distance of travel allowed for slower, cost-effective travel which has also reduced the volume of carbon emissions into the environment. These routes can't necessarily guaranteed to be cheaper however; "ice-breakers" are often needed, with the implication being potential increases of $400,000+ in operational costs [9]. Russia has the largest icebreaker and the only nuclear-powered icebreakers in the world and have used this advantage to increase prices for other countries and competitors to use them. Fears also exist that Russia could hold their fleet, enticing states to fund their own icebreaker ships.

Activity through these routes, in the Arctic, has been reported to be decreasing 4.3% every 10 years. However, this number changes when looking at seasonal averages, with an average of 10.7% decrease every 10 years in September, but 2.8% in March [10]. Annual variability is also an important factor. 2007 saw the lowest ice extent on record, the NSR was cut off by a protruding ice floe, highlighting that access to these routes is not necessarily always reduced. The impact of increased shipping should also be taken into account when considering the accessibility of routes in the Arctic, as activity here will have an impact and contribution to changing climatic conditions.

Emissions from ships like black carbon and sulphur are in some ways controversial; they have the potential to cause further warming as they stain and darken ice/snow, reducing the albedo effect and reflecting less solar radiation, or alternatively could have a cooling effect over longer terms as they promote circulation in the region and increase the albedo effect [12].

Arctic shipping accounted for 9.8% of global shipping traffic in 2014 [13], including 5.9% of dry bulk and 4.2% of bulk cargo. It is noted the period in transit through Arctic routes isviable between 4 and 5 months [14], and as trends see ice cover will continue to decrease this period will only grow longer. The main drivers behind the emergence of the NEP will be domestic and destination shipping, with large focus on that of bulk and general cargo [15]. While Arctic shipping has the potential to be of great economic benefit, savings will be felt in some cases more than others.

Taking for the example the southern part of Shanghai in China, the advantages be less substantial than those felt by European destinations, even where the North Western Passage (NWP) presents a faster route than via the Suez [11].
