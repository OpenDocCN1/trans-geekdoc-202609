Individual disciplines, Government agencies, and private sector stakeholders possess their own approach to executing such assessments in a manner that is consistent with statutory requirements as well as social and institutional norms [2]. Risk assessment processes work effectively within an environment of clearly defined hazards and threats to thoroughly understand recipient risks. Uncertainty concerning the interactions with complex inter-connected systems, together with an understanding of both the cost and effectiveness of remediation make the approach of hardening systems ineffective in this type of event.

Such risk assessment becomes less capable of characterizing and prioritizing threats, clarifying system vulnerabilities, or reasonably assessing threat consequences within an environment of high complexity and interconnectivity and between systems – there is not enough data to perform a risk assessment, nor is there sufficient expertise to identify all of the threats that might exploit vulnerabilities and generate cascading impacts to the system and its dependencies [3].

Approaches for Arctic risk assessment and resilience as defined by both civilian and military agencies have been properly informed, focus on quantifying security, reducing uncertainty, and improving mitigation. These solutions can be a cost-effective way in which the components of a system that we have extensive knowledge about be managed. A focus on the constellation of mitigation measures is cost prohibitive. Solutions built in resilience focus on the features allowing a complex, integrated system to recover function following a disruption (see Figure 2). Infrastructural and environmental resilience strategies emphasize the role of recovery and adaptation to disruption, ensuring a system that has the capacity to persist and reacquire its full capabilities regardless of the hazards or events that it

encounters.
