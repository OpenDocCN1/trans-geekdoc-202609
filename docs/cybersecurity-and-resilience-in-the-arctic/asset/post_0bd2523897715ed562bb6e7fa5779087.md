## 3.1.1. The Case for Remote Operations

One specific aspect enabled by connectivity is the capacity to operate remotely. Remote operations allow for the provision of services difficult to provide in a direct capacity physically (e.g., cost, resource availability). Domains of applications are very diverse and include industry, public services, healthcare, and culture. While remote operations are far from novelty, their development and implementation are increasing significantly due to advances in digital capabilities and the overall greater availability of digital infrastructures. The development of such capability is of great relevance to Arctic regions, which combine harsh conditions and access difficulties in vast areas with sparse populations. A specific example is provided by the emerging concept of the Remote Tower in Air Traffic Management (ATM). We will use this example in this section as a way to illustrate the cyber security challenges the Arctic is exposed to when relying on remote access and operations, and discuss directions for increasing resilience.

In the domain of Air Traffic Management (ATM), increased connectivity is seen as one of the main drivers of improvement of operations and building of capacity to handle the traffic increase. The concept of the Remote Tower provides the capacity to manage tower operations remotely based on: (1) a tower equipped with various sensors (especially camera) and (2) controllers accessing this at a remote center, which might be used to control multiple aerodromes (Kearney and Li, 2018). Remote towers increasingly appear as a valuable alternative in the context of various cases, for aerodromes located in remote areas and managing limited amount of traffic. Besides the operational relevance of this capability, one can wonder about the demands and potential risks introduced by necessary reliance on digital infrastructures. What levels of coverage and reliability are required for such capabilities? What happens when operations is not functional? How dangerously dependent on digital infrastructure are the capabilities introduced by remote operations?

Such questions carry particular significance in the face of cyber threat, whether due to intentional attacks or malfunctions. Interruptions or degradations of digital infrastructure and services can impair the capacity to perform Air Traffic Management (ATM) safely or efficiently while Resilience represents the capacity to handle two interrelated, but different, disruptions: (1) the digital services; and (2) operations. In the first case, challenges are associated with the system's capacity to identify, and address the cyber issue. In addition, reestablishing digital services in a timely manner is critical because operations performed in degraded mode risk not being sustainable with regard to safety and costs. In the second case, which constitutes the primary emergency, the system needs to adapt and mitigate the impact on operations (e.g., switch to other modes or divert traffic before returning to sound operations).

The concept of the Remote Tower in ATM is representative of the kind of digital capabilities that allow for the provision of services in Arctic regions and, as a result, for which digitalization and connectivity rapidly progress, the complexity and exposure of networks becomes increasingly impossible to fully manage (Bochman, 2018). In other words, it becomes less and less possible to anticipate and build security barriers for all situations. The main consequence pointed out by experts is that cyber events will occur despite organizations best efforts – it is no longer a question of if, but a question of when. In addition to securing their systems and networks through traditional means, organizations providing or using digital services will need to consider how much capacity they have to handle the two types of disruptions mentioned above: (1) the digital infrastructure itself; (2) operations running on the digital infrastructure.

### 3.1.1.1. Disruptions of the Digital Infrastructure

Branlat et al. (2011) have described the essential challenges of cyber defense. Essentially, defending against cyber events corresponds to two questions: 1) Is the activity on the network legitimate or illegitimate? 2) How suspicious activity be handled? Both questions are deceptively simple and obscure the underlying fundamentals.

Identifying nefarious activity occurring on the network corresponds to a weak signal/noise problem with users and services generating a lot of traffic. As networks expand due to the development of connected services, the network is never entirely, and some legitimate activity might be detected as suspicious. In addition, some organizations live in a context of constant probing for cyber vulnerabilities, most of them being non-critical but participating in the noisy background. To support cyber defenders, automated systems are used to treat the massive amounts of network and systems data. However, in part of the impossibility of fully describing the network and its potential activity, those tools generate significant amounts of false positives and false negatives. Interestingly the investigation of cyber activity is a question that lies at the intersection of independent roles, tasks, and domains of expertise: network data is not only critical data, but need to be correlated with relevant activity on the systems (e.g., changes in configuration file), on databases (e.g., new transactions), or on software. Especially in large organizations and networks, these pieces of information are the responsibility of different teams and individuals. Cyber activity detection can therefore be affected by coordination breakdowns within and across teams, as well as potential conflicts (e.g., the need to maintain support to distributed services vs. the need to limit connections between network assets).

When suspicious activity is detected (which can, in itself, take months), the question of what to do is non-trivial: answering the consideration of trade-offs. If the network and its assets provide valuable services that may be jeopardized by responding closing connectivity, a trade-off between security and production in the organization that benefits from the network services. Furthermore, cyber defenders are typically not the network owners, i.e they need to justify the potentially interrupt connectivity (and related services) on the basis of evidence – this is not a simple task due to the general level of uncertainty mentioned above. This relates to the scope of the responsibility, authority, and autonomy of the network defense teams, issues which should be addressed before events occur. Trade-offs may even arise from different security goals, with situations benefiting from letting the situation develop in order to better understand (and ultimately) address.

Due to all of these elements, and in spite of the undeniable support of technical systems and approaches, cyber security remains a domain that relies heavily on human context-sensitivity, analytical ability, and the unique capacity to balance difference, potentially conflicting goals. Bochman (2018) argues that, as connected systems grow in scale and complexity, organizations’ capacity to provide cyber defense is being overwhelmed. Advocates for strategies that aim at limiting connectivity and complexity as much as possible, i.e. finding a balance between the valuable capabilities created and their exposure to the threat.

### 3.1.1.2. Disruptions of Operations

For critical infrastructure providers, strategies for handling emergency can be inspired by approaches and practices (Branlat et al., 2017), which include the following:

- The capacity to coordinate with each other; as coordination is critical and costly in times of crisis.
- The capacity to adapt in the face of unforeseen events, for which procedures normally follow might prove ineffective.
- The capacity to develop and maintain alternative ways of functioning; when operations depend on a piece of equipment, it is critical to perform if that piece of equipment is not available. Alternative ways of functioning might correspond to old ways, as in paper-based flight strips used in case of radar loss in ATM (Save et al., 2018).Importantly, organizations need to manage their resilience, i.e. to regularly assess and consider these different capabilities in order to improve their ways of operating, coordinating and adapting. This is what produces resilience, e.g., what formal or informal strategies implement successfully when disruptions occur, but also what hinders resilience, e.g., what resources are assumed to be available in emergencies but might not be during actual events.
