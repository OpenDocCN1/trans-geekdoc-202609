For NATO and DoD systems -- including Navy systems -- to incorporate these principles and abilities, they would need to have broad utility, to be able to absorb, recover from, and adapt to disruptions, whether known or unknown, and to be able to degrade gracefully.

Systems designed to be resilient in these ways will strengthen NATO and the U.S. Navy, enabling them to better withstand and cope with unexpected disruptions, whether due to natural disasters or malfunctions, targeted attacks. Given that many disruptions -- of which we can predict, and some of which cannot -- will occur in the harsh Arctic environment, the importance of resilience is especially vital considering the Navy’s role and future in the Arctic.

But what about cost? Perhaps the biggest obstacle to the adoption of resiliency-based norms is the cost, especially the upfront costs, in the process of designing and building resilient systems [157]. However, in the long run, systems which are engineered to be resilient from the very beginning will cost less than those that are not. Given how essential resiliency is for safeguarding our national security, it may be best not to skip on engineering resilient systems. Furthermore, gains have been made regarding how to streamline and improve the process of engineering resilient system such that is more cost-effective. Neches and Madini (2002), for instance, delineate ways to design resilient systems that are also affordable:

...advances in computational technology can potentially transform the system development in new and novel ways to enable fast, efficient, and inexpensive upfront engineering—the key to engineering resilient systems. These, in turn, can enable rapid development, deployment, and operation of affordable adaptable and effective systems. [157]

As Neches (2011), resilient systems are "affordable for fast eng[ineering]" and "less rework," as well as "Adaptable design & test for wider range of mission contexts" [155]. All these characteristics make resilient systems cost-effective. Adaptability is built into resilient systems, such that they do not frequently have to be re-worked, re-designed, or refined. Refilient systems are explicitly designed to be able to adapt and change easily, and effectively. They are sustainable. Additionally, the adaptive nature of resilient systems makes them better equipped for a wider variety of tasks - that is, they have broad utility. Accordingly, a system-of-systems (such as the USN or NATO) which is composed of resilient systems will need to be on fewer highly specialized, inflexible, and high-cost systems or components.
