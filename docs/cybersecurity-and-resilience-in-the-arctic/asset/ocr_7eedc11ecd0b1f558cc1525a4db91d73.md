To establish international cooperation in the far north, the Arctic nations — Canada, Denmark, Iceland, Norway, Sweden, and the U.S. – in 1996 formed the Arctic Council in partnership with native groups and other observers.[8] The Arctic Council promotes sustainable development, environmental protection, and conservation of the culture, traditions, and languages of Indigenous Peoples of the North. While the Arctic Council is specifically not involved with military security, the Arctic has necessitated increased engagement around geopolitical security in the past decade. While development remains a priority for many member nations (e.g., Iceland, Norway, Denmark, Sweden), others Arctic nations prioritize security (e.g., U.S., Finland) and sovereignty (e.g., Canada, Russia). While relations have been handled peacefully thus, these eight nations have differing priorities concerning the Arctic.[9] Care must be taken to ensure that this diplomacy remains intact as the Arctic environment changes in years.

The current rate of physical development in the Arctic shows little signing. Small rural communities are increasingly dependent on new infrastructure and technological improvements to access power and other necessary resources that are often concentrated in southern urban centers.[10] However, the cyber infrastructure required to support Arctic infrastructure creates new questions regarding its governance, maintenance, and effects on local peoples and environment.[11] Defense, transportation, and business opportunities in the Arctic are accompanied by the danger of cyber-threats, especially to critical infrastructures. Such infrastructures are “extra critical” due to the harsh climatic conditions coupled with environmental fragility and remoteness.[11] These conditions are subject to change in the face of global climate change, globalization, and increasing global connectivity. Assessing the intersections between Arctic governance and cybersecurity therefore requires new methodologies of risk and resilience assessment.

Effective governance frameworks are needed to ensure critical infrastructures and the industrial and military activities it support remain effective and adaptive in an era of rapid and uncertain change. For example, laying undersea fiber-optic cable could bring reliable internet connections to Arctic North America, but none has yet undertaken such an endeavor.[12] Questions surrounding the governance and construction of such a multinational project remains unanswered. What effects does laying the cable have on the marine environment, What are the implications if the cable is laid when nations are at peace but peace shatters later on? Who dictates where and where cable can be laid under international waters? Who is responsible for maintenance and protection of the cables? Both the construction and disruption of new Arctic infrastructure could have lasting effects on the physical, ecological, and human environments of the Far North.
