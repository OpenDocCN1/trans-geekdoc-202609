# 4. Challenges for Security and Forensics in the Smart Grid

Currently, most energy service providers use traditional security measures, including authentication, access control, authorization, data encryption, key infrastructure, firewalls, log analysis, intrusion detection systems, network security protocols, and more. However, recent cyberattacks on power systems have a significant gap between the possibilities of protecting and restoring traditional systems and new security requirements, in the context of intellectualization of energy systems and technologies for distribution and transmission of energy. Through cyber attacks, the network may encounter operational crashes and loss of synchronization. Such failures can damage the physical components of the power system, which can interrupt power supply and lead to system instability and, consequently, significant financial losses.

When discussing smart grids, we should take into account that attacks can arise from various parts of a power system using Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA), electric transportation infrastructure, smart meters, advanced metering infrastructure (AMI), the energy storage subsystem, and vital components of the smart grid.

To cope with these challenges in distributed networks, we need information that will enable the effective investigation of cyber crimes, the prediction of system failures, and the management of the Smart Grid. Recently, Cohen (2015) discussed a potential that cyber attackers can-anonymize up to 60% of Bitcoin coins in the network and open a new way to forensics in blockchain.
