
## 2.3.1.1.3. Scientific Research

China is very active in scientific research in the Arctic. China has carried out nine scientific expeditions to the Arctic to date [118], and since 2012 committed to at least one expedition every two years [113]. Additionally, Beijing has maintained an Arctic station in Svalbard, Norway, since 2003 [107], and in Iceland since 2018. China has two icebreaking ships. The more senior is the Xuelong, which has completed nine Arctic expeditions and was the first Chinese vessel to travel across the Northwest Passage [120]. The second ship, the Xuelong 2, built in China, can break ice if it is 1.5 meters thick. Additionally, the Xuelong 2 is the only polar research vessel in the world which can break ice while moving backwards as far as forwards [120].

Beijing's scientific contributions form a large part of its justification -- as out in the Chinese Arctic white paper -- for its involvement and stake in the Arctic [103]. China's interest in civilian scientific expeditions in the Arctic has also prompted fears that this be a means to surreptitiously build an enhanced Chinese military presence in the Arctic [120].

## 2.3.1.2. Conflict on the Horizon?

Scholars are divided over the extent to which China's ambitions could lead to conflict. To date and judging by it's Arctic white paper, Beijing has played nice, expressing respect for the rule of international law, a desire to aid in environmental conservation efforts, and the goal of assisting in building infrastructure which help local communities, including indigenous communities [103]. However, an examination of China's general international guiding doctrine and policies, as well as its recent actions in the Arctic, provides for concern.

Under Xi Jinping, Chinese foreign policy has placed a greater emphasis on protecting China's "peaceful rise" [121]. Since the early 2000s, China has defined its foreign policy doctrine as that of the "peaceful rise" (later referred as "peaceful development") as so not to worry other nations) [121]. The "peaceful rise" / "peaceful development" doctrine holds that a peaceful and stable external environment will ensure China's economic reality and its path to Power status [121]. However, since Xi took power things have shifted somewhat. While "peaceful rise" / "peaceful development" remains an important principle, China has continued to that Chinese national interests come first [121]. In a 2013 session with members of the Communist Party of China Central Committee Political Bureau, Xi outlined his priorities:

> We will stick to the road of peaceful development, but will never up our legitimate rights and will never sacrifice our core interests. No country should presume that we will engage in trade involving our interests or that we will swallow the 'bitter fruit' of harming our sovereignty, security or development interests [122].

In a 2014 address to the People's Liberation delege to the National People's Congress, Xi further expressed that China can be expected to prioritize its national interests over peaceful development [121], stating "We expect peace, but we shall never give up efforts to maintain our legitimate rights, nor should we compromise our core interests, no matter when or in what circumstances" [123]. A major driving element of Chinese policy is that the Communist Party of China requires a stable and strong economy to remain in power [124]. As a massive consumer of energy and raw materials, China will not hesitate to source resources from the globe, even in situations doing so is controversial [124]. Thus Bertelsen and Gallucci (2016) view “This global sourcing of energy and raw materials for fueling Chinese economic growth” “the necessary framework to understand China’s interest in Arctic energy and raw materials” [124].

When it comes to the Arctic specifically, Beijing has presented the narrative that its interests in the region are legitimate and long-standing [103], [105]. In 2010, Admiral Yin Zhou controversially declared that “The Arctic belongs to all the people under the world as no nation has sovereignty over it” [125] – something on which the actual Arctic states do not agree. As Bennett (2015) puts:

- Many of the Arctic states do not view the Arctic as the common heritage of mankind, because commodity cycles in recent years have occasionally made the seabed more valuable than before. It is because they view it as their own. [126]

As China pursues its foreign policy of prioritizing its national interests -- which it certainly sees as including the Arctic -- above peace and the international order, tensions are likely to rise. Taking into account China’s ambitions and many of the Arctic nations’ claims are overlapping and conflicting, some sort of conflict inevitable.

Already there been tensions and competition, particularly over development in Greenland. Sørensen (2018) refers to China’s behavior in Greenland as a “diplomatic offensive” [127]; the use of the word “offensive” is hardly accidental. Chinese attempts to invest in infrastructure projects in Greenland have proven worrisome to the international community, prompting fears for the “future of Arctic security” [105]. For example, a Chinese firm reached the shortlist for a project to develop three airports in Greenland, a concerned Denmark stepped up and promised financial support from Copenhagen in order clearly meant to obstruct the Chinese bid [105], [107]. The Greenlandic and Danish Prime Ministers came to an agreement in September 2018 according to Denmark would put forth 70 million USD in funding, in addition to supplemental loans [107]. This move was deemed necessary to prevent China and Chinese companies from encroaching on Greenland.

Danish Defense Intelligence Service risk assessment have recently expressed fears over China’s Arctic ambition, “stressing” in more and more direct language how Chinese investments in Greenland could bring certain dependencies and vulnerabilities [127]. The Danish Defense notes that Chinese commercial and strategic interests tend to be closely connected it [127]. The United States is also concerned, and has concerned launching its bid for “strategic investment” in airports in Greenland, potentially for “dual use” (civilian and military) purposes). Blaksjer et al. (2018), this U.S. interest highlights “the emerging importance of Greenland, as well as concerns about these interests there” [107].

## 2.3.2. Russia

As compared to China, Russia likely presents a more acute and near-threat in the Arctic [130], [131]. Over 10,000 miles, Russia’s border with the Arctic is the longest of any state — and represents one-third of Russia’s national borders [132]. Much of Russian territory is also within the Arctic Circle. Currently, Russia views control over the Arctic (which it defines rather broadly, as she shall be) as integral to its national security — and has so for centuries [132]. It was security made apparent by the 1905 Russo-Japanese War, for instance, prompted Russia to begin developing the NSR [133]. Even in the economically troubled years after the downfall of the Soviet Union, Russian security, military, and government leaders were forced to come by the Strategic bastion doctrine and massive Northern Fleet exercises [132]. Indeed, though the Yeltsin era’s Northern Fleet was always provided with adequate funds to maintain its nuclear submarines and infrastructure — even when other defense spending fell by the wayside [136].
