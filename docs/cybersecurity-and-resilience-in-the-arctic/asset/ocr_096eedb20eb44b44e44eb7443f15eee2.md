# 2.2.2. Cyber Threats to USN Arctic Infrastructure

There are many examples of infrastructural systems utilized by the USN and NATO in the Arctic which are vulnerable to cyber attacks, including submarine cables, energy grids [84], on-land communication networks, operations centers, medical support centers, and naval ports. Additionally, commercial and civilian infrastructure utilized by residents of the American Arctic, which includes native populations, is indeed at risk; the USN has committed to help protect this civilian infrastructure as well. The Navy’s Strategic Outlook for the Arctic includes as one of its strategic objectives “defend[ing] its sovereignty and the homeland from attack. A Primary Navy responsibility is to defend the homeland, its citizens, and critical infrastructure [2].

This section will concentrate on cyber threats to undersea cables as an example of the ways in which malicious actors can attack Arctic infrastructure. Submarine cables represent another potential vulnerability for the USN and NATO in the Arctic. The Navy uses cables for fleet underwater and training ranges, sensor systems, communications and monitoring systems, which require considerable infrastructural and technical support to monitor, utilize, and repair—making consideration of their management needs and a balancing of risk and benefit [85], such as power transmission and placement of acoustic sensors [8]75,000 km of different types of active seafloor cables [84]. Additionally, commercial fiber optic undersea cables provide internet access for the military and civilians alike. The Defense Information Systems Agency, “relies on commercial cables for 95 percent of strategic communications” [87].

Currently, there is a 1900 km fiber optic cable which snakes through the northern coast of Alaska, from Nome to the Prudhoe Bay in the east [88], with plans for extensions to Japan and across Beaufort Sea and northern Canada to the UK [89]. The USN and NATO become increasingly reliant on such cables to carry out operations and transport important data and information, as well as to access the internet, the consequences of a successful cyber attack on these cables are rendered more severe. While it is difficult to launch a successful cyber attack on fiber optic cables than other types of cables, cyber attacks are still possible, and the results could be devastating the extent of reliance on submarine cables [90]. Physical attacks on undersea cables are also possible, and in some ways easier to carry out, but they are not as difficult to scale up to [90]. Cyber attacks are likely to constitute a substantial portion -- if not the -- of attacks on submarine cables in the fleet. As Fitzlit (2019) “Cybertattacks are identified as the leading threat to the integrity of undersea fiber optic networks” [91].

There are two forms by which attacks on undersea cables can be taken: denial of service (DoS) attacks and espionage. In other words, attacks can either destroy or disrupt cables or can tap them. Bellovin and Gansner (2003) describe methods like dispatching routers through bugs such as CERT Advisory-CA-2002-03, and directing packets to the router to crash under high CPU loads [92]. Additionally, Bellovin and Gansner (2003) explain a method by which attackers can spy on undersea cables: the attacker destroys unsurveilled parts of the network, forcing traffic onto other cables that the attackers control and can easily monitor [92].
