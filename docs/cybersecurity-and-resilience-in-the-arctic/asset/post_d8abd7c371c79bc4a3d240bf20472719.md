## 6.3. Canadian Arctic Policy

Canadian Arctic Strategy and Policy detailed by “Our North, Our Heritage, Our Future” released in 2009 [47] and a follow up “Statement on Canada’s Arctic Foreign Policy” [48] can be viewed as a single entity and analysed as such.

As much of Canadian Arctic policy denotes not international policy and strategy but also the goals being sought domestically the government’s domestically. The main concern for the Canadian government is establishing and exercising sovereignty. Along the mainland, Canada also lays claim to a large extent of continental shelf and the waters between the islands of the Archipelago. The status of this water is contested most notably by the United States, who consider these waters to be international. Moreover, Canada also shared a territorial dispute over Hans Island with the Kingdom of Denmark, as both claim the island as being in part within their jurisdictions.

In the Arctic, nations-states often resort to contesting sovereignty through performances of effective occupation in a overt manner than they would otherwise do with their territorial regions. Wood-Dolly in their 2018 book "Performing Arctic Sovereignty," Imagine, if the Canadian Prime Minister was making similar statements regarding establishing sovereignty over Ottawa’s province of Ontario, as it does over Arctic Islands! It’s clear that sovereignty over Arctic territories and waters is an incredibly important priority for Canada, despite much of these disputes taking the form of words rather than actions.

The Strategy identifies four areas of priority, of which the statement expands out and fully promotes:

- "Exercising Arctic sovereignty"; achieving this through good governance and responsible stewardship.
- "Promoting social and economic development"; by taking steps to facilitate other states in implementing sustainable development, seeking economic prospects that benefit all Canadians, and encourage a better understanding of the human component within the Arctic.
- "Protecting our environmental heritage"; by promoting an ecosystem-based management approach amongst actors in the Arctic. In accordance with Canada’s Oceans Act, Canada is working with land authorities, governments, industry and communities to implement an ecosystem approach in the Beaufort Sea and has identified ecologically significant marine species and places, contributing mitigation and adaptation strategies in the Arctic. Moreover, Canada aims to strengthen international standards and protocols where necessary, e.g., Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs), and other contaminant and waste management programs.
- 4. "Improving and developing governance"; facilitating Northern governments and indigenous communities to actively participate in shaping foreign policy, achieved by engaging with Northerners on Arctic Foreign Policy through the Canadian Arctic Advisory Committee.

The Canadian Government can be seen as having taken an active role in Arctic discussions and international cooperation efforts; the last few decades Canada has been responsible for promoting the establishment of the Arctic Council, publishing sustainable development goals and human security as the focus of Arctic cooperation. Even further back in the 1970s Canada was taking action in the North, the Arctic Waters Pollution Prevention Act (AWPPA) to protect marine environments in its archipelago.

Though the government expressed its intention on developing a strategy in advance, the release of the 2010 statement was also met with criticism for failing to consult with northern indigenous organizations and northern communities as well as the academic community. The government, however, continued on this track and launched "Statement on Canada's Arctic Foreign Policy" in August 2010 to promote the Strategy and be "government's Arctic foreign policy statement.

In the documents Canada has published its Arctic policy, it's important to note that they define themselves as a "Northern nation". The North is a central characteristic of Canadian national identity, and moreover the North is noted as being "first and foremost people – the Inuit, other Aboriginal peoples and Northerners". Yet, with this being the case, the human dimension of the Canadian Arctic is not among the priorities of the Strategy, although empowering northern people is included as a priority in the 2010 statement [48] [43].

## 6.4. Danish Arctic Policy

Denmark's approach to Arctic policy can be seen in the document "Kingdom of Denmark Strategy for the Arctic 2011-2020" [50]. Adopted by the Government of Denmark, the Government of the Faroe Islands and the Government of Greenland, the strategy was launched in August 2011. In summary, the document provides an insight into promoting a peaceful, secure and safe Arctic, with specific focus on self-sustaining development and economic growth. This is to be achieved in consideration of the Arctic's fragile and harsh climate, working alongside international partners.

Also previously in May 2008 was a draft strategy for Denmark and Greenland. This document describes two main objectives; first, supporting and enhancing Greenland's development in order to increase its autonomy, and secondly, to maintain Denmark's position as a key Arctic actor [51].

While not specifically outlined, priority areas and tasks of focus for the Kingdom of Denmark are:

- 1. "Enhance maritime safety, and ensure sovereignty and surveillance;
- 2. Exploit mineral resources and new economic opportunities, and use renewable energy sources, maintaining a leading role in Arctic research, and promote Arctic cooperation on human health and sustainability;
- 3. Pursue knowledge building on climate change, and manage the Arctic nature based on the best scientific knowledge; and
- 4. Prioritize global cooperation and enhance cooperation in the Arctic Council and under the ‘Arctic 5’, with the EU as the Nordic countries.’ [43]

The 2011-2020 Strategy aims to focus upon the relationship between Denmark and Greenland, with special consideration going towards the devolution of responsibility and channels of authority. Other issues prioritised in the document are the Northwest passage; globalization and trade; partnership with the European Union, Arctic Council and specifically the Kingdom of Denmark’s chairmanship from 2009-2022, and the boundaries of the Kingdom of Denmark’s continental shelf in the Arctic.

One important emphasis in the strategy is that on new and emerging economic developments and industrial activity in Arctic. Fishing as being traditionally prioritised, however, hydropower, mining, tourism, oil exploration, and of other minerals; exploration of off-shore fuels and other energy resources are viewed as critical to development in Greenland [43]. While attention is greatly paid towards these endeavours, shipping and transport on opening sea routes in the North are less prioritised. A strong need for integration in international trade is identified, however, it’s noted that for the exploitation and use of renewable marine resources requires high standards, indicating sustainable use of natural resources alongside elements of environmental protection.
