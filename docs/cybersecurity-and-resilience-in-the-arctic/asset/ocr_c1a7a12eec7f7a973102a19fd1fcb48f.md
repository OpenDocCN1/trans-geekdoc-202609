In addition to navigation, other important naval operations can be affected by the volatility of the TDS. For instance, the clean-up of hazardous materials, or HAZMAT operations, such as cleaning oil spills, relies upon accurate information about current patterns. Oils and fuels tend to stay on or near the surface of the water; accordingly, their trajectory is based most accurately. If these currents are not modeled accurately, it may make HAZMAT operations additional time which is critically necessary to find the hazardous materials. Search and rescue operations similarly use information about currents to predict the location of a missing person or floating object based on its last-known position [63]. As part of its collaborative work with the Coast Guard, the US participates in both HAZMAT and Search operations. The 2019 Strategic Outlook for the arctic “Defense Support of Authorities (DSCA) and Humanitarian/Disaster Relief (HA/DR),” includes HAZMAT, and “Search and Rescue” as two of its seven core functions [2]. # 2.1.2.5.2. The Beaufort Gyre

The Beaufort Gyre is a 1000-km spinning pool of fresh water and ice located just north of Canada and Alaska, in the Beaufort Sea. To the TDS, the Beaufort Gyre has increased as ice extent has decreased [64]. Meneghello et al. (2018) found the primary factor limiting the speed of the Beaufort Gyre is ice cover; the “resulting deeper, swifter gyre will likely exhibit more variability in its freshwater storage and flow speeds” [64]. This increased speed and variability present many of the problems described as above regarding the predictability of the Transpolar Drift Stream.

In addition to ice leading to the ice-albedo feedback loop (see above), the increasing instability of the Beaufort Gyre has the potential to change itself. There is a substantial cold pool of surface and near-surface freshwater, which the halocline separates from the warmer deeper ocean waters [66], [67]. The halocline can be disrupted by an unstable Beaufort Gyre, with deep warm ocean waters able to rise up to near-surface, increasing the rate of ice melt [64], [66].

The changing Beaufort region presents problems for underseas communications, mainly by a phenomenon known as the Beaufort Lens. The Lens is a layer of warm water which comes from the Pacific and enters the Arctic via the Bering Strait. It has been persistently surging throughout the Beaufort Sea.
