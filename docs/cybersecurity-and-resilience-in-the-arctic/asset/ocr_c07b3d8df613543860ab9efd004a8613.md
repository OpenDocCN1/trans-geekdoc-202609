# 3. The Advantages of Resilience: How Resilience Can Help the USN Approach Arctic Threats, Disruptions, and Challenges

Resilience is a concept that has been widely utilized in a variety of disciplines, from psychology to ecology and socio-ecology to engineering. There are many different definitions of resilience, varying across discipline, era, and even individual researcher. Still, some key elements can be extracted from most definitions: resilience refers to the ability of a system to plan and prepare for, absorb, recover from, and adapt to disruptions [150], [9]. According to Linkov et al. (2013), these stages are expounded upon by the National Academy of Sciences follows:

+   1. Plan/Prepare: Lay the foundation to keep services available and assets during a disruptive event (malfunction or attack)
2. Absorb: Maintain core asset function and service availability while repelling or isolating the disruption
3. Recover: Restore all asset function and service availability to their pre-event functionality
4. Adapt: Using knowledge from the event, alter protocol, configuration of the system, personnel training, or other aspects to become more resilient [152].

Each of these stages will be referred to in greater detail in the following sections.
Resilience differs in ways from risk management, representing a “new way of thinking about safety” [151]. While both resilience and engineering seek to minimize disruptions, traditional risk management is focused on finding and rooting out vulnerabilities to known threats. Risk management is an element of resilience management, but it is not the only or best approach for addressing and minimizing disruptions. Risk management is weak when faced with unknown threats; risk can only be minimized when there is an awareness of potential threats.
Resilience, on the other hand, represents a strategy for minimizing disruptions which are not just known but also unknown—a large category when considering threats in the rapidly changing Arctic context. Resilience is “based on insight,” whereas resilience is grounded in proactive behavior [151]. Additionally, resilience “puts emphasis on speeding recovery and facilitating adaptation” as risk management [110]. This is because resilience-based thinking acknowledges that not only are there threats we do not know, but also many threats—both known and will penetrate into our systems to protect. Rather attempt the impossible (stop all intrusions), resilience management accepts that intrusions and disruptions will happen. Unlike risk management, resilience-based thinking gives us a way to cope with the aftermath of these disruptions: we can learn to recover more quickly from them and adapt to them. Risk management and resilience are separate and easily confused. But, as Linkov et al. (2013) describe,

> ...resilient systems should utilize generalizable concepts distinct from but complementary risk assessment to inform and support cooperation among diverse federal agencies responsible for critical infrastructures. [152].
