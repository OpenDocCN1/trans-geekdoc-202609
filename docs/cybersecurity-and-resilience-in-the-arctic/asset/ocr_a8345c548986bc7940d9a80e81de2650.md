If it wishes to confront these threats that may disrupt Naval assets and mission
requirements, the U.S. Navy might benefit from resilience thinking in order to improve
it ability to withstand disruptions. Resilience has many varied definitions. For
this chapter, we use the definition set forth by the National Academy of Sciences
(NAS): resilience is "the ability to prepare for, absorb, recover, and, more,
successfully adapt to adverse events" [9]. Notably, in contrast to risk-based
thinking, which focuses on fixing weak links in a system and patching them, resilience-
based thinking accepts that not all weak links can be identified or strengthened and that
trusions will happen. System hardness is just one element of a resilience-based
strategy, which also focuses on how systems can recover from attacks and adapt
in their aftermath [10], [11].

The 2018 National Defense Strategy accords with the need for a resilient USN,
stating:

> A more resilient, and rapidly innovating Joint
Force, combined with a robust constellation of allies
and partners, will sustain American influence and
ensure favorable balances of power to safeguard
the free and open international order. [5]

Threats and disruptions to the Navy, whether from state or non-state actors, whether
to due human error or nature, occurring in the air, on the surface of the world, or
under, and when taking place in the cyber domain or more traditional domains,
are acute when the USN is operating in extreme conditions. This chapter will
examine how resilience-based thinking helps the USN – and consequently, NATO to
a whole -- achieve its strategic goals and maintain competitive advantage within
the particularly extreme environment of the Arctic.
