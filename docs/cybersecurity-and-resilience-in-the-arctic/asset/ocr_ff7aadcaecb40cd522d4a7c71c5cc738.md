Loss of sea ice and a lack of action are the most significant challenges to the survival of polar bears according to a 2016 conservation management plan published by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Undeniable trends with decreasing sea ice from 1979-2014 comes a decreasing polar population [25]. The polar bear's role as an apex predator means that any impacts on them will have a cascading effect on the Arctic's complex food web, causing imbalances in species at lower trophic levels.

Beyond the polar bear, other species are also sensitive to changes in climate. The musk ox has been historically responsive to changes in the Arctic ecosystem, serving as a strong indicator for the impact of climate change on Arctic ecosystems. Warmer winters mean more rainfall when it freezes on snow forms a hard layer, preventing oxen from finding patches of grass. Change in marine ecosystems are also occurring, where invertebrates are being lost and new species are gaining dominance. One example of this is highlighted in the species within Arctic circles; as no longer are the predominant ones that dwell on the went but now more commonly found near or at the surface, associated with warmer waters. Rising temperatures also have an impact on the required processes for survival in numerous organisms. As water warms, species processes accelerate more energy and food, perhaps more than is potentially avoidable. Hence, a variety of freshwater species are expected to be lost completely in a number of Arctic areas [26].

Arctic species are limited in habitat by how far north they can go in order to adapt to rising ice and air temperatures. The nowadays movement of species from southern latitudes threatens and the vulnerability of those species already settled in more northern areas, and the new competition with other species has the potential to drastically reduce some species populations, pairing with an increased likelihood of species extinction [26].

Furthermore, vegetation and plant species in the Arctic are different from those found elsewhere in the world, because these plants must adapt their metabolic and reproductive processes to deal with the extreme frigid environments. Access to nutrients is also restrictive, due to freezing permafrost permitting a thin soil layer [27]. Rapid alterations to environmental factors, driven by climate change, will negatively impact many plant species. An extended growth season, snow-melting, changing precipitation patterns, deeper layers of soil and increased mineralisation and decomposition will affect the development and processes of these plants [27].

A warming climate and melting permafrost in lower latitudes also see the tree line move forwards. Impacts of this include the suppression of pre-existing vegetation in these regions, and the species composition of plant communities will also likely change. These changes will not occur in isolation, and cascading impacts on animal species dependent on disappearing plants will also take place, applying to arthropods such as insects and spiders, but also to mammals and birds.
