The industrialized and militarized Arctic of the Cold Beg to dissipate in the late 1980s and was transformed from a state of confrontation to one of international cooperation. The alternative discourse, a largely majority view, sees the Arctic and its oceans having the capacity to generate armed conflict, directly as a result of a “race for resources”, particularly those related to energy.

The region is greatly characterized by an increasing pressure for these natural resources, including mineral resources and fish stocks. Maritime border disputes, conflicting Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZ) and incoherence in regard to the concinental shelves of Arctic states pose threats and shape the parameters of northern geopolitics. The climate crisis, however, has been intensifying military build-up in the Arctic. Regional powers attempt to secure and protect their borders which previously were reinforced by a continental-sized division of ice. While the vast expanses of ice, industrial emissions and global warming are now heating this region of the planet as fast as the global average, decreasing not only the extent of sea ice but also its thickness, and the period in which sea ice is at its peak [56]. It is estimated by Wang and Oxford [57] that as soon 2037, Arctic summers could come to entirely ice-free Arctic.

With a rapidly changing environment, the modern geopolitical and strategic importance of the Arctic cannot be understated. As a location between three continents, with an abundance of valuable resources, including shipping lanes and rapidly increasing accessibility, the Arctic has the potential to become valuable to those able to effectively lay claims to it. This means that the Arctic attracts attention not only from Arctic states but also from Europe and Asia (namely China, Japan, South Korea, Singapore and India). These states see the benefits of engaging activity through the Arctic and each differently positioned within the collective decision making of Arctic politics. Each possesses, either directly and/or indirectly, an ability to influence the long-term direction of the informal regime, not least if interests coalesce. The deposits of natural resources will have economic importance for those able to obtain them, claiming and performing sovereignty over these regions is subsequently of vital importance to many of the Arctic states including the US and Canada, who currently have disputes over borders in the Beaufort Sea.
