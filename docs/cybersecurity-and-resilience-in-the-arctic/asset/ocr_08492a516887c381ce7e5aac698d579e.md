# 1.5.2. Tourism

Tourism in the Arctic is not a new phenomenon. Traveling in the footsteps of explorers and adventurers has lured people toward the North & South Poles for many years. Indeed, tourism along the Norwegian coastline and to the northern parts of the Scandinavian mountain has taken place over 100 years [48].

With changing Arctic conditions, the region has become heavily inundated by tourism in recent years, with Nordic countries and Greenland leading the way. In 2016, half a million people visited Iceland alone, and in 2017 Russia saw a 20% increase in visitors [49]. Also saw a surge in cruise ships setting sail for the Arctic, with Crystal Cruises’13 Serenity traversing the Northwest Passage for the first time. “Last-change tourism” is on the rise – global warming and climate change threaten to permanently alter the Arctic environment, and those who have previously thought about wanting to go before it's too late.

While tourism is often assumed to be environmentally damaging, the assumption that it necessarily brings economic benefits is also challenged. Adams [50] found that in a number of coastal communities the magnitude and intensity of passenger visitors raises social cost, causing economic regimes to shift that rapid socioeconomic restructuring. Managing and developing tourism connections between remote communities and their residents has to be a challenge across all Arctic regions [51][52]. These obstacles make these areas difficult to develop, fostering a tension between local economic development and cultural preservation [53].

Furthermore, widespread standards of regulation are sparse. A range of permits are required for cruise operations across different arctic regions, the rules ruling what is and isn't acceptable are challenging to enforce. Monitoring every cruise ship would be a large task, and they are expected to follow the rules The Polar Code provides guidance on discarding food waste and sewage, yet catching ships breaking these rules is hard, and enforcement of these is up to the IMO’s 172 member states, with no pre-specified permits [54].
