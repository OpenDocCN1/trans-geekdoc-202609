## 1 Introduction

The World Wide Web (WWW) is an integral part of everyday life in the Arctic. In particular mobile internet services contribute to the security and wellbeing of Arctic residents and Arctic communities. While technology cannot bridge all social gaps which result from the long distances between villages, settlements, towns, and cities in the Arctic and the comparatively limited service infrastructure (for example in the health or rescue services) in Arctic regions, communication technology plays an important role in securing a long-term presence in the extreme North.

While perceived as nearly empty, the Arctic and nearby areas are crucial elements of global connections. This includes not only flight routes between Europe and North America over the Arctic but also, subsea cables in the North Atlantic. As demand for connectivity grows, more subsea cables will be required. At the time of writing, in spring 2019, Finland is connected by subsea cables to Sweden, Estonia, Russia, and Germany [1]. The Aland Islands are connected to the Finnish mainland and to Sweden [1]. The Gulf of Finland is home to a particular network of submarine cables with at least seven connections to Helsinki, for which are between Helsinki and Tallinn, and two additional connections between Tallinn and Sweden, connections between Helsinki and Kotka, and one connection between Kotka and Logi in Russia.

Submarine cables are limited to Alaska (both along the northwest coast and connecting southern Alaska with the Canadian west coast and the mainland States), Greenland (along the southwest coast and connections to Canada and Iceland), Iceland (connections to Greenland, the Faroe Islands, the United Kingdom, and continental Europe), Norway (along the west coast and connecting to Svalbard) and in the Russian Federation. In the long run, as the economic development of the Russian Far North continues and as climate change leads to the melting of permafrost, making large areas of Russia potentially difficult to access, as subsea cables might become a viable tool along Russia's Arctic coast, too. At the time of writing, several cable projects are already underway in the Arctic, including a trans–Arctic cable which could connect Europe (via existing and planned cables in the Baltic Sea, including planned cables in the northernmost part of the Baltic Sea, in the Bay of Bothnia, Finland, which is fewer than 100 miles from the south of the Arctic Circle) and through the Barents Sea and the Arctic Ocean with Alaska (and therefore the North American continent), the Russian Far East, Japan, and China (for a map, see [5]). This planned connection is also expected to have a significant impact on inland areas of Finland far from the Arctic Ocean [5].

The high practical importance of subsea cables in a world dependent on fast intercontinental communication raises the question of how subsea cables are governed under current international law. This not relates to the question of who may place subsea cables where, but given that the cables largely unprotected simply at the bottom of the sea, on the floor [1], also to the matter of responsibility – for intentional or unintentional – damages to subsea cables. This is hardly a new concern in international law [6]; as early as 1984 the protection of marine cables was the object of regulation in an international treaty [7].

While the Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea [8] contains specific rules on how subsea cables behave in different marine spaces (Article 112 [8]), as well as on questions of liability (Articles 113-115 [8]), this text is concerned with the question of security of this vital infrastructure and the ability of different actors to ensure protection against outside interference.

## 2 Interference with subsea cables

Incidents involving fishing vessels, in particular trawlers [9], show that the cables, are about the same diameter as a garden hose [1] and which run to a total length of more than 1.2 million kilometers, are vulnerable. Already in 2014, the Canadian Supreme Court had to decide on a case involving damage caused by a fishing vessel where a fisherman operating the ship found that anchor caught a cable but, based on his recollection of a map he had seen a year before, thought the cable was not in use [10]. The fisherman then cut the cable in order to free his anchor. To contrary to his assumption that the cable was not in use, the cable in question was actually a fiber optic cable owned by some respondents. The Canadian Supreme Court had to rule on questions regarding the Marine Insurance Act [31] and the Convention on Limitation of Maritime Claims (LLMC), an international treaty which limits the liability for unintentional damages. The court had to deal with the question of whether the fisherman had caused the large damage (and thereby lost protection) [10]. While subsea cables are located near the coast, or in deeper water they are simply located on the top of the sea floor, which means that they are less protected against intentional interference. Although fishing zones are to be avoided when laying cables and despite the fact that locations are indicated on maritime maps, accidental damage to subsea cables is a distinct possibility. From the perspective of public shipping the international legal community has long been in the process of developing norms against protecting third parties against harmful effects ranging from major oil incidents to the damage of cruise vessels [11].

In addition with subsea cables, the threat of targeting subsea cables has a security concern for some time already. The technical capacity to physically connect to subsea cables (almost in the literal sense of the term “wire”) has been around for decades and it is possible to cut subsea cables which are at a depth of several miles using deep sea submersibles launched from submarines. Dependence on international communication has grown rapidly in recent years, so have concerns of hostile actions against subsea cables. In the words of two journalists specializing in national security: "The ultimate Russian hack could involve severing the fiber-optic cables at some of the hardest-to-access locations to halt the instant communications with the West; economies and citizens have grown dependent" [13].

Cutting a cable from shore under meters of water, will result in a significantly higher effort to fix the damage caused by a trawler's net close to the coast. Indeed, while Russia is said to look for ways to be able to interfere with cables (a capacity at present in principle), the United States as well as several other states are expected to always present and is also working on the possibility of maintaining a functioning national network if Russia were completely disconnected from the world. "Countries view the undersea cables as critical infrastructure and projects have been parallel geopolitical disputes. Last year, Australia stepped in to block the Chinese giant Huawei from building a cable connecting Australia to the Solomon Islands, for fear it would give the Chinese government entry into its networks" [9]. A look at a map of global subsea cable routes shows redundancy is limited due to the high capacity of modern cables, as well as the costs of laying subsea cables. The effectiveness of successful interference is significant. Cutting a cable from global internet access by destroying subsea cables can have dramatic effects on national and international security, and social cohesion, to just a few examples. The island of Tonga suffered a severe loss of connectivity after a fiber optic cable was cut on 20 January 2019 [14]. Limited to satellite communications, the national government's effort prevented high volume sites such as video streaming services [15]. In addition to inconvenient consumers, local business was particularly hard as it is now aware that a ship dragging its anchor damaged the cable [15]; the incident serves as an example and a warning, especially for other nations which are dependent on one or few cables. The detriment of users in Tonga showed how easy it is to disrupt a national economy within minutes. It has been proven that cutting subsea cables can be an effective tool, even in a hybrid context. While the risk of an armed conflict in the Arctic is to improve their military capabilities in the North [16] and a significant increase in Russian force deployments within Arctic parts of the Russian Federation (see e.g. [17]), the threshold for hybrid warfare and technical forms of interference appears to be significantly lower. The repeated disruptions by Russia of Global Position System (GPS) signals in Northern Norway [18] is a case point. Disturbing the functioning of GPS, which is of vital importance in remote regions, highlights another of military operations against subsea cables: while some cables like Guantanamo Bay to the US mainland [19], subsea cables do not necessarily fit the military / civilian distinction, which is at the heart of the jus in bello or international humanitarian law [20], easily a cable which is dedicated at least in part to military communications could become a legitimate target under Articles 48 and 5 (2) of the 1977 First Additional Protocol (hereafter: AP I) to the Geneva Conventions [20] [21].
