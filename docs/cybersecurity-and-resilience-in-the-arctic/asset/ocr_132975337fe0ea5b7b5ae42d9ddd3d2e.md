This multitude of actors corresponds with a range of government tools which are at the disposal of this diverse group of actors. While governments might focus on traditional state-centered governance methods on the national, sub-national, and international levels, non-state actors might find the possibility of providing input into the creation of soft law or technical standards to be of interest. In particular, in the maritime sector technical standards are often binding through UNCLOS (cf. IMO, 2014: p. 7).

This chapter meant to provide an introduction to the governance of the Arctic in this time of rapid change and multiple challenges, as well as the tools available to governments of Arctic states. To this, we will look at institutions and actors from the perspective of Arctic governance. We will focus on existing international cooperation.

The chapter focuses on the international level of Arctic governance. However, Arctic realities and developments are primarily shaped by national rights and actions and within the Arctic states. In addition to the primary focus on international governance, we therefore discuss the dynamics playing out at other levels of governance and in particular their interlinkages with international rules and policymaking.

# 2 Governance challenges in the Arctic

# 2.1. Effectiveness of law in the Arctic context

Sheeban (2018: p. 123) highlighted that “[m] of the challenges facing European Arctic populations relate not to military armed forces of other states, as realistic political theory would assume, to a broader range of economic, environmental and social insecurities.” The same applies in principle to the other parts of the Arctic as well, and it is these aspects that are at the focus of this chapter’s discussion on governance effectiveness. Addressing these needs requires international cooperation and Arctic governance, in particular the governance of Arctic spaces through the use of international (and also to a lesser degree, through national or sub-national) law, in particular international treaty law, faces a number of challenges. Law as a regulatory tool has limits. To begin, in a democratic society, law reacts to the demands of society and is reactive in nature where democratic societies may be seen as tool for governments to maintain control of society. Not all of the states which are located in the Arctic or have a significant interest in the Arctic are democracies that are committed to the rule of law and for human rights not only on paper but in practice. Different levels of respect for the rule of law and the human rights of those affected by regulation, or even the complete absence of enshrined human rights guarantees or the rule of law, also show that the same tool might not be equally appropriate in all circumstances. As international treaties require consensus, they are too rarely utilized. While the precautionary approach an increasingly relevant role in the Arctic in the context of (international) environmental law, the precautionary dimension of treaty-based international cooperation in the Arctic is otherwise limited. This also means that the capacity of international treaty law to mitigate risks in the Arctic is limited. As the experience of international maritime safety has shown, international legal developments are often triggered by specific events which have negative effects become visible; these events highlight the need for further (legal binding) regulation. In addition, the use of (international) law to govern risk-related issues requires infusions of technical expertise into the process of creating legal norms.
