Many papers investigate resilience, without directly and explicitly defining what that means, leading to uncertainty within their results and scope of work. Papers that implicitly all four phases of resilience, are well represented in the literature, often with metrics for resilience. Of the 19 that investigate all phases of resilience, eight use only Case Studies and seven use prior defined Lists of events for Disruption Representation. This shows a much greater reliance upon case studies than the other papers. These case studies, however, often rely heavily upon qualitative data, which qualitative generally comes from surveys and interviews, which are well suited for the case study process. This data is difficult, however, to use in modeling, simulation and prediction contexts.

Investigating all phases of resilience also appears to be a recent trend; most reviewed papers investigating all four stages of resilience were published after 2012, while most prior did not investigate all four phases of resilience. This indicates a positive trend; future work should continue this trend by addressing all four stages of resilience. However, most papers investigating all four phases of resilience do not complete supply chains, but instead limited sub-sets, which allows the authors to ignore issues of uncertainty and incomplete information. Beyond causing confusion among readers accustomed to different definitions of resilience, a lack of clarity on how to properly proceed in supply chain metric quantification is paramount.

# 3.3 Supply Chain Network Representation

The Supply Chain Network Representation is the method in which the physical networks compromising the supply chain are represented. This shows the locations of the goods at each step from representation, to transportation, and finally to distribution to customers. Each of these phases, extraction, transformation, and distribution may take multiple steps. This can be modeled as a linear process, where goods must go through steps, with no option, as shown in Figure 3A; a design process with multiple branches of options for some portion of steps, as shown in Figure 3B; or a complex graph where outputs from later phases may be components in earlier stages, as shown in Figure 3C. In the reviewed literature, Modeling Supply Chains as complex graphs was the most common approach, with 20 of the 52 reviewed papers utilizing them, followed by branching networks, at 16 reviewed papers. Linear supply chain networks, while not uncommon, were the least of the main groups with 11 reviewed papers modeling networks.

Complex and complete real-world supply chains are complex graphs, as raw material extraction does require finish goods. Modeling this, however, can be computationally expensive. Branching models of supply chains may be useful when modeling the resilience of any component of the supply chain that a single actor can control, over which a control may be more in its own processes than those of others. A strictly linear graph is of limited use when measuring resilience as any disruption completely completes performance. Supply chain graphs have also been generated from Economic Input-Output (EIO) models. EIO uses national-level data to connect monetary flows among industries (BEA).
