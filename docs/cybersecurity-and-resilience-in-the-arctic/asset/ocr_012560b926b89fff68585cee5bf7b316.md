# 2. Framing Context: Applying Resilience Concepts to the Arctic

The Arctic, a domain dominated by sea, snow, and a continental-sized division of ice, is facing a new norm in the twenty-first century — one of rapid change. The substantial consumption of fossil fuels has already been identified as accelerating the rate of climate change (Hansen et al., 1981; Cox et al., 2000; Karl, 2019) — a phenomenon that is heating the Arctic at a rate as fast as the rest of the world (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, 2018; Sand et al., 2015). For centuries, the region was locked away by harsh climate conditions and polar ice, as sea ice continues to decline, new markets and opportunities have emerged with them new challenges to those operating in the Arctic. These new challenges include contested industrial drilling rights, exclusive economic zones and continental shelf boundaries, emerging trade and cruise ship routes, military expansions reminiscent of the Cold War, and protection of indigenous communities, cultures, and languages.

Traditional risk approaches applied to a global system identifying the likelihood and impact of an adverse event on the system. From these calculations, risk management techniques focus on strengthening components to address potential threats within degrees of acceptable disturbances in order to prevent system failure (Linkov, Trump, & Keiser, 2018; Linkov and Trump 2019). These strategies fail to be effective where threats are unspecified, changing, and independent. Moreover, our social, technical, and economic networks are vastly more interconnected than ever before, making the hardening of specific components complex (IRGC 2018). Multiple failures of standard risk management approaches have been identified (Elms, 2009; Boulevard & Lee, 2019; Naime; Stulz, 2008), and these failures are increasingly pronounced in large, complex systems having numerous components are time-consuming, expensive, and impractical (Linkov et al., 2014). These conditions require designing resilience into systems, allowing for quick recovery when disruptions occur, and facilitating adaptation in the wake of adverse events to ensure that the system is prepared to deal with threats (Larkin et al., 2015). To understand what resilience looks like in the Arctic, it is first to understand what it is the Arctic can make traditional risk analysis and management techniques effective.

The Arctic is a region of shared responsibility — a home, a source of resources, and a key part of a global climate system. Definition of latitudes above 66°33'N (Nuttall, 2005), the Arctic circle is categorized by cold temperatures (Labe, 2019), melting ice (giving way to waters of natural worlds) (Lindholm and Grosnord, 2012), political quarrels (Lanteigne, 2019), fragile ecosystems, and significant indigenous populations (constituting about 6% of the population in the region; see Arctic Council, 2015). Environmental, ecological, and social change in the region are now accelerating at rates that have never been seen before (Arctic Council, 2016).
