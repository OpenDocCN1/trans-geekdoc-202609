
## 3.1.2. Subversion of Cyber Infrastructure

While safety concerns exist that affect the availability of ICT infrastructure, security events are characterized by malicious attacks on the integrity of the security infrastructure offers and usage of the infrastructure resources. A good percentage of malicious cyber attackers are not interested in cracking the infrastructure but in subverting its behavior for profit or for other purposes (Ablon 2018).

The detection mechanisms that can discover these subversion attacks strongly differ from those that signal fault or crashes. What characterizes a subversion attack is that the system components that are the attack targets continue to work even if the attack is successful. Furthermore, the components may continue to implement their task as specified. The first example of a subversion attack may be highly successful if the target infrastructure cannot be monitored (Cont et al. 2016). The man-in-the-middle attack allows attackers to eavesdrop and manipulate the communication between two legitimately communicating hosts while they should not be able to listen to the conversation. This explains the name "man-in-the-middle." We can describe the attack with an example. Assume Alice and Bob are having a conversation; Eve could eavesdrop on the conversation but also tell Alice she is Bob and Bob she is Alice. This would lead Alice to believe she is speaking to Bob, while she is actually revealing her part of the conversation to Eve. Eve could then get information from this, alter the response, and the message to Bob he thinks is talking to Alice. As a result, Eve transparently hijack and manipulate their conversation. This attack may be successful because Eve create two conversations: one with Alice and another with Bob.

If the target infrastructure is deployed in a remote and unfriendly environment, this attack is implemented by inserting a route node that plays the role of man-in-the-middle between two existing nodes. Another strategy is inserting a rogue access point that interacts with the correct one. As an example, devices equipped with wireless cards will try to connect to the access point that is emitting the signal. An attacker can connect his own access point and nearby devices to join its domain. As a result, the attacker can manipulate all the traffic of these devices. This is a dangerous attack because the attackers can implement it even when they do not access the target infrastructure. The attacker simply requires adequate physical proximity. Discovering rogue access points is complex because it requires physical inspection of the infrastructure.

Crypto hijacking is another malicious attack that subverts infrastructure behavior (Marczak et al. 2018) to use the infrastructure's computational resources to mine digital currency. We cannot assume all the details; in short, mining is a computationally intensive task related to the solution of a complex mathematical problem that underlies digital currency like Bitcoin. A miner uses computational resources to compute a solution and is rewarded with some time they compute a correct solution. There is a competition among distinct miners only the first one who can find a correct solution is rewarded; after that, a new problem is proposed. Hence, all the other miners have the chance of reward only by working on the new problem. To increase the number of computational resources they can access, some miners have developed malware that can hide in a popular website and secretly hijack the browsers of the visitors to mine digital currency using visitor resources.

Other attacks target the smart meters of the metering infrastructure, i.e. an infrastructure created to measure water, gas, energy consumption. This example stresses that the simplest computational devices can be attacked for a profit if the number is huge. These episodes are part of a larger trend: security experts have seen in recent years a spike in cyberattacks that are stealing power for mining operations. Many experts forecast that in the future the intelligent sensors connected to an ICT infrastructure to monitor physical processes will become a popular target because of their increasing computational power. Discovering the malware that infects these devices may be complex because the affected components continue to offer their services. As an example, smart meters measure the consumption of water or energy. The only difference in an infected device is a lower performance because discovering this attack is a subset of the malware's computational resources. It is obvious that only if the infrastructure is continuously monitored and the design has adopted proper choices on non-functional behavior such as the execution time of a function. The complexity increases if insiders are involved in the attack. Here, an insider may be a customer who supports the attack because they are not satisfied with the service, or an activist who is protesting the company that created or manages the infrastructure.

One of the problems posed by attacks targeting smart meters or actuators to monitor and control some physical process is that most users are not aware of the computational nature of smart objects. The awareness of the computational power of smart objects, and of the objects themselves, disappears as they become more pervasive and diffuse (Bertino and Ital 2017). A revealing example are surveillance video cameras, as attackers have used their computational resources to implement some of the most serious distributed-of-service attack (DDoS) attack. A DDoS attack disrupts the normal traffic of a computer network by overwhelming the target infrastructure with a flood of internet. A DDoS attack is like a traffic jam clogging up the highway which prevents regular traffic from arriving at its destination. DDoS attacks achieve effectiveness by utilizing a large number of computer systems as sources of attack. In recent DDoS attacks, the sources were surveillance video cameras (Klias et al. 2017). Most of the camera owners learnt of the camera computational resources only after the attack. Further potential attack targets are smart lamps that can be dimmed by the push of a key on a keyboard. It is worth recalling that since all infrastructures are connected, resources connected to one infrastructure can be used to attack any infrastructure connected to the attacked one.

All attacks that subset the normal behavior of infrastructure result in the creation of a botnet. A botnet consists of some interconnected resources of the infrastructure under an attacker's control. Hence, a botnet is a subset of the infrastructure that has fallen under the control of an attacker who accesses the botnet and uses the botnet resources for its benefit. It is in the attacker's interest that the target infrastructure works as smoothly as possible. Smooth infrastructure behavior reduces the likelihood that the attacker can, through continuous monitoring, discover the botnet. Discovering a botnet requires continuous infrastructure monitoring; even then may not suffice if the botnet creator exploits some 0-day vulnerability (Ablon and Bogart 2017) or insiders are involved.

Usually, botnets are used or rented to other criminal organizations to broadcast spam or messages or to launch an attack against other infrastructures, it uses the attackers to hide the real source of an attack. Accordingly, botnets are one of the main reasons for the complexity of cyber-attack attribution (Boebert 2010).

## 3.1.3. Cyber-Resilience of Ships

The tight integration of Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) within ship systems brings new cyber risks and makes the maritime industry a potential target for various kinds of cyber attacks. Moreover, traditional safety and risk analysis methods, such as Fault-Tree Analysis (FTA), Failure Mode Effect Analysis (FMEA), HazOP, do not work well with the emerging cyber-physical systems (Leveson 2011). These models are best suited for analysis of the faults by a single component but do not describe failures in complex systems.

As Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) have advanced, shipping industry has started relying on ICT. This has ushered in a new era in the maritime industry. The bridge and engine components of the ship such as Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), Electronic Chart Display Systems (ECDIS), Automatic Identification System (AIS), propulsion control, and propeller are interconnected with the on-board communication network. Tight coupling between ICT and physical systems on ships have introduced a lot of risks related to cyber attacks, faults, and failure. Moreover, systems are becoming increasingly complex and difficult to manage. The maritime industry has come more vulnerable to cyber attacks because of its adoption of ICT. For instance, the 2018 attack on Maersk is a well-known example of a cyber attack that spreads to the shipping industry (Safety4Sea 2018). Therefore, it is important to make sure that security and safety requirements are identified in ship systems and adequately addressed.

The CyberShip (Sahay & Sepúl 2018) is a proposed framework comprising of different components, on which System-Theoretical Process Analysis (STPA) method for assessing cyber risks is applied. STPA identifies and enforces required constraints to eliminate or reduce the damage caused by risk. It can be used for threat modeling and vulnerability analysis for cyber attacks against cyber-physical systems on ships. Figure 1 shows two main controllers in the cyber-physical infrastructure of a ship: the Integrated Bridge (IBC) and the Engine. The IBC provides a centralized interface to visualize all the information from bridge devices such as AIS, ISIS, radar, etc. The engine controller is comprised of all the systems related to propulsion, propeller, rudder, ballast, and etc. sends information report to fuel, fuel consumption, engine output, and water level in the ballast compartment to the IBC which is broadcasted in case help is required.

Further connected systems and components that are relevant to the CyberShip framework include:

- The ballast water system, a compartment in a ship that holds water and is used to provide stability. Water in a ballast tank enables the easier adjustment of weight.
- The cargo system includes shipment tracking details of the goods to be delivered.
- The AIS device broadcasts and receives data about a ship's name, size, type, status, position, speed, IMO, and MMSI number to avoid collision with other ships (CML 2015). AIS devices do not employ authentication and integrity checks, they are vulnerable to spoofing (Deoitte 2017).
