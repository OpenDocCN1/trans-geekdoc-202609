Many investigators have reviewed resilience in the Supply Chain context, either in focus or in part. Table 1 summarizes 12 review papers on the state of supply chain resilience. It notes whether work uses the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) or a similar definition of resilience as the basis for analysis; whether the work addresses the modeling/simulation of supply chains; whether the paper addresses supply chain dependence on other networks, such as Command and Control or transportation networks; and whether the paper addresses the usage of metrics to measure supply chain resilience. A smaller subset of literature review is focused on representation of the supply chain as part of a larger system that includes supply nodes, locations, delivery paths, or transactions. This review indicates that the majority of reviewed supply chain resilience literature tends to ignore furthermore supply chain system and network views or to implicitly integrate some portion of other networks into supply chain components and models. This despite the fact that supply chain networks or graphs is common practice and is recommended by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (Boyens et al. 2015). Explicit acknowledgement of these networks is conceptually accounted for in more qualitative supply chain resilience literature, but these papers do not enable objective quantitative measurement of resilience. Without proper modeling of the supply chain itself, supply chain management and resilience modeling of the supply chain cannot be achieved.

Table 1 indicates a continuing lack of agreement on the standardization within the supply chain resilience field. While a slight majority of these papers investigated supply chain modeling or resistance, under half of those papers also investigate how quantify resilience with specific metrics, either in general or for specific circumstances. Our analyses indicate that none of these literature reviews have investigated chain dependency on other networks, including both Command and Control and transportation networks, in the modeling context. There is a need to investigate existing practices from the perspective of modeling the interdependent networks that form a supply chain to understand its resilience. Additionally, there is a need for a comprehensive supply chain resilience review in order to determine the current state of the field with respect to utilizing a system-approach to supply chain resilience. This paper adds to the field of literature by presenting a literature review of supply chain modeling and quantification from a simulation of multiple-dependent networks, system-approach, using the NAS definition of resilience.

This paper argues that awareness of these multi-network interactions and explicit accounting for how they are all modeled or simulated is vital to understanding supply chain resilience. This paper reviews the current supply chain resilience literature that incorporates both a systems perspective and at least some aspects of modeling and simulation. This paper provides supply chain definitions (Beamon 1998)'s definition of supply chain as processes, as discrete processes lend themselves to simulation. This paper starts with a bibliometric review, to identify the literature space and trends. It then moves to a thematic review, to describe the ways in which the current literature models the interlaced networks that drive a supply chain's functionality. It comparatively reviews the strengths and weaknesses of the different approaches as well as some of the scenarios and purposes for which these approaches are more valid. It identifies the gaps in current research and how addressing these gaps may improve understanding of supply chain resilience.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Review Process and Inclusion Criteria

The biometric review process included two literature search phases. The first phase was a keyword search of all the Web of Science databases. Literature review papers found in this search were used in a “Forward Backward Search,” whereby the papers that they cited and papers that cited them were added to the search phase. In both phases a filtering process was used to filter out low quality, irrelevant, or unavailable papers.

The first search used the Web of Science (WOS) “All Databases” collection for the literature search. Papers were limited to those published in English between 2007 and 2017. Peer-reviewed papers were identified in the search and available through USACO and CMU library subscription access, university repositories or other public sources were included in the review. Proceedings and other non-reviewed papers as well as duplicate publications were excluded. A Web of Science search for the intersection of “supply chain” and “resilience” resulted in 673 papers. Twenty of these papers were restricted access and counted for under 3% of the total results the majority from Francis and Taylor publications. Abstracts from excluded papers were reviewed by reviewers and found not to differ significantly in distribution of content from reviewed papers. The authors believe the inclusion of these papers would not significantly change the results.

For the first filtering stage, the papers were filtered for prominence in the field based on a combination of citation numbers and year of publication, with older papers requiring more citations to be included. This was done to filter for papers which continued to perform well (i.e., maintain relevance) over time. The minimum number of citations needed to be included in the final literature review, by year, is summarized in Table 2.

For the second filtering step, literature reviews were set aside for backward search and not included in the general results. Duplicates were excluded and defined as sharing the majority of authors, published in the same year, at multiple conferences, or appearing annually multiple years. The authors vetted these duplicates to determine if they effectively covered the same material or substantially different findings. For duplicate groups only one paper was included in the final literature review; with journal articles retained over conference proceedings, which were in turn kept over chapters; otherwise the first published paper was selected.

The remaining papers were screened for relevance. Only papers focused on supply chains and resilience modeling and simulation were included. For example, papers about organization characteristics that affect resilience that did not include a supply chain model were excluded. These papers often utilized surveys of managers and executive officers over aspects of their organizations and used these to correlate with resilience, without incorporating models or simulations. Resilience screening was done by two of the authors, who coordinated with each other. Overlap in reviewed papers was used to ensure consistency in relevancy designations.

In phase 2, additional papers were added through forward backward search. This process, as described by (Levy and Ellis 2006), improves the rigor of the initial search by revealing the search space as the keywords evolve and continue, rather than a single moment in time. The existence of significant differences in relevant papers found in this phase may also help identify inadequacies in the initial search. Literature reviews that passed phase 1 criteria were not reviewed themselves, but instead used for backward search. Literature reviews were excluded to focus on papers presenting original research and supply chain modeling methodology. Any methods the search space of papers could be captured from their sources (backwards) or citing (forward) the literature reviews found in the Web of Science.
