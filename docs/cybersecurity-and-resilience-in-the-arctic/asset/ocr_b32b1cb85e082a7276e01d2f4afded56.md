Complicating matters further are the abundant evidence that human operator error is the cause of cyber breaches and incidents. The isolation, uncomfortable temperatures, and unfamiliar environment of the Arctic could increase the likelihood of operator errors, putting Arctic operations at serious risk. Accounting for human error and unpredictability, through redundancy, multi-layer security, or other means, is a pressing issue in the development of the Arctic workforce.

The eight nations — Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Russia, Sweden, and the U.S. — have formed an inter-governmental high-level forum, the Arctic Council, in partnership with native and indigenous groups and other observers to manage Arctic affairs. Relations have been well and peacefully so far, these eight nations have differing priorities concerning the Arctic, and care must be taken to ensure that diplomacy remains intact even as the Arctic environment changes dramatically in coming years. International discourse is all more important as the Arctic Council currently lacks a clear mandate to address security issues facing the Arctic region.

# Seeking 21st Century Solutions for 21st Century Arctic Problems

This book presents a diversity of perspectives and opinions inspired by the January 2019 NATO SPS ARW, as well as chapters from invited experts on differing topics of cybersecurity and resilience within the Arctic domain. Grouped roughly into the categories noted above, these seek to fully explore the extent of cyber-development concerns within the Arctic, and/or offer possible ideas or solutions to begin to mitigate or harmful consequences that cyber disruptions may pose to communities and the environment. Prior to this, Hall et al offer a framing overview of what the Arctic is, what its emerging risk and governance challenges are (with particular emphasis on cybersecurity), and what factors exacerbate or mitigate the highly uncertain threats facing actors within the Arctic.

Thorisson provides a thorough overview of infrastructure resilience challenges and concerns, including the characterization of cybersecurity threats in the Arctic region as well as necessary steps actors in the public and private sectors should consider to reduce their exposure and/or consequences to such threats. Their perspectives are bolstered by Humholm, Paulson, Kirchner, and Mersky et al. Bairdi and Wandji et al. respectively provide an analysis of infrastructural and mission-oriented challenges associated with shipping and naval operations in Arctic latitudes, and argue that resilience-based approaches are a necessity to (a) ensure mission continuity in the face of multiple uncertain threats, and (b) to adapt to changes in increasingly accessible Arctic waters. Humholm provides an exhaustive overview of cyber-infrastructure threats within Arctic lands and waters, while Paulson notes the unique cyber-infrastructure management and governance concerns facing Iceland and its risks and implications of undersea cables within the Arctic and sub-Arctic, where the large and growing network of such cables allows digital systems to operate with minimal disruption.
