Figure 1 shows the Arctic region defined by these criteria, comprising the area inside the red line. The Arctic region's inhospitable weather and other challenges have limited human activity and settlement. This is the reason that there are no permanent settlements above 78° latitude. The Arctic region is one of the least populated areas in the world and there are only a few large cities. About half of the Arctic population centers around the Arctic Circle in Russia: Murmansk (population around 300,000), Norilsk (over 100,000), and Vorkuta (around 60,000). Tromso, Norway has 71,000, and Reykjavik, Iceland has more than 100,000 bordering the Arctic Ocean. The population is a mix of Siberians and several major groups of indigenous people who lived in the north polar lands for centuries. Siberians make up sizable portions of Siberia and Greenland, and a near-majority in Iceland. However, there are many different groups in the region such as Inuit, Chukchi, Yupik, Inariat, and others. The people who live in the far north are nomadic hunter and gatherers with the emphasis on hunting rather than gathering, though the Sami people of Scandinavia, amongst others, reindeer herders. From the 20th century, immigrants to the Arctic region because there were job opportunities attracted them. For most part they did work that relates to the natural resources in the region one way or another. This large influx of immigrants has dramatically changed the balance of non-indigenous and indigenous peoples in many areas.

This migration has also affected the infrastructure, towns, and villages, throughout the whole of the Arctic region, including the available services and the entire ecosystem. There are already companies in the region that take advantage of oil and gas resources. The employees of these companies require a variety of services, such as health care, children's schools, banking services, leisure facilities, etc. The growing tourism in the region requires the indigenous peoples in this context, and better services must be offered to indigenous peoples in relation to health care, children's schools, the services provided by shops, and so on.

The Arctic region contains a wide range of landscapes including plains, mountains, some large rivers and lakes, rolling hills, huge spans of tundra and the largest biome in the Taiga. The ice at Arctic sea is largely formed from the frozen sea. It contains a part of the world's multi-year sea ice, which is 3-4 m thick on average with even thicker ridges. Greenland holds the largest glacier in the Arctic. When a part of the Greenland breaks off, it forms an iceberg from the frozen-glacier. Most of the iceberg is located underwater, but it is not quite as dense as the water, about one-nin of it remains above the surface. The Arctic Ocean and the bordering countries with summer ice cover are seen in Figure 2. The Arctic region has some of the extreme climatic conditions on Earth. Average winter temperatures may vary from -40 to 0°C and there are areas where temperatures often fall below -50°C. These temperatures also mean that all structures above the ground are covered in the winter by thick ice, as well as the snow blow by the wind. There are also strong storm.
