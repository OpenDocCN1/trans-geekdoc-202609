
In other words, the Navy, as a core component of the Joint Force, can maintain and gain the competitive edge while optimizing its capabilities across key locations and domains to force the enemy to defend itself at multiple vulnerable areas.

“The U.S. Army in Multi-Domain Operations 2028” goes on to characterize the future operational environment as shaped by several interrelated characteristics:

- Adversaries are contesting all domains, the EMS, and the information environment and U.S. dominance is not assured; smaller armies fight on an expanded battlefield that is increasingly lethal and active; nation-states have more difficulty in imposing their will within a politically, culturally, technologically, and strategically complex environment; and near-peer states meet more readily competition below armed conflict, making difference more challenging. These characteristics allow adversaries, particularly near-peer threats like China and Russia, to expand the battlefield in time (a blurred distinction between peace and war), in domains (space and cyberspace), and in geography (now extended into the homeland) to create tactical, operational, and strategic stand-off. [74]

Adversaries are contesting all domains across a geographically expanded battlefield. Should the U.S. Navy and NATO seek to contest the threat posed by enemies such as China and Russia, they would benefit from being equipped to contest all domains and in this extended geographical battlefield. That battlefield— whether competition or conflict -- will almost certainly include the Arctic. Although the Arctic has long been a peaceful region rarely subject to the geopolitical order which governs the rest of the world, this could change. Competition below the threshold of armed conflict is more likely than full-scale. The goal of preventing armed conflict can be assisted through the addressing of competition. The Arctic may very well become another region via Russia, China, and other adversaries seek to press their advantages against NATO, the West, and the United States.

The examples of China and Russia should serve to illustrate the extent and contours of the problem.

## 2.3.1. China

In 2018, China published a white paper laying out its policy for the Arctic [103]. According to this paper, China wants “to work with the international community to safeguard peace and stability in, and the sustainable development, of the Arctic" [103]. However, China also describes itself as a "near-Arctic state, one of the continental States that are closest to the Arctic Circle" [103] -- Mike Pompeo points out out, an essentially meaningless classification [104], but one perhaps revealing of China’s ambitions concerning the Arctic.

### 2.3.1.1. Why China Wants In

China is attracted to the Arctic for three main reasons: its potential for new shipping routes, its natural resources, and its scientific potential. China is committed to building a "Polar Silk Road" through the Arctic, which will entail developing Arctic shipping routes. China is also interested in the oil, gas, energy, and resources of the Arctic, as well as its fishes. Finally, China is interested in the Arctic as a platform for valuable scientific research on climate change, which will likely affect China’s significant coastal population [105]. China also intends to continue to fund and build infrastructure, essentially for tourism [103]. This may be a means to gain geopolitical influence and power in the region.

### 2.3.1.2. Shipping Routes

China sees the Northern Sea Route (NSR) as a significant element of its long-term economic strategy. In fact, while there are doubts the potential of the NSR a legitimate alternative to pre-existing major shipping routes, China seems to believe the NSR will be viable for commercial transit sooner or later than others. Beijing is prioritizing investment in opening the NSR for shipping [106]. The NSR would cut on shipping times from northern Chinese ports to northern Europe and to the coast of the United States by at least 40% compared to the current standard routes, which go through the Suez Canal or the Panama Canal. China has deemed the NSR and other passages through the Arctic as the "golden route" [105].

Access to the NSR is vitally important to China. Beijing’s policy "directly encourages Chinese companies to assign priority to the construction of infrastructure linked to the Arctic sea routes and emphasises that China is ready to cooperate with anyone interested in the development of the "Polar Silk Road" [106], [107].

### 2.3.1.2.1. Oil and Gas

The Arctic has readily offered up significant petroleum reserves through onshore areas in Canada, Russia, and Alaska. In total more than 400 oil and gas fields have been discovered in the Arctic, comprising around 240 billion barrels of oil and oil-equivalent natural gas [108]. This constitutes nearly 20% of the world’s "known conventional petroleum resources" [108]. However, there is much oil and natural gas still to be discovered in the Arctic, mostly in offshore areas. A study by the U.S. Geological Survey estimated that "90 billion barrels of oil and 44 billion cubic feet of natural gas may remain in the Arctic."
