"Permanent participants" in the group of six Arctic indigenous groups are also represented in the Arctic Council. These groups are Aleut International Association, the Arctic Athabaskan Council, Gwich' Council International, the Inuit Circumpolar Council, the Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North, and the Saami Council. These indigenous councils represent native Arctic actors and work as official organizational bodies to represent a diverse range of indigenous groups. They have full consultation representing discussions and decisions of the Council. "permanent participants" are eligible to join, so long as they represent a single indigenous people residing in more than one Arctic State, or multiple indigenous peoples residing in a single Arctic State (Arctic Council, 2015).

# 2.2 Indigenous populations

Indigenous peoples have lived in the Arctic for thousands of years. There are currently over 500,000 indigenous people in the Arctic out of a total of about 8 million (Arctic Council, 2015). While 40 different ethnic groups are recognized in this region (Arctic Centre, 2019). While many differences exist between these groups, similarity is certain; all indigenous populations have undergone substantial changes, primarily due to Western globalization, state policies and legislation, modern transportation, and the introduction of mixed economy. Furthermore, human activity increasing the rate of climate change is believed to have had negative impacts on these communities, unpredictable economic, social and ecological changes disrupt their way of life (Lehti et al., 2009). Traditional methods of subsistence are becoming increasingly challenging as weather conditions become variable in both extremes and melting sea ice ecosystems and animals reliant on ice cover, such as polar bears, seals and catch fish. As a result, the livelihoods of many indigenous peoples and communities are under threat. Fears of increased contaminants, restricted use, security, and access to resources are also present among indigenous communities.

Modernization has increased the frequency, extent, and impact of outsider interaction with indigenous communities, which in turn has had a number of discernible negative consequences. Recent introduction of new stressors has had significant impact on indigenous communities' health (Bjjergaard et al., 2004), and changing lifestyles are introducing atherosclerotic diseases and diabetes, as well as cancers related to consumption of alcohol and cigarettes (Bjjergaard, Mulwaard and 2004; Mulwaard and Pedersen, 1997). Suicide rates among young indigenous peoples in the Arctic are very high, and abuse is common (Lehti et al., 2009). The spread of globalization in this region has forced communities to adapt to urbanization, introducing new changes ranging from new authority structures to modern life and the decline in traditional livelihoods. Moreover, the clashing of traditional and modern values is problematic for younger generations; trying to balance life in the twenty-first century with learning about cultural roots and historical practices can prove mentally draining and confusing. Here risk of inducing a diminishing sense of community and widening the generational gaps is of great concern (Hicks, 2007; Kirmayer, 2000).

While indigenous communities are traditionally accustomed to an environment with a high degree of variability and unpredictability, they in the past have been able to successfully adapt and manage this variability. Communities that maintain their traditional livelihoods and culture have adopted new and useful technologies.
