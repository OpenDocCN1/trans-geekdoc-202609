# 2.1.2.3. Thawing Permafrost

Climate change in the Arctic region is causing problems which are affecting the land as
well as the sea. Many of these problems can be expected to increase in severity as
w accelerates. For instance, thawing permafrost harms entire infrastructure
-- including Department of Defense (DoD) installations and other infrastructure upon
which the USN and NATO rely and makes it more difficult to build new infrastructure
[16]. The functioning of the Arctic as a situation is heavily reliant upon ground temperature
conditions [37], [38]. Terrestrial permafrost, soil or bedrock which has
sistently stayed freezing temperatures for last two years, both supports and
allow for the development of infrastructure [37]. Unfortunately, as the climate warms,
this critical permafrost is expected to thaw; in fact, 70% of all within
the Arctic is situated directly on top of permafrost which is projected to thaw within
years [18], [39]. Thawing permafrost can lead to ground subsidence, instability, and
erosion, pushing infrastructure at high risk [40], [41], [42], as As.
Karjalneen et al. (2019) note, "many recent national-scale studies suggest that to
infrastructure permafrost degradation will decrease over the next decades" [37]

done, quickly, in order to bolster existing infrastructure and create new infrastructure.

# 2.1.2.4. Coastal Erosion

The effects of climate change -- thawing permafrost, waves, and rising sea
levels due to melting ice -- are having a significant effect on the Arctic coastline,
leading to substantial and accelerating coastal erosion [49], [50], [51], [52]. The
main geological processes eroding the Arctic coast are thermoababrasion, which brings
the "combined mechanical and thermal effects of impacting wave energy at the
shoreline" [50], and thermodegradation, the impact of solar insulation and heat transfer
above the water line [53]. In combination, processes of thermoabrasion lead to coastal erosion rates in the Arctic which reach as high as 25
meters per year [52]. To put this into perspective, where sandy shorelines are
eroding, they are doing so at rates of up to 0.5 meters per year and half of the world’s
sandy shorelines are not even eroding [56]. Rates of coastal erosion in the American
Arctic (which, with rates in the Canadian Arctic, is the highest in the Arctic)
double since the 1950s and are accelerating [50]. In the Arctic as a whole, annual
rates of coastal erosion are among the highest in the world [50].
