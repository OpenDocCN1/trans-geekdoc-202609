## 6.6. Icelandic Arctic Policy

In September 2009 the Icelandic Ministry of Foreign Affairs published Iceland’s official position in regard to Arctic issues in the report titled “Island á norðluðum”—“Iceland in the High North”. This report was followed up by a “A Parliamentary Resolution on Iceland’s Arctic Policy” which was adopted by the Icelandic Parliament, Althingi, in March 2011 [53]. From the report and parliamentary resolution, and six key highlights can be taken, alongside identification of twelve principles.

The six highlights of the Report are:

- 1. Multilateral cooperation with neighbouring Arctic states, with a focus on promoting Iceland’s interest and long-term interests.
- 2. Environmental and geopolitical security achieved through close international cooperation, based on the writings of international law, with a prominent focus on SAR, emergency response and environmental protections in the wakes of increasing traffic in Arctic waters.
- 3. Protection of the environment in response to resource development with interest to the Icelandic fishing industry, practising caution in resource development ensuring the sustainability of fragile environments and ecosystems isn’t damaged. Resource development should first serve the interests of the people of Iceland and its communities, helping to contribute further towards future long-term economic prosperity.
- 4. Close monitoring of the transportation of natural resources (specifically oil and gas) through Icelandic and other Arctic waters, ensuring the protection of the marine environment and spawning grounds of fish stocks. Inadequately equipped in ice-infested areas are of main concern, a priority that the International Maritime Organization (IMO) updates and makes mandatory in the application of Guidelines for Ships Operating in Arctic Ice-Covered Waters;
- 5. Preserving the Arctic communities and cultures with unique heritages, strengthening cultural identities through cooperation and promoting their worries and concerns;
- 6. Strengthening international scientific cooperation, facilitating Arctic research and monitoring in the Arctic turning science into a useful tool for decision-making and policy guidance in response to the emerging challenges faced by environments and social conditions in the region.

The twelve principles go on to further support and outline ways in to achieve these objectives, emphasizing the priority for securing Iceland's position as an Arctic coastal state, while also improving the wellbeing of Arctic populations and advancing the awareness and knowledge available in relation to northern issues. These twelve principles can be summarised into the following:

- 1. Strengthening international cooperation, particularly with the Faroe Islands and Greenland in order to promote and strengthen the Arctic Council as a mechanism for resolving issues, primarily using UNCLOS as a legal foundation in this otherwise largely soft-law regime.
- 2. Promoting Iceland as a coastal state in the Arctic region, alongside increasing an understanding that the Arctic region extends to parts of the Northern Atlantic Ocean.
- 3. To promote and enhance Icelandic knowledge of Arctic and the global importance of the Arctic region, with aims to utilize Iceland as a venue for international meetings, conferences and dialogues related to the Arctic.
- 4. Doing whatever possible to prevent and reduce the impacts and effects of human induced climate change, in attempt to improve social wellbeing, preserve cultures and languages, life, and rights of northern populations.
- 5. Safeguarding against militarization of the Arctic, and instead promoting security interest through civilian means and international cooperation.

The key takeaway from both the report and parliamentary resolution is Iceland's focus and promotion of multilateral, international cooperation with neighbouring countries, using the Arctic Council as a mechanism for this, but also excluding the Barents Euro-Arctic Council. Moreover, the report illuminates that Icelandic foreign policy is well aware of Arctic and northern issues, making it one of its key areas moving forward.

Also is emphasized that Iceland continues to be involved and to remain an active member of international and intergovernmental organizations such as the UN, NATO, and Arctic Council. Processes and policy within these international forums have already been influenced greatly by Iceland, as shown in changes to UNCLOS processes in regards to the extension of EEZs up to 200 nautical miles [43]. Moreover, Iceland already acts as an active leader in current international cooperation, hosting the offices of two working groups of the Arctic Council (CAFF and PAME), as well as successfully launching the Arctic Climate Impact assessment reports and the Arctic Human Development Report (AHDR) whilst it held the Arctic Council chairmanship in 2003-2004.

## 6.7. Norwegian Arctic Policy

Since the turn of the millennia Norway was the first state to produce an Arctic strategy and policy; 2003 was met with an expert report outlining the strategic interests of the Arctic. This report was followed in 2006 with “The Norwegian Government’s High North Strategy” [54], which defined the High North as an area of interest for the government, placing explicit attention on the environment, indigenous populations, business, scientific knowledge and foreign policy. Furthermore, in 2009 Norway produced another follow-strategy. This was again presenting a series of Arctic areas which support and focus on the main priorities set out in the 2003 report [55], 2014 saw the publication of Nordkloden, yet another government strategy. The most recent report was published in 2017, “Between geopolitics and social development” [56], serving as another update to Norway’s Arctic strategy.

The 2017 report identifies a vision for the Arctic, and presents strategies for helping build peace, innovation and sustainability in this region, encompasses both foreign and domestic policy. The four overarching goals of the report are:

- 1. Peace, stability and predictability
- 2. Integrated, ecosystem-based management
- 3. International cooperation and the international legal order
- 4. A stronger basis for employment, value creation and welfare

The five priorities in the 2017 report, and separated into sections, are as follows:

- 1. Cooperation with specific regard to Arctic states and the EU, using existing formed in the Arctic, promoting security and respect to indigenous communities.
- 2. Providing sustainability whilst utilizing the resources of the Arctic, supporting research-based value creation and supporting greater connection between business development and the research community. Furthermore, increasing accessibility of North Sea Routes is utilizing the region to play a large role in an expansive shipping and commerce industry, thus instigating Norway to strengthen their supplier industry.
