China is already involved in several major petroleum projects in the Arctic. For instance, the Bank of China and the China Investment Corporation are in talks to provide 75 percent of the funding for the Alaska natural gas project after U.S. oil companies stepped [105], [109]. In return, China's largest oil company, China Petroleum Corporation, will have a 75 percent stake in the project (with the Alaska Gasoline Development Corporation being responsible for the remainder) [105], [109]. China is also heavily invested in the Yamal Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) project, which worth approximately 27 billion dollars. The project's “proven and probable reserves... are estimated at 926 billion cubic meters and it has a capacity of 16.5 million tons per year [110]. China's investment in Yamal is also providing dividends the plant beginning commercial operations in 2017. Within seven months of the plant's opening, China received its first LNG cargo delivered via the NSR [111]. A new Russian LNG project is in the works and expected to begin producing by 2022-2023 [111]; Chinese companies have also acquired a major stake in the project [112].

Through investing in Arctic and natural gas projects, China helps ensure receives a steady stream of natural energy resources. Additionally, these investments allow China to influence over Arctic infrastructure projects, Arctic resources, and even Arctic nations.

# 2.3.1.1.2.2. Fisheries

China is excited by the prospect of Arctic fisheries. The Chinese public demand for fish has been increasing rapidly as populations in China's coastal seas have collapsed [113]. As result, China has enlarged and upgraded its distant-water fishing fleet [113]. Meanwhile, Chinese scientists are advocating for Beijing to prioritize overseas fisheries in its long-term agricultural development strategy [113]. The Arctic has major fish stocks, which are “some of the most productive in the world” [114]. Climate change is also likely to create new fisheries in the Arctic, and fish stocks are steadily moving northward in recent years [114], [115]. Accordingly, it is possible that China will seek to expand its access and control over portions of the Arctic Ocean in order to satisfy its population's demands for fish.

# 2.3.1.1.2.3. Minerals

The Arctic is rich in mineral deposits, and climate change is making the mining of these minerals increasingly economically and logistically feasible [116]. Minerals in Siberia are expected to be worth up to two trillion dollars [117]. Arctic Norway and Sweden have billions of tons of iron between them, while Arctic Alaska is rich in zinc and lead [117]. Canada has many valuable minerals, including diamond, gold, iron, zinc, salt, and uranium [117]. Most important right now for China, though, are the resources of Greenland, which consist of gold, molybdenum, nickel, platinum group elements, and rare earth elements [117]. Rare earth elements are high-technology and sustainable technologies.

Chinese companies are actively involved in Greenland's emerging mining sector. China's Sheng Resources has a 12.5 percent stake in the Kvanefeld mine, one of the largest potential deposits of rare earth elements in the world [107]. China Nonferrous Metals, also views the Citronen zinc mine [117]. Chinese firms are expected to continue to seek opportunities to invest in mineral, particularly in Greenland but potentially across the Arctic region. This gives China both a financial and geophysical interest in Arctic resources.
