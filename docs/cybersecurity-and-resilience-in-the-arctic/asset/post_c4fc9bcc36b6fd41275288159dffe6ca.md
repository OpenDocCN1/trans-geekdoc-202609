For example:

- Studies to human factors have shown that cognitive biases, born of hierarchical power structures present in the airline cockpit, can result in airline disasters by compromised communication and lack of action. During the approach of Air flight 6560, the relatively inexperienced first officer noticed that the aircraft was veering off-course. The captain—who, unlike the first officer, had flown many times over the Arctic region—believed the instruments were adversely impacted by the proximity of the north magnetic pole. Assuming it was the first officer’s lack of experience affecting his perception of the situation, the captain ignored the repeated warnings and suggestions to go around. The mistake proved fatal to the captain, the first officer and 10 passengers when the plane crashed. The higher one perceives oneself to be in the hierarchy, the less negative feedback individual will accept. Moreover, the greater power imbalance between the captain and the first officer, the higher the risk of false-consensus bias (as an authoritative position based on belief that approval is automatically granted by the less experienced pilot) in the cockpit. Communication that validates and assesses personal perspectives to a given situation is critical to informed decision-making in any context (Fabre, 2018).

When people reside there that they have no voice in decisions which affect them either directly or indirectly, this intervention is viewed with distrust. As highlighted above, the rural township of Cigliano, was frustrated by a centralized policy process, which imposed a primary sector dominance. Citizens felt marginalized, especially due to insufficient infrastructure, population decline, rising unemployment, and outmigration of economy-active groups. A sense of belief—in one’s ability to potentially bring about change—is positively reinforced through experiential transformation of that belief in enough agency to act or mitigate a situation. People are thinkers and, in that regard, have the inherent ability to become directing their own actions. Active agency enables one to distinguish between a conscious agency, and an actual experience (Bayne & Pacher, 2007), to become an active agent for change. In Arctic, the importance of active agents (which literally translates to soul), is often very significant. These individuals are deeply engaged in local activities, and plan and carry out activities that benefit their community in some way (for example, they organize festivals, build facilities, and initiate start-up businesses to fill a void), driving climate adaptation.

According to Frankl (1992), the constitutive characteristic of humans to extend one's purely existence to something beyond oneself which he attaches, gives the individual a cause for living. Self-transcendence is necessary in order to make sense of conditions which cannot be willed away. Self-transcendence allows one to see situations beyond one's vantage point. Personal actions, including relating to adaptive behaviors, are perceived to bring positive results, become preferred behaviors which can be replicated to some extent (1990, 1996; 1997). When accompanied by optimism that these experiences can be replicated in the future (Radden & Walker, 1997), personal adaptive processes become hardwired and can be applied in creative ways to transform disruptive regime into sustainable resilience (Arctic Council, 2016).

## 3.2. Creativity

Agency mobilizes creative processes that are particularly important in complex situations. Creativity is critical to survival of both individual and society. That dependency can be expressed in terms of four categories (Tannenbaum, 1983):
a) Scarcity-talents in short supply but needed by all people;
b) Surplus-talents designed by society but not essential for survival;
c) Anomalous talents between scarcity and surplus;
d) Exceptional exceptions with practical use.

Extraordinary creativity is particularly important in the face of substantial uncertainty. The human system of adaptation is a complex, integrated formation, related to experiential history. Vygotsky's (1978) theoretical framework on behavioral psychology offers insight into the complex and nuanced dynamics of functional systems in response to internal and external stimuli, in varying contexts, as required under adaptive conditions. Viewing humans as highly plastic, and malleable organizations situated in cultural environments that will eventually change, Vygotsky emphasizes "the interactions between social change and the biological status of behavior" (p.123).

Perhaps this ability to think outside the box is related to experiencing outside the box. Current social theory diverges from the Cartesian model of dualism (René Descartes' theory of mind and body as separate functional systems), and embraces functionalism as a combination of cognitive, sensory and behavioral processes (Bandura, 1978, 1989), the long-neglected concept of reflexivity—the capacity of individuals to physically and mentally experience and realize their actions in a meaningful way (Archer, 2007, 2013). Reflexivity offers the opportunity to learn from past experiences in order to “coordinate and re-shape practices” (Ostuki et al., 2018), influence self-agency, and encourage resilience (Archer, 2007, 2013). Individual actions from personal reflection on experiences which are deemed meaningful—especially experiments—ultimately lead to varying degrees of changes in cultural, ecological, and political domains as personal reflections initiate efforts in coordination within a system (Smith, Dubois, & Krasny, 2016; Freire, 2001). An SES approach taking a nonlinear view of systems development, is exemplified by the adaptive cycle of the four phases—exploitation, consumption, release, and reorganization (Gunderson & Holland, 2006).

While reflexivity is important, some of the most creative efforts involve people. Gladwell (2013) describes how urgent situations create in people a sense of urgency, whether due to overwhelming looming deadlines, or compelling drives—as in the biblical victory over Goliath—conceived highly creative solutions. People who are successful are often unwilling to be disagreeable; act despite a lack of external approval. Similarly, Peter Diamands, founder of the PRIZE foundation, asserts that the privileged are able to overcome conventional wisdom (the risk aversion of government) to achieve possible feats (Diamands & Kolter, 2012); modern examples including commercial space flight, nanomaterials, and artificial intelligence. Some of today’s most successful inventors and entrepreneurs fit the mold, such as:

- Dean Kamen — college drop-out and inventor of disruptive mobility, health, and quality of life inventions, most famous as the inventor of the Segway
- Steve Jobs — college drop-out who was put up for adoption as a child started later, today’s most valuable company
- Bill Gates — college drop-out who was banned from Computer Center’s terminals for finding software bugs as a teenager for experimenting during off-hours with friend, Paul Allen. Founded Microsoft and later the Bill and Melinda Foundation, which is leading highly-effective efforts to improve life on a global scale.

In the Arctic, this phenomenon of creativity of urgency is alive and well. A study of Kinalina (2004) observes that the nature of the traditional subsistence culture reflects a shared process that combines motivation and responsibility towards the environment. Many suggest that this connection places inhabitants in a unique position to find solutions in the face of complex change.
