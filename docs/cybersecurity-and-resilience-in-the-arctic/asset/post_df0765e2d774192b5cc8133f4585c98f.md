As mentioned above, the STPA method can be used to analyze the risks in the CyberShip framework is a risk and hazard analysis technique approach (Leesson 2011). It focuses on individual component failure as well as complex system interactions including humans in the loop. Systems fail when the safety or security constraints are violated. The focus is to enforce and security constraints on system behavior to defend the system from cyber attacks. The STPA analysis consists of five steps:

1. System identification and description: System goals, the boundaries of the system, and information flow between components of the system have to be specified.
2. Boundary identifications in three domains: Unacceptable losses, hazards, and control actions should be identified. The unacceptable losses or accidents are events occurring during system operation. For example, collision of the ship is an accident. The hazards are those states which can cause accidents if conditions are violated.
3. Unsafe control action identification: Unsafe control actions are actions which cause a hazard. There are four types of unsafe control actions:
   (a) Control action is performed, causing the hazard,
   (b) Control action is not performed, and this leads to the hazard,
   (c) Control action is performed too early or too late, causing the hazard
   (d) Control action is applied for too long or too short time, and this leads to the hazard.

While the STPA methodology support risk assessment in the CyberShip framework, more work is needed to propose recommendation for mitigating these risks for improved resilience.

## 3.2. Organizational Challenges

Cyber resilience with regard to critical infrastructure in the Arctic region must be built on a foundation of resilient Arctic societies. Policies, established through governmental efforts, are key drivers of strengthening societal resilience in the Arctic. Societal resilience is defined here as "the ability of individuals and communities to adapt, reorganize and improve in the midst of shocks and stresses" (Trump, et al. 2016). Examining cyber resilience in the Arctic through the lens of societal resilience is useful to understand the level of international governance relations in addressing cyber security concerns and building cyber resilience.

Societal resilience must be considered in terms of the capacity of a society to respond to vulnerabilities. Societal vulnerabilities stem from inequalities in access to resources and information and political marginalization within and between communities (Cutter et al. 2008). These vulnerabilities account for differences in preparedness, impact, and response to shocks and stresses across a society (Cutter et al. 2008). Arctic resilience is threatened by the compounding impacts of a number of vulnerabilities, including climate change, vast changes and a sparse population, cold pollutants and contaminants, increases in resource development and tourism, environmental degradation, rapid economic development, industrialization, integration into global markets, erosion of cultural traditions, limited political participation of local and Indigenous communities, disputes over autonomous, conflicts over land use, and access to digital connectivity and broadband (Salimen & Hossain, 2018 and Hossain, Joer, Grever, Roncero and Sheenan, 2017). The Arctic is comprised of complex socio-economic, ecological, and infrastructure systems. Arctic systems are especially intertwined; therefore, threats to one system have a profound effect on the security of another. For example, environmental and health "threats" to the socio-ecological systems in the Arctic caused by oil spills affect critical infrastructure and cyber systems as well. Thus, a holistic, societal resilience approach to Arctic security is useful to understand how building resilient Arctic societies strengthens cyber resilience.

## 3.2.1. Role of International Relations in the Arctic

The vulnerabilities affecting Arctic societies are multi-jurisdictional in nature and are not contained within the confines of national borders. Thus, an effective approach to addressing these vulnerabilities will be multi-jurisdictional as well. International cooperation to address vulnerabilities in the Arctic from all governance levels and must provide space for interactions between scientists and policy-makers (Smieszek, Stepien & Kankaanpa, 2016). The national Arctic strategies of the eight Arctic states, as well as the Arctic Council Ministerial Declarations, have all recognized the indispensable role of international cooperation in addressing Arctic challenges (Arctic Council, 2016).

The Arctic is a low-conflict region characterized by international cooperation (Hossain et al. 2017). Cooperation in the Arctic occurs at the community, local, subnational, regional, and national levels, as well as Arctic-specific platforms. The result is a complex framework of overlapping and linked stakeholders. While actors involved in Arctic governance may have varying interests, the resilience of local communities is a function of decisions made at many levels (Arctic Council, 2016). Thus, governance organizations can play a role in enhancing or societal resilience.

International relations and diplomacy are tools that allow states to align incentives and allow for meaningful cooperation towards shared priorities, for example, safety and security, environmental protection, and supporting Arctic communities (Trump et al. 2016). These tools are useful for fostering stability in the region because they promote trust and confidence-building between states. Where there may be tension in the context of general bilateral relationship, cooperation on shared interests is a channel for maintaining open dialogue. Mr. Pertti Salolainen, Member of Parliament of the Republic of Finland, highlights how the Arctic Council has this to effect, "as an example of Arctic resilience: in spite of the generally negative trend in relations, the Arctic Council has managed to strengthen regional stability and even expand the area of constructive cooperation" (House of Commons, 2018). (Arctic Council, 2016). International cooperation based on trust and confidence is essential for Arctic governance organizations to be effective at strengthening societal resilience. To facilitate international cooperation, specifically with regard to cyber security and protecting critical infrastructure, stakeholders in the Arctic require a "coherent and cohesive framework governance prevention and response to threats" (Cassotta & Sidortsov, 2019). Through this legal framework, governance organizations play a role in improving the resilience capacity of systems in the Arctic before, during, and after a shock or stress affects the system (Trump, et al. 2016). Governance organizations must engage with each other on an international level to negotiate and adopt legal frameworks through which they can promote societal resilience, anticipate various shocks and stresses, and take appropriate steps to fund systems that build societal resilience (Trump, et al. 2016).
