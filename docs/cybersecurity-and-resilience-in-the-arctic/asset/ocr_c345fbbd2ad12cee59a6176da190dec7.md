# 3. Arctic Development: Emerging Challenges and the Need for Resilience

Defense, transportation, social/indigenous lifestyles, and business opportunities in the Arctic are accompanied by the danger of cyber-threats, especially to critical infrastructures in the Arctic “extra critical” because of the harsh environmental conditions and remoteness (Chapter 1 for a discussion of the growing resilience upon complex interconnected digital systems and infrastructure in the Arctic and sub-Arctic). Critical infrastructures in the Arctic are also crucial for military and security since its data hubs, significant energy resources are critical for digitalization, internet and computers'. Interferences with climatic conditions, ice, and disasters, require new approaches to risk and resilience assessment and also effective legal frameworks to protect critical infrastructures and sustain both industrial and military activities.

The melting of Arctic ice presents new opportunities for growth and new challenges as various nations expand their footprint into the region. The private sector, shipping and tourism efforts benefit from access to the Northwest Passage through Canada and the Northern Sea Route above. The thaw also exposes oil and gas fields that grow up to a quarter of the Earth’s obtainable petroleum, offering an incentive for oil and gas companies to move more north. In the public sector, military transportation, defense, and search and rescue operations these interest groups, both civil and military, will need to keep pace with private sector growth. As they operate in the Arctic, they bring with them the need for expanded Arctic infrastructure, both temporary and permanent [63].

This additional infrastructure brings with it the potential for larger disasters due to cyber disruption. For example, the Kolos data center to be built in Ballangen, Norway plans to take advantage of hydroelectric power and use the cold to cool out its servers. The large amount of data stored makes it a prime target for hacking or physical attack, and damage to the facility, whether cyber or physical, will be difficult to repair due to the facility’s remote location. Even temporary infrastructure could potentially pose risks; consider the voyage of the American Crystal Serpent, which in 2016 was the largest cruise ship to navigate the Northwest Passage. While this voyage was successful, the disastrous results of the ship’s onboard computer being hacked were numerous, including loss of life of the passengers and spillage of oil and other materials due to the purposeful scattering of container and passenger ships near Arctic waters.

The traditional approach to an attack is to “harden” the system, in order to prevent it from penetrating. However, this approach to security is generally ineffective in the long term; more necessary is resilience, the ability for a system to recover from disruption or failure [33, 62]. In terms of the previous examples, resilience for the cruise ship means building checks along the ship’s path and having search-and-rescue and clean cleanup booths to minimize damage. For the data center, it means extra staff to correct errors introduced by hacking and developing evacuation plans in case of physical emergency. The challenges of Arctic infrastructure are not unique to the Arctic, but they are certainly exacerbated by harsh conditions and must be handled differently from lower latitudes.

What makes resilience critical objective for Arctic survival is that increasing scale and complexity of activity within previously uninhabitable regions, as well as the growing reliance upon complex digital systems to operate various elements of infrastructure make modern life possible.
