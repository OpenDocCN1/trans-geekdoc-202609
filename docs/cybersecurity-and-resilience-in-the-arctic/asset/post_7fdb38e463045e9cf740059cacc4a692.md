## 1.4.1. Land-Based Infrastructure

Road networks in the Arctic are sparse; being restricted to populated areas, Arctic roads serve local rather than inter-regional needs. Outside northern Scandinavia, good roads are increasingly uncommon. Unlike parts of the world, Arctic land is 'travel-friendly' in winter, when the tundra is frozen and ice bridges serve thoroughfares for vehicles from sleds to heavy industrial equipment. In summer, when the top layer of permafrost thaws and the terrain is buggy, overland travel is diff.

The Dempster 11, more commonly known as the Inuvik–Tuktoytuk Highway (or ITH), is a new road link between Inuvik and Tuktoytuk in Canada's Northwest Territories. It is the "first all-weather road to Canada's Arctic Coast". Officially opened in November 2017, Tuktoytuk was finally opened up to traffic all year-round. Initiatives like this in the Arctic are rare, with increasing activity by commercial and military sectors, may become more common and necessary.

Russia's Trans-Siberian railway travels 9,000km and connects Moscow with Russia's Far Eastern provinces, Mongolia, China and Japan. Other Arctic railways are sparse; the 7200km Nordland line runs the length of Norway, up into the Arctic circle, and Rail connects across Norway, Sweden, Finland and Denmark. Train lines from Alaska and Canada also exist, but rarely venture into the Arctic Circle. Initiatives to build a new railway in Europe through Finland and the north of Norway are also being discussed, with Finland continually pushing for its establishment, the Arctic Business Forum in the spring of 2018 [34].

### 1.4.1.1. Airports

Many airports populate the Arctic; however, few are large enough to land large commercial airlines and are mostly intended for smaller aviation activities. Kangerlussaa in Greenland is one of the only civilian airports in the Arctic to receive large airlines, as well as Longyear in Svalbard, and Sabet Airport in Russia as of 2015.

Ports of entry are places where one could legally enter the foreign country. There are 676 airports currently maintained around the Arctic and majority of them are situated in Alaska. There are 79 airports in Arctic Canada, 71 in Russia, 62 in Denmark (Greenland and Faroe Islands), 56 in Norwegian Arctic including Svalbard, 55 in Finland, 35 in Sweden and close to 20 in Iceland. Not all of them exist as ports of entries for international traffic as many are only domestically [35].

## 1.4.2. Marine Infrastructure

### 1.4.2.1. Ports

Cargo transit along the Northern Sea Route reached a volume of seven million tons in 2016, rising to 17 million in 2018 and is expected to reach 75 million tons per year in 2025 [37]. With these figures in mind, Russia has been proceeding with the construction and development of deep-water ports and associated infrastructure along its Northern and Eastern coastlines, with $300 billion already invested, and another $82 billion declared in investments over the next five years.

In Russia, major ports include Murmansk, Dikanga, Igarka, Tiksi, Arkhangelsk, Dickson, Pevek, Sabetta and Uelen. Major Canadian ports include Churchill, Iqaluit, Mittimatalik, and Tuktoyaktuk. In Norway the ports of Kirkenes, Varda, Tromso, Hammerfest, Harstad and Kokkola are the largest, while the USA’s largest Arctic ports reside in Anchorage, Dutch Harbor, Barrow, Valdez and Red Dog [38].

## 1.4.3. Telecommunication

Connection to the world's largest telecommunication network, is an integral part of everyday life in the Arctic. Communities separated by vast across the Arctic are often far from the outside world, and while technology cannot bridge all of the resulting social gaps, the security and wellbeing of people in these communities greatly facilitated by communication technology, helping to secure social stability in their northernmost locations.

The Task Force on Telecommunication Infrastructure in the Arctic (TFTIA) was established by Ministers of the Arctic States at the 2015 Arctic Council Ministerial in Iqaluit. Ministers noted "the importance of telecommunications to Arctic communities, science, navigation and emergency response" and created the TFTIA to "develop a circular infrastructure assessment as a first step in ways to improve telecommunications in the Arctic, and report to Ministers in 2017"

## 1.5. Alternative commercial elements

### 1.5.1. Fishing

As the effects of climate change are, the Arctic warms at twice the speed of the rest of the planet, the volume and distribution of fish stocks will change. Populations of cod and halibut are likely to move northward, and overfishing in traditional grounds will make new areas more appealing [47]. Temperature increases in the ocean have created favorable conditions that typically boost fish populations such as an increase in levels of plankton, which this is countered by the impacts of ocean acidification which may limit expected growths.
