Governance can be understood as "the reflexive self-organization of independent actors involved in complex relations of reciprocal interdependence" (Jessop 2003). As such, it encompasses all processes of governance taking place within the modalities of command (by hierarchical government), dialogue (with networks), and exchange (as a basis of market and dictated by market forces). While the governmental authority is vested with responsibility and accountability, from perspective of governance it is limited or transformed in the modern world. This is precisely because of the existence of various actors and the operation of markets, including autonomous rules through these markets operate. Only totalitarian authoritarian dictatorships claim the capability of limiting all governance to the processes of governmental command.

The aforementioned complexity of Arctic governance landscape and changes taking place in the region mean today a number of different actors are engaged in the governance of the Arctic. Not all of these actors are actually from the Arctic, nor in line with the aforementioned multidimensional character of governance – are all of those with interest in governing the Arctic nations of states. From the governance perspective, the national governments are powerful actors, but joined in governance by private sector actors and the global market forces as well as diverse non actors engaged in networks of interdependence. In addition, networks between different components of state bureaucracies have helped building further foundations for long-term international cooperation. This is not surprising, given the growing interest in the region and the importance of international cooperation for the Arctic. Indeed, the Arctic has long since for peaceful cooperation. International cooperation is characterized by a palpable sense of exceptionalism insofar particular between the West and the Russian Federation – often play a reduced role in the Arctic. The notion of Arctic exceptionalism builds upon the existence of networks of interdependence among various Arctic state and non-common interests, major conflicts of interest as well as the appreciation of economic value of a peaceful and well-regulated region (Byers 2017). However, as some authors point out (Käylä and Mikkola 2015), the Arctic cannot be viewed as a closed international system and thus the perceived exceptionalism may be superficial in character. In a way, cooperation in the extreme environment of the Arctic remains cooperation in outer space that takes precedence over local political considerations. In both areas, the risk that outside political concerns or domestic politics in Arctic states may hinder international cooperation is not to be ignored. The 2019 ministerial meeting of the Arctic Council – the key forum for international governance and cooperation in the Arctic – which took place in Rovaniemi, Finland, was a good example of the potential for the dynamics outside the Arctic to damaging influence on Arctic cooperation. The US Secretary of State voiced rightly concerns about the presence of China in the Arctic and prevented option of a ministerial declaration by the inclusion of climate change language into the document – undermining the Arctic cooperation by subverting it to the ideological stance of trustance and the US domestic policies. In addition to nation states, the indigenous peoples of the Arctic play an active role in shaping the future of the Arctic. This is visible in the Arctic Council where indigenous organizations hold a unique status – the so-called permanent participants. The Arctic Council is also demonstrative, as is the Arctic Ocean Fisheries Agreement, of an increasing interest in the Arctic from outside actors, such as the People’s Republic of China.
