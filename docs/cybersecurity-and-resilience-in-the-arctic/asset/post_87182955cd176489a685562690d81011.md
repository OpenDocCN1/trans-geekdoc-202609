New actors in the Arctic are predominantly from Asian states and with an increasingly active role in the Circumpolar North decision-making and governance. They include (see Box 1 for a more comprehensive list):

- Non-Arctic States (i.e., Japan, People's Republic of China, Republic of Korea)
- NGOs (i.e., environmental and conservationist)
- Private corporations (i.e., shipping, gas/oil, tourism, fishing, etc.)

For example, Japan released its Arctic Policy in 2015, emphasizing their aims towards international cooperation and ensuring the power of the rule of law in the Arctic [71]. On the other hand, China's Arctic Policy in 2018 advocated the creation of a 'Polar Silk Road,' highlighting its economic power and attempting to strengthen its position as a critical actor in Arctic governance. Additionally, a few NGOs have impacted Indigenous Peoples' subsistence and livelihoods by shaping laws that curtail access to sealing, whaling, and hydrocarbon extraction. Moreover, private interests have attempted to utilize accessible sea routes and resources through international law of the seas legislation.

Nevertheless, Arctic vs. non-Arctic dichotomy remains, especially in key international laws such as the Agreement to Prevent Unregulated High Seas Fisheries in the Central Arctic Ocean, the melting polar sea ice straining existing legal and policy arrangements, particularly due to the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). With sea ice in the Arctic, an increasing number of Arctic and non-Arctic actors will try to exercise their national freedoms for economic and political interests. Geopolitical tensions are growing with renewed strains in relations between global powers (e.g., Russia). The existing governance structure is complex and fragile. While the Arctic Council has so far successfully as forum for negotiations, its current structure presents challenges towards adapting to a rapidly changing Arctic. With such a political environment, challenges abound. The Arctic is mostly governed by transnational law. Law is often reactionary and conservative, and not precautionary by nature. Law is not a good mechanism to mitigate risks. Often an incident has to occur before a law is made. Moreover, most legal systems are not adept at dealing with issues that are poorly defined and unamenable to the prediction.

## Challenges in Establishing Legal Frameworks and Governance

342 K. Klasa et al / New forms of governance are arising, the locus of power is shifting. What might be effective governance is not necessarily an effective government or the result of an effective government. Governance is not government. This is particularly important with the numerous agents driving the Arctic. Existing tools, networks, concepts, and frameworks developed for midlatitude regions may not be appropriate for rural Arctic areas. What is the creation of a comprehensive governance system that ensures accountability and responsibility for actions demanding peace, stability, and equity [67]. The governance challenges facing the Arctic fall into four distinct themes: (1) perception, (2) globalization, (3) environmentalism and sustainability, and (4) militarization (see Figure 15). The first challenge is one of perception. Public opinion of the Arctic, especially among non-Arctic states, is characterized by a misguided view as an unregulated, lawless place. The reality of climate change remains despite a preponderance of scientific evidence [73]. Other public perceptions of nature and the environment (such as polar bears, walrus from cliffs, polluting the oceans) have led to no-tolerance conservationist agendas that stifle traditional ways of life. Perception also differs within the Arctic Council—different countries having differing political and economic interests and interest in collaboration. Likewise, there is a strong division between “southern” views of problems and “northern” views of problems. This dichotomy is similar to the urban-rural divide which the Arctic being predominantly rural and most urban centers located in the southern parts of a country (i.e., Helsinki in Finland, Oslo in Norway, Ottawa in Canada). At the national level this difference in perspectives has led to tensions between the areas above the Arctic and below. Most governmental decision-making is made in the southern, urban centers, leaving out Northern citizens from the decision-making process. Moreover, national policies and laws try to use southern and urban solutions to fix northern—a strategy that does not allow for the local context to be on the governance of Circumpolar Northern regions. The multitude of varied perceptions between countries and within countries are difficult to understand and balance. The Circumpolar North is home to numerous, diverse Indigenous people with unique cultural traditions and activities.

The second challenge comes from globalization. It is bringing new actors into the Arctic region, changing the political, social, and economic environments. Globalization is also altering which actors are present in the high north and should have a say in Arctic affairs. The Arctic has seen a regime change from a paradigm of “regional” to “global” [74]. The Arctic is not secluded to just Arctic states and actors. Global warming and a changing polar environment have allowed others to enter the Arctic political area, increasing the number of players in decision-making and the complexity of governance and law-making.

The third challenge arises from the politics of sustainability. Sustainability is a growing political conflict, especially in the Arctic where it is given new meaning with political implications [75]. Sustainability is a relationship between identity, space, and time, often used to understand human development (i.e., human identity) and its relationship with nature [75]. Conflicting visions of the future, sustainability narratives, and what should be sustained and by whom are ongoing discussions driving Arctic governance.
