Territorial disputes in the name of claiming resources are not the only issues that have to be solved in the Arctic. Search and Rescue in international law has a complex issue, with the solution in the Arctic coming in the form of the first legal binding instrument negotiated and adopted under the guidance of the Arctic Council (Kao, Pearce and Firestone, 2012). The Arctic Council’s ‘soft law’ regime promotes cooperation through the goodwill of participating states, rather than enforceable law where states are held to word through enforceable legal mechanisms. Previously, a state’s duty to provide maritime assistance for individuals and vessels in distress have fallen under multiple different frameworks, including UNCLOS and other conventions under the International Maritime Organization (IMO), as international conventions for Safety of Life at Sea, Maritime Search and Rescue, and International Convention on Salvage. The SAR convention, established in 1979, was the first to directly establish responsibility for SAR across global regions, as the Maritime Safety Committee divided global responsibilities across 13 areas (IMO, 2000). Within these areas, states are responsible for delimiting where all coastal states choose to address and rescue issues, is unilateral.

The purpose of the Arctic Search and Rescue cooperation was therefore to strengthen arctic and maritime SAR cooperation (Rotterdam, 2014). With this came delimination of territories which having taken into account the existing reality disputes became problematic. These issues of territorial sovereignty needed to be circumvented, was achieved by Article 3(2) (Arctic Council, 2011), whereby a clarification was made, noting that the delimitations of SAR regions are in no way related to, and not prejudice, the delimitation of any boundary between states or their sovereignty, rights, or jurisdiction. It could be argued that a shift towards a law regime in the Arctic will foster resilience in maritime domains, as greater political and bureaucratic commitments, inspiring firmer institutions, and enhancing long-term viewpoints alongside inroads of shifting personnel, will improve stability in the region.
