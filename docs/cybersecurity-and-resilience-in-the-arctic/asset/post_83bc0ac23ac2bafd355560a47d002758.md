## 2.1 Current actors in the Arctic

Defined by the Arctic Circle, the Arctic encompasses portions of eight states: The United States, Russia, Canada, Norway, Finland, Sweden, Denmark (via Greenland), and Iceland. Some of these states have territory within the Arctic Circle but do not share a border with the Arctic seas: Finland, Sweden, and Iceland signed the eight Arctic Environmental Protection Strategy (AEPS) a multilateral, non-binding agreement on environmental protection in the Arctic. Numerous significant environmental problems were identified at that time, including persistent organic pollutants (POPs), oil pollution, heavy metals, radioactivity, and acidification (AEPS, 1991). AEPS was a guide and commitment from these states to begin tackling these issues through the development of Action Plans, organized through six working groups. These groups have since addressed a range of issues including consuming fauna and flora, environmental monitoring and assessment, and reducing the level of contaminants in the Arctic (Exerc-Pierot et al., 2019; Rothwell, 2008)

AEPS soon laid the groundwork for more-ranging governmental commitment to cooperation, which led to the formation of the Arctic Council five later in 1996. The Arctic Council is comprised of the eight AEPS signatories, as well as states granted "observer status." Because the Arctic is an area of global importance, it attracts significant attention from states below the Arctic Circle. The Arctic Council represents these interests by providing observer status to several non-Arctic states, including Germany, the Netherlands, Poland, the United Kingdom, France, Spain, China, India, Italy, Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland. These member states have no voting rights but are invited to visit Council meetings. Furthermore, approved intergovernmental and interparliamentary organizations and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) can obtain observer status. Some of these organizations include the Nordic Council, the UN Development Programme, and the World Wide Fund for Nature-Arctic Programme (with the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis).

"Permanent participants" in the group of six Arctic indigenous groups are also represented in the Arctic Council. These groups are Aleut International Association, the Arctic Athabaskan Council, Gwich' Council International, the Inuit Circumpolar Council, the Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North, and the Saami Council. These indigenous councils represent native Arctic actors and work as official organizational bodies to represent a diverse range of indigenous groups. They have full consultation representing discussions and decisions of the Council. "permanent participants" are eligible to join, so long as they represent a single indigenous people residing in more than one Arctic State, or multiple indigenous peoples residing in a single Arctic State (Arctic Council, 2015).

## 2.2 Indigenous populations

Indigenous peoples have lived in the Arctic for thousands of years. There are currently over 500,000 indigenous people in the Arctic out of a total of about 8 million (Arctic Council, 2015). While 40 different ethnic groups are recognized in this region (Arctic Centre, 2019). While many differences exist between these groups, similarity is certain; all indigenous populations have undergone substantial changes, primarily due to Western globalization, state policies and legislation, modern transportation, and the introduction of mixed economy. Furthermore, human activity increasing the rate of climate change is believed to have had negative impacts on these communities, unpredictable economic, social and ecological changes disrupt their way of life (Lehti et al., 2009). Traditional methods of subsistence are becoming increasingly challenging as weather conditions become variable in both extremes and melting sea ice ecosystems and animals reliant on ice cover, such as polar bears, seals and catch fish. As a result, the livelihoods of many indigenous peoples and communities are under threat. Fears of increased contaminants, restricted use, security, and access to resources are also present among indigenous communities.

Modernization has increased the frequency, extent, and impact of outsider interaction with indigenous communities, which in turn has had a number of discernible negative consequences. Recent introduction of new stressors has had significant impact on indigenous communities' health (Bjjergaard et al., 2004), and changing lifestyles are introducing atherosclerotic diseases and diabetes, as well as cancers related to consumption of alcohol and cigarettes (Bjjergaard, Mulwaard and 2004; Mulwaard and Pedersen, 1997). Suicide rates among young indigenous peoples in the Arctic are very high, and abuse is common (Lehti et al., 2009). Fears of increased contaminants, restricted use, security, and access to resources are also present among indigenous communities.
