3.1.1.1. Disruptions of the Digital Infrastructure
Branlat et al. (2011) have described the essential challenges of cyber defense. Essentially, defending against cyber events corresponds to two questions: 1) Is the activity on the network legitimate or illegitimate? 2) How suspicious activity be handled? Both questions are deceptively simple and obscure the underlying fundamentals.

Identifying nefarious activity occurring on the network corresponds to a weak signal/noise background problem, with users and services generating a lot of traffic. As networks expand due to the development of connected services, the network is never entirely, and some legitimate activity might be detected as suspicious. In addition, some organizations live in a context of constant probing for cyber vulnerabilities, most of them being non-critical but participating in the noisy background. To support cyber defenders, automated systems are used to treat the massive amounts of network and systems data. However, but in part of the impossibility of fully describing the network and its potential activity, those tools generate significant amounts of false positives and false negatives. Interestingly, the investigation of cyber activity is a question that lies at the intersection of independent roles, tasks, and domains of expertise: network data is not only critical data, but need to be correlated with relevant activity on the systems (e.g., changes in configuration file), on databases (e.g., new transactions), or on software. Especially in large organizations and networks, these pieces of information are the responsibility of different teams and individuals. Cyber activity detection can therefore be affected by coordination breakdowns within and across teams, as well as potential conflicts (e.g., the need to maintain support to distributed services vs. the need to limit connections between network assets).

When suspicious activity is detected (which can, in itself, take months), the question of what to do is it non-trivial: answering the consideration of trade-offs. If the network and its assets provide valuable services that may be jeopardized by responding closing connectivity, a trade-off between security and production in the organization that benefits from the network services. Furthermore, cyber defenders are typically not the network owners, i.e they need to justify the potentially interrupt connectivity (and related services) on the basis of evidence – this is not a simple task due to the general level of uncertainty mentioned above. This relates to the scope of the responsibility, authority, and autonomy of the network defense teams, issues which should be addressed before events occur. Trade-offs may even arise from different security goals, with situations benefiting from letting the situation develop in order to better understand (and ultimately) address.
