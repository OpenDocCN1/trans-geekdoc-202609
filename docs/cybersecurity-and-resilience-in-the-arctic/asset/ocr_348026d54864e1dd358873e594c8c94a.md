The Arctic Council engages with many issues that are part of a broadly understood human security – in particular in the context of adaptation, environmental change impacts and resilience – even if the word “security” is usually avoided.

The Arctic Council is not an international organization in the classic sense of the term. Instead, the Arctic states define the AC as an intergovernmental cooperation body serving as “high-level forum” (Arctic Council, 1996). The Arctic Council, opposed to any other regulatory body, has an entity that produces, through its working groups, technical recommendations and guidelines and influential scientific assessments. It offers a confidence building mechanism through which countries have been able to continue and develop to Arctic governance that helps prevent tension. In the last decade, the Arctic Council has also been used to negotiate international Arctic-specific agreements (on maritime and aeronautical search, on oil response, and on scientific cooperation) as well as attempts to agree on commitments on pollution reduction (as regards black carbon and methane emissions). Through its work, both soft and hard, through the international treaties negotiated by the member states under its auspices, the Arctic Council has successfully attracted the attention of key policymakers to the matter of specific environmental and economic developments. As a result, it has member states and policymakers outside the area (Young & Kankaampää, 2012; Young, 2013). Today, the AC has gradually emerged as a new source of authority where both regionalism and regionalization hold a strong place. Regionalism and regionalization do not necessarily run in parallel; while regionalism is about the promotion of integration of various sectors, as environmental protection, governance, and security, regionalization, on the other hand, is a result of cohesion of social and economic interaction among private enterprises and civil societies (Ingimdanson 2014: 186). Various non-regional actors acknowledge a source of authority. The EU, for example, has declared in its number of policy documents on the Arctic that the AC is the most important cooperation forum to deal with Arctic (European Union and High Representative 2016). Acknowledging such authority of the AC, a number of actors from the Arctic region, including non-Arctic states participate in the AC’s structure. Many today observe that the AC has exceeded expectations of being a body of cooperation for shaping policies (Young & Kankaampää, 2012), and that it now serves as a policy-making and well as policy-shaping body.

Because of the complexity and dynamic nature of the Arctic environment, balancing and integrating political, security, business, environmental, and indigenous interests has become more urgent. Consequently, the AC is becoming an influential regional body in the Arctic.
