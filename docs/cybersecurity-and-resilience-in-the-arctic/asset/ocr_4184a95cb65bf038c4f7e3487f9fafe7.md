Moreover, "Observer status" is open to non-Arctic states and outside organizations, approved by the Arctic Council at the Ministerial Meetings that occur every two years. A number of observer countries are involved, including the UK, Germany, France, China, India, and Japan, though others invited for two-year periods, between ministerial meetings, and aim to contribute to the Arctic Council by providing significant expertise and experience related to the Arctic. These states can be suspended, however, if they participate in activities which are deigned to avoid the declarations of the Council.

The Ottawa declaration marked a shift in focus to the Arctic states; concerns of environmental protection into broader concerns of sustainable development [37], and provides the Arctic Council as a mechanism for achieving goals of promoting cooperation, coordination and interaction between Arctic states and those involved. Since then, the Arctic Council has exerted greater oversight for monitoring and implementing the AEPS, with a permanent Secretariat based in Tromsø, Norway. Yet the Arctic is still a "soft-law regime". Unlike its compared to the Antarctic, there have been few intentions to create binding obligations for the states involved [38]. A number of treaties including the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL 73/78), the Polar Bear Treaty, and various other bilateral and multilateral agreements oversee most, but not all decto Arctic activity [39]. These treaties do not address all prospective issues - instead of a treaty system, the Arctic Council exists to serve as an advisory body. The first time that the Arctic Council facilitated leading to a binding agreement this treaty coordinates international search and rescue (SAR) coverage and response in the Arctic and establishes the area of SAR responsibility of each state. However, these SAR regions do not outline or relate to the disputed boundaries and territories of each state.

Since the late twentieth century, the impact of climate change has only been growing more significant. As a consequence, the regime established in the 1990s was not specifically designed in a way to address these issues. Focusing on the benefits and weaknesses of the Arctic regime, arguments for its replacement or at least shift towards a hard regime can be made, allowing enforceable policy to be made. A hard law regime is thought to be able to encourage stronger political commitments, establish firmer institutional and financial foundations, give legal "teeth" to compliance and enforcement, and establish mechanisms for settling international disputes [39]. In contrast a soft law regime provides better flexibility, are more rapidly agreed to and finalized, and can be implemented more quickly in address to urgent issues.
