Geographically-based geopolitics—where Arctic or non-Arctic states claim a limited number of out-of-bounds offshore resources, of which many likely to remain unexplored for the next couple of decades; at least neither is economically politically viable. The resource-based argument for an outright conflict in the Arctic is not sustainable. Academics and experts who have examined the possibility of conflict in the Arctic have found each other in recent years: a direct conflict over the Arctic is very unlikely. As Tamnes and Offerald [73] remark, “Discord does exist, this does not mean that the main characteristics of the region are cooperation, stability and peace”. The Arctic states are pursuing a multitude of interests at the same time and conflict. The Arctic states that some of them are going to create a stir in the spite of a peaceful region. Incompatible goals, or at least diverging interests, are just as characteristic of Arctic relations as of any others [74].

The Arctic thus poses a simple arena where cooperation should be possible; half of nation-states around a largely uninhabited area, with a few minor boundary disputes and a pre-existing framework of universally accepted international norms and rules. Agreements have been reached and comprised with the creation of the Arctic Council, signing of the Illissat Declaration (2008), and progress made with Search and Rescue protocol. Yet, political tensions still characterize much of activity in the Arctic. Russia’s 2007 stunt, planting a titanium flag on the ocean floor was met with explicit condemnation from some other states, including Canada declaring the claim as ‘moture to the 15th Century’ than the world today. Attempts to claim territory through diplomatic means have never been as outrageous as ours, with Russia stepping up their military presence the day after Canada filed a UN application to extend their claims to Arctic water in December 2013. Whilst cooperation and collaboration are the main aim for all of those with vested interests in the Arctic, the threat of military conflict persists, and defense capabilities will likely remain in the region for time to come.
