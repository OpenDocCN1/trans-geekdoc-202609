Other attacks target the smart meters of the metering infrastructure, i.e. an infrastructure created to measure water, gas, energy consumption. This example stresses that the simplest computational devices can be attacked for a profit if the number is huge. These episodes are part of a larger trend: security experts have seen in recent years a spike in cyberattacks that are stealing power for mining operations. Many experts forecast that in the future the intelligent sensors connected to an ICT infrastructure to monitor physical processes will become a popular target because of their increasing computational power. Discovering the malware that infects these devices may be complex because the affected components continue to offer their services. As an example, smart meters measure the consumption of water or energy. The only difference in an infected device is a lower performance because discovering this attack is a subset of the malware's computational resources. It is obvious that only if the infrastructure is continuously monitored and the design has adopted proper choices on non-functional behavior such as the execution time of a function. The complexity increases if insiders are involved in the attack. Here, an insider may be a customer who supports the attack because they are not satisfied with the service, or an activist who is protesting the company that created or manages the infrastructure.

One of the problems posed by attacks targeting smart meters or actuators to monitor and control some physical process that most users are not aware of the computational nature of smart objects. The awareness of the computational power of smart objects, and of the objects themselves, disappears as they become more pervasive and diffuse (Bertino and Ital 2017). A revealing example are surveillance video cameras, as attackers have used their computational resources to implement some of the most serious distributed-of-service attack (DDoS) attack. A DDoS attack disrupts the normal traffic of a computer network by overwhelming the target infrastructure with a flood of internet. A DDoS attack is like a traffic jam clogging up the highway which prevents regular traffic from arriving at its destination. DDoS attacks achieve effectiveness by utilizing a large number of computer systems as sources of attack. In recent DDoS attacks, the sources were surveillance video cameras (Klias et al. 2017). Most of the camera owners learnt of the camera computational resources only after the attack. Further potential attack targets are smart lamps that can be dimmed by the push of a key on a keyboard. It is worth recalling that since all infrastructures are connected, resources connected to one infrastructure can be used to attack any infrastructure connected to the attacked one.

All attacks that subset the normal behavior of infrastructure result in the creation of a botnet. A botnet consists of some interconnected resources of the infrastructure under an attacker' control. Hence, a botnet is a subset of the infrastructure that has fallen under the control of an attacker who accesses the botnet and uses the botnet resources for its benefit. It is in the attacker's interest that the target infrastructure works as smoothly as possible. Smooth infrastructure behavior reduces the likelihood that the attacker can, through continuous monitoring, discover the botnet. Discovering a botnet requires continuous infrastructure monitoring; even then may not suffice if the botnet creator exploits some 0-day vulnerability (Ablon and Bogart 2017) or insiders are involved.
