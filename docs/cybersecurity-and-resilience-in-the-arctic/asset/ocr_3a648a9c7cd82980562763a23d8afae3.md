It is important to start by defining one of the key operational concepts, the supply chain. According to the definition as set forth by Beamon, the supply chain is an "integrated process wherein a number of businesses work together in an effort to: (1) acquire raw materials, (2) convert these raw materials into specified products, and (3) deliver these final products to retailers" (Beamon 1998). A supply chain can alternatively be defined as "the network of companies involved in the upstream and downstream flows of products, services, finances, and information from the initial supply to the customer" (Petti et al. 2010; 1992; Lambert et al. 2005; Mentzer et al. 2001). Both definitions emphasize the complex and layered structure of supply chain networks and how a supply chain can be constructed merely by the direct connections of one on the process or relation chain.

Increasing complex supply chains may face a diverse array of disruptions, which have the potential to harm performance, especially in large-scale infrastructure projects. Global supply chains have faced disruptions from natural disasters, water, and terrorism, as well as business and economic shocks (Peck and Christopher 2004; Knemeyer et al. 2009; Goldsby and Rao 2009; Petti et al. 2010; Dolgui et al. 2018). As discussed above, one approach to managing these disruptions is to minimize the likelihood that they occur and harden the system to make it less vulnerable, i.e., the risk-management based approach. This approach can lead to supply chains that are strong against frequent and predictable events, but often leads to supply chains that are brittle against low probability and high consequence disasters (Klib et al. 2010; J. Wang et al. 2016). Risk management strategies can fail when faced with unpredictable or unpredictable scenarios (Hochstein et al. 2015; Klib et al. 2010).

An alternative is a resilience-based approach, which views some disruptions as inevitable and unpredictable (Merad & Trump 2020). This leads to a focus on recovering from these disruptions, rather than preventing them. The National Academy of Sciences defines resilience, for systems and infrastructure as, "the ability to prepare and for plan, recover, and more successfully adapt to adverse events" (National Research Council 2012). This four-stage definition of resilience is increasingly used by organizations and governments to assess resilience (Office of the Press Secretary 2013; Executive Office of the President 2013b; Larkin et al. 2015; Linkov et al. 2014; Linkov and Palma-Oliveveira 2017; National Research Council 2012). For example, in 2013, the U.S. Executive Office of the President released multiple Executive Orders (EOs) addressing resilience across issues such as cyber security, physical infrastructure, and supply chain management and these EOs had the NAS definition of resilience (Executive Office of the President 2013a; Executive Office of the President 2013b; Executive Office of the President 2017b; Linkov & Trump 2019). Resilience is particularly important when managing complex supply chains.
