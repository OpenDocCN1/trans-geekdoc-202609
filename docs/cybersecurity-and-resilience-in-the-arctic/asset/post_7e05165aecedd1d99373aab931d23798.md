## Collective Mobilization, Crowd-Sourcing, and Digital Toolmaking

Whether in crisis or everyday life, humans are natural tools and tool users. It is also well known that in crisis situations citizens can express social resilience through altruism[36] - expanded role of citizenship[37] - by helping themselves and others.[38] During crises, people excessively seek and resort to various information sources in order to optimize gains regarding the risks of the crisis.[39] Computer-mediated communication, the speed and vitality in information sharing social media platforms and models to capture geo-referenced, generated data enable individuals and groups to use new types of tools such as platforms, thematic network maps, visualization dashboards in all phases crisis management.[40] These are all manifestations of digital toolmaking that empower those affected by crises to self-collect, edit, create and share powerful information in real-time. Use of digital tools and services is observed to enable the creation of localized content, to better self-organize, network and mobilize resources which give rise to improved activities and temporary organizations which form responsible organizations can utilize for relief and recovery benefits.[41]

Improvation, self-organization and collective coordination can vary in intensity in post-crisis social responses - from the informal, self-organized collective personal networks to collective action through organically organized networks.[42] Whether informal or highly coordinated, communication via social networks provides access to information and resources. Crowdsourcing user-generated data in real time is another powerful dimension assisting individuals, communities and toolmakers to better navigate, gain and share useful information more effectively. When reliable, crowdsourcing reduces uncertainty about situations, offers more information than that of mass media or authorities’ theirs enhancing groups’ collaborative problem-solving capacities and citizens’ ability to make sense of the situation and to cope with it.[43] Crowdsourcing and further tighter sense of group affinity, sentiments of belonging and social cohesion are particularly welcome in trying times.

Good examples of social resilience included tools creation and self-organizing tasks, mutually reinforcing self-organization during the 2014 terrorist attacks in Paris. Or sitters, Twitter became a go-to site during disasters and a way to share methods for donating to help in recovery efforts.[45]
