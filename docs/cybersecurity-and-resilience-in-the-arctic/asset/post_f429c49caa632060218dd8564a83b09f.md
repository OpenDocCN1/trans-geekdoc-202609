The mix of technologies addressing connectivity needs in the Arctic is likely to remain heterogeneous in the future (Arctic Economic Council 2016). In addition, the harsh climate damages physical infrastructure more than in some other areas of the world. In sum, the first point of concern is the thinness of infrastructure and its accelerated erosion, of which increase pressure on, e.g., maintenance, to ensure equality between people whose everyday lives the functioning of infrastructure maps. Moreover, repairs in parts of national infrastructure that concern only a small number of people or relatively limited economic activity are not prioritized.

The thinness of infrastructure exists to the second point of concern, that is, a natural hazard or an intentional attack (a manmade hazard) on critical infrastructure that disrupts, alters, or influences the function of infrastructure. Regardless of the source of interruption (natural or fabricated), it will have a similar immediate impact on people's everyday life. In the longer term, the duration of breakouts, as well as the number of societal sectors affected and the severity of damage in each sector, can begin to cause different consequences in everyday life. Additionally, the effects on distinct people will be diverse. Unauthorized changes in the ways that critical infrastructure operates, may cause, for instance, flooding, traffic chaos, inappropriate healthcare provision, or problems in financial and monetary systems, which may affect a big of local businesses. Both disruptions and other kind of influence in the functioning of critical infrastructure have the potential to cause insecurity in terms of all seven aspects of human security, from personal and community security to environmental security. People are likely to worry about most aspects they experience in their everyday life, such as electricity, heat, water, and food supply, as well as access or non-access to services.

Inversely, digitalization has changed the forms of public and private service provision. For example, a big part of the population in the Arctic depends on digital services when they connect to healthcare, social security, schooling, banking, running errands with authorities, and acquisition of any other goods than daily groceries. Thus, the third point of concern is the ways that people use or are obligated to use utilities and services on critical infrastructure. People keep contact with distant relatives or friends via ICTs, which contributes, for instance, to their mental health and community needs. They can reproduce their culture online, develop it, or find new groups for socialization on different platforms. Nonetheless, there may be negative implications in terms of lost knowledge, disappearing cultural identity, polarization, crime to hate speech on a global scale, which would not have taken place without contemporary infrastructure. Some municipalities broadcast council meetings via YouTube, which contributes to the transparency of decision-making—and to political security. Reindeer herders and hikers make use of GPS, as a big number of tourists finding their way into the Arctic wilderness, which contributes to food and economic security in the region. Planning and technology development for automated traffic on land (both on roads and rails), at sea, and even in Arctic conditions is taking place.

While operation is also presented as a way to improve cyber security by taking the human being (who can make mistakes) out of the loop, it is not clear how well sensors, for instance, recognize in sea ice and whether the controlling software manages to respond accordingly. For people removed from the loop, in any awareness and skills, the developer may enhance their economic security. People's awareness and skills, as well as the voluntary/involuntary nature of the utilization of information infrastructure, again have a strong link to the realization of human rights. Simultaneously, if majority of the corporations and other organizations developing ICTs or services running on them know little or nothing about, or lack interest in, the local conditions and people's interests and needs, the utilities and/or services may not use the purpose they are used for people who have a limited chance of influencing the ongoing development of ICTs if decision-making takes place far away.

To conclude, improving cyber security of critical infrastructure from a human security perspective requires knowledge of and familiarity with the local conditions, both natural and fabricated. Those best abide on the topic are people living in the Arctic region, whose live less or more smooth functioning of infrastructure impacts. They can also define what are the actual human interests and needs, as well as the threats to human well-being in the increasingly digitalizing environment in the Arctic. Critical infrastructure development, maintenance, and its protection then ought to be designed and implemented by the General Data Protection Regulation of the European Union. Cyber security will then contribute to the reduction of people's everyday life, the improvement of human well-being, and the realization of human rights. The human security approach is an empowering framework, valuing the duty of governments (duty bearers) to their citizens (rights holders) (Maschi 2016). The framework offers the possibility to bring discussions beyond a technical or national strategy level to the level of the people involved and their unique and local conditions.

Yet, there is a need for more research on a balanced and integrated approach for safeguarding human rights and cyber security, and on what human security means in the context of digitalizing critical infrastructure in the Arctic. Cyber security laws, policies, and practices should respect human rights. Only by including human rights and human security approach can meaningful research on the human impact of cyber security challenges to critical infrastructure be done (Deibert 2018). Moreover, "rather than viewing human security as a list of threats [...] the concept could be viewed in terms of a "threshold" (Martin & Owen 2010). Thus, any cyber security of critical infrastructure related threat that crosses the threshold, defined by the people whose everyday life it affects, should be prioritized.
