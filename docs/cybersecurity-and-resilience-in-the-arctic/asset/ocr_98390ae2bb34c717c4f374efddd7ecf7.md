Coastal erosion presents a major risk to key infrastructure installations, which are often
located to the coastline. In particular, "shoreline fuel storage and delivery systems
are at greater risk" [50]. Result of this rapid, accelerating, and unpredictable
coastal erosion is that maps of the Arctic quickly become obsolete and inaccurate.
Melting ice compounds the issue. Navigation and out-of-date maps make Arctic
navigation more difficult for ships. The situation is rather dire; as in an egregious example
of the inaccuracy of maps, the National Ocean and Atmospheric Administration
note that "most of the shoreline along Alaska's northern and western coasts are not
mapped since 1960" [57]. Additionally, many maps of soundings on modern U.S.
navational charts of the Arctic are from the 1800s; [57]. Some areas, especially those
overed with ice, have never been charted; not only do maps accurately each
these Arctic sea regions, but confidence in U.
nautical maps of the Arctic is extremely low. According to Karjalainen et al. (2019),
"only 4.1% of the U.S. military Arctic is charted to modern standards,
raising safety concerns for marine operations"

The NOAA plans to map more of the U.S. Arctic to modern standards; however, it
is woefully-equipped to do so. In the words of the NOAA itself, "Estimates
range up to 25 years for surveying those periods, if resources remain in their current
level". The huge increase in resources or major scientific
avancements -- or both -- would allow for the creation of accurate and timely nautical
charts of the Arctic such that the U.S. Navy and NATO can safely and efficiently
avigate Arctic seas and carry out their strategic missions.
