Negotiating and adopting agreements: A key function of governments is coordinating the negotiation process with stakeholders, including their counterparts in foreign governments, non-governmental organizations, relevant industry and Indigenous groups. Trump et al. (2016) describe the actions stakeholders that may strengthen or weaken resilience, during, and after a disruption to a system. These actions take the form of: “i) the resources and readiness to commit to promoting resilience for a given system and community, (ii) the priorities defined and the risks evaluated and (iii) the efforts crafting effective involvement and risk communication to align agencies, reactive or preventive” (B. Trump et al., 2016). Governments define resources, priorities, and risks through the process of negotiating international agreements in order to align priorities between stakeholders.

Carrying out exercises: Governments have an ongoing function of carrying out exercises under established agreements. These exercises allow governments to test capabilities and needing changes. Exercises are an important part of the diplomatic role of governments to ensure agreements are continually relevant up-to-date, and to improve upon gaps or weaknesses. International cooperation on joint-exercises also builds trust and relationships between parties that strengthen both the effective implementation of the agreement as well as the overall bilateral relationship.

Implementing exercises: Governments, specifically Ministries of Foreign Affairs, have a coordination function to respond to actual security events with international implications by implementing international agreements and triggering, and in some instances, coordinating a response. The international relations aspects of cyber security threats to Arctic infrastructure involve communication and engagement with governments, other international bodies, and Indigenous organization.

## 3.2.2. Structure of International Relations in the Arctic

A number of governs operate in the Arctic, including inter alia:

- Intergovernmental cooperation forums: The Arctic Council, the Barents EuroArctic Cooperation, the Northern Council of Ministers, the International Maritime Organization, and the World Meteorological Organization;
- International organization: the Northern Dimension;
- Joint policies: the Northern Dimension;
- Various governance organizations.

### The Arctic Council: The Arctic Council is the primary venue for multilateral cooperation on Arctic issues. The Council was established by the eight Arctic states (Canada, the United States of America, the Kingdom of Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Norway, and Iceland) through the Declaration on the Establishment of the Arctic Council. A soft law agreement reflecting political commitment towards enhanced cooperation in the region (Smieszek et al. 2016). Hard security considerations are explicitly excluded from the Council’s mandate (Hossain et al. 2017). The Council contributes to decreasing the potential for conflict in the Arctic by facilitating cooperation on soft policy areas including environmental protection, economic development, emergency preparedness and resilience, and political representation for Indigenous peoples (Hossain et al. 2017 and Gwemars, 2017). As states include substantial government representatives within their delegations, the Arctic Council provides a platform for engagement between multiple levels of governance which enables links to be drawn between decisions made at the international level and their impacts on the community and societal level. Smieszek et al. (2016) have argued that it is "exactly the institutional structure and soft-law mechanism of the Arctic Council that enable trust-building and bottom-up evolution of the Council’s working structures and practices" (Smieszek et al. 2016). The Arctic Council’s ability to foster trust and confidence between states on soft policy issues has a key factor in enabling cooperation to address societal vulnerabilities in the region.

The Arctic Council is a unique intergovernmental organization including Indigenous Peoples as Permanent Participants on the Council; states with the Arctic International Association (AIA), Athabaskan Council (AAC), Gwich'in Council (ICI), Inuit Circumpolar Council (ICC), Russian Indigenous Peoples (RIP) and Sami Council (SC). Permanent Participants are consulted in negotiations but do not have voting rights (Arctic Council, 2015). Indigenous peoples constitute a significant percentage of the Arctic population. For example, in Canada, Indigenous make up 53% of the total population across the three territories (StatsCan, 2016). The inclusion of Indigenous peoples in the decision-making process at the Arctic Council builds societal resilience. Salminen and Hussain argue that empowered peoples “are capable of avoiding risks and improving the protection” which transforms them from “passive receivers to active producers” (2018). The Arctic Council empowers Indigenous peoples to exercise agency and actively contribute to the development of secure Arctic.

The Arctic Council produced a report in 2016 on the resilience of socio-ecological systems in the Arctic, building on collaboration with Arctic countries, Indigenous peoples, and scientific organizations. The report summarized the role of the Arctic Council as a platform for facilitating the alignment of interests between Arctic societies, communities, and individuals. The Council contributes to the development of sustainable ecosystems by coordinating international responses to threats in the region (Arctic Assessment, 2016). House of Commons Standing Committee on Foreign Affairs and International Development highlighted the importance of international cooperation through the Arctic Council to “mitigate climate change, to adapt to emerging situations and build resilience” (House of Commons, 2018). Arctic societies are “able to come together to identify and respond to challenges” (Arctic Council, 2016), including security and cyber security challenges.

Under the Arctic Council, three legally-binding agreements were negotiated: on search and rescue, oil-spill preparedness, and scientific cooperation. The Agreement on Aeronautical and Maritime Search and Rescue in the Arctic and the Agreement on Cooperation on Marine Oil Preparedness and Response in the Arctic demonstrate an expansion of the role of the Arctic Council through the deployment of military and paramilitary assets of states to address vulnerabilities in the region (Greaves and Sommers, 2017). Casey and Sofia (2019) draw the link between environmental governance, resilience policies, and cybersecurity to demonstrate how cooperation in areas of soft policy such as search and rescue and oil spill response contributes to resilience of critical infrastructure in the Arctic (Cassidy & Sofia, 2019). The impacts of the aforementioned two agreements on Arctic resilience are discussed below.

### The Agreement on Cooperation on Aeronautical and Maritime Search and Rescue in the Arctic (2011): The Agreement on Cooperation on Aeronautical and Maritime Search and Rescue in the Arctic, signed at the Nuuk Ministerial in 2011, builds on existing international and regional search (SAR) frameworks and is the first legally-binding agreement negotiated under the auspices of the Arctic Council (Sydnes 2017). The policy process began in 2009 at the Arctic Council Ministerial in Norway, where the Ministers mandated the creation of a Task Force to develop an international instrument for cooperation on SAR operations in the Arctic. The Agreement, which came into force in January 2013, facilitates international SAR cooperation in the Arctic by detailing cooperation procedures including SAR assistance, border crossing, information sharing, and hosting regular meetings (Arctic Council, 2011). The Agreement allows for coordination of the military, coast guard, police and transport services of Arctic States (Greaves and Pomerants, 2017).

Governments have an ongoing function of carrying out exercises under the SAR Agreement, which-to-date has included series of tabletop and full-scale exercises involving the coast guards and armed forces of participating nations to test communications, equipment, and procedures (Sydnes 2017). Participating governments have produced reports on the successes and gaps in the exercises to adapt to regional operational needs and improve the effective implementation of the Agreement. Joint exercises are a crucial function of international relations because they encourage effective cooperation, the gradual development of working relations between participating agencies, and the strengthening of the overall international relationship between stakeholders (Sydnes 2017).

### The Agreement on Cooperation on Marine Oil Pollution Preparedness and Response in the Arctic (2013): The Agreement on Cooperation on Marine Oil Pollution Preparedness and Response in the Arctic is the second legally binding agreement negotiated under the auspices of the Arctic Council, signed at Kiruna, Sweden, and entering into force in March 2016. The two-year negotiation process, began with the establishment of an interdisciplinary task force at the May 2011 Nuuk Declaration of the Arctic Council Ministerial Meeting, involved five meetings over a one-year period from October 2011 to October 2012 (Trigatti, Bjerkemo and Evertit 2014). The Task Force included a number of Arctic stakeholders in the negotiation process including Arctic States, Arctic Council Permanent Participants, observers, industry representatives, and invited subject matter experts; decision-making was based on consensus among stakeholders (Trigatti, Bjerkemo and Evertit 2014). To give collective capacity to respond to oil spills by providing mutual assistance, undertaking monitoring, carrying out joint exercises and training, promoting the exchange of information, and conducting joint reviews (Canada, 2017). The Agreement priorities shared among Arctic States, including those relating to the promotion of social and economic development and environmental protection, and provides a vehicle for international cooperation on these issues.
