## ## Introduction

The Arctic has quickly become a focal point for the impacts of climate and environmental change; the region is more than just an area of cold temperatures, snow, and ice. Environmental changes are affecting agricultural practices and, in many cases, habitability conditions among traditional Arctic societies. Melting ice packs are opening access – in particular waterways – to new routes and mineral resources; tourism and immigration are bringing new energy, water, food, and waste management demands; and governments and international agencies are becoming increasingly involved. In the High North, important questions about sovereignty and jurisdiction have begun to create challenges of resource expansion and infrastructural development in problematic conditions.

Proposed solutions to many of these challenges have involved advanced technologies, bringing them large server installations, network infrastructure, and personal services. Communication over vast distances allowed regions to connect with the rest of the world, modernizing communities and their operations. Many have since looked to the Arctic as a place for profitable growth as frozen expanses open up in response to warming temperatures. Across the globe, governments, transnational corporations, and billions of dollars are entangled in a resource-based economy where pursuit of one often comes at the cost of others. This results in a lack of sustainability in policies, yet in an environment as fragile as the Arctic, the new challenge is not simply to create survival pathway for indigenous peoples, but to maintain sustainable development and ensure protection of infrastructure.

## Analytical Strategies to Operationalize Arctic Infrastructure (M. Pollock et al.)

In the modern era, data are more important than ever. The twenty-first century has seen a massive expansion and adoption of complex interconnected systems that simplified management of processes across vast expanses. But development of these systems has brought looming challenges for users and operators, inadvertently exacerbated vulnerabilities where targeted disruptions can significantly affect system function. In an environment as extreme as the Arctic, these types of disturbances could reduce economic output while dictating life or death.

This chapter explores six themes:

- What does "Arctic resilience" mean, and how is it validated?
- Responding to Arctic incidents and disasters poses a substantial challenge that could be eased by enhancing predictive capabilities for Arctic installations. What technologies could foresee hazards due to weather, adversarial attack, or human error?
- What types of information Arctic planners need to ensure they do not undertake a significantly disruptive or dangerous mission?
- Is "Arctic resilience" predicted? What modifications to current testing methods are warranted to account for Arctic conditions (e.g., cold)?
- One of the most effective methods for recovering from a cyber breach is to have saved copies of information. In the Arctic, where resources are already limited, storing on-site backups of all information may be impractical. What steps could be taken to increase Arctic storage potential, or where could data be stored to expedite operations after a breach?
- As commercial and military operations to the Arctic becomes more profitable, competition for Arctic projects is anticipated to intensify. How can different approaches to the same mission be compared to determine which provides the best value? What criteria are most important and relevant for Arctic operations?

In considering these themes, we seek to identify relevant information and research purposes as well as key data gaps. Our goal is to accurately quantify and assess whether an operation is sufficiently resilient. Scenarios presented by Arctic characteristics have promoted ignorance in regard to what data was collected and maintained to inform safe actions and behaviors. To address this issue, we aim to survey analytical approaches relevant to resilience in the Arctic, in order to increase our capacity to verify safe operations, minimizing the risk of calamitous scenarios.

# 2. Framing Context: Applying Resilience Concepts to the Arctic

The Arctic, a domain dominated by sea, snow, and a continental-sized division of ice, is facing a new norm in the twenty-first century — one of rapid change. The substantial consumption of fossil fuels has already been identified as accelerating the rate of climate change (Hansen et al., 1981; Cox et al., 2000; Karl, 2019) — a phenomenon that is heating the Arctic at a rate as fast as the rest of the world (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, 2018; Sand et al., 2015). For centuries, the region was locked away by harsh climate conditions and polar ice; as sea ice continues to decline, new markets and opportunities have emerged with new challenges to those operating in the Arctic. These new challenges include contested industrial drilling rights, exclusive economic zones and continental shelf boundaries, emerging trade and cruise ship routes, military expansions reminiscent of the Cold War, and protection of indigenous communities, cultures, and languages.

Traditional risk approaches applied to a global system identifying the likelihood and impact of an adverse event on the system. From these calculations, risk management techniques focus on strengthening components to address potential threats within degrees of acceptable disturbances in order to prevent system failure (Linkov, Trump, & Keiser, 2018; Linkov and Trump 2019). These strategies fail to be effective where threats are unspecified, changing, and independent. Moreover, our social, technical, and economic networks are vastly more interconnected than ever before, making the hardening of specific components complex (IRGC 2018). Multiple failures of standard risk management approaches have been identified (Elms, 2009; Boulevard & Lee, 2019; Naime; Stulz, 2008), and these failures are increasingly pronounced in large, complex systems having numerous components are time-consuming, expensive, and impractical (Linkov et al., 2014). These conditions require designing resilience into systems, allowing for quick recovery when disruptions occur, and facilitating adaptation in the wake of adverse events to ensure that the system is prepared to deal with threats.
