# 3.1.1. The Case for Remote Operations

One specific aspect enabled by connectivity is the capacity to operate remotely. Remote operations allow for the provision of services difficult to provide in a direct capacity physically (e.g., cost, resource availability). Domains of applications are very diverse and include industry, public services, healthcare, and culture. While remote operations are far from novelty, their development and implementation are increasing significantly due to advances in digital capabilities and the overall greater availability of digital infrastructures. The development of such capability is of great relevance to Arctic regions, which combine harsh conditions and access difficulties in vast areas with sparse populations. A specific example is provided by the emerging concept of the Remote Tower in Air Traffic Management (ATM). We will use this example in this section as a way to illustrate the cyber security challenges the Arctic is exposed to when relying on remote access and operations, and discuss directions for increasing resilience.

In the domain of Air Traffic Management (ATM), increased connectivity is seen as one of the main drivers of improvement of operations and building of capacity to handle the traffic increase. The concept of the Remote Tower provides the capacity to manage tower operations remotely based on: (1) a tower equipped with various sensors (especially camera) and (2) controllers accessing this at a remote center, which might be used to control multiple aerromes (Kearney and Li, 2018). Remote towers increasingly appear as a valuable alternative in the context of various business cases, for aerodromes located in remote areas and managing limited amount of traffic. Besides the operational relevance of this capability, one can wonder about the demands and potential risks introduced by the necessary reliance on network infrastructures and remote sensors. What levels of coverage and reliability are required for the digital infrastructures for such capabilities? What happens when operations is not full, if at all, functional? How dangerously dependent on digital infrastructure are the capabilities introduced by remote operations? Such questions carry particular significance in the face of the cyber threat, whether due to intentional cyber-attacks or network systems' malfunctions. Interruptions or degradations of the digital infrastructure and services can impair the capacity to perform Air Traffic Management (ATM) safely or efficiently while. Resilience represents the capacity to handle two interrelated, but different, disruptions: (1) of the digital services; and (2) of ATM operations. In the first case, challenges are associated with the system's capacity to identify, and address the cyber issue event. In addition, reestablishing impaired digital services in a timely manner is critical because ATM operations performed in a degraded mode risk not being sustainable with regard to safety and/or costs. In the second case, which constitutes the primary emergency, the system needs to adapt and mitigate the impact on operations (e.g., switch to other modes of operations, reduce or even divert traffic to alternative airports) before returning to sound operations.

The concept of the Remote Tower in ATM is representative of the kind of digital capabilities that allow for the provision of services in the Arctic regions and, as a result, for their rapid development.
