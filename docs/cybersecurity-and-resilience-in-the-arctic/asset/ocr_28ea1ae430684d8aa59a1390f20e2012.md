Understanding the Far North as a complex adaptive system organizes interconnected transformations into a coherent framework (Figure 2). In complex adaptive systems, resilience is understood as emergent property arising from complex interactions among the Arctic's subsystems. Interactions are top-down and bottom-up. All types of interaction can reinforce or erode the processes that maintain the system in a qualitatively predictable state. Top-down drivers are so-called 'agentless drivers' that shape local conditions [2] [1]. They include climate change, globalization, and industrialization. Bottom-up, nested (i.e., within sub-system) drivers include local ecology, culture, and governance. When resilience is eroded, critical processes that maintain the system state are vulnerable to disruptions that instigate a system reconfiguration. Due to the long-term scales in which natural systems operate, fast reconfigurations are typically undesirable from both an environmental and a human standpoint. People and nature organize around fluctuating but reliable processes. When those system processes are disrupted, so are the system features that underpin stability and reliability.

An example of system reconfiguration with unwanted outcomes is the effect of changing river morphology and ice cover in interior Alaska. Climate change is bringing thawed permafrost, extreme precipitation events, and warmer springtime temperatures in the Far North. A consequence is a shift in the location, timing, and magnitude of river flooding, freezing, and thawing [3] [4].
