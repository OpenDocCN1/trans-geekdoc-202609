In the Arctic, however, such disruptions can have rapid consequences that place individuals and communities in jeopardy. As such, resilience is not just a preferred characteristic of the infrastructural, and environmental systems that make modern life possible in a climatologically hazardous region, but instead it an absolute necessity.

This is especially true for cybersecurity for interconnected digital systems. Virtually all modern equipment, buildings, and infrastructure are reliant on underlying digital systems to execute intended functions – design intended to simplify the labor requirements to achieve modern comforts and capabilities regardless of the environment or climate. While digital systems also represent a ‘single-point-of-failure’ for that, if exploited, could disrupt multiple directly related systems that have a common nest through information systems. From cruise ships, to industrial mining plants, to large buildings and facilities, a reliance on centralized or interconnected digital systems must be accompanied by resilience-based strategies for efficient recovery of services in the event of disruption. This is unpacked for further discussion in Chapter 3.

For the remainder of this chapter, we frame the governance challenges with the Arctic from a national and transnational perspective. We argue that thorough exploration of such governance issues will help unpack the policy realities within the region, as well as to highlight the development priorities that various stakeholders may have in the region. Further, such discussion highlights how resilience may be integrated into policy actions for facilities, infrastructure, missions, or overall human activity in the region.

# ## 4. Unpacking Arctic Governance: A Short History of the Arctic Council

The Arctic Council (AC) is a high-level intergovernmental forum established to foster international cooperation between Arctic states. Towards of the end of the Cold War and the relaxation of international tensions, the Arctic was considered as a region for prospective theater of war operations between NATO and USSR countries [34]. In 1989, Finland sent a letter to the other Arctic states proposing a conference on the protection of the Arctic environment. Two years later in 1991, eight countries (Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden, and USA) signed the Arctic Environmental Protection Strategy (AEPS): a multilateral, non-binding agreement on Environmental Protection among Arctic states, three indigenous peoples’ organizations – the Saami Council, the Inuit Circumpolar Conference, and the Association of Indigenous Minorities of the North, Siberia and the Far East of the Russian Federation, were also present [35]. Major environmental problems identified at that time included persistent organic contaminants, oil pollution, heavy metals, radioactivity, and acidification. AEPS was a guide to tackling these issues through the development of Action Plans and six working groups, addressing a range of issues including conserving fauna and flora, environmental monitoring and assessment, and reducing the level of Arctic contaminants [36]. AEPS laid the groundwork for moving into a more wide-ranging agreement between Arctic states, in the form of the Arctic Council. Formally established in 1996, following the signing of the Ottawa Declaration, the Arctic Council was designed to provide a forum for discussion of issues of common interest.
