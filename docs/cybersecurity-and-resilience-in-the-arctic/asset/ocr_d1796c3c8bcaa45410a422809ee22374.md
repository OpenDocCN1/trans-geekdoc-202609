While infrastructure development is mentioned, cybersecurity is given less focus in that report despite a growing reliance on interconnected digital systems to manage and govern such infrastructure. We argue that cybersecurity resilience must be incorporated as a core consideration of any Arctic resilience roadmap to the extent that modern infrastructure is predicated upon efficient digital command and control, whereby disruption to such capabilities could leave various populations exposed to critical levels of health and safety risk (see Figure 3).

Arctic communities rely on digital systems for which virtually not all daily functions. The Arctic does not have the capacity to rapidly recover and adapt from a disruption that impacts the entire system, especially its archaic or unprotected infrastructure. Redundancies in the system are necessary to ensure that communities, facilities, and businesses face minimal disruptions from unpredictable indirect threats. Policymakers and stakeholders move beyond outdated practices of risk prevention and move towards resilience by design policies that can better address physical, social, and unresolved governance challenges with high uncertainty.

Solutions based on resilience focus the necessary features allowing a complex, integrated system to recover function following a disruption [14] [15]. Resilience-based approaches are essential for Arctic threats, where the complex and high-value infrastructure components are held by Arctic organizations and countries are increasingly under threat of cyber disruption. A resilience approach would empower Arctic infrastructure and relevant stakeholders to better understand (i) where vulnerabilities and risks may arise within interconnected Arctic infrastructural systems, (ii) how cyber-attack or intrusion could cause cascading risks to the safety and operation of Arctic sites, and (iii) identify strategies to secure critical infrastructure against cyber threats, particularly exposed or brittle infrastructural targets.

# Areas of Arctic Resilience Research

On 27-30 January 2019, fifty-five scholars and practitioners of resilience, cybersecurity, and the Arctic met in Rovaniemi, Finland for a NATO for Peace and Security Programme (SPS) Advanced Research Workshop (ARW). Participants came from over 20 countries, including NATO members as well as NATO countries. The workshop brought together top scholars in cybersecurity risk assessment, governance, and resilience to discuss potential analytical and governance strategies and offer perspectives of how to improve critical digitized Arctic infrastructure against various anthropological and natural threats. It will discuss threats to digitized infrastructure include anthropological (i.e. cyber-attack or misuse) as well as natural (i.e. space weather or other extreme events). Workshop participants were organized into three working groups: (A) Cybersecurity infrastructure and its threats; (B) metrics, tools, and methods to understand and analyze Arctic cyber resilience, and (C) legal frameworks and governance options to protect Arctic cyber resilience.
