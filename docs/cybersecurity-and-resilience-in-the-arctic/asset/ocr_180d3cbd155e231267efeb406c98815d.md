While it would go beyond the scope of this text to provide a template for the determination of whether and under which conditions attack on vital infrastructure can be armed within the meaning of international law, some conclusions can be drawn from existing legal norms: for example, attacks against submarine cables are not acts of piracy. This follows from their exclusion from the definition provided in UNCLOS Article 101 [8] [26]. While an attack on subsea cables does not necessarily lead to a right of self-defense — a question of *jus bellum* — the trend towards hybrid warfare makes it more likely that subsea cables might become attacked as part of a hybrid attack or in anticipation of an armed attack. When international humanitarian law, *jus bello*, the practical importance of subsea cables for internet access and communication can have several legal implications: “Attacking any direct military purpose are forbidden, even though their indirect or ultimate purpose may be terminate the conflict. Thus, acts of terror directed against the civilian population with the purpose of compelling party to terminate its military activities are forbidden” [22]. The question of which acts can be answered in different situations. In today’s interconnected societies, reliance on internet particular in the thinly populated areas of the Arctic and Sub-Arctic is high. As online services, such as online education or telemedicine, become more important, the physical infrastructure which is the backbone of the internet is growing in rural and remote communities. Accordingly, attacks on communication, energy, or transport infrastructure can constitute acts of terror at Arctic populations, if the attack on physical infrastructure does not directly claim humans. In rural areas the risk to human life as result of attacks on infrastructures is unlikely: the disruption of internet services can cause chaos in urban areas and is likely to have dramatic economic impacts, in particular the necessity of telemedicine services in sparsely populated areas that a disruption of internet access can be life-threatening. As this social-technical development continues, the potential for terror continues to evolve, at the same time there is somewhat of a lack of legal clarity concerning the legality of military activities in the waters of other states [27]. UNCLOS does not restrict the rights of coastal states to relate subsea cables in their times of armed conflict; however, the permissibility of attacks on subsea cables owned by the enemy state but located, for example, in the Exclusive Economic Zone of a neutral third state is far from. “For the purposes of naval warfare, the contiguous zone, the exclusive zone, and the continental shelves of neutral States are considered areas of high seas.
