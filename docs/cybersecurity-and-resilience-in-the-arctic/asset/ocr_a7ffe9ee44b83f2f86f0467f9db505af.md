**Finland:** The electricity sector in Finland relies on nuclear energy, the energy industry, hydro-power, and food consumption; some electricity is imported from neighboring countries. Finland is one of the first countries where all electricity users have electric meters that can be tracked, with the data used to measure the efficiency of the electric grid.

Finland does not have a policy regarding the smart grid but is pushing some initiatives both in research and development. One example is CLEAN (Cluster for Energy and Environment), which manages the SGEM (Smart Grids and Energy Markets) program and involves a range of smart grid stakeholders (The Nordic Electricity Sector, 2014)

**Greenland/Denmark:** In fact, as in most Nordic countries, energy in Greenland was generated by diesel-driven power plants. Now the main source of energy in Denmark is thermoelectric while wind energy generates 20% of the country's total energy supply (Nordregio, 2010). In 2017, the wind turbines in Denmark delivered power equivalent to 43.6% of all electricity consumption in the country (Bergreen, 2018). Greenland also has been replacing its diesel power plants with hydropower. Since 1993, Greenland has been partly self-supplying energy from hydropower. There are now solar and wind energy that were rejected. Recent experiments in Greenland and studies from Alaska show that wind turbines can work effectively in Arctic conditions. Each town in Greenland generates its own energy and distributes it via a micro-grid and local district heating network.

**Iceland:** Iceland actually meets all its electricity needs from environmentally friendly renewables. This is the first country with 99.99% of its electricity needs with renewable energy. Virtually all of them come from hydropower (71.03%) and geothermal (28.91%). Wind power generates 0.04% of the electricity. Fossil fuels combs a distant fourth, with only 0.01% of the energy production. According to a previous Nordic electricity sector report (The Nordic Electricity Sector, 2014) Iceland does not have an explicit policy on implementing smart grids. Landsnet, responsible for the transmission and power system on Iceland, mentions smart grids as the next generation transmission that it intends to be part of its strategy.

**Norway:** According to 2011 data from the Norwegian government agency on Water Resources and Energy, over 95% of the country's electricity is produced by hydropower. Approximately 97.8% of electricity needs of Norway are covered by renewables (Iceland magazine, 2019).

There is a smart grid (Smartgrid) in Norway that coordinates activities linked to smart grids and smart meters. There are ongoing discussions between stakeholders from the research, energy, and government sectors on establishing a smart grid policy in Norway (The Nordic Electricity Sector, 2014).

**Russia:** Russia is one of the top producers and consumers of electric power in the world. It produces more than 240 GW of installed generation capacity. In 2016, Russia generated 1071 billion kilowatt-hours and consumed about 900 billion kilowatt-hours.
