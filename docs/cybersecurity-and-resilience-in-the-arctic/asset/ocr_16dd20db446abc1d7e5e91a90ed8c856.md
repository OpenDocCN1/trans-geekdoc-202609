# 3. Microsoft Norms for Cybersecurity to limit conflict

+   1. States should not target ICT companies to insert vulnerabilities (backdoors) or take actions that would otherwise undermine public trust in products and services
2. States should have a clear principle-based policy for handling product and service vulnerabilities that reflect a strong mandate to report to them rather than stockpile, buy, sell, or exploit them.
3. States should exercise restraint in developing cyber weapons and ensure that any which are developed are limited, precise, and not reausal.
4. States should commit to nonproliferation activities related to cyber weapons.
5. States should limit their engagement in cyber offensive operations to avoid creating a mass event.
6. States should advise private sector actors to detect, respond to, and recover from events in cyberspace.

Source: McKay, Angela, Jan Neutze, Paul Nicholas, and Kevin Sullivan. "International Cybersecurity Norms–Reducing conflict in an Internet-dependent world." Microsoft, December (2014).

# 4.1 21st Century Challenges for 21st Century Problems

Characterized by low populations, a lack of infrastructure, extreme natural environments and specific cultures, including the cultures of indigenous peoples, Arctic regions pose particular challenges for effective governance. Tools, networks and frameworks which have been developed for the mid-latitudes may not be appropriate for the Arctic, in particular parts of the Arctic. Effective governance requires taking into account the specific reality of the life of those who are governed. In the case of the Arctic there are often more similarities between a rural community in Canada and in Norway than between a remote community and the national capital city. International efforts at Arctic governance, for example in the Arctic Council, are noteworthy not only for their ability to cross political divides but because of the role afforded to non-state actors, such as indigenous peoples' organizations.

Cyberattacks are an emergent risk in the Arctic. Currently, governance, including that of cybersecurity, relies on norms and principles developed in the Post-Cold War 20th century. Cybersecurity threats are distinctly modern. 21st century threat. Dominant players can include non-state actors, adversarial actions which can be hidden and difficult to detect, and targets both tangible (e.g., physical infrastructure) and intangible (e.g., knowledge) assets. It remains unknown whether governance norms for the 20th century are enough to address such threats.

There is a need for resilience-based solutions to guide planning and preparation for novel, and unpredictable threats, as those presented by cyber security failures, that Arctic systems can absorb shocks, recover from damage, and adapt to constantly changing hybrid threats.

Existing tools and indicators used extensively in risk analysis (see Box 4) can provide a foundation from which to build. But they are not in solution and of themselves for achieving-cyber resilience. As globalization continues to connect the world in a myriad of surprising ways, shrinking ice in the Arctic North is driving emergent global opportunities, risks, and conflicts with no historical anchor.
