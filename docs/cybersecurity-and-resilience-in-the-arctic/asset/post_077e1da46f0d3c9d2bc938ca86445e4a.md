The extent to which the Beaufort Lens has changed the Arctic soundscape was made apparent at the 2016 Ice Exercise (ICEX), a biennial exercise that the U.S. Navies conducts to practice and assess operational readiness in the Arctic and conduct research. The 2016 ICEX took place in March in the Beaufort Sea. Researchers had expected that the Beaufort Lens would alter range acoustic propagation; ICEX16 showed, “the effect is dramatic for short range acoustic communication and navigation systems” [68]. Schmidt and Schneider (2016) describe a particular USN experiment at ICEX16 in which researchers attempted to deploy, operate, and recover an autonomous underwater vehicle with a towed array identifying the changes wrought by global warming on the Arctic acoustic environment [68]. Schmidt and Schneider (2016) explain some of the results:

> the double-duct SVP [i.e. the Beaufort Lens] creates a dramatic variation in the Arctic channel, with severe implications for the performance of underwater acoustic navigation and communication systems, even at relatively short range. The USN could adjust and update its sensors and systems to cope with the changing Arctic soundscape. Schmidt and Schneider (2016) also note that the Beaufort Lens will likely have an effect on the spatial composition of ambient noise, from distant source, further complicating underwater communications [68]. They ultimately conclude that receivers must be able to change depth on an as-needed basis [68]. This represents a significant increase in technological complexity and costs. As climate continues to affect the Arctic environment, additional threats are likely to emerge which challenge and disrupt the Navy’s conventional wisdom operating in the Arctic. The USN could benefit from a new method and strategy for coping with these disruptions and threats.

## 2.2. Cyber Threats

Cyber threats pose a major challenge to U.S. Navy operations, especially in the heightened intensity of the Arctic environment. Director of the Defense Intelligence Agency and equal Deputy Commander of the U.S. Cyber Command of the DoD, Vincent R. Stewart, highlighted cyber threats as part of his 2015 worldwide threat assessment to the U.S. Senate:

The global cyber threat environment presents numerous persistent to the security and integrity of DoD networks and information. Threat actors demonstrate an increased ability and willingness to conduct aggressive cyberspace operations - including both service disruptions and espionage against U.S. and allied defense information networks. Similarly, we note with increasing concern recent destructive cyber actions against U.S. private-sector networks demonstrating capabilities that could hold U.S. government and defense networks at risk. [70]

We have yet to see the results of a successful cyber-attack on USN forces, certain weakness in USN cyber systems and infrastructure make the Navy exceptionally vulnerable.

### 2.2.1. Threats to Ship Cyber Systems

The capability to disrupt or destroy key USN and NATO cyber systems already exists; it is just a matter of time before a successful attack is carried out. An example from the private sector illustrates the ease with which a major ship system can be disrupted. In 2013, a group of researchers from U.T. Austin successfully spoofed the GPS of an $80 million yacht with a custom-made device, spoofing the 213-foot ship hundreds of meters off course with the crew or the ship's hundreds of meters off course [71].

Imagine a bad actor -- whether terrorist group, lone wolf, or foreign government -- using a similar strategy. Military ships could be easily hijacked without detection, resulting in catastrophic effects. The U.S. Navy uses encrypted systems on its battle force ships and maritime vessels, which makes them harder to spoof GPS systems on civilian vessels [72]. However, even this encrypted GPS can be spoofed by sophisticated actors given inherent vulnerabilities.

Furthermore, as over 90 percent of DoD deployment and distribution transactions are handled on unclassified systems [73], it can be assumed that the same percentage holds for the Navy in particular. The DoD Arctic Strategy charges the Navy's 2nd Fleet with 'responsibility for ensuring the readiness of, and dynamically employing, maritime forces in the Arctic, and the ability to fight across multiple domains, thereby defeating aggression' [16]. This is a major element of the Navy's and DoD's strategy (including incorporation of the new Multi-Domain Operations doctrine, to become increasingly important to the Joint Force [74], [75]). Spoofed GPS systems could severely impair the Navy's ability to transport troops and material, to efficiently and dynamically employ forces, and to fight across multiple domains. At best, ships spoofed by GPS systems could be routed off-course and significantly delayed, costing valuable time and money. At worst, they could be tricked into crashing with other ships or natural hazards (such as ice floes) or entering enemy territory.

In recent years, the accelerating rate of technological development has dramatically altered the ways in which ships communicate and operate. Modern ships are equipped with "zens of microprocessors running over 100 million parameters of control software," and ship infrastructure is now "a mixture of electromechanical components and highly integrated hardware and software forming a set of computer networks" [76], [77].

Concurrent with this increased reliance on software has an increased vulnerability to cyber-attacks.

This has been confirmed by the Office of Naval Research (ONR) which found that the frenetic pace at which the sheer amount of software and complexity of software have been growing introduces the attack surface. ONR determined that “Virtually all software products are affected, from applications to low level system” [78].

DiRenzo et al. (2015) and Garcia-Perez et al. (2017) have highlighted some of the technical vulnerabilities of ship systems along with GPS already discussed, such as Electronic Chart and Information Systems (ECDIS), Automatic Identification Systems (AIS), Very Small Aperture Terminals (VSAT), and Inmarsat, all crucial to operation [79].

ECDIS provides navigation information through means of electronic chart which integrates data from a number of sources, including GPS, speed, gyrocompass, and indicates a ship’s location in relation to charted objects, land, and unseen obstacles. Specifically, ECDIS updates are downloaded from an external source, whether through thumb-drives or the internet. This provides the opportunity for an adversary to access the ECDIS system and modify the maps, potentially resulting in inaccurate navigation which in turn causes ship delays, shifts, and even collisions. As crew members become increasingly reliant on such digital mapping, they leave themselves vulnerable. In 2014 a security group found vulnerabilities to read, download, replace or delete any file on the machine hosting ECDIS [79].

AIS are used for vessel positioning and tracking electronically connecting with other ships, bases, and satellites. Somali pirates have already used this data to gain information about the cargo loads of vessels and determines which one to take [80], [81]. Additional attacks might use AIS to misreport the position of ships or create false obstacles. Bad actors “could impersonate authorities and trick the crew into disabling their AIS, which would make the ship invisible to authorities and others (except attackers)” [79]. AIS systems are “easy to penetrate” [79], [82].

VSAT are satellite communications which can help connect ships to the internet. In 2014, around 10,000 VSATS were found to have open ports – potentially providing a means for adversaries to access the local network.

Inmarsat provides ships with data services via mobile terminals that connect to ground stations through satellites. While hard to attack, Inmarsat and similar companies use gateways in countries that, if technologically advanced and powerful, could intercept satellite communications.

The U.S. Navy’s fleet is even more vulnerable to cyber-attacks than many other countries due to slow adoption of updated software. Up to 100,000 USN computers are still running Microsoft Windows XP, which was replaced by Windows Vista in 2007 [83].
