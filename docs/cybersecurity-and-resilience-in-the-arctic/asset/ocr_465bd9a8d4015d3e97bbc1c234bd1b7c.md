# 174 A. Hummelholm / Threat Characterization for Various Layers of Infrastructure

Natural threats include threats as sharks, earthquakes, landslides, volcanoes, eruptions, tsunamis, icebergs, sea currents, storm winds, and so on. Accidental threats can be caused by everyday work at sea, such as fishing, dragging the anchor, dredging, etc., and can damage submarine optical cables, threatening the performance of submarine optical cables systems. We also need to look at potential adverse as the submarine cable routes are long and submerged. There are many countries that have the ability to join (“tapping”) fiber cables to collect the information transmitted therein. In every situation we must look for opportunities for malicious actors to hack or make cyber-attacks on submarine cables whether those are tapping operations or other methods such as side channel attacks or side channel spying. We must also implement encryption systems for layers 1, 2 and 30 that we can protect our communications systems against hacking and cyber-attacks.

The undersea cable types to be used depend on the depth of the sea and the vicinity to the coast in the areas where the above-mentioned threats occur, and where the threats are realized. If we consider the attackers’ opportunities to join the optical cable, which would be easiest to penetrate the cable exactly where the armor layers are thin. In practice, only a few large states have the capabilities to do so. When we look at this planned undersea cable system with its length of 18,000 km, distances between optical amplifiers of around 50 km, attackers will be able to connect to underwater optical cables after each optical amplifier.
