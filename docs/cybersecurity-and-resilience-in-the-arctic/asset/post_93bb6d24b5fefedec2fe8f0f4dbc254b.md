## 2.3. The Human and Social Environment

Climate change and globalization are two dominant drivers of human and societal change in the Arctic.[52] Arctic cities and communities vary in size, culture, and growth—are shrinking while others are urbanizing. Overall, most Arctic settlements are small and not populated. These are directly impacting urban vitality and human well-being, especially with shifting demographic and migration trends.[47]. While migration and tourism have brought advancements in education, economic opportunity, and professional development, these trends have also caused displacement, a rural exodus (see Figure 13), and direct impacts to traditional ways of life (e.g., unsafe hunting, travel risks, risks to subsistence activities).[52],[53] Advocates have grown concerned about the rise in food and water security, with changing patterns of infectious diseases for humans, animals, and plants, as well as the increase in exposures to environmental pollutants and pesticides.[52],[54–[57]. For example, a changing climate and increased human activity have led to permafrost thaw, extreme weather, melting sea ice and coastal erosion. Transportation and industrial activity has brought with it contaminants and pollutants.[58] Glacial snow degradation may lead to the release of hazards, which are risks to human health.
