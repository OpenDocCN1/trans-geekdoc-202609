The meaning we attach to our life is elastic, ever-fluctuating as the momentum of personal experience moves us beyond our present circumstance. But it is the ability to transcend oneself, become infatuated and motivated by what exists beyond the limited sphere of self that cements a purpose for living in our soul and meaning (beyond tangible reward) to live in general (Wong, 2012).

During Viktor Frankl's stay in the concentration camp at Auschwitz, he observed how different prisoners dealt with their peril. It became clear to him what differentiated them from the level of torture that they endured or whether they had succumbed to a sickening disease; rather, their character was a result of internal decision-making. Realizing that it is the spiritual freedom to choose how one responds to situations that make life meaningful, he concluded: "if there is meaning in life at all, then there must be meaning in suffering" (Frankl, 1992). One's attitude is the ultimate deciding factor between life and death. Those who died, Frankl observed, did not from lack of food or medicinal treatments, but from lack of hope; lack of a purpose for living. For what matters is, to transform tragedy into triumph, to turn one's unique potential at its best, which is to ferment the creativity and one's predicament into a human achievement” (Frankl, 1992, p. 112).

Meaning is a fundamental underpinning, both for brewing and distillation of understanding, and that drives solutions. Metaphorically speaking, meaning is “the upon which something becomes something” (Hung, Burpy, Lori, & Koh, 2007, p.713), and can be effectively communicated through collective action (Donnellon, Gray, & Bougon, 1996). Meaning, cannot be transmitted; it requires an active participation of the individual in an intrapersonal process (Drageset, Haugan, & Tranvåg, 2017), however meaning can be derived and modified through both inward and outward dialogue, Donnellon, Gray & Bougon, 1986).
