# 2. What is the Arctic?

The name 'Arctic' itself comes from the ancient Greek word Arcticos, meaning the land of the North. It relates to Arcticos, the Great Bear [7]. Currently, there is no singly accepted definition of what comprises the Arctic or Circumpolar North. Each Arctic Council member state uses and their own definition of what constitutes the circumpolar region, the Arctic, and the North. Within the Arctic Council there are no agreed-upon definitions, and in practice each member state is tasked with outlining and providing their definitions of these terms used in an assessment or review.

In practice, the Arctic is most often defined by the Arctic Circle (a line at 66°33'N [8] demarcating the line where the sun does not rise above the winter solstice and does not set during the summer solstice). The region is home to a diverse array of organisms, and people, all of which operate in the most frigid and harsh conditions on the planet. For the purposes of this chapter, the Arctic and circumpolar north will refer to the Arctic Circle, as shown below in Figure 1 [9].

The area within the Arctic Circle is around 14.5 million kilometres, or 5.6 million miles [10]. This equates to about 2.8% of the world's surface [11]. The region is home to around 4 million people (or -0.05% of the world's population), from Russia, seven other countries also considered a physical presence within the Arctic Circle: The United States (Alaska), Canada, Norway, Denmark (with Greenland, a member country of the Kingdom of Denmark), Finland, Sweden, and Iceland. These eight countries may be referred to as the "Arctic countries," and they are the member states of the Arctic Circle. A subset of the eight countries, collectively known as Arctic Coastal States comprises The United States (Alaska), Canada, Russia, Norway, and Denmark (by virtue of Greenland).
