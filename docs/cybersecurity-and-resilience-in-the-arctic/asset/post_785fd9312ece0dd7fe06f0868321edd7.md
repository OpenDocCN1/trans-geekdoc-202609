- Resilience is not well-understood. This lack of understanding can lead to duplication of or mismatched functionality for resilience and security requirements and use. The inverse might also occur: not covering all resilience domains as some aspects are assumed to be covered by security. For instance, ENISA mentioned resilience in the context of networked assets, the Arctic Council talks about resilience from a socio-ecological perspective.

- Resilience is nuanced. Despite a large body of work resilience remains neither well-defined nor universally-accepted as a standalone term and can overlap with several interpretations and viewpoints. There are differences of opinion in what resilience encompasses, and what the overlap between resilience, risks and cybersecurity, lead to disagreements among practitioners and researchers. Furthermore, improvements in security and resilience may not be reflected or captured by metrics. Different stakeholders are also likely to takeaway different things from resilience discussions.

- Models and works are largely conceptual. The works discussed mainly propose procedures in order to tackle the resilience issue. This methodological approach to examining resilience has its merits, but few works propose specific Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) that can be universally applied to organisations, and how they relate to the operations.

- There is a severe lack of tools for analytics about organisation or mission resilience. This prevents us from measuring, assessing, improving and communicating resilience to systems with a variety of stakeholders. Most security applications today treat threats from a network-centric perspective. It is difficult to discern real-world consequences of resilience concerns such as attacks or when investigating network-centric alone, and a broader look at what resilience is necessary, to include social, mission processes and other factors.

- Resilience models and efforts remain fragmented and incompatible. With many perspectives on how to achieve resilience, few efforts have to harmonise the various models.

These themes motivate us to examine whether an understanding of analytics can help refine the concept resilience further, so it may be adopted moving forward. We do by identifying requirements for resilience analytics, and discuss how existing analytics techniques might be used for arctic resilience.

## 2.2. Resilience Challenges

Many threats exist in the resilience space, these include:

- Attackers. Malicious agents such as hacktivists, terrorists, state actors, criminals and script kiddies (people who simply make use of attacking tools without understanding them) who will all attack for different reasons. Each agent will have different resources available to achieve their goals.

- Accidents. Incidents that are not caused with malicious intent, this might (e.g) be an unintended insider threat: a person is well-meaning, but due to incompetence, neglect or oversight a threat through action or inaction.

- Wear and tear. Maintenance needs that may result in an incident if not addressed at appropriate intervals. Examples include risks related to security and safety patching, hardware maintenance (note: this is not only computer hardware, but any hardware is a physical piece of technology such as vessels for travel, radio equipment or weaponry).

- Natural forces or other incidents outside human control. Examples of natural disasters can be “bad weather”, “earthquakes” or “icebergs breaking and hitting mission objects”.

It should be noted that threat actors are not all mutually exclusive. For instance, an attack may rely on wear and tear before a state actor can be successful in their attack. Cohen [12] described this as synergy between attacks. In our discussion, we also note that intent is not a pre-requisite to be a threat. We view that natural forces and accidents are also threats (even if they have no ‘bad intent’), because they have the ability to cause harm when we relate them to our systems. The arctic region presents distinct threats to system resilience.

Regional influences can exacerbate the impact of actuated threats, such as:

- A cold climate can affect the likelihood of certain threats such as wear and tear natural disasters.
- Arctic physical objects such as ice-bergs can damage vessels and other infrastructure.
- Climate change can affect the likelihood of certain threats such as wear and tear natural disasters similarly to the cold climate issue. The overwhelming evidence that speaks in support of climate change cannot be ignored. Over a longer period of time (e.g. 50–100 years), the arctic climate may change dramatically, making it a resource-heavy environment now suddenly available – which may increase the number of threats in the area. Warm weather may also have an effect to resources in the north.
- Harsh weather and darkness during winter seasons limit visibility, and can also make traversal for people and technologies more challenging.
- A lack of access to real-world resources (e.g., food, water, staff, and electricity) can have an adverse effect on vital interests, and limit adaptability during missions in the arctic.
- A sparse distribution of technologies including telecommunications towers/cables and remote sensors limit the ability to communicate or obtain enough samples from the real world. This may limit our ability to make well-informed decisions based on limited access to data. This sparse distribution of technologies also increases our reliance on the technology that is deployed.
- A lack of redundancy and recovery options – data to predict when issues may emerge. Redundancy is the duplication of critical components of a system with the aim to increase reliability. Reliability here refers to the probability of success, whereas robustness refers to a system’s quality in success. Redundancy typically takes the form of a backup or fail-safe mechanisms in safety-critical systems (systems that do function correctly may cause death, severe property damage or other fault-tolerant systems).
- Large distances between technologies and civilisation simply means that travelling anywhere would take a substantial amount of time.

## 3. Analytics

Next, we review broad capabilities of analytics and how we may use these moving forward. Analysts, risk owners and decision makers can employ analytics in the interest of well-informed decisions. Effective decision making is predicated on receiving the most useful data available at the fastest possible time in order to get good situational awareness. Analytics helps us discover, interpret and communicate data and information (data insight). Some of this could be machine-driven through automation. Other tasks ought to be investigated by the human visual system – particularly the human observer excels at detecting certain types of patterns and correlations which might take longer to identify. This can come from sensors that measure or operate in the real world (e.g. weather-related, GPS data, radar or similar).

Correct use of analytics tools is not straightforward however, decision making from data does not always rely on having 'enough' data, but rather making good use of data that is available. Filtering amounts of noisy data. We also need data that is accurate, precise, relevant (to the mission), actionable (ability to act on the data either or in context) and has intrinsic value. The intrinsic value of data might be determined by a number of factors, such as freshness (how recent is the data?), provenance (origin of data), timeliness (data may be fresh, but it needs to be used when needed), competence (is the source capable?), or corroboration (multiple sources agreeing?).

This is not an exhaustive list of factors, and it is assumed other factors are also important to consider. While statistics is used to identify anomalies and measurements of significant statistical merit, analytics is broader as practice. Analytics can come in many forms: which relates to logical deduction or inference based on data or models.

### 3.1. Uses of Analytics

Any analytical capabilities, need to address each of these concerns, in order to support decision-making.

- Physical and situational awareness. We require sensors and tools that provide data about our surroundings – both as they are and how we experience them. This data requires such sensors as on vessels, and ability to query any far and nearby objects, including positioning, directional, temperature, colour and other, spatially relevant. Different sensors will deliver different types of data - some pertaining to weather, living beings or other vessels in the vicinity. Such data only capture a snippet in time, but with enough background and mission appropriate selection of sensors and technologies made available, experienced analysts ought to be able to make use of their physical or or surroundings better.

- Predictive analytics make use of historical data to anticipate the future. However, prediction often makes mistakes.
