# 2.2. Decision-makers' knowledge of Arctic developments

The question of expertise highlights the need for interdisciplinary cooperation and uninterrupted flows of information. This applies in particular to the exchange of information between different areas of expertise and different knowledge systems (traditional, lay, and scientific knowledge). Such cooperation can contribute to raising awareness and to the understanding of environmental, social, economic, cultural, and other aspects of the challenges which international law is expected to address. The awareness of both problems and of one's field of view is particularly relevant in the Arctic as many governance decisions are far from the places and people affected by the decisions. The distance between urban centers and remote settlements is not only geographical but also cultural. Arctic regions, for instance, livelihoods and lands with which decision-makers are unfamiliar. Arctic conditions may present different challenges and for different practices than other parts of the world, other marine areas, and even those areas of the Arctic. Decisions made in Washington, Moscow, Stockholm, or Brussels (see 2012 and Stepien/Kivooura, 2017: pp. 16 et seq.) dramatically affects the lives of people who live in the periphery, thousands of miles away. The lack of professional and Arctic experience among policymakers located in the centers of power needs to be compensated for input provided by Arctic experts and residents. Ideally, engaging with Arctic rights holders, stakeholders, and experts will allow policymakers to better understand and consider their decisions in the diversity of the Arctic, the multiple definitions of the region's boundaries, and the challenges that specific regions or urban locations face. Doing so allows for reason among decision-makers that there is not one single definition of the Arctic and within the Arctic, urban settings raise different questions than remote settlements or completely populated regions. Different regulatory approaches are used to respond to these factual challenges, with examples regarding the involvement of private actors in the creation of norms and standards, different levels of centralization among the different Arctic States, and so forth. # 2.3. Responding to climate change

Another challenge to international Arctic governance is the divergence among Arctic States concerning evaluation of the issue facing the Arctic today. Climate change is not limited to the melting sea but also affects the biosphere and permafrost and is a threat to traditional livelihoods (AACA, 2017). Some governments, however, perceive the increasing accessibility of the Arctic as an economic opportunity, in particular with regard to extractive industries (cf. Byers, 2010: para. 38). The increasing interest in the Arctic has been described as “21st century gold rushes, [which are] challenging the political will and governance capacities of national governments who, for decades, have largely ignored the Arctic” (Byers, 2010: para. 38).
