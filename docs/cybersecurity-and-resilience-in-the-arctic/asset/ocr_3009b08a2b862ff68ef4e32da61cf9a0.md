# 2. The Arctic Context

The Arctic has unique environmental and climatic features. The Arctic is characterized by its remoteness, harsh conditions, rich resources, and diverse Indigenous Peoples and other local communities. Moreover, it has strategic importance due to the Arctic Circle's maritime border with rival countries (e.g., United States and Russia), physical maritime choke points (e.g., the Bering Strait), and the role the region plays with respect to military operations (i.e., air bases, missile defenses). With changes in global temperatures, the Arctic Ocean has grown more accessible, presenting newly attainable economic opportunities (i.e., mineral resources, hydrocarbon deposits, tourism, new shipping routes). Such rapid changes present new opportunities as well as challenges in ensuring resilience in Arctic governance.

The Arctic's geographical boundaries are not easily or clearly defined. Different organizations, countries, and individuals define the Arctic in different ways. Most, the Arctic is the region surrounding the North Pole that consists of ocean and land.[13] While similar in size to Africa, the Arctic is mostly covered by ocean.[14] Many scientists define the Arctic as the region below the Arctic Circle—latitude that circles the globe at 66°34' N and where the sun remains continuously above (summer) or below (winter) the horizon for at least twenty-four hour period during the year (see Figure 1).[15] However, the Arctic is also defined in terms of the arctic tree line, temperature (see Figure 2), or policy (see Figure 3).[13] Here, we define the Arctic simply as the area above the Arctic Circle. However, the numerous definitions are one of the reasons governance and lawmakers is so difficult to establish and enforce in this region.
