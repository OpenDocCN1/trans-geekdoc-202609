Clearly, agency is essential to action. Whether that agency is reinforced by reflexivity or disagreeable urgency, a key driving force is hope (e.g., Mauro, Sato, & Tucker, 1992; Roseman, Spindel, & Jose, 1990; Smith & Ellsworth, 1987). Hope is fundamental to finding answers about self-identity and purpose for and the meaning of life (Stoddard, Hardy, Sieving, & Bolland, 2011). Hope and hopelessness are dynamic, yet enigmatic and transitory, upon maturation and life events (Frank, 1992; Hinds, 1984); Snyder, et al., 1991; Tong, Fredrickson, Chang, & Lin, 2010). Hope—is not the conviction that something will turn out, regardless of how it turns out (Havel, 1990, p. 18). When an individual becomes aware that through selected actions, he can bring change (agency), he feels hopeful regarding future events, and that hope reinforces reflexivity to take on challenging tasks and overcome hurdles that would derail agency and inhibit deliberate action. Hope becomes an empowering factor related to the ability to develop strategies that build immunity against giving up in the face of future challenges (Frankl, 1992; Hellman, Pitman, & Munoz, 2014). Snyder (1994) claims agency paired with pathways thinking (the ability to identify alternative paths to a desired outcome), is the experience of hope (perceiving them as affective cognitive). Other researchers find that hope is the emotion—an effective component—by agency and so extent, pathways thinking (Aspinwall & Leaf, 2002; Averill, Catlin, & Chan, 1990; Bruinniks & Malle, 2005). Snyder's model is relevant when individuals are able to change their environment in favor of it; but in contexts where an individual's talent and capabilities (perceived solutions) are without hope may lose its relevance (Tong, et al., 2010). Constructs of hope foster social understandings of life experience in address stressors and the spectrum of perceived convictions (Eggerman & Panter-Brick, 2010). Available literature on hope links both individual and collective resilience, social identity, and social dynamics across multi-generational populations (Carbonella, 2003; Loizos, 2008; Miyazaki, 2004; Hage, 2003).

Hope is particularly foundational to building identity as a youth and finding purpose in life. According to Stoddard, et al. (2011), it is a necessary to design family, youth group, and school-supported interventions which at the individual level help "instill a sense of hope and empowerment in today's youth" (p. 292). Hope/hopelessness is a developmental construct (Bolland, Lian & Forniche, 2005), with particular solace during middle adolescence (Stoddard, et al.). "During adolescence, a hopeful self for the future can facilitate positive development and successful transition into adulthood" (Stoddard, et al., p.278). Research shows that positive connections may encourage hope, enhance positive health and social outcomes, and serve as a buffer in increasing risky behaviors, enhancing the chances that the adolescent can develop personal resilience (Resnick, et al., 1997; Resnick, Ireland, & Borovsky, 2004; Wickman & Bryant, 2003). Stoddard and his colleagues reported that supportive relationships with pro-social adults both within and outside of the adolescent's family appear to nurture development of hope. Ethnic research on suffering, hope, and well-being in Afghanistan confirms the relationship between positive connections and the ability of youth to ensure a free of the entrapment that poverty, and lack of education sustains them in currently.

Hopelessness in agency is reduced through strong family relationships. Dupoint (2004) finds that, in country, family provides the only truly institution available to youth which helps perpetuate hopefulness in overcoming social suffering and violence. In another study of Aghan youths (Eggerman & Panter-Brick, 2010), we find a poignant example of adolescent hope. The report includes two figures drawn by a 14-year-old boy. One illustrates his present plight, barefoot and pushing a wheelbarrow also does jobs to earn small amounts of money for the family; the second, a future (adult) self, becoming an “excellent” doctor, now well-dressed and carrying a medical bag.

Wellbeing is more closely tied to everyday experiences, including structural violence, in Afghanistan, than memories of wars; and suffering from one generation to the next. “Resilience and fortitude resort upon hope” and the “belief that adversity can be overcome and a process of meaning-making gives coherence to past, present, and future” (Eggerman & Panter-Brick, 2010, p. 81). Unfortunately, there is a dearth of which has empirically tested the role of longitudinal data and focused on experiential histories of youth (Stoddard et al., 2011). However, because positive experiences have potential to generate hope at later time during adolescence, a hopeful sense for the future may actually emerge from calamitous situation to lessen the effects of hopelessness. At the group level, objectives of these interventions must be “develop hope, cohesion-social norms, and collective efficacy, focusing on the ability of youth to act in their communities; and developing a sense of hope and purpose” (Stoddard et al., 292). Eggerman & Panter-Brick (2010) assert that data collected in their research indicate that “interventions focusing on everyday ecology – strengthening family and wider social networks – need to go in hand with everyday material ecology” (p. 82).

## 3.4 Meaning

Forces beyond your control can take everything except one thing, your freedom to choose how you will respond (Kushner, 1992, p. 4).

Man’s primary motivational force is the “will to mean” (Frankl, 1992, p. 98). Meaning is the core of being human; a motivating, vitalizing force that drives action in individuals. Identifying future goals, striving for them by creating tasks that are meaningful, achieving a greater goal (Baumeister, 1991; Hunter, Dik, & Bann, 2010); having the fortitude to endure, upon believing in one’s life has meaning. Frankl (1992), in his book, reported a public opinion poll in France where 89 percent of respondents indicated the need for something or someone for whom they would die. Another survey of US college students indicated 16 percent aside to “making a lot of money,” while 78 percent prioritized “finding a purpose and meaning to their life” (p. 99). Meaning is the reason for caring, ahead despite setbacks and momentary periods when life comes to stand still with no clear way forward. It is what sustains us in critical times of peril. The meaning we attach to our life is elastic, ever-fluctuating as the momentum of personal experience moves us beyond our present circumstance. But it is the ability to transcend oneself, become infatuated and motivated by what exists beyond the limited sphere of self that cements a purpose for living in our soul and meaning (beyond tangible reward) to live in general (Wong, 2012).

During Viktor Frankl's stay in the concentration camp at Auschwitz, he observed how different prisoners dealt with their peril. It became clear to him what differentiated them from the level of torture that they endured or whether they had succumbed to a sickness; rather, their character was a result of internal decision-making. Realizing that it is the spiritual freedom to choose how one responds to situations that make life meaningful, he concluded: "if there is meaning in life at all, then there must be meaning in suffering" (Frankl, 1992). One's attitude is the ultimate deciding factor between life and death. Those who died, Frankl observed, not from lack of food or medicinal treatments, but from lack of hope; lack of a purpose for living. Nietzsche (1998) believed that "he who has a Why to live can bear almost any *How* (German: Italics)" (12). Frankl (1992) re-emphasized that in this way: “(w)e must never forget that we lose meaning in life when confronted with a hopeless situation, facing a fate that cannot be changed. For what matters is to transform tragedy into triumph, to turn one's unique potential at its best, which is to ferment the creativity and one's predicament into a human achievement” (Frankl, 1992, p. 112).

Meaning is a fundamental underpinning, both for brewing and distillation of understanding, and that drives solutions. Metaphorically speaking, meaning is “the upon which something becomes something” (Hung, Burpy, Lori, & Koh, 2007, p. 713), and can be effectively communicated through collective action (Donnellon, Gray, & Bougon, 1996). Meaning cannot be transmitted; it requires an active participation of the individual in an intrapersonal process (Drageset, Haugan, & Tranvåg, 2017), however meaning can be derived and modified through both inward and outward dialogue (Donnellon, Gray & Bougon, 1986).
