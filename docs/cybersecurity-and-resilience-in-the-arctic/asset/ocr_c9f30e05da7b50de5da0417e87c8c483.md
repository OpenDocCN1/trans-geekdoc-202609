To date, two exercises have been carried out under the Agreement; the first was a communications exercise hosted by Canada in 2014 and the second was a tabletop exercise hosted by the United States in 2016. States have also collaborated through an Emergency Prevention, Preparedness and Resilience (EPPR) workshop in September 2015 which gathered Arctic stakeholders to cooperate on “common goals to ensure stewardship of the Arctic’s critical natural resources” (Arctic Council, 2015). Exercises have allowed participating states to identify best practices and adapt to future responses as appropriate.

States operate bilaterally as well, and negotiate and enter into similar security arrangements, which complement those undertaken at the multilateral level.

Resilient systems continue evolve to emerging environments by assessing and integrating new information, lessons learned, and best practices into resilience strategies (Conference Board, 2014). The vulnerabilities of the Arctic region will continue to evolve at exponential rates and Arctic governance mechanisms must keep pace to foster resilience in Arctic societies. As cyber security across the Arctic region is a growing challenge, continued effort is required at the diplomatic level to enhance international bridge-building and support Arctic societies, and to ensure remain resilient to the vulnerabilities and accelerated changes taking place in the region.

Understanding how governance play a role in strengthening societal resilience in the Arctic is an essential pillar to addressing the cybersecurity and resilience of Arctic critical infrastructure. Threats to Arctic societies cross international boundaries and require cooperation among various governance structures operating in the Arctic because decisions taken at the international level have complex ramifications at the societal and community level. This overlap requires coordinated engagement between stakeholders to align security and resilience priorities. The Arctic Council and the agreements negotiated under it exemplify the role of governmental and diplomatic efforts to coordinate negotiations among Arctic stakeholders, including Indigenous organizations, communities, and national governments to strengthen societal resilience.

# 3.3. Human and Societal Challenges

Human challenges related to security of critical infrastructure are manifold. Most often, the human being in cyber security is treated as either the best vulnerability in the system (due to his or her limited awareness and skills) or as a potential malicious actor (a terrorist, hacktivist, criminal, or state-sponsored wrongdoer). However, when moving the object of cyber security from critical infrastructure to human beings experiencing the consequences of potential malfunctions in the infrastructure, both threat imageries and means of producing security change (Salminen 2019). Threats are then defined in terms of human well-being in particular contexts and security measures (Salminen & Hossain 2018). This section presents a perspective to the relationship between critical infrastructure protection and human well-being in the Arctic by building on human and security rights.
