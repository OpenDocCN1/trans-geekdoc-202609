Other definitions of the Arctic include factors such as temperature, the extent of the northern tree line, the extent of the permafrost on land, the extent of sea ice in the ocean, and may also be described by jurisdictional or administrative boundaries [13]. It can be seen that multiple definitions of the Arctic exist, and descriptions of the land and sea areas encompassed by the term are applied in different contexts. Policy discussions relating to the Arctic can employ varying definitions of the region, and readers should bear in mind that the definition used in one discussion may differ from that used in another [13].

As a region of special importance to the world, the Arctic has been historically dominated by vast expanses of ice, cold temperatures, and regions of tundra. Presently, it is facing a new problem in the 21st century - of rapid change that will have a significant impact on all elements of life in the world's highest latitudes.

# 2. 1. The physical environment

When investigating complex challenges such as climate change, biodiversity loss or chemical pollution, which are adequately understood without an insight into the interactions that exist within intertwining systems. Seemingly disparate phenomena are interconnected, and with complex chains and structures, the disciplinary approach and categories through which science has been pursued comprehensively understand changes within and across them [1]. Interactions occur across physical, ecological, and social systems, at micro-, meso- and macro-level; understanding systems and the relationships between them is the first step in understanding some of the issues the Arctic faces.
