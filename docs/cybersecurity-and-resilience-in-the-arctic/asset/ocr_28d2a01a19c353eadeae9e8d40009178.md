# Introduction – Arctic Infrastructure, Cybersecurity, and Resilience

Earth’s far northern latitudes were a romanticized domain of forbidding ice. Historically, the region was difficult to traverse until recent decades, making it difficult to access except by resourceful peoples and an eclectic collection of explorers. Until well into the 20th Century, even the geography and scientific societies concluded that the Arctic had not fully mapped as pervasive ice and difficult climate made the region incapable of supporting industrialized and complex forms of Western life. In the past ten years, however, longstanding reality has been dramatically and permanently altered.

Receding sea ice, coupled with growing geopolitical contention of the region, has competition in the Arctic and sub-Arctic region into frenzy. This is due to multiple drivers, (i) to exploit open waterways as the Northwest and Northeast Passes and the Northern Sea Route, (ii) to reach desirable resources such as fishing grounds or raw materials, and (iii) achieve national security objectives as well as gain an upper hand in emerging geopolitical dynamics in the region (see Figure 1 for a topology of military and civil infrastructural and economic developments in Arctic and sub-Arctic regions).

From a military perspective, the Arctic is a region of strategic importance for various nations such as Canada, the United States, Russia, Norway, and several others, for the other hand, the US Energy Information Administration [1] estimated that the Arctic holds 13% of the world’s oil reserves, 30% of undiscovered gas, and substantial deposits of metals such as palladium, iron ore, and many others. Private companies such as the Shell Corporation, Norwegian oil drillers (Equinor, Aker BP and Lundin), and Gazprom (Prazraznomnye) have already begun to explore offshore Arctic oil reserves, while Russia’s Arctic mining efforts account for nearly 50% of the global supply of palladium, and 20% of the global supply of nickel [1].

Further, the decline in Arctic ice has opened sea lanes through the Northwest and Northeast Passes across Scandinavia, Russia, the United States, Canada, and Greenland. While these lanes were prohibitively difficult to navigate in centuries past, open water has never seen maine transit routes that link North America, northern Europe, and Asia.
