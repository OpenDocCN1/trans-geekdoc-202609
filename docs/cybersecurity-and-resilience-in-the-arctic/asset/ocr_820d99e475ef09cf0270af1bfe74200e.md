The potential threat of such actions is thought by many to already exist; Britain's Military Chief, Sir Stuart Peach, said that Russia could pose a major threat to NATO by attacking undersea cables for international trade and the Internet [42]. In 2018 the US Treasury Department accused Moscow of "tracking undersea communication" and imposed punitive measures on Russian firms [43].

The network of an estimated 378 submarine cables, spanning more than 1.2 million kilometers in length [44] is thought to include choke points—where crucial cables converge and a single disaster in such an area has the ability to cause catastrophic loss of regional and global connectivity. Intentional damage and harm caused by these is not the only threat of disruption. Environmental threats such as earthquakes and other seismic events have been known to disrupt cable integrity. Video surveillance has also caused life damaging undersea cables, andfish with biting them through, or rough sea vegetation and debris scraping causing damage. Accidental damage may also occur when dragging anchors or dredging in shallow waters [45] Whilst intentional threats exist, the cables also have a need to be resilient to accidental and unintentional damage.

A melting Arctic presents an alternative sea route; Trans-Arctic cables could have the potential to cut physical distance, the shorter time taken for information travel. Arctic Council permanent members Canada, Russia, the US, Norway, and Denmark have all to varying degrees, undertaken assessments of potential Arctic data highways [46]. The development of two undersea cables has been underway. The first, dubbed "Arctic fiber", runs along the North West Passage, and was once originally a Canadian venture but was acquired by Alaskan-fam Quintillion in 2016. The other, dubbed "TOTACS" (Russian Optical-Arctic Cable System), has been revived by Finland and China, in order to meet an expected rise in European/Asian communication, as an effort by Finland as Chair of the Arctic Council trying to achieve increased connectivity, and China as part of their desire for an increased role in the Arctic specifically through spreading their Beijing Belt Road Initiative [46].

# 1.5. Alternative commercial elements

# 1.5.1. Fishing

As the effects of climate change are, the Arctic warms at twice the speed of the rest of the planet, the volume and distribution of fish stocks will change. Populations of cod and halibut are likely to move northward, and overfishing in traditional grounds will make new areas more appealing [47]. Temperature increases in the ocean have created favorable conditions that typically boost fish populations such as an increase in levels of plankton, which this is countered by the impacts of ocean acidification which may limit expected growths.
