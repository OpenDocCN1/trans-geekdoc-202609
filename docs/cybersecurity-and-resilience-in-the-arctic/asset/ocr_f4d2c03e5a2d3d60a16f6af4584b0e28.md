# 2.5. The Political Environment

The Arctic environment emerged from principles of cooperation, bilateral relationships among global powers. The Arctic was an “area of unique cooperation” as well as an international “peace zone.\[67] Thus, the Arctic earned its distinction in the late 20th century as a distinct and peaceful region. In addition to undermining the marginalization of the Indigenous Peoples and other local communities, this notion failed to account for the destabilization brought by upstream drivers of change including climate change and globalization. Specifically, a warming climate is opening novel opportunities, bringing with it a flood of new, non-Arctic actors into the region, exacerbating old and new socio-political tensions. For example, countries that identify themselves as near-Arctic states have no official legal status on the Arctic Council. This rise in third actor participation in international organizations has altered traditions for global governance.\[68] After a 20 year-long process, only eight countries are officially recognized as Arctic states and make up the permanent members of the Arctic Council (Finland, Iceland, Denmark, Norway, United States, Canada, Sweden, and Russia).\[69] Yet, the Arctic designation is still contested. Notably, within Arctic States, there is a division between the A8 (Arctic which consists of Finland, Iceland, Denmark, Norway, United States, Canada, Sweden, and Russia) and the A5 (Five countries of the which are Arctic coastal states such as Canada, Denmark, Russia, and the United States.)[70] Since 1996, the Arctic Council has promoted cooperation among the Arctic states and Indigenous Peoples, providing a unique model for including non-state actors into the Arctic governance.\[71] In addition to Indigenous Peoples, the Council includes other non-state actors including non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and sub-national units that administer Arctic governance (e.g. North Forum).\ While the Arctic Council is itself an informal body that lacks authority to make binding decisions, it is a law-making forum where legally treaties are negotiated (e.g., 2017 Agreement on Enhancing International Science Cooperation, 2017 Agreement to Prevent Unregulated Commercial Fishing on the High Seas of the Central Arctic Ocean, etc.), typically through working groups.\[71] However, the inclusion of non-Arctic interests and perspectives in Arctic environment is a growing challenge to emerging legal frameworks and governance.
