Understanding the Far North as a complex adaptive system organizes interconnected transformations into a coherent framework (Figure 2). In complex adaptive systems, resilience is understood as an emergent property arising from complex interactions among the Arctic's subsystems. Interactions are top-down and bottom-up. All types of interaction can reinforce or erode the processes that maintain the system in a qualitatively predictable state. Top-down drivers are so-called 'agentless drivers' that shape local conditions [2] [1]. They include climate change, globalization, and industrialization. Bottom-up, nested (i.e., within sub-system) drivers include local ecology, culture, and governance. When resilience is eroded, critical processes that maintain the system state are vulnerable to disruptions that instigate a system reconfiguration. Due to the long-term scales in which natural systems operate, fast reconfigurations are typically undesirable from both an environmental and a human standpoint. People and nature organize around fluctuating but reliable processes. When those system processes are disrupted, so are the system features that underpin stability and reliability.

An example of system reconfiguration with unwanted outcomes is the effect of changing river morphology and ice cover in interior Alaska. Climate change is bringing thawed permafrost, extreme precipitation events, and warmer springtime temperatures in the Far North. A consequence is a shift in the location, timing, and magnitude of river flooding, freezing, and thawing [3] [4]. ### Tier 1: Simple ranking at a screening level

All begins with Tier 1, which organizes findings from Indigenous partners and investigators' co-assessment of resilience and risk. It provides a way to characterize the interdependencies among infrastructure, societal needs, ecosystems, and economics. Outputs of Tier 1 can include a list of sources of resilience and risk, degree of need, benefit and complexity for addressing sources of risk; mapping system connections; investigator and community capacity.

### Tier 2: Quantitative actions based on metrics

Tier 2 builds on Tier 1 with a more detailed mapping of interconnected drivers. In this Tier, collaborators use formal decision support analyses to assess system performance given potential exposure to stressors. The conceptual model developed in Tier 2 has increased fidelity in terms of representing infrastructure systems, ecosystems, social institutions, and their connections. Tier 2 analyses can address longstanding historical challenges or questions that require more rigorous decision support.

### Tier 3: Methods to predict and accommodate uncertainty

Tier 3 quantifies and predicts system interconnections to analyze how connections improve or hinder resilience. The objective of Tier 3 is to develop a quantitative or semi-quantitative model linking the subsystems and their key dynamics as co-identified by all investigators in Tiers 1 and 2. The approach should consider interactions in ecological and infrastructural components of the system along with an analysis of the impact of decisions affected on social institutions and vulnerable populations. As it is the most time-and resource-intensive, only those areas with the greatest degree of need or concern are required to advance to this level of analysis.
