# 3.1. Technological Challenges

Traditional infrastructure in the Arctic regions faces challenges due to degrading permafrost, coastal erosion, flooding, and other factors (Hjort et al. 2018; Melvin al. 2017; Doré, Niu, and Brooks 2016; Ford, Bell, and St-Hilaire-Gravel 2010). Yet the rapidly changing conditions provide new opportunities in information and communication technology, such as data centers, and transportation. Due to low population density and vast distances between various populations and civilian and military installations, remote sensing and operations can create efficiencies. Satellite, radar, and infrared sensors are the technologies that can support remote operations (Kim, Schibilla, and Bouquet 2015). Environmental factors such as fog, wind, rain, and extended periods of darkness during winter can create challenges against these technologies. Proximity to the magnetic pole makes GPS more sensitive to the natural drift of the magnetic pole. Databases on sea ice (Walsh et al. 2017) are important for the navigation of commercial, military, and tourist vessels; those have been developed to assess operational limitations of ships under different scenarios (Stollard et al. 2018). Remote sensing plays an important role in monitoring and understanding human activities in the Arctic (Eksen and Olsen 2018). Innovative methods to overcome the technological limitations of satellite, radar, and other technologies include citizen science and data collection, in particular contributions from inhabitants of Arctic regions (Raymond-Yakoubian 2018), as well as integration of open source data using cyber search engines (Li 2018); Li, Wang, and Bhatia 2016).

In order to find and define threat and patterns for various layers of infrastructure, the characteristic of the current infrastructure in Arctic region be viewed realistically: where people live, how type of buildings are used in the region, where the cities are located, which kind of communications systems are used, what transportation options are available, etc. (Hummelholm 2019). There is also a need to go through cases when defining requirements for future infrastructures and information and communication systems. From the use cases, system requirements can be defined, and further dependencies can be identified by system components that can be targets or vulnerable to cyber threats. With these cases, the different types of system architectures required for different services can be defined. These architectures include an Arctic with infrastructure architectures, integrations architectures, security and cybersecurity architectures, and smart device architectures. The architectural descriptions can be as precise as needed, depending on the service needs of the region and the specific requirements of the service. Once the Arctic architectures are defined, dependencies, risks, vulnerabilities, and cyber threats can be explored. In addition, different types of threats such as natural events, accidents, terrorists, and cyber attacks must be taken into account.

In order to provide services to the right place and at the right time over such a large area, it is useful to group the Arctic regions' services in the different segments where the current structures and services can be located. Future Arctic regions' structures can be divided into different service sectors: infrastructure, energy, buildings and mobility, communications, public and private services (Hummelholm 2018). Each of services has its own service and communication needs depending on the user group.
