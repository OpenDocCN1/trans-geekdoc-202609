Stevens (2015) views cyber security through the various responses to perceived risks and threats of information and communication technologies and the achievement of freedom from risks and threats to the infrastructures which societies and technologies rely on. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU), the United Nations agency for information and communication technologies, have a development center (ITU-D) which fosters international cooperation and solidarity in the delivery of technical assistance and in the creation, development, and improvement of telecommunication and ICT equipment and networks in developing countries (ITU 2019). One of the areas of action of the ITU-D is cyber security. In the context of this paper, cyber security is defined as “the collection of tools, policies, guidelines, risk management approaches, actions, trainings, best practices, assurance and technologies that can be used to protect the availability, integrity and confidentiality of assets in the connected infrastructures pertaining to government, private organizations and citizens; these assets include computing devices, personnel, infrastructure, applications, telecommunications systems, and data in the “cyber-environment” (ITU et al. 2018).

Cyber security is a broad concept and it is useful to put it in the context of this Arctic critical infrastructures. There is no universally recognized definition of critical infrastructures. However, some descriptions and definitions can be found in literature. The European Union set up the Programme for Critical Infrastructure Protection (EPCIP), which aims at improving the protection of critical infrastructures in the EU (European Commission 2006). The EPCIP defines critical infrastructure as an asset or system which is essential for the maintenance of vital societal functions. The EU to developing a National Cybersecurity Strategy describe critical infrastructure as assets that are essential to functioning, security, and economy in nations (ITU et al. 2018). Dunn (2007) describe critical infrastructures as vital points “fundamental to the national security and the essential functioning of industrialized societies as a whole, and not necessarily to the military in particular.” Which sectors vary between countries; governments need to consider which entities and services to include based on national risk assessments. A few non-exhaustive examples are energy (electricity, oil, and gas), finance and banking (credit institutions, trading venues, and central counterparties), and healthcare (healthcare organizations, hospitals and private clinics).

The security of critical infrastructures has been part of national security for decades (Klitou 2014). With an increasingly smart and connected world where critical infrastructure is connected and often dependent on the internet, the protection of critical infrastructure becomes a priority for many countries in recent years (Klitou 2014). Cyber security measures and procedures are thus one of the significant means to protect critical infrastructures. The European Union Agency for Network and Information Security (ENISA) stated in one of its latest publications (ENISA 2018): “cybersecurity must be a shared responsibility and all the actors should facilitate a secure and resilient digital world. However, to achieve that, it is essential to have a thorough understanding of related risks and threats, as well as secure and protect against them.” Clark and Hakim (2017) note the increase in both number and sophistication of cyber attacks against critical infrastructure as infrastructure becomes more reliant on internet and software technology.
