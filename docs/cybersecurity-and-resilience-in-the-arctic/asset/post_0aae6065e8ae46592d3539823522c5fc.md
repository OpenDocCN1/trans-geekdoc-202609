## Why Cybersecurity Matters for the Arctic

Many of the emerging risk concerns in the Arctic are predicated upon the growing reliance upon complex digital technologies. With other areas in the world, industrial and military activity in the Arctic is increasingly predicated upon the use of complex digital technologies and information systems. From GPS navigation, to mine, such technologies are intended to allow a small number of decision makers to monitor and govern complex system processes nearly instantaneously. These technologies have greatly expanded stakeholder capabilities, and have reduced the number of personnel needed to construct and manage a facility or vessel within a remote and climatologically harsh Arctic.

However, such digital technologies have critical vulnerabilities, that if exploited or disrupted, can trigger extensive and long-lasting system failures. Such disruptions can arrive in various forms, from cyberattacks and hacking events, to the unanticipated degradation or failure of digital systems due to ambient activity (solar storms, violent climatic events, etc.). More concerning is the unpredictability of such events, where cyberattacks or environmental disruptions will likely arise in unexpected scenarios, and trigger cascading failures that are beyond existing countermeasures or plans of action.

## The Need for Resilience

Not all disruptions and risks are preventable. In the Arctic, unanticipated risks are common and may take various forms from environmental disruptions (i.e. weather changes, solar storms, violent climatological events) to newer digital threats (i.e. cyberattacks, hacking, degradation of infrastructure). Historical datasets are less reliable for making predictions, and disruptions can trigger cascading failures beyond the scope of existing countermeasures and contingency plans. Resilience accepts the possibility of system failures and focuses on recovery and adaptation [12]. Recently, adaptation and resilience were noted as important themes for the future of the Arctic. Likewise, in 2015, the concept of resilience was noted in three international instruments (e.g. the Sustainable Development Goals, the Paris Agreement on climate change, and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Reduction) in order to ensure that humans and nature can sustain adapt to new systems of change that have cascading effects across socio-ecological systems.

The Arctic Report (2016) defines resilience as "the capacity of people to learn and make use of their knowledge of social and ecological interactions and feedbacks, to deliberately and effectively engage in shaping adaptive or transformative social-ecological change" [13]. This differs from US National Academy of Sciences defines resilience as "the ability to anticipate, prepare for, and adapt to changing conditions and withstand, respond to, and recover from disruptions." ## Areas of Arctic Resilience Research

On 27-30 January 2019, fifty-five scholars and practitioners of resilience, cybersecurity, and the Arctic met in Rovaniemi, Finland for a NATO for Peace and Security Programme (SPS) Advanced Research Workshop (ARW). Participants came from over 20 countries, including NATO members as well as NATO countries. The workshop brought together top scholars in cybersecurity risk assessment, governance, and resilience to discuss potential analytical and governance strategies and offer perspectives of how to improve critical digitized Arctic infrastructure against various anthropological and natural threats include anthropological (i.e. cyber-attack or misuse) as well as natural (i.e. space weather or other extreme events). Workshop participants were organized into three working groups: (A) Cybersecurity infrastructure and its threats; (B) metrics, tools, and methods to understand and analyze Arctic cyber resilience, and (C) legal frameworks and governance options to protect Arctic cyber resilience.
