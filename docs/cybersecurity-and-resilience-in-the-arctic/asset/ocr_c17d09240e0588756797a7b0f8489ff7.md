The vulnerability of the chip in the Estonia ID card was dealt with in an open manner, with the breach closed and prompt action taken to improve the system and issue new ID-cards. The smallness of the society, where most of those needed to deal with the problem know each other, have contributed to the agile and quick resolution of the problem.

In a small society, the leadership role can be fragile and rely on relatively few individuals. Furthermore, smallness can also mean that the level-of-expertise may be relatively uneven and fragmented in related fields. It thus be a real challenge to take into a complex field such as cyber security, a field that requires widely ranging knowledge (adapted to local conditions) in many areas (not only technology but also law, sociology.). Many small societies in such societies may start by building up a cluster of experts who can work together and specialize in some aspect(s) of cyber security. This can be a sensible approach from a university's point of view, increasing the likelihood of good quality research and publications, as well as being an attractive and stimulating environment for students. In a small society this focused approach can result in the cost of expertise in cyber security becoming too narrow. Temporary visiting experts can only solve the problem if they do not possess local knowledge. Additionally, cyber security is itself relatively immature academic unlike traditional well-established disciplines with coherent foundational knowledge from secondary to university. There have been attempts to address this problem and some of the material being developed available on the Internet. As an example, in the UK-funded CyBOK project (CyBOK, 2019) a group of experts from different countries and various fields mapped and produced text and webinars on what the project considered to be the 19 knowledge areas that cyber security should cover. A comprehensive textbook covering of these areas was published on the project's website on October 31st. Compilations like these can be especially important for small and remote societies where knowledge about both the nineteen areas and their relevance to cyber security may be inadequate.

Lack of formalization in many societies and particularly in the small ones. They are often driven by close-knit social networks (“everyone everybody”) and procedures are often not formalized or written down. People work on the basis when they needed how to react and whom to contact. The capabilities of small societies can easily be underestimated if the assessment is only based on documented procedures and the effectiveness of the social network is not considered. The small size and social network (well-functioning) mean that a small society can be more agile and adapt faster and to an incident, especially if small incidents have been dealt with or exercised. It can, however, challenging to ensure that an undocumented social network would work well (e.g. evaluate that it really works) and to secure that what needs to be formalized and properly done so that the framework is compatible with the international framework in the field. Although Iceland the 1st place in the ITU’s ICT Development Index, it ranked much lower when it came to cyber security (ITU, 2009). This seems partly to due to undocumented procedures and documentation not having translated from Icelandic to (e.g.) English.
