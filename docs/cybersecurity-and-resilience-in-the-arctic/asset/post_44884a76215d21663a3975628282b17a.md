## 3.2. Visual Analytics

Visual analytics is the aspect of visualization that focuses on analytical reasoning delivered by interactive visual interfaces [35]. Specifically, visual analytics aims to synthesise information and deliver a presentation to enable people to derive insight from dynamic, massive, ambiguous, and conflicting data; help users detect the expected and discover the unexpected; provide timely, defensible, and understandable assessments; and communicate these assessment effectively for [35]. Visual interfaces often communicate the raw log insights for detection purposes, or output patterns of detection systems so analysts can see the big picture as well as unnoticed activities in order to prioritise their actions. Harrison and Lu [31] note that cybersecurity visualizations fall short in several areas, mainly related to scalability, and the lack of explicit representations of network topology and heterogeneous data. Shiravi et al. [50] outline the common types of cybersecurity visualization in a table: scatter plots, tree maps and parallel coordinate plots.

We break down analytics into two classes: Data Models (mathematical abstractions) and Semantic Models (representing structures). Data models are mathematical abstractions such as time series, scatter plots, bar charts, tree maps, pie charts, parallel coordinate plots, glyphs [9] etc. Network statistics and graphs [46,13,45], geographic map overlays [22], plotting of activities (e.g., time series, histograms, parallel coordinate plots) or other common techniques. Visualizations [15] largely show the anomaly data with respect to normal, expected data. Li demonstrated the use of geographic information systems to visualize the natural hazards exposure and damages.

Semantic models are visuals with underlying structures (often based on models of the world) that rely on data. Analysts may not be viewing the raw data, but rather the output of having another system transform the raw data. Semantic models can relate raw (low level) data to more high-level interpretations of that. For same data, we might receive a severe anti-virus alert on a host. We may be able to infer how risk might propagate to the rest of the system.

Figure 1 shows for instance, CyberVis [14], a visualization that shows the potential impact of attacks by connecting how-based alerts relate to mission processes (formal with Business Process Modeling and Notation BPMN). The visualization aims to improve decision support by having an asset-to-business process dependencies mappings for a risk propagation logic to populate the variables on risk-owners and tactical capabilities of possible attacks, and how those might affect missions in the arctic. Other examples include: VisAlert [44] aims at situational-awareness and decision-making and consists of multiple co-centric circles. Securescope [49] addresses business impact by mapping enterprise units to geographic workspace-locations, and Tandel [54], which summarizes vulnerabilities in networks in a network graph. From the visualization literature, the common uses of visual variables include: [13,46,50,31,52].

## 3.3. Machine Analytics

Machine analytics means to use computers to do bulk processing of data to automatically discovering useful information in large data repositories, while machine learning deals with programs that automatically improve with experience. We can use these techniques and automation broadly to identify statistical information such as: what are the KPIs of a system, and is the system performing differently with some statistical significance? Or indeed, most of the metrics proposed by ENISA [20] could be computed, such as: mean to incident discovery, mean to patch, management coverage, vulnerability coverage over all metrics. We can visualize these data points or use them to predict the best or course of action a given situation. An example of this is the work by Erola et al. [21] demonstrate a regression-predictive model to determine expected behaviour of systems of a fictional organisation. Through simulation we were able to detect anomalies in industrial control systems correlating with a recently published vulnerabilities, see Figure 2 and 3.

## 3.4. Common Pitfalls and Limitations

Any analytics or approach of choice should be the one that is most appropriate for the task, user and data in mind. Many pitfalls and limitations exist [47], especially those with visualization in mind, including:

- **Underestimating the problem domain.** It is necessary to understand the complexity of the data we are attempting to investigate. Instance, how many dimensions are there to data?; what correlations may be of interest?; I have classified my data correctly? are just some of the questions that may be relevant to ask.
- **Underestimating the volume of data.** The sheer amount of data to process may be too much for some tools, this may have a detrimental effect on the tool's performance. Ensure the hardware on the project is capable of the visualizations in the project.
