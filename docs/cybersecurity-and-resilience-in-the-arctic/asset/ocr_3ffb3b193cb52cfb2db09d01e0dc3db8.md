Traditional groups embody deep understanding and have a strong stake in the Arctic; but the future clearly enables new interactions and hybridization of new and old.

Given the comprehensive resilience and rapid rate of change, resilience is a logical concept to embrace. Various definitions describe resilience in terms of threatening or restoring pre-existing or stable conditions. Many describe resilience in a system context; for example, "the capacity of a dynamic system to adapt successfully to disturbances that threaten system function, vitality, or development" (Masten, 2014). The American Psychological Society (2010) proffers a more human-centric perspective: "the process of adapting well in the face of adversity, trauma, tragedy or significant sources of stress." A common thread in context of dramatic, short-term events such as weather or seismic shocks, restoration of existing services and infrastructure are important goals. However, longer-term evolution of environment, technologies and social structures create new conditions which warrant recognition and understanding, and which may render old goals and solutions obsolete.

Much of the emphasis in resilience research and activities is the importance of top-down enterprise systems. Each Scandinavian country has an Arctic strategy or policy to promote economic development and collaborate toward security and climate resilience (Althing[Legislature of Iceland], 2011; Government Offices of Sweden 2011; Kingdom of Denmark 2011; Ministries of Norway 2017; Russian Federation, Canada, and the United States also have relevant policies, and all these nations participate in the Arctic Council as a forum for cooperation. These and other government and non-government institutions provide resources and coordination on large scales.

Meanwhile, the Arctic is experiencing disruption of its natural, demographic, social, and technology milieu, which is unique to each location. Neither international organizations organize or manifest the future of complex, dynamic communities; ordinary people do. Throughout history, citizens and groups of them, have recognized and responded to perturbations; new shared understanding; and responded with creative solutions. The future of the Arctic depends upon the abilities of individuals and local groups to create, and adapt within their communities, and help guide transformation within the region.

Agency and meaning have been identified as important and interactive factors organic resilience-building behaviors among individuals and local groups. The former represents competency and confidence to act; the latter provides purpose. Moreover, place offers the unique context for meaning, creation, and ultimately collective action to establish resilient capacity.
