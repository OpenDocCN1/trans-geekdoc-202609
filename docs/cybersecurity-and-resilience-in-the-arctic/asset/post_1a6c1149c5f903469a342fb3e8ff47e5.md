To forecast and manage futures in the Far North, there is an urgent need to determine and use existing and readily available information. Often, gathering additional information beyond a certain threshold adds time and costs and may promote confirmation bias rather than driving better predictions [11]. As a result, we argue that novel sensors, experiments, or any mechanisms needed to collect data may not be as valuable as leveraging patterns and trajectories using existing information. However, organizing and synthesizing existing information is not necessarily less arduous than gathering data. A tiered approach can provide the structure needed to carry out this task, as its outputs are only as valuable as its inputs. Those seeking to leverage information must gather the appropriate information holders (including Indigenous partners) and auxiliary data to identify and link datasets and knowledge domains.

## 3. DOMAINS OF THE ARCTIC

We identified four major domains of knowledge in the Far North that, together, can provide a more holistic and actionable accounting of Arctic change.

**Domain 1: *Indigenous Knowledge and knowledge co-production***

Indigenous knowledge is often experimental and observational. It is generally organized in a way that emphasizes local strengths and environmental longevity. Indigenous Peoples' adaptation strategies rarely fail along disciplinary boundaries and it is essential to formulate strategies that build-in the long-term adaptive Indigenous Knowledge for tribes in the Far North. But engaging Western knowledge and Indigenous Knowledge systems requires responsibility to the sourced Indigenous group. As Western knowledge where proper referencing of a scholar’s work is demanded, a similar method must be navigated within the context of information coming from Indigenous Knowledge and not just about it. When including Indigenous Knowledge in a framework with other disciplines, Indigenous knowledge must retain its identity as a standalone knowledge or ‘discipline’ while contributing in meaningful ways to the understanding of the larger system. However, most researchers lack the experience and decolonized academic understanding to recognize the value of IK in informing broader systems.

Indigenous Knowledge is easily misunderstood, misconstructed, misattributed to other tribes, or misappropriated as one's own. Including Indigenous Knowledge holders in the process as partners and not as participants is critical to the ethical and beneficial inclusion of Indigenous Knowledge in frameworks that also use other sources of information. There are many ethical complications associated with engaging Indigenous. Many researchers and analysts are non-native and have had little experience with any analysis that includes Indigenous Knowledge, including Elders. Trust is core to any analysis that includes Indigenous Knowledge, and can be lost if what is at risk and there is no dialogue, if researchers alone find that. There are a number of research guides that circumpolar Indigenous Peoples have agreed to, including Principles for Conducting Research in the Arctic [12]; Ethics in Research [13]; Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research [14]; Ownership, Access and Possession (OCAP) and Negotiating Relationships with Inuit: A Guide for Researchers [16]. There are also discipline-specific guidelines, such as the American Association of Geographers’ (AAG) Indigenous Peoples’ Declaration of Key Questions about Research with Indigenous Communities [17]. Many Indigenous communities have their own specific guidelines of ethical collaboration.

For instance, one of our couriers worked with the Sami people to co-develop an ethical guideline for Sami-specific research. The Sami people identified and answered the questions: What values should our research? What strengths can we build upon? What are critical issues in Sami research? How do we establish relationships? Any investigators interested in engaging with Indigenous Knowledge can explore these questions with partners and other communities. Additional questions include: What makes research ethical and useful? What is still needed to bolster adaptation and resilience? Any ethical guidelines should also include research timelines that are acceptable to Indigenous peoples.

As investigators embark on the tiered approach, it is crucial to co-identify variables and outputs of interest. Criteria selection and weight assessment is the most subjective and delicate portion of the tiered approach to decision support and falls within Tier 1 is less rigorous than other Tiers but better acknowledges that these activities are functional and required for the tiered approach to move forward. Under this research, engagement with Indigenous Knowledge should be framed as strength rather than deficit. A strength-based approach emphasizes the positive capacity that exists and can be fostered within communities. Areas of risk can be used to direct investigations, but the emphasis should be amplifying capacity to overcome and mitigate risks. Notably, if it is not possible to co-identify variables of interest, establish research guidelines, and agree upon a research timeline, then knowledge from Indigenous partners and other communities may not be included in the tiered approach. In this scenario, investigators must acknowledge that the utility of tiered approach outcomes will be curtailed.

## Domain 2: *Development and the built environment of a changing Arctic*

When engaging with Indigenous Knowledge and knowledge co-production in a tiered approach to adapt to a changing Arctic, our workshop participants identified specific objectives to be:
- co-develop research protocols with Elders, youth, and communities in the Arctic and sub-Arctic to ensure an ethical approach to all other research activities
- describe the relationship between values and knowledge (Indigenous and Western) and how different value systems assist communities contending with uncertain change in the Arctic;
- identify sources of self-reported resilience, strengths, and risk in the Arctic using input from different communities across coastal and inland ecosystems;
- establish various features of interest in the tiered approach to decision making;
- co-develop tools for adaptation and decision making in a changing Arctic based on outcomes from all themes within the tiered approach.

As forces bring rising industrial and military activity to the Arctic, it is necessary to co-develop local strategies to manage complex change to local communities and the built environment. Arctic installations and infrastructure are subjected to a variety of stressors that hinder their capacity to operate and fulfill missions [18], including cybersecurity (see Chapter 7) [19]. They must execute a prescribed function in a manner that realistically works with the surrounding environmental, ecological, economic, and social drivers within a given area. For example, road development projects in inland Alaska may cause the material extreme of cold and frost/snow events, the potential environmental impacts of disrupting local communities and ecosystems with road construction and use, and the numerous benefits and drawbacks of situated roadways in proximity to Indigenous Communities and other settlements. Disregarding the effects of nested-ecological dependencies upon infrastructure development can undermine the value of an infrastructure project [20]. Such unwanted outcomes include unpredictable costruns, environmental and social externalities, and even the complete collapse of the infrastructural system. Additionally, development has environmental impacts with decadal persistence in the Arctic. Ecosystem fragmentation and pollutants from development in Arctic lands and waters remain problematic due to the fragility of local ecosystems and the limited opportunities for remediation of contaminants.

Historically, climate infrastructure has been characterized by its 'hardness', or its capacity to withstand disruption in the face of harsh Arctic and sub-Arctic conditions. However, infrastructure faces new, interconnected threats in the changing Arctic, including profound and rapid shifts in Arctic sea ice cover, and changes for infrastructure that adapts to uncertain threats and continues to operate. In this context, there is a need for infrastructure that provides valued resilience and infrastructural recovery from disruption in a wide range of areas, including highway management, telecommunications, shipping, and more.
