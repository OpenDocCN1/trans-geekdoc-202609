Individual disciplines, Government agencies, and private sector stakeholders possess their own approach to executing such assessments in a manner that is consistent with statutory requirements as well as social and institutional norms [2]. Risk assessment processes work effectively within an environment of clearly defined hazards and threats to thoroughly understand recipient risks. Uncertainty concerning the interactions with complex inter-connected systems, together with an understanding of both the cost and effectiveness of remediation make the approach of hardening systems ineffective in this type of event.

Such risk assessment becomes less capable of characterizing and prioritizing threats, clarifying system vulnerabilities, or reasonably assessing threat consequences within an environment of high complexity and interconnectivity and between systems – there is not enough data to perform a risk assessment, nor is there sufficient expertise to identify all of the threats that might exploit vulnerabilities and generate cascading impacts to the system and its dependencies [3].

Approaches for Arctic risk assessment and resilience as defined by both civilian and military agencies have been properly informed, focus on quantifying security, reducing uncertainty, and improving mitigation. These solutions can be a cost-effective way in which the components of a system that we have extensive knowledge about be managed. A focus on the constellation of mitigation measures is cost prohibitive. Solutions built in resilience focus on the features allowing a complex, integrated system to recover function following a disruption (see Figure 2). Infrastructural and environmental resilience strategies emphasize the role of recovery and adaptation to disruption, ensuring a system that has the capacity to persist and reacquire its full capabilities regardless of the hazards or events that it encounters.

Resilience-based approaches are essential for Arctic threats, where the complex and high-value critical infrastructure components held by Arctic organizations and countries are increasingly under threat of disruption – with a rising potential for cascading systemic risk through cybersecurity concerns. A resilience approach would empower Arctic infrastructure and relevant stakeholders to better understand (i) where vulnerabilities and risks may arise within interconnected Arctic systems, (ii) how cyberattack or intrusion could cause risks to the safety and operation of Arctic sites, and (iii) identify strategies to secure critical infrastructure against cyber threats, particularly against exposed or brittle infrastructural targets.

### 1.1.1. Shipping and Transport

Projects, originally abandoned in the 19th century, have given a second wind as melting ice allows businesses and governments to find shorter sea routes between Europe and Asia. As routes which allow through the Arctic reduces distances ports in East Asia and Europe by 11-37%, with the biggest decrease in route length being between Yokohama to Rotterdam – a decrease of over 4000 nautical miles sailing the North East Passage (NEP) opposed to the Suez Canal. The implications of these changes mean that ships have the option of arriving quicker, or potentially utilizing slow-sailing techniques to reduce fuel costs without increasing shipping times when compared to other routes. Coupled with the large energy and mineral potential of Arctic regions have made destination-based resource shipping the most viable sea trade activity in the NEP.

### 1.1.2. The history of Arctic Sea routes

The activity of the NEP (also referred to as the Northern Sea Route or NSR) initially peaked under the USSR in 1987, when a total of 6.58 million tons of cargo was carried by 331 ships, over 1306 voyages. Comparing these volumes to the last decade, we see a large increase in the amount of activity through this region. In 2011, four ships sailed the entire length of the NEP, increasing in 2012 to 46, and then falling down to 19 in 2013 [6]. Since 2014 however, increases in cargo tonnage have been on the rise, with 2018 seeing a record-setting year for the NEP as 18 million tons of cargo travelled along Russia's coastline, a growth increase of 80% from 2017 [7].

The route is not a perfect replacement for all shipping however, as the unpredictability of ice and ice floes causes disruptions and slows down shipping – something which has been a limiting factor for the growth of trans-Arctic shipping in Container shipping is traditionally reliant "just-in-time" supply chains which require precise schedules, and the Arctic poses many challenges to these. A more suitable fit for these conditions is bulk cargo, where tight schedules are less of an issue. The NEP has typically been utilized for this type of transit, with the reduced distance of travel allowed for slower, cost-effective travel which has also reduced the volume of carbon emissions into the environment. These routes can't necessarily guaranteed to be cheaper however; "ice-breakers" are often needed, with the implication being potential increases of $400,000+ in operational costs [9]. Russia has the largest icebreaker and the only nuclear-powered icebreakers in the world and have used this advantage to increase prices for other countries. Fears also exist that Russia could hold their fleet, enticing states to fund their own icebreaker.

Activity through these routes, in the Arctic, has been reported to be decreasing 4.3% every 10 years. However, this number changes when looking at seasonal averages, with an average of 10.7% decrease every 10 years in September, but 2.8% in March [10]. Annual variability is also an important factor. 2007 saw the lowest ice extent on record, the NSR was cut off by a protruding ice floe, highlighting that access to these routes is not necessarily always reduced. The impact of increased shipping should also be taken into account when considering the accessibility of routes in the Arctic, as activity here will have an impact and contribution to changing climatic conditions.

Emissions from ships like black carbon and sulphur are in some ways controversial; they have the potential to cause further warming as they stain and darken ice/snow, reducing the albedo effect and reflecting less solar radiation, or alternatively could have a cooling effect over longer terms as they promote circulation in the region and increase the albedo effect [12].

Arctic shipping accounted for 9.8% of global shipping traffic in 2014 [13], including 5.9% of dry bulk and 4.2% of bulk cargo. It is noted the period in transit through Arctic routes is viable between 4 and 5 months [14], and as trends see ice cover will continue to decrease this period will only grow longer. The main drivers behind the emergence of the NEP will be domestic and destination shipping, with large focus on that of bulk and general cargo [15]. While Arctic shipping has the potential to be of great economic benefit, savings will be felt in some cases more than others. Taking for the example the southern part of Shanghai in China, the advantages be less substantial than those felt by European destinations, even where the North Western Passage (NWP) presents a faster route than via the Suez [11].
