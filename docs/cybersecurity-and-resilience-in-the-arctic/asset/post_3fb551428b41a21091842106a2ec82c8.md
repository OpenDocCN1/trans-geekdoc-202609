
## 3.3.2. Cyber disruptions

The USN is highly vulnerable to cyber attacks. Some of the most vulnerable systems and tools have been delineated above, including GPS, ECDIS, AIS, VSAT, and Inmarsat. GPS can be spoofed, ECDIS can be modified, data gleaned from an AIS can be used to determine cargo loads of vessels or misreport their positions, VSAT can provide attackers a means of access to the local network, and through its gateways, Inmarsat satellite communications can be intercepted. In addition, Arctic critical infrastructure, especially the submarine fiber optic cable, is vulnerable.

Rather than combatting these vulnerabilities after the fact, the Navy could engineer software that highly reduces these vulnerabilities and adopts resilience principles [180]; [188]. It is impossible to harden all Navy systems against all possible cyber intrusions, given the number and complexity of these systems and the rapid pace at which threats develop and mutate. Therefore, the USN might choose to view system hardness, i.e. risk prevention, as just one element in its toolkit for the fight against cyber threats. The full toolkit, as offered by resilience theory, includes risk prevention, but also quick detection of intrusions, restoration of necessary measures as swiftly as possible, and adaptation to future cyber threats [188]. If the Navy focuses on ensuring the resilience of its systems, cyber attacks could cease to be such critical threats. When an attack occurs, resilient areas affected by the attempt utilize redundancies or other techniques to restore system function.

The Navy has already implemented some elements of resilience-based thinking. For example, celestial navigation has recently been reintroduced to the service member curriculum. Services are now being taught to navigate with sextants and paper maps as a backup in the case of GPS or other system failure [182]. In creating redundancy -- both humans and GPS capable of navigation -- the Navy is ensuring that if one of these systems (like GPS) fails to an attack, the other (humans) can still navigate the ship, rapidly absorbing the disruption and maintaining normally functioning.

As one of the most promising steps toward implementing resilient systems, the Navy has contracted with River Analytics Inc., to design a cyber-resilient framework for Navy physical systems. This framework, the Embedded Architecture for Cyber-Resilience (EAC), allows Navy systems to maintain functionality in the face of disruptions, fulfilling the second "absorb" stage of resilience. However, the funding that this project represents only a small fraction of the USN budget; additionally, the project does not focus on cyber physical systems capable of all stages of resilience.

Ward et al. (2018) advocate making GPS systems resilient by augmenting them with an "independent, dissimilar, and complementary system" which will "lead to lower-term costs over any other approach" by exploiting economies of scale [183]. Little (2003) explores potentially fruitful areas for further research in resilient infrastructure systems [184]. They refer Perrow (1984) [158] in noting that:

> Potential failure nodes are recreated at the intersections of tightly coupled, highly sophisticated transportation, electric power, and telecommunications systems are commanded by their reliance on information systems and software. As a first step in protecting these systems, the "vulnerability of complexity" must be resolved [184].

Little advocates close cooperation between different disciples, as the social and physical sciences and engineering, in order to create infrastructure systems resilient to cyber-attacks [184]. For example, "shock absorbers and circuit breakers" -- in both the "physical and operational sense" -- can be used to increase resilience [184].

The research of Lincoln et al. may prove particularly useful; the authors provide a matrix to assess and measure the resilience of cyber systems [152, 187]. The USN could use this matrix to pinpoint its most vulnerable and resilient cyber systems. After research, the USN could implement some of the most effective and cost-efficient strategies for cyber resilience. This could be critical to the USN’s ability to successfully carry out its Arctic-based missions.

## 3.3.3. Geopolitical disruptions

Designing resilient under- and above-water systems and resilient cyber systems will go a long way toward preparing for attacks from both enemy countries and non-state actors, such as terrorists and pirates. Additionally, the Navy might focus on increasing the resilience of its battleships. One approach is to utilize control algorithms that rapidly respond to "dynamic changes in conditions" in order to enable these systems to continue attack even damaged by maintaining the "functionalities of the electrical system, the chiller system, and the communication system on-board" [183–184]. More research into the resilience of Navy battles, and all USN vessels, is urgently needed. Many USN vessels are nearing the end of their lifecycle, degrade, and are unable to adapt or be based on recent technological gains. In order to fulfill its objectives, maintain its superior strength, and operate-effectively, the USN could design future vessels with resilience in mind from the outset.

The USN also might explore the possibility of building more icebreakers to ensure interests in the Arctic are not curtailed by a lack of infrastructure and/or material limitations. Large logistical support for USN vessels operating in the Arctic is a major vulnerability has been noted but not yet adequately addressed [185]. The need for a new strategic post in the Arctic has been recognized; authorization for which is currently working through the Senate. This is a positive step, but not sufficient for USN Arctic resilience in the face of geopolitical threats. Exercises such as ICEX, Trident juncture, and the Arctic Expeditionary Capabilities Exercise can help the USN pinpoint areas, teams, and plans which hold resilience, which could in turn fix these vulnerabilities, eventually ensuring adequate resilience among all systems.

## 4. Conclusion

As climate change wreaks havoc on vast and significant elements of the environment, the USN’s Arctic strategy may help no longer be insignificant and impassable ice and permafrost. The changes which will come in the decades will often be unpredictable; when predictable, many will be momentous, leaving the United States ill-equipped and ill-prepared if it does not its strategy. The U.S. Navy Strategic Outlook for the Arctic (2019) This chapter has examined the literature in the military context, integrating various definitions into a comprehensive, five-stage definition of resilience for military systems. Resilient military systems:
- have both utility and are prepared for disruptions
- are able to absorb, repelling intrusions with minimal loss
- are able to recover full functionality swiftly
- adapt based on knowledge gained from the disruption
- degrade gracefully as the product lifecycle comes to an end

While this focuses on the importance of resilience for USN operations in the Arctic, it has broader applications. Researching, investing in, and implementing resilient systems can help U.S. Navy to maintain and grow their competitive advantage in all regions which they operate.
