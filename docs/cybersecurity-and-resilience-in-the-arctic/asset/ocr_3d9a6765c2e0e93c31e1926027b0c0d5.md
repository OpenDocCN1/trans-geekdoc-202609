The Finnish and Swedish parts of Sámi (Lapland) host testing grounds for cars are used by producers from a number of countries, including the world's largest winter testing site Arvidsjär, Sweden. Likewise, rescue helicopters are tested in Finnishland. The particular lack of infrastructure raises concerns among residents in the Sub-Arctic as to the reliability of technology. Mobile phones are a point: according to Apple, iPhones and iPads are designed to operate between 0°C and 35°C and should not be exposed to temperatures below -20 °C. It is reasonable to assume that other devices which rely on lithium batteries have similar limitations. Such requirements are impossible for residents of Arctic and Sub-Arctic regions where temperatures significantly below -30 °C are common in the winter months. Despite excellent mobile phone coverage e.g. in the northernmost parts of Finland, the feature of practically all smartphones constitutes a risk in environmental situations. Technology therefore reaches physical limits in cold regions which ought to be taken into account when designing security solutions.

This expansion of Arctic economic activity has complicated traditional roles, relationships and interests between global actors. While some countries and organizations have tried to focus on tandem economic and environmental sustainability, many seek to optimize their economic gains even at the expense of social and environmental well-being. Estimates predict that the Arctic is likely to reach over $100 billion in investment to support future economic activity over the next decade [35] Sustainable economic growth depends on the creation and implementation of robust regulatory frameworks and widespread environmental stewardship.

# 2.3. The Human and Social Environment

Climate change and globalization are two dominant drivers of human and societal change in the Arctic.[52] Arctic cities, and communities vary in size, culture, and growth—are shrinking while others are urbanizing. Overall, most Arctic settlements are small and not populated (see Figure 12). These are directly impacting urban vitality and human well-being, especially with shifting demographic and migration trends. While migration and tourism have brought advancements in education, economic opportunity, and professional development, these trends have also caused displacement, a rural exodus (see Figure 13), and direct impacts to traditional ways of life (e.g., unsafe hunting, travel risks, risks to subsistence activities).[52],[53] Advocates have grown concerned about the rise in food and water security, with changing patterns of infectious diseases for humans, animals, and plants, as well as the increase in exposures to environmental pollutants and pesticides.[52],[54–[57] For example, a changing climate and increased human activity have led to permafrost thaw, extreme weather, melting sea ice and coastal erosion. Transportation and industrial activity has brought with it contaminants and pollutants.[58] Glacial snow degradation may lead to the release of hazards, which are risks to human health.
