- Hurricane Katrina (USA) – The costliest storm in US history at the time (Gibbons, 2019), Hurricane Katrina overwhelmed flood management structures around New Orleans, Louisiana in August 2005, leading to widespread inundation of communities, property destruction, and loss of lives. Some 1,200 people died and 400,000 were permanently displaced by flooding. As the disaster unfolded, large numbers of citizens with recreational boats recognized the need and opportunity to help fellow citizens, approximately 600,000 of whom had not chosen to evacuate before the storm. Between 350-400 people responded to former state senator Nick Gatreaux's request over radio and television to help the effort, the Cajun Navy matched the property (approximately $125b USD) of Hurricane Katrina. - Northern Ghana – Otsuki, Jasaw, and Lolig (2018) share four stories of farmers in northern Ghana who created climate adaptation strategies in the context of their communities. In the face of land and water scarcity, poorly developed markets, and especially established constraints, farmers used experiential learning techniques including work (in other regions) as migrant farm labor; planting different or diverse crops that favor various conditions; offering products such as butter to different markets and challenging expectations—especially associated with gender. Each farmer learned from his/her experiences and built upon early success to improve community or diversified capabilities. Interactions with others in their families invoked skepticism and reinforcement; eventually, the effort informed new local practices and cultivated more resilient socio-economic ecosystems of farmers, families, suppliers, et cetera.

- Caggiano, Italy – In 2011, in this small community near Salerno began a series of place-based projects that increased community cohesion, self-reliance, and prosperity. Salvia and Quarantine (2017) report that the initiatives precipitated from a shared dissatisfaction with the tactic of relying upon “top-down” governments, so thespeople took into their own hands. Being with a project to promote the use of locally grown foods into the school menu, community partnerships developed that evolved to community water distribution and food processing projects, and ultimately promoted prominent restaurants, food festivals, and a winery. The researchers found that the efforts not only promoted self-reliance, but led to streamlining of resilience-related factors such as sense of community, trust, and education.

- Kivalina, Alaska – Kivalina is one of coastal villages in Alaska facing inundation as sea levels rise. Although Inupiaq culture evolved to provide a resilient response to change over seasons and years, Kivalina has remained near location at least since the 19th Century (Brubaker, Berner, Bell, and Warren, 2010). The community infrastructure gradually lost over the past century with the introduction of imported building materials and methods. With the advent of the Twenty-first Century, Kivalina began to experience significant erosion, apparently due to rising ocean levels and temperatures. Especially given the lack of financial resources, the community could not simply pick up and move as the sea began to encroach upon the village. Nevertheless, fueled by a culture of self-reliance and necessity, villagers undertook individual and collective initiatives with conversation, art, and social media (Relocate Kivalina, n.d.) to build common understanding and stimulate creative ideas. In addition to collaboration with other villages, Kivalina has garnered support through such channels, notably Alaska Village Initiative, arts-based entities (National Endowment for the Arts, ArtPlace America, and dozens of others), and has been able to renovate the important community center and to cutting-edge projects as relocable settler systems.

## 3. Individuals at the Root

Individual resilience has received increasing attention in recent years, especially with growing issues of social pressure, sustained conflict, and substance abuse. Much of the literature about resilience and individuals focuses upon the physical and emotional wellbeing of each person. Graber, Pichon, and Carabine (2015), summarizing, stated that:

> “(E)Experts in the field have described psychological resilience as resolving the interaction of protective mechanisms across levels, including factors such as supportive family and relationships, effective coping skills, culture and neurology. Resiliency has also been described and measured as a set of characteristics that facilitate adaptation.” (p. 5).

Substantial literature addresses individual resilience among vulnerable populations such as women and children, especially those living in difficult social conditions and gender-inequality, and depressed economic conditions (e.g., Eggerman & Panter-Brick, 2010; Master 2014). Many of these works inform how, policies, and cultural factors impact the potential for individuals to transcend difficulties; provide useful insights to those seeking self-help); and possibly inform initiatives by powerful entities with the capacity to help. In 2013, the US Army established its Resilience Directorate at the headquarters, largely in response to unprecedented suicide rates attributed to the epidemic of emotional wounds (e.g., Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder) in the ongoing war on terror, with additional emphasis on other issues such as sexual harassment. Backed by strong senior leadership attention, this program includes an extensive Master Resilience Training (MRT) protocol and enterprise communications. A follow-up study (Griffith & West, 2013) finds that interventions have improved resilience competencies and coping skills, although the socio-emotional vulnerabilities remain high and the consequences tragic.

Personal resilience is important, not only for the sake of individual welfare but because of its impact on broader community resilience. The missing link in resilience research is a local model that answers the question, how do individual capacities and experiences interact and combine to support resilience on a community or larger scale? The Army Resilience Program, for example, does not explicitly address how the enterprise’s resilience builds from healthy soldiers to flexible teams and adaptive strategic capabilities. The following analysis explores relationships between individual, collective, and place factors are addressed separately in literature but are not broadly understood as interacting components of community resilience. We frame these into a local model that invokes key factors such as agency, creativity, hope, meaning, reflexivity, communication, and place as catalysts in the growth of our collective capacity to sense, respond, recover, and especially to learn.

### 3.1. Agency

To the degree that individual action contributes to resilience, outcomes depend on how capable and ready people are to require action. Agency is the exercise of one’s capacity to act (Stanford, 2015). When individual agency is supported through the process of reflective practice and communicated to a larger group to whom individual experiences are recognized as valuable, individuals are empowered and collective agency tends to increase (Berkes & Ross, 2013). Agency emerged from social cognition theory, which originally founded self-efficacy but later expanded to generally identify individuals as self-organizing, proactive, self-reflecting, and self-regulating, capable of agentic learning. Reciprocal interaction between the cognitive and behavioral factors, and the external environment predicts behaviors. Among the mechanisms of personal agency, the belief that one is in control of how events on their life happens the most critical. "Self-efficacy belief functions as an important set of proximal determinants of human motivation, affection, and action. They operate on action motivational, cognitive, and affective intervening processes" (Bandura, 1989, p. 1175).

Understanding individual agency requires knowledge of the myriad of skills and strategies that can be drawn upon in order to transform a complex-ecological system (SES). There is little consensus in the literature however, related to the dominant forces driving individuals to engage in making adaptive changes in their communities. Evidence from case study shows a diverse range of demonstrated skills that appear to be crucial to transforming complex systems. As adaptive agents, individuals can be grouped into categories: knowledge carriers and retains, stewards and leaders, interpreters and sense-makers, workers and facilitators, visionaries and innovators, influencers and entrepreneurs, and followers and reinforcers (Folke, 2003).
