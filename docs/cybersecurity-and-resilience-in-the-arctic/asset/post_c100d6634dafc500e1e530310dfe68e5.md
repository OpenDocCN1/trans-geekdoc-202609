## 3.1. Resilience as a System Property

In determining how to create a resilient USN, it is important to be aware that resilience is a system property. As Linkov et al. (2013) describe:

> Real-world networks are comprised of technological, sociological, ecological, and economic components, such that a combination of interconnected elements, communication devices, humans, and environmental forces form an integrated whole (Halvin et al. 2012). Resilience is a property of the entire system and should be assessed accordingly. [152]

A system is only as resilient as its weakest component. Because all elements of a system are working together as an “integrated whole”, the failure of any single element can trigger substantial losses, potentially incapacitating the entire system.

Perrow (1984) describes how failure is a normal element of complex, tightly-coupled systems [158]. It defines a complex system with complex interactions between components, such that “two or more failures among components” will interact in some unexpected way” [158]. A tightly-coupled system is one in which “processes happen fast and can’t be turned off, the failed parts cannot be isolated from other parts, or there is no other way to keep going safely,” that is, individual components of the system are so closely linked that if one fails it will affect the others around it, like domino topping each other [158]. Most modern systems, especially those built by NATO and the U.S. Navy, are tightly-coupled and [159]. As Little (2003) notes, “a particularly troubling characteristic of these tightly-coupled, complex systems is that they predictably fail in unpredictable ways” [160]. This makes resilience all the more relevant for the Navy. It also means that in order to optimize Navy resilience, we must examine individual components of systems to identify perform accomplish the first stage of resilience (preparedness / breadth).

> For decades, DoD, and the Navy approach for security and interoperability has been the use of tightly coupled services. [159]

Complex systems and system-of-systems can consist of networks operating in multiple different domains at once. Accordingly, a failure in one component of a complex, tightly-coupled system can have ramifications across different domains and areas of operation:

> ...increasing interdependence... means that disruptions from natural (e.g., hurricanes, earthquakes, tsunamis) and man-made (e.g., terrorist attacks, civil wars, financial fraud) can quickly propagate through a complex web of supply-chain, transportation, and communication networks to cause unpredictable failures in parts of complex, interconnected networks (Rinaldi et al. 2001; Chang 2009; Vespignani 2010). Under such conditions, risk analysis and management alone are insufficient. A better understanding of design for resilient, coupled, complex systems must be emphasized. [161]

This is especially case for a highly complex system-of-systems like the Navy, which transportation systems (i.e. ships), supply chain, logistical systems, operational supply systems, weapons systems, medical treatments systems, and so forth, are all connected and highly dependent upon one another. Disruption and failure of an individual system or system components in this case will happen: the only question is how the Navy is prepared to cope with failure once it. Resilience offers the toolkit to minimize the impact and consequences of disruptions, whether natural or man-made, whether accidental or malicious, whether known or unknown.

## 3.2. Enhancing Resilience in Different Navy Theaters

The following sections will explore specific ways in which the Navy can incorporate resilience-based design in some of the theaters in it operates. The future is on surface and underwater operations; while the USN also operates in the air and on land, its fundamental domain is the sea. The strategies for enhancing resilience outlined below can be further investigated and researched by the USN; these strategies are applicable to the Navy as whole.
