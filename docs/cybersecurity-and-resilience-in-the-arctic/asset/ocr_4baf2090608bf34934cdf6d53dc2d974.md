A complex adaptive system framework describes the process of cross-scale feedbacks, and it also provides a way to predict and manage systems change. Meaningful prediction and management cannot alter the agents drivers of climate change, globalization, and industrialization, but it can modify the aspects of those over local and regional conditions. We identify domains of challenges and describe a 4-tier system of complex adaptive systems.

# ## PREDICTING CHANGE IN A COMPLEX SYSTEM

Converging Diverse Information
A complex systems framework organizes system feedbacks that do not necessarily fall all disciplinary boundaries or within domains of expertise. Through the series of expert workshops, we identified four major bodies of knowledge that can together explain and predict local emergence of adaptive capacity: (1) Indigenous knowledge and knowledge co-production, (2) development and the built environment, (3) health and well-being, and (4) arctic ecosystems and permafrost change. We identified these in part based on what we heard from Indigenous knowledge holders of the Huslia and Nato tribes during a 2018 workshop. Not all Indigenous communities are alike in how they identify risk and capacity in the changing Arctic given the differences in food sources and local history, but these participants were also able to share the aspects of Indigenous knowledge that went beyond their local knowledge [7] to guide the larger group to these broader categories of domains of knowledge that could be important for multiple Indigenous peoples.

# A process for convergence

Successful convergence of information provides more value than any single domain while preserving the integrity of information from all domains. A tiered approach provides one such process for achieving this success (Figure 3). Many U.S. regulatory agencies and policymaking address complex or uncertain decision-making problems through tiered approaches, which categorize research and policy challenges based on their potential degree of difficulty and impact. For example, straightforward or low risk policy challenges are given overall screen or qualitative review while complex systems are analyzed more-depth and with quantitative assessments. A tiered approach organizes complex interconnected resilience based on community-reported risk, community needs, and researcher capacity [8].
