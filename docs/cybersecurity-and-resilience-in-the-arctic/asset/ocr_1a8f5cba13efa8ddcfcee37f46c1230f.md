On the other hand, the role of climate change as a driver shaping Arctic developments is far from straightforward. Arctic regions are influenced by a variety of economic, social, and environmental factors related to globalization, market forces, as well as cultural and social transformation. For many developments and activities, changes caused by global warming are not necessarily the decisive considerations.

Furthermore, there are high levels of unpredictability and variability regarding the specific impacts of climate change in the Arctic. While studies toward understanding and assessing the impacts of climate change, as well as adaptation needs brought by it, have been numerous, significant uncertainties remain. Multiple impacts occurring at the same time within the Arctic as well as global and inter-regional implications of climate change mean that it is unclear whether any expected increase in human economic activities will be beneficial and ultimately benefit. Some communities and actors may profit in the short term but it is unclear if positive benefits continue in the long term. Far so, the observed increase in economic activity has not responded to the expectations of rapid expansion five-to-ten years ago. Transit shifts are rare and mostly have a character of test voyag and and Arctic routes are unlikely to be in any way competitive to the main seas lanes. The export of Arctic resources is growing but this is primarily due to the new Russian offshore natural gas projects. Other developments are present but they are still at the stage of delayed planning. The climate change, even if clearly visible, is a secondary driver to market conditions and other considerations (AACA 2017; Stephan et al. 2016).

The high level of uncertainty and complexity - with governance deficiencies and politics related to climate and development — mean that policymaking, and regulating the Arctic present a particularly difficult set of challenges. This adds to more general difficulties related to introducing adaptive elements and resiliency into law and policymaking. The static nature of legal system translates in particular difficulties in governing nature, biodiversity, resources when environmental undergoes rapid changes affecting the assessment of environmental impacts, the conduct of activities or the status of protected ecosystems and species. The changing climate poses challenges to establish risk management frameworks or to spatial planning (AACA, 2017; Garmamani and Craig 2014).

# 3 Institutions and tools for international governance in the Arctic

# 3.1. Arctic Council

The Arctic Council (AC) is a forum of eight circular Arctic states, five of which are Arctic ocean states. Established in 1996, the primary idea of the creation of the AC was to offer a framework for promoting cooperation, coordination, and interaction among the Arctic States, with the involvement of the Arctic Indigenous communities on common Arctic issues; in particular, issues of sustainable development and environmental protection in the Arctic. The AC has a broad mandate of addressing regional issues concerning environmental protection and sustainable development, while military security aspects have been seen as outside its scope. This does not mean that the Arctic Council does not deal with security issues at all. In practice, the definition of what constitutes “security” often exceeds the narrow confines of security as a military concern.
