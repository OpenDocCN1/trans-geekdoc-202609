A follow-up study (Griffith & West, 2013) finds that interventions have improved resilience competencies and coping skills, although the socio-emotional vulnerabilities remain high and the consequences tragic.

Personal resilience is important, not only for the sake of individual welfare but because of its impact on broader community resilience. The missing link in resilience research is a local model that answers the question, how do individual capacities and experiences interact and combine to support resilience on a community or larger scale? The Army Resilience Program, for example, does not explicitly address how the enterprise’s resilience builds from healthy soldiers to flexible teams and adaptive strategic capabilities. The following analysis explores relationships between individual, collective, and place factors are addressed separately in literature but are not broadly understood as interacting components of community resilience. We frame these into a local model that invokes key factors such as agency, creativity, hope, meaning, reflexivity, communication, and place as catalysts in the growth of our collective capacity to sense, respond, recover, and especially to learn and adapt in an uncertain world.

# 3.1. Agency

To the degree that individual action contributes to resilience, outcomes depend on how capable and ready people are to require action. Agency is the exercise of one’s capacity to act (Stanford, 2015). When individual agency is supported through the process of reflective practice and communicated to a larger group to whom individual experiences are recognized as valuable, individuals are empowered and collective agency tends to increase (Berkes & Ross, 2013). Moreover, those individuals give a voice in adaptive decision-making in their community (Otsuki, Jasaw, & Lolig, 2016; Arctic Council).

Agency emerged from social cognition theory, which originally founded self-efficacy but later expanded to generally identify individuals as self-organizing, proactive, self-reflecting, and self-regulating, capable of agentic learning. Reciprocal interaction between the cognitive and behavioral factors, and the external environment predicts behaviors. Among the mechanisms of personal agency, the belief that one is in control of how events on their life happens the most critical. "Self-efficacy belief functions as an important set of proximal determinants of human motivation, affection, and action. They operate on action motivational, cognitive, and affective intervening processes" (Bandura, 1989, p. 1175).

Understanding individual agency requires knowledge of the myriad of skills and strategies that can be drawn upon in order to transform a complex socio-ecological system (SES). There is little consensus in the literature however, related to the dominant forces driving individuals to engage in making adaptive changes in their communities. Evidence from case study shows a diverse range of demonstrated skills that appear to be crucial to transforming complex systems. As adaptive agents, individuals can be grouped into categories: knowledge carriers and retains, stewards and leaders, interpreters and sense-makers, workers and facilitators, visionaries and innovators, influencers and entrepreneurs, and followers and reinforcers (Folke, 2003).
