## 2.3 Reviewed Paper Classification Process

The 52 papers were split between the two reviewers, with overlap. Each reviewer read the full text of their assigned papers and conducted a thematic discourse analysis. Key themes, methods, and scientific contributions within each paper were used to code article performance across each of the 5 domains, according to the prior agreed coding scheme. The overlap papers were reviewed in sub groups after each of the reviewers would compare and discuss their coding. This was continued until consistently identical coding results were obtained. This process helped ensure-reader reliability.

After acquiring and coding literature from formal scholarly sources and indexes, a review of grey literature was completed in order to verify the results. This final review included government reports, business reports, and other publications that may provide important knowledge and discussion on academic topics, but do not go through normal peer review or are excluded from academic databases as Web of Science. A search of these sources, including Google Scholar, Federally Funded Research Centers, and government labs, was conducted to explore and characterize the grey literature, as it compares to the peer reviewed academic space. The MITRE Corporation, the National Transportation Center, Argonne, Oak Ridge, Los Alamos National Labs, the National Renewable Energy Lab, and the RAND Corporation.

The grey literature was classified using the same methods as those used for the formerly published, noting (1) whether the work used the National Academy of Sciences definition of resilience, or equivalent, as for its analysis, (2) whether the work addresses the modeling and simulation of Supply Chains, (3) whether the paper addresses supply chain dependence on other networks, and (4) whether the work addresses the usage of metrics to measure supply chain resilience. The decentralized nature of search prevented an exhaustive search of all grey literature, the grey literature review did not identify any additional aspects of supply chain resilience modeling and simulation that were not found in the academic literature space and followed the results of the peer-reviewed literature. ## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Bibliometrics

The initial Web of Science search shows a significant growth in publications on supply chain resilience over the decade. Publications start at 4 papers in 2007, and quickly grow to 185, by 2016. It might seem that this could be attributed to growth in publication about supply chains in general over the same frame. A topic search for “Supply Chain” shows a growth from approximately 2,700 papers to 6,400 papers over the years. The proportion of supply chain papers showing up under this search increases more than 10-fold. This shows that interest in chain resilience is growing, both in general and within the field of supply chains. This growth in resilience’s share of supply chain literature is summarized in Figure 5.

Over 90% of authors are from academic institutions and all papers were authored by academics. Many of these authors claim connection with industry in their papers (e.g., in data acquisition), yet direct authorship was separate from only one paper. Government perspective is even more lacking. Among the reviewed academic literature, university collaboration with government was more rare than academic collaboration. While the reviewed authors were geographically diverse, over 80% of the reviewed authors came from Very High Development economies, including the EU, OECD, Russia, Taiwan and Iran (HDI >0.8) (et al. 2016). Developing countries, with High or Medium Human Development, were represented by China, including Hong Kong, India, and Iran (HDI >0.6) (et al. 2016). This suggests a limit in perspectives from authors whose economies and infrastructure are under developed.
