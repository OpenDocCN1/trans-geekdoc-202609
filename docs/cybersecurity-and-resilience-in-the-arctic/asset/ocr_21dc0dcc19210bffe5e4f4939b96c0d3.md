Yet predicting impacts of permafrost on the social, ecological, and built system features presents a unique challenge. Permafrost is a varied and complex substance that cannot be conceptualized or managed as a homogeneous entity. In the Western Canadian Arctic, thawing permafrost released mercury, creating threats to social and ecological health and well-being [33] and a recent metanalysis estimates that permafrost contains twice the mercury found in the atmosphere, ocean, and all soils so combined [34]. In human settlements, thawing permafrost undermines infrastructural integrity of homes, community buildings, and community resources (e.g., wastewater treatment facilities) [35]. Plant, animal, and human pathogens may also be released from permafrost thaw [36]. Such unexpected highlight the major research challenge of predicting the impact of permafrost: community identified risk of permafrost is rarely accounted for in disciplinary models [37]. When community-identified risk is excluded from models, decision-makers could be left with partial information when they seek to manage permafrost transformations towards desirable social and ecological outcomes in a changing Arctic. Understanding other sources of resilience are typically included in disciplinary permafrost models, such as Indigenous community-identified considerations, can illuminate emergent risks and sources of resilience brought about permafrost transformations.

Including information and knowledge about arctic ecosystems and permafrost in a tiered approach includes the following activities:

+   (1) understanding how permafrost modifies Indigenous communities’ self-reported resilience and risk in the changing Arctic,
(2) addressing data gaps needed to answer research questions co-developed with Indigenous communities about permafrost thaw,
(3) developing decision support tools that integrate community identified risk and biophysical permafrost data to describe and predict how rapid permafrost transformations may alter the natural, human, and built environment; and
(4) co-identifying how permafrost transformations can be managed to reduce unwanted outcomes.
