Kivalina has remained near location at least since the 19th Century (Brubaker, Berner, Bell, and Warren, 2010). The community infrastructure gradually lost over the past century with the introduction of imported building materials and methods. With the advent of the Twenty-first Century, Kivalina began to experience significant erosion, apparently due to rising ocean levels and temperatures. Especially given the lack of financial resources, the community could not simply pick up and move as the sea began to encroach upon the village. Nevertheless, fueled by a culture of self-reliance and necessity, villagers undertook individual and collective initiatives with conversation, art, and social media (Relocate Kivalina, n.d.) to build common understanding and stimulate creative ideas. In addition to collaboration with other villages, Kivalina has garnered support through such channels, notably Alaska Village Initiative, arts-based entities (National Endowment for the Arts, ArtPlace America, and dozens of others), and has been able to renovate the important community center and to cutting-edge projects as relocable settler systems.

In each of the above, the respective communities recognized needs and rapidly cultivated resilient capacity through organic processes involving individuals, their interactions, and their connection to place. These respective adaptations did not negate the value of institutional efforts; rather, they informed or created more effective pathways to external support. # 3. Individuals at the Root

Individual resilience has received increasing attention in recent years, especially with growing issues of social pressure, sustained conflict, and substance abuse. Much of the literature about resilience and individuals focuses upon the physical and emotional wellbeing of each person. Graber, Pichon, and Carabine (2015), summarizing, stated that:

> “(E)Experts in the field have described psychological resilience as resolving the interaction of protective mechanisms across levels, including factors such as supportive family and relationships, effective coping skills, culture and neurology. Resiliency has also been described and measured as a set of characteristics that facilitate adaptation.” (p. 5).

Substantial literature addresses individual resilience among vulnerable populations such as women and children, especially those living in difficult social conditions and gender-inequality, and depressed economic conditions (e.g., Eggerman & Panter-Brick, 2010; Master 2014). Many of these works inform how, policies, and cultural factors impact the potential for individuals to transcend difficulties; provide useful insights to those seeking self-help); and possibly inform initiatives by powerful entities with the capacity to help. In 2013, the US Army established its Resilience Directorate at the headquarters, largely in response to unprecedented suicide rates attributed to the epidemic of emotional wounds (e.g., Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder) in the ongoing war on terror, with additional emphasis on other issues such as sexual harassment.
