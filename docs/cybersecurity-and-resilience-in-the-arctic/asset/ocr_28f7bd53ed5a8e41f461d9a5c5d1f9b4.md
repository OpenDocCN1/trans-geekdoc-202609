Therefore, resilience provides a common ground for cybersecurity governance by enabling systems to cope with uncertain system change and adapt as needed when disruption occurs.[122] The effects of cyberattacks to exceptionally critical infrastructure are unknown.[122] Resilience allows actors to (1) identify vulnerabilities and risks within the Arctic infrastructure, (2) understand how cyber-attacks can cause cascading risks to the operation and safety of Arctic sites, and (3) identify strategies that secure critical infrastructure against threats.[122] A cohesive framework does not exist, to our knowledge. Most of governance mechanisms are framed across different industries such as telecommunication, aviation, space, and maritime.[122], [133], [134] A combination of hard and soft laws within a governance framework are necessary in order to establish a coherent framework that can ensure cyber-resilience in Arctic and critical infrastructures

One challenge for establishing such a framework for Arctic cyber-resilience is that regions do not easily enter national decision making—especially if those concerns come from remote areas with small populations. Arctic problems are often perceived as “far-away”—it is not easy to visualize disruptions in the polar north can directly affect the national economy and safety.[135] To redefine cybersecurity legal frameworks, policies need to include human security concerns simultaneously with the acknowledgement that successful future digitalization depends upon robust cybersecurity. Cybersecurity is just a mechanism to protect national security. Cybersecurity is a mechanism to protect human rights by ensuring that there are minimal to disruptions to everyday life (e.g. loss of electric supply, supply of internet access, loss of financial transactions capabilities, etc.)[136], While seemingly “far-away” to the midlatitudes, Arctic cyber-resilience is critical both within the region and globally

Experts have argued that a solution focused on cyber-resilience is paramount as a universal code.[144] More importantly, the investment and build-out of undersea fiber optic cables[145] presents new and dire vulnerabilities. Cutting cables can be a security threat since World War I.[146], [147] Most global communication and data is carried through fiber optic cables. Millions of financial transactions occurring daily through these cables. This has led to geopolitical tensions as the control of such a cable would allow Russia and China global influence over communications and data transfers. Recent tension between Russia and China and other powers has degraded.[148]–[150] Hybrid threats such as cyber attacks—physical or virtual—to these cables are a national security risk.
