The duty contained in UNCLOS Article 115 can be traced back to Article 29 of the High Seas Convention [29] and Article 7 (1) of the 1884 Convention for the Protection of Submarine Cables [30] [8]. It is to be noted, however, that it will be difficult in practice to provide sufficient evidence to support a claim against the owner of the cable.

Individuals who are affected by an intentional interference with subsea cables will seek redress in domestic legal systems: UNCLOS Article 113 [8] obliges states to create norms in domestic criminal law [30] which aim at protecting cables. UNCLOS does not provide a direct basis for compensation for non-state cable owners, nor for individual users. The practical question will be whether national courts are able to provide justice, in particular in cases of intentional interference by foreign agents as part of a campaign of hybrid warfare.

# 4 Conclusions and outlook

When it comes to subsea cables, international law is still stuck in the old, state-centered paradigm. But the Westphalian approach to international law remains at the core of 21st century international law requires new solutions which are not adequately provided by the lex lata – at least on the international level. It appears surprisingly that especially large corporations, which operate globally and have the technical need, competence, and financial ability to engage in the laying of intercontinental subsea cables, should be content with their Westphalian-era approach to the nation state in order to protect their rights internationally. While it must be noted there are non-public discussions between corporations and states in this regard, the problem of intentional or unintentional damages to subsea cables is hardly new.

What is the need to corporations, states and individual users depend on the functioning of subsea cables and the significance a disruption can have only in economic terms but also in terms of interpersonal relationships and public health.
