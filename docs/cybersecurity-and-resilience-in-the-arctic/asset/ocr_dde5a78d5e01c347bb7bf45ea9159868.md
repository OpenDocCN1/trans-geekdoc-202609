# 4.3 Probabilistic Risk Assessment

When analysing complex systems, one useful technique is probabilistic risk assessment (PRA). PRAs model how, software, and hardware elements of a system interact with each other. Also, they assess the most significant contributors to the risks of the system and determine the value of the PRA (Modrarres, Kaminsky and Krivsov, 2017). In its essence, PRA aims to provide a framework for answering three key questions (U.S. NRC, 2019): (a) What can go wrong within a system and what are the catalysts to lead to adverse consequences? (b) What and how severe are the adverse consequences that the technology may be eventually subjected to as a result of the outcome of the catalysts? and (c) How likely to occur these undesirable consequences; what are their probabilities/frequencies?

At a fundamental level, every risk assessment requires deep knowledge of a system's processes as well as the adverse consequences in the occurrence of failure (Yatsalo et al., 2016). Without this information, performing an analysis will prove ineffective (Linkov et al., 2016). For PRA, the procedure for quantifying risk can be outlined in 8 steps: (1) identification of ends of interest; (2) system familiarization and data collection; (3) identification, selection, and screening of catalyst events; (4) definition and modelling of all scenarios linking catalyst event to the ends state; (5) modelling of pivotal events; (6) risk quantification for each pivotal event and scenario - risk aggregation for all ends states; (7) full uncertainty - sensitivity analysis as needed; (8) risk assessment ranking (Ostrom & Wilhelmsen, 2012).

In order to safeguard people and environments from consequences, identifying the potential hazards and the causes within the system of these hazards is necessary. Hazard analysis can be used to do this. However, only quantify threat, and not the likelihood or uncertainty of them to occur. PRA works to first first what can happen, how *likely* it is to happen, and then if it does happen what the consequences are. For some applications this technique works well. In Aerospace and the nuclear sectors, analysis of this kind is extremely useful. It allows for the quantification of well-known risks and provides evaluations that can inform decision making and risk management strategies.

However, in the case of the Arctic, where the conduct of a malicious actor or human error is indirect than analysing and quantifying such risks may prove to be increasingly challenging. PRA works as a useful decision-making tool, focusing on components that require hardening, however, for events where there is high uncertainty and a lack of historical data to reference, using this method to analyse risks is insufficient to comprehensively protect a system. Moreover, numbers taken from results of risk analysis using this tool can be taken as objective fact, rather than what they are: subjective evaluations of hazard level and probability (Wiggins, 1985). Ignorance of this could lead to dangerous negligence in Arctic operations where the risks are fundamentally fast-changing and unpredictable.
