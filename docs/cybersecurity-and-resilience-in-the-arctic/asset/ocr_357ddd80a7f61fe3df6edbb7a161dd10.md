Arctic Infrastructure
The melting of Arctic ice presents both new opportunities for growth and new challenges to overcome as Arctic expands into this largely unexplored territory. The private sector, shipping and tourism efforts benefit greatly from the Northwest Passage through Canada and the Northeast Passage and Northern Sea Route. The thaw exposes oil and natural gas fields that could house up to a quarter of the Earth's obtainable petroleum, offering an incentive for oil and natural gas companies to move north. In the public sector, military transportation, defense, and search and rescue operations will need to keep pace with private sector growth. As these interest groups, both civil and military, seek to operate in the Arctic, they bring with them the need for expanded Arctic infrastructure, both temporary and permanent.

This additional infrastructure brings with it the potential for larger disasters due to cyber disruption. For example, the Kolos Data Center was built in Ballangen, Norway plains to take advantage of hydroelectric power and use the cold climate to cool its servers. The large amount of data stored makes it a prime target for hacking or phishing attack, and damage to the facility, whether cyber or physical, will be difficult to repair given the facility's remote location. Even temporary infrastructure could pose risks; consider the voyage of the American *Crystal Serenity*, which in 2016 was successful, the potentially disastrous result of ship's onboard computer being hacked, including loss of life of the passengers and spilling of oil and other materials into Arctic waters.

The traditional security response to an attack by an outside enemy is to "harden" the system, in order to prevent the enemy from penetrating it. However, this approach to cyber security is generally ineffective in the long run; more resilience is necessary, the ability for a system to recover from disruption or failure.
