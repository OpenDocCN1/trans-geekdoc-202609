# 6.1. United States (US) Arctic Policy

Domestic lobbying efforts emphasizing the need for an American Arctic agenda have been occurring since the turn of the millennia (eg. Commonwealth North). The United States are called out for their lack of participation and importance placed as Arctic Arctic, and many argue that they need to understand and embrace their identity as an Arctic Nation [44].

President Bush's administration released the "National Presidential Directive/NSPD-66" in 2009, overtaking previous directives such as the "Presidential Decision Directive/PDD; issued 1994) with respect to Arctic and Antarctic policy. The Arctic policy came late in the Bush administration's final weeks, due to its bipartite nature the document is still considered to be relevant for current and future administrations.

The Policy begins by defining US interest in the Arctic in light of several developments:

+   1. Changes in national policies of Homeland Security;
2. The effects and impacts of climate change and human activity;
3. The establishment and ongoing work of the Arctic Council; and
4. A growing awareness that the Arctic is both fragile and rich in resources.

Other interests denoted are those concerning the delineation of territorial and maritime boundaries, scientific research and monitoring, transportation, energy, and environmental protection. The document states that the United States is dedicated to cooperation, but also leaves room for unilateral action deemed necessary.NSPD-66 then goes to include policy deliverables, covering six keys through which said policies are likely to be organized. Based on these statements, the Arctic Region Policy of the United States is:

+   1. Meet national and homeland security needs relevant in the Arctic;
2. Protect the Arctic environment and safeguard its biological resources;
3. Ensure sustainable resource management and economic development;
4. Strengthen and utilize the mechanisms and institutions for cooperation with other Arctic states can be achieved;
5. Recognize and involve indigenous communities in Arctic policy making;
6. Enhance scientific research and monitoring for local, regional and global environmental issues.

The USA's policy puts a focus on solving national/homeland security issues and border concerns, particularly those relating to maritime areas through bolstering military presence in aiming of projecting power throughout the region. America's shortcomings in ratifying UNCLOS not, however, prevented them from wanting to establish continental shelves, and also come to an agreement with Russia surrounding the 1990 US-Russian boundary agreement.
