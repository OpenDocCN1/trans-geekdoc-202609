# Analytical Methods and Tools

In the modern era, all missions survive on data. A correct knowledge of environmental conditions, incoming threats, and available resources is critical to any mission. In the case of Arctic operations, where an error as innocuous as overestimating the outdoor temperature could mean the difference between life and death, preserving the integrity of critical data is especially vital. An instrument could help, a breaker could miss an incoming attack, or an adversary breach security and compromise the reliability of measuring systems. For these situations, having a data collection system in place that is both hard — able to resist attacks and resilient — able to recover from partial or total failure — is essential.

Also considering how to tell whether an Arctic operation is sufficiently resilient in these types of disaster scenarios before the operation begins. With improved modeling and resilience standards, Arctic missions could be assessed during their planning phases to account for these conditions. Additionally, as Arctic conditions become increasingly favorable to commercial activity, resilience standards could play a significant role in determining the Arctic economic environment. Unfortunately to date, there are no international standards for Arctic resilience. This lack of standards does more than complicate modeling; it leaves Arctic facilities with no guidelines for what data they need to collect to maintain operation and no way to validate that they are operating safely.

# Social Inquiry and Governance

The cyber infrastructure required to support Arctic physical infrastructure creates new questions surrounding its governance, maintenance, and effects on local peoples and environment. For example, laying undersea fiber-optic cable is widely considered appropriate for bringing internet connections to Arctic North America; one company has expressed its desire to run cable under the Pacific Ocean to Japan and under the Arctic Ocean to the UK. As such a task has never been undertaken, many questions surrounding it remains unaffected. What effects does laying the cable have on the environment, particularly given jurisdictional andor national security concerns for Arctic and sub-Arctic nations? What are the implications if cable is laid when nations are at peace but peace shatters later? Who assesses and whether telecommunication can be laid under international waters? Both the construction and disruption of new Arctic infrastructure could have long-lasting effects on the environment and global policy, potentially conflicting policies and objectives by the various nations engaged within Arctic development.
