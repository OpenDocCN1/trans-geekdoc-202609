## 2.1.2.5. Arctic Ocean Currents

There are two main currents in the Arctic: the Transpolar Drift Stream, which transports ice and fresh water from the Laptev and East Siberian Seas towards the Fram Strait (near Greenland), and the Beaufort Gyre, which swirls ice and water in a circle around the Beaufort Sea. Climate change is affecting both currents, resulting in unprecedented changes and potential ramifications for Navy operations.

### 2.1.2.5.1. The Transpolar Drift Stream

The Transpolar Drift Stream (TDS) is substantially disrupted by warming in the Arctic. Every year this current transports around 3000 cubic km of ice—constituting 15% of the total area of the Arctic Ocean -- its route, which starts in the Siberian shelf and eventually reaches the North Atlantic [58], [59]. The average speed of the TDS is 7km per day; however, this speed is accelerating in recent years due to global warming, specifically the decrease in multiyear ice [58], [60]. Haller et al. (2014) found that “drift drifts in the TPD region do not follow straight lines, but curves and loops instead” [58]. As a result:

> ...when the position of an ice floe is taken at late intervals (e.g. of one or more days), as it is typically done by satellite data, the length of the trajectory and consequently the drift speed is underestimated...The speed underestimate is of the order of 10 % for 24 h intervals, of 20 % for 3 day intervals, and of 30–50 % for monthly time steps. The area and orientation of the D07 array show no monotonous variation with time...Furthermore, the variation of divergence and rotation is nonuniform for the various sub-areas. This is due to the fact that the entire array is rarely under the same atmospheric forcing. Polar lows and highs affect the different sub-areas in a different manner. In addition, inhomogeneous ice conditions (ice thickness, concentration, strength) can result in small horizontal correlations of the ice kinematic properties such as divergence or deformation. [58]

That is, typical estimates of ice drift underestimate the speed of ice floes because ice does not flow in a straight line; unavoidably, navigational maps and dials which rely on this information to determine the location of ice floes are likely to be inaccurate and unreliable. Typical estimates of current speed are likely inaccurate as well.

This presents a substantial problem when ship navigation is substantially dependent upon knowledge of current speed and direction. Mannarin and Carelli (2019) note that numerical schemes to aid in “robust control and positioning” of ships tend to assume currents are “constant in time” or “at least slowly varying” [61].
