Andrew Holland, COO at the American Security Project, stated: "The Arctic is the only theatre of operations where the U.S. Navy is outclassed; Russian surface warships have demonstrated the ability to carry out complex operations in the High North, meanwhile the American Navy maintains a policy that only allows submarines to operate above the Bering Strait [61]. This attitude towards activity in the Arctic has been described as 'American reluctance' and is thought to be caused by the economic importance of America's territories around and above Alaska is less substantial than that of Canada, Norway and Russia. While the U.S. has Silicon Valley and numerous large cities as economic powerhouses, Russia lacks this and views the Arctic as their strategic resource base."

If Russia can prove that its continental shelf extends to the Lomonosov and Mendeleyev ridges, they would control 60% of the hydrocarbons detected in the region – an incentive for expansion. Russia supports UNCLOS border decisions but also accepts that disputes may lead to conflict. Economic prosperity and sustainable commercialization in the Arctic are the main priorities for Russia, however, competition to make conflict inevitable. Russia has a willingness, if necessary, to use military force in order to protect their interests in its sovereign waters [62]. Russia also has 38 ice-breakers, some being nuclear powered. In comparison, the combined number by ten other countries (USA, Canada, Sweden, Norway, Finland, China, France, Germany, Japan and South Korea) is only [22].

The ignition for increased militarization of the area can be traced back to 2007 when the extent of sea-ice hit record lows, and Russia used this as an incentive to ‘make moves’ in the Arctic, planting a titanium flag 14,000 feet below the surface of the North Pole. Since then Russia has doubled the number of operational bases within the Arctic Circle, now maintaining twenty-seven bases [64]. Furthermore, a number of strategic bombers patrol the Arctic, based in Engels Base in Southern Russia. An additional 16 deep-water ports are located along the Northern Sea Route, alongside a further 10 Search and Rescue (SAR) bases. In comparison, the United States maintains only one; Thule Air Base, in Greenland.

Admiral Paul Zukunft, Commandant of the U.S. Coast Guard stated, "They’ve [Russia] got all their pieces on the board now, and we’ve got a pawn and maybe a rook. If you look at this Arctic game of chess, they’ve got in checkmate rate at the very beginning [65]."

Most analysts believe there is little that sovereignty issues will lead directly to conflict [66] [67]; the current security concerns associated with the pre-2030 Arctic are directed towards SAR efforts, for which the US Coast Guard has primary responsibility. The main concern for the US Military will be to plan and adapt to the human response to changing conditions in the Arctic [68]. An alternative for the US is increased commercialization, with fears that it may lead to an ecological threat from unsustainable activity. This activity may prevent eco-terror, and there is fear that radical terrorists may identify the Arctic as a new venue [69]; For the US, the concern is not "what are the security risks" but, "How will security risks as the geopolitical and economic expansion play out?"

Changes in the political environment require Canada to understand its Arctic interests. The Arctic strategy has become a core Canadian priority, and their North Strategy lists multiple objectives. Six offshore patrol vessels were commissioned, renewing the Canadian Navy’s ability to operate in the North for the first time since the 1970s. Furthermore, construction of icebreakers were enlarged, and a focus on Canadian Regions, a volunteer group of indigenous northern people patrolling and assisting the Canadian Forces, was increased, as the number of those involved, their training and operations have grown.

Canada's security concerns are derived from performing sovereignty and ensuring their territories are declared and respected. They have since tried to achieve this through diplomatic means, engaging with other Arctic states in May 2008 to ratify formal solutions, coming to the agreement that they would respect UNCLOS and manage cooperatively and peacefully. Furthermore, a large concern for Canada is not just providing international security for an increasingly sensitive and complicated environment, but also the security of its people in the northernmost regions, where climate change threatens to impact the most.

Increasing military activity in the Arctic can also be seen from other nations outside of the Arctic. China, a self-described 'near-Arctic' state, has deployed two breakers (Xuelang and Xuelong 2), with plans to build its first nuclear-powered icebreaker [71]. A Chinese mining company also aimed to build an abandoned base in Greenland from Denmark but were denied Denmark did not want to jeopardize their relationship with the United States [72].

## 2.1.3. A Geopolitical Assessment

The Arctic becomes a symbol for climate and environmental change, but it also brings strong relationships among nations, raises questions about sovereignty and jurisdiction, and faces resource expansion. The north is a rapidly changing region, with the potential to have enormous economic value limited only to the eight Arctic states but also the rest of the world. The Arctic has created a new challenge for governments and policy makers; never before such conditions existed.

Combined with the lack of oversight, legal frameworks and global risks, the Arctic stands out as an area where precious access and rising oil, gas and mineral prices will spark twenty-first-century gold rushes, challenging the political will and governance of governments who, for decades, have largely ignored the Arctic. Trial and error what states are forced to turn to, yet error could lead to catastrophic damage affecting an extremely endangered and fragile environment, as well as the rest of the world who may feel impacts such as rising seas and changing climate. The geopolitical situation in the Arctic is a rare one, but advancements have been made.

Throughout out of the 20th Century the Arctic represented little in terms of importance for Arctic states. It was a region to be conquered and explored. We set over the North Pole, flew over in planes and submarines to dive under—exploration until the 1990s, politics began to tighten their grip on the Arctic. The Arctic Council founded five years later; governments took a greater interest in Arctic activity and the effects of global warming and climate change to become. The permafrost—the foundations of its highly specialized ecosystems—have begun to melt away, and with them the traditional way of life of indigenous communities. Once a vast, ice-bound, impenetrable ocean is being transformed into a Mediterranean Sea, a "middle sea" over which powers will thrive. These changes present the ultimate collective action problem; what has been described as the 'tragedy of commons'. Hundreds of governments, thousands of transnational corporations and billions of consumers are all entangled in a resource-based economy, pursuing your own gain comes at the expense of the common good. Limiting the potentially catastrophic impacts of climate change stands as the most improbable collaborative effort ever attempted by humankind, yet there is no other option. We either cooperate or face such disastrous consequences.

The disputes between Canada, Denmark-Greenland and Russia regarding the sovereignty of the North Pole by means of extended continental shelf claims is also likely to become anything but diplomatic point of contention. Because of the high costs involved and the limited benefits any state would derive from ‘claiming’ the seabed. The Arctic states have neither economic incentive to undertake any significant operation to assert and establish claims over the seabed of the North Pole. Symbolism is undoubtedly of great value, but the cost of North Pole operations does not match the perceived gains.

Geographically-based geopolitics—where Arctic or non-Arctic states claim a limited number of out-of-bounds offshore resources, of which many likely to remain unexplored for the next couple of decades; at least neither is economically politically viable. The resource-based argument for an outright conflict in the Arctic is not sustainable. Academics and experts who have examined the possibility of conflict in the Arctic have found each other in recent years: a direct conflict over the Arctic is very unlikely. As Tamnes and Offerald [73] remark, “Discord does exist, this does not mean that the main characteristics of the region are cooperation, stability and peace”. The Arctic states are pursuing a multitude of interests at the same time and conflict. The Arctic states that some of them are going to create a stir in the spite of peaceful region. Incompatible goals, or at least diverging interests, are just as characteristic of Arctic relations as of others [74].
