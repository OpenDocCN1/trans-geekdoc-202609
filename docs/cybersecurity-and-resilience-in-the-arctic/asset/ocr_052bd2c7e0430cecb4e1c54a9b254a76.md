# 2.1 The Natural Environment

Changes in the environment, atmosphere, and human development are radically altering the Arctic ecosystem (see Figure 4).[18] The Arctic is warming faster than any other part of the world, almost twice the rate of the average (a 2.3°C increase since 1970).[19] Warming winter and spring temperatures in the Arctic, a loss in sea ice, thawing permafrost, and the conversion of the tundra into shrub and wetlands have negatively impacted both marine and terrestrial ecosystems.[20] For example, a warmer climate has led to a steady melting of Arctic sea ice (see Figure 5 and Figure 6). Most marine life relies on sea ice, but this ice is projected to vanish by 2100.[21] Unique Arctic species are at increasing risk of starvation, reproductive failure, and ultimately extinction.[22], [23] Additionally, a warmer climate has allowed opportunistic species to exist in regions that were previously uninhabitable (e.g., red fox versus arctic fox).[24][26] Arctic vegetation zones are shifting, causing an expansion of boreal forest and a decrease in polar zones and tundra.[27],[28] A growth in boreal forests leads to an increase in forest fires and insect outbreaks.[25],[29] Alterations in sea and land surface temperatures have altered Arctic aquaculture and vegetation, aggravating both marine fish stocks, freshwater ecosystems, and terrestrial wildlife.[30], [31]These environmental changes present new risks, uncertainties, and opportunities across the Arctic region.
