The tiered approach generates ideas and tools that are useful to the people with whom they are designed, their development must follow ethical considerations laid out by Indigenous partners or other local communities with whom they are developed.

The tiers range from straightforward and practical (Tier 1) is to sophisticated and predictive (Tiers 2 and 3). Moving through Tiers 1, 2, and 3 can enable researchers to organize knowledge in a way that is transparent, collaborative, and preserves the integrity of the information (i.e., does not obfuscate or minimize Indigenous Knowledge). Each tier has a set of decision support tools. Tools are placed in tiers as input availability and output requirements. Users can assess their system state and objectives at each level, incorporate available information, and determine if the model is sufficient to describe the system and address the scenario at hand. Because it is both structured and can freely accommodate multiple information streams, the tiered approach is suited to navigating the high uncertainty and stakes of complex transformations occurring in the Far North. The tiers (Figure 3) are as follows:

# Tier 1: Simple ranking at a screening-content level

All begins with Tier 1, which organizes findings from Indigenous partners' and investigators' co-assessment of community resilience and risk to various stressors. It provides a way to characterize the interdependencies among physical infrastructure, societal and Indigenous partner needs, ecosystems, and economics. Outputs of Tier 1 can include a list of sources of resilience and risk, degree of need, benefit and complexity for addressing sources of risk; mapping system connections; investigator and community capacity; and research topics that enabled outcomes.

# Tier 2: Quantitative actions based on metrics and performance in a systems model

Tier 2 builds on Tier 1 with a more detailed mapping of interconnected system drivers. In this Tier, collaborators use formal decision support analyses to assess system performance given potential exposure to stressors and shocks. The conceptual model developed in Tier 2 has increased fidelity in terms of representing infrastructure systems, ecosystems, social institutions, and their connections. Tier 2 analyses can address longstanding historical challenges or questions that require more rigorous decision support. Outputs of Tier 2 are contingent on Tier 1 outcomes, but can include mixed-method, semi-quantitative assessments of system exposure to compounding or cascading threats.

# Tier 3: Methods to predict and accommodate probabilistic uncertainty

Tier 3 quantifies and predicts system interconnections to analyze how connections connections improve or hinder resilience. The objective of Tier 3 is to develop a quantitative or semi-quantitative model linking the sub-systems and their key dynamics as co-identified by all investigators in Tiers 1 and 2. The approach should consider interactions in ecological and infrastructural components of the system along with an analysis of the impact of decisions affected on social institutions and vulnerable populations. In this Tier, Indigenous partners and other stakeholders remain central to framing, weighting parameters (including identifying for Bayesian approaches; i.e., “expert elicitation”), and validating the model. As it is the most time-and resource-intensive, only those areas with the greatest degree of need or concern are required to advance to this level of analysis.
