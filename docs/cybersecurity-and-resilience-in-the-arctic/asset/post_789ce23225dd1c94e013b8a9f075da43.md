## 6.8. Swedish Arctic strategy

Sweden's Arctic Policy and strategy dates back to May 2011, when the Swedish Government adopted “Sweden’s Strategy for the Arctic Region” [50].

Before the publication of this strategy, the Swedish government had been largely quiet on northern affairs. The country has been active in international Arctic cooperation from the beginning, as it is after all member of the Arctic Council and a co-founder. While Swedish territory goes beyond the Arctic Circle, ties to the Arctic demographically also strong; Saami groups have lived in Sweden for centuries, and Sweden has greatly contributed to Arctic polar research [43].

Sweden are particularly concerned with three key areas, which they define in the strategy as their priorities. These areas are:

- 1. Climate and the environment;
- 2. Economic growth and growing business field and interests;
- 3. The human dimension

The document states several reasons why there’s a growing interest in the Arctic, and why a strategy for the region is needed. Among these are climate change and global warming, the challenges facing the living conditions of indigenous peoples and the struggles for natural resources.

The main priorities of the document, and a list of objectives in order to achieve these goals in the near future are as follows:

- 1. Climate change and the environment; outlined here are sub-priorities, each of which relate to climate, environmental protection, biodiversity and scientific research. Sweden aims to reduce greenhouse emissions, whilst also emphas the impacts of climate change as highlighted in line with international climate agreements and negotiations. Moreover, investing in scientific research is key to becoming a leader in climate and environmental change, as well the impacts they will have on society.

- 2. Economic growth and growing business fields/interests; with a changing environment has increased interest and growth in emerging economic ventures, ranging from mining, petroleum extraction, fishery, forest, land transport, Search and Rescue (SAR), tourism and more. Sweden aims to promote these industries, in a way that ensures economic, social and environmental sustainability, highlighting the importance of adhering to international law, while simultaneously promoting Swedish expertise in environmental technology.

- 3. The human dimension; focusing on sub-categories of the conditions impacting human health, the impacts of climate change and spread of relaxed toxins on people, the impacts of climate change on traditional indigenous cultures and industries and the resilience of Saami languages and knowledge in response to changing conditions. Sweden aims to support the problems facing the human domain of the Arctic in forms such as the Arctic Council, promoting preservation, support, active participation in political processes and cooperation between Nordic and other Arctic states in an effort to endorse knowledge between communities.

Sweden’s strategy traverses multifaceted challenges, with effort to include several features of a modern strategy by setting ambitious goals and objectives, with a broad pathway to achieving them. The strategy can be a response to mounting pressure inciting them to publish a strategy, as well as a reflection on the significant changes facing the Arctic and the northernmost territory and communities in Sweden.

## 7. Sub-Arctic states and their policies in the Arctic

Those concerned with what happens in the Arctic are solely limited to those who hold territorial claims within the Arctic circle. An issue of concern for many states globally is the disruptive impact Climate Change is extremely likely to have on coastlines. McGranan, Balk and Anderson [57] estimated at the time that the world 634 million people live in Low Elevation Coastal Zones (LECZ), areas that are less than 10 meters above sea level. In island nations, such as the UK and Japan, sea level rise poses a large human, environmental and economic threat. Furthermore, in China, where a trend of urbanization is driving more and more people to coastal regions every year, the risk of disaster is increasing. These nations each hold observer status on the Arctic Council and have outlined policies towards the Arctic.

### 7.1. China

China produced a white paper outlining its official Arctic policy in January 2018 [58], describing themselves as a “near-Arctic state” – being one within the Arctic circle but still close to it, and each stakeholder in Arctic affairs. In the past, China has made this connection on the basis of the impacts of climate change, especially those in the Arctic that will impact the climate system for the rest of the world. Furthermore, this will impact China’s environment, ecosystem, agriculture, and flooding threat. More recently, however, China has clarified its assertion into Arctic affairs, claiming that China holds “rights and interests” in the Arctic. The white paper highlights that China intends to extend its involvement and scope of activities in the Arctic, with a view of seeing the “historic opportunity” presented by the increasing viability of commercial shipping opportunities emerging as a result of melting sea ice and the longer shipping periods along Arctic routes in recent decades.

The Chinese white paper covers many policy goals; however, they can be briefly summarized as:

- 1. Deepening the exploration and understanding of the Arctic
- 2. Protecting the eco-environment of the Arctic and addressing climate change
- 3. Utilizing Arctic resources in a Lawful and rational manner:
    * Including participation in the development of Arctic shipping routes
    * Participation in oil, gas, mineral and other non-living resource extraction
    * Participation in conservation and utilization of fisheries and other living resources
    * Participation in developing tourism resources
- 4. Participating in Arctic governance and international cooperation
- 5. Promoting a peaceful, secure and stable Arctic order.

Furthermore, China also declares that its policy on the Arctic are to understand, develop and participate in the governance of the Arctic, so as to safeguard the common interests of all countries and the international community in the Arctic and promote sustainable development of the Arctic. The white paper goes on to identify four key areas for achieving these policy, in “cooperation”, “win-win result”, and “sustainability”. They aim to promote international law, respect sovereign rights, and work with Arctic and sub-Arctic states in producing sustainable development in the Arctic region.

The white paper suggests China has a strong commitment to upholding the institutional and legal frameworks for Arctic governance and simultaneously acknowledging the sovereign rights of the Arctic states and the Indigenous communities. However, the paper also asserts China’s right as a non-Arctic state to be involved and participate in Arctic affairs under International law.

### 7.2. Japan

Japan’s interest and claims in the Arctic stem largely from similar concerns that China has, the irreversible impacts of Climate Change on global sea level, the increased frequency of extreme weather, and its impact on ecosystems. Furthermore, Japan specifically outlines its interests in commercial shipping routes, that are now increasingly accessible to the extent to which sea ice in the Arctic has melted.

Japan released a report in 2015, outlining the country’s policy in the Arctic [59]. First, the report outlines areas of topic and issues that Japan believes need to be increasingly focused upon in the Arctic; these include:

- 1. Global environmental issues
- 2. Indigenous peoples of the Arctic
- 3. Science and Technology
- 4. Ensuring the rule of law and promoting international cooperation
- 5. The Arctic Sea Route
