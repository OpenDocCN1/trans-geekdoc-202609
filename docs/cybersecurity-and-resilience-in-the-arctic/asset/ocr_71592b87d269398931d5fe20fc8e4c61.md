Russia has worked hard in recent years to modernize its military, focusing especially on fortifying its Northern front [128]. Russian submarines are considered "some of the most deadly and silent in the world" [147], and the Northern Fleet alone has six conventional attack submarines (SSK), eleven nuclear attack submarines (SSN), six cruise missile submarines (SSGN), and eight ballistic missile submarines (SSBN). Commander of U.S. Northern Command General Terence O'Shaughnessy, in a 2019 talk at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, said that "existing and planned ballistic defenses would meet the threat from North Korea... they were never designed to meet the challenges posed by Russia or China with large stockpiles of missiles capable of flying various ranges" [148]. O'Shaughnessy urged the United States to have a "clear-eyed" view of security challenges in the Arctic, citing out the capabilities of Russian submarines to launch missiles from the region as especially troubling [148]. The Arctic, said General, could serve as "an avenue of approach" for the Russians [148].

In a 2014 U.S. House Committee on Foreign Affairs hearing regarding the United States' role in the Arctic, an expert witness put the even more bleakly:

> Put simply, the United States is where others are gaining in strength. We are behind our competitors in planning for an open Arctic and this imbalance is most apparent in the military power while countries like Russia see Arctic power as central to their national affairs, the United States pays less than min to its status as an Arctic power. Nowhere else in the world is the U.S. so clearly outclassed in its ability to perform surface operations in the Arctic. [149]

More of Russian land falls within the Arctic, and ultimately the Arctic is more integral to Russia's security than that of the United States. However, the other four Arctic states besides the United States are all in NATO -- Iceland, Norway, Canada, and Denmark – have more land in the Arctic than the United States and view the region as more important to their national security.

Ultimately, just because the Arctic of today is peaceful and governed by international norms and the rule of law, does not mean that the Arctic of tomorrow will be the same. Just as climate change has altered the environmental contours of the Arctic, so could it alter the geopolitical situation. The United States, and especially the U.S. Navy, as well as NATO as a whole, could take more action to enhance their level of preparedness.
