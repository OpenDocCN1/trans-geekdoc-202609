The governance challenges facing the Arctic fall into four distinct themes: (1) perception, (2) globalization, (3) environmentalism and sustainability, and (4) militarization (see Figure 15). The first challenge is one of perception. Public opinion of the Arctic, especially among non-Arctic states, is characterized by a misguided view as an unregulated, lawless place. The reality of climate change remains despite a preponderance of scientific evidence.[73] Other public perceptions of nature and the environment (such as polar bears, walrus falling from cliffs, polluting the oceans) have led to no-tolerance conservationist agendas that stifle traditional ways of life. Perception also differs within the Arctic Council—different countries having differing political and economic interests and interest in collaboration. Likewise, there is a strong division between “southern” views of problems and “northern” views of problems. This dichotomy is similar to the urban-rural divide which the Arctic being predominantly populated and rural and most urban centers located in the southern parts of a country (i.e., Helsinki in Finland, Oslo in Norway, Ottawa in Canada). At the national level this difference in perspectives has led to tensions between the areas above the Arctic and below. Most governmental decision-making is made in the southern, urban centers, leaving out Northern citizens from the decision-making process. Moreover, national policies and laws try to use southern and urban solutions to fix northern—a strategy that does not allow for the local context to be out on the governance of Circumpolar Northern regions. The multitude of varied perceptions between countries and within countries are difficult to understand and balance. The Circumpolar North is home to numerous, diverse Indigenous people with unique cultural traditions and activities.

The second challenge comes from globalization. It is bringing new actors into the Arctic region, changing the political, social, and economic environments. Globalization is also altering which actors are present in the high north and should have a say in Arctic affairs. The Arctic has seen a regime change from a paradigm of “regional” to “global.”[74] The Arctic is not secluded to just Arctic states and actors. Global warming and a changing polar environment have allowed others to enter the Arctic political area, increasing the number of players in decisionmaking and the complexity of governance and law-making.

The third challenge arises from the politics of sustainability. Sustainability is a growing political conflict, especially in the Arctic where it is given new meaning with political implications.[75] Sustainability is a relationship between identity, space, and time, often used to understand human development (i.e., human identity) and its relationship with nature.[75] Conflicting visions of the future, sustainability narratives, and what should be sustained and by whom are ongoing discussions driving Arctic governance.
