Arctic places are largely rural, have been thought of by outsiders from fringe. Beyond enterprises to extract natural resources, indigenous populations historically have been left to their own endurance while industrial, social, and military revolutions in more temperate climates. Twenty-first Century phenomena, however, are bringing change front and center to Arctic trends make the region more attractive and accessible to interlopers, threatening to upset well-known relationships and balances. The very nature of change and adaptation is evolving as the rates accelerate. Arctic regions face extraordinary resilience challenges. Rural has been misunderstood by those unfamiliar with life in self-reliant areas, commonly defined simply as "not urban" [designation: not identity] by the U.S. Census Bureau (US Census Bureau, 2010). "Rural is not "ism." Citizens living in rural areas are often perceived as disadvantaged when contrasted with more technology-intensive metropolitan areas—where ironically, people are dependent upon enterprise systems for essential services. This mindset perpetuates the disparity that plagues these small communities, and the growing expectation in society is that "someone else" is in charge (Salvia & Quantum, 2017). Top-down government regulation and policy tend to focus on the circumstances and needs of cities—largely driven by the corresponding bias in research. The inclination is to overlay urban policies onto small rural locales without awareness or acknowledgement of the economic, social, cultural, and natural factors unique to that community. Taken together, these factors leave small communities in a predicament of diminished individual self-agency (to ‘own their future’), accompanied by a vacuum in external help.

Rural communities are not just smaller versions of urban centers. The evident evidences in resources and community services in rural areas can be significant influencers of talent (Rosenfeld, 2013); they are becoming magnets for creatives within which the creative capacity to innovate for tomorrow’s days is nurtured (Florida, 2012). Bottom-up initiatives and projects have potential to uncover this creative capacity within a community and often multi-generational, cross-sector dialogue, in building a sense of belonging within the community-residents who are normally confined to their particular “camps.”

As alliances unite diverse groups and individuals, trust is established through non-competitive, shared-purpose goals. Once achieved, these serve as tangible reminders that each individual matters as an integral, functioning member of the whole, who contributes value to the community.
