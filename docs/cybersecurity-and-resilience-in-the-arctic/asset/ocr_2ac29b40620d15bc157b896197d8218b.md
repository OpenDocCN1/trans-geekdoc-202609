Bochman (2018) argues that, as connected systems grow in scale and complexity, organizations’ capacity to provide cyber defense is being overwhelmed. Advocates for strategies that aim at limiting connectivity and complexity as much as possible, i.e. finding a balance between the valuable capabilities created and the exposure to the threat. 3.1.1.2. Disruptions of Operations

For critical infrastructure providers, strategies for handling the operational emergency can be inspired by approaches and practices for resilient performance in crisis management. Strategies especially include building different kinds of capabilities in the involved organizations (Branlat et al., 2017), which are included the following:

+   - The capacity to coordinate with each other; as coordination is critical and costly in times of crisis, efforts need to be made in preparation for such events by building networks and ensuring mutual understanding of roles and responsibilities within and between organizations that might be involved. Examples coordination-building activities include common exercises or workshops and the establishment of a common language.
- The capacity to adapt in the face of unforeseen events and in the context of existing procedures; crises events represent when abnormal characteristics and challenge the usual ways of operating, i.e. for which procedures normally follow might prove ineffective. Activities therefore need to be regularly carried out in organizations, for instance on actual or fictional events, in which the applicability of procedures is discussed across organizational perspectives and responsibilities and the authority to adjust whatever procedure is clarified.
- The capacity to develop and maintain alternative ways of functioning; when operations depend on a piece of equipment, it is critical to perform if that piece of equipment is not available for some reason. Alternative ways of functioning might correspond to old ways, as in paper-based flight strips used in case of radar loss in ATM (Save et al., 2018). A difficulty for organisations to identify which ways of operating might still be needed, and to devote resources to ensure the required competencies, for instance by maintaining associated training.

Importantly, organizations need to manage their resilience, i.e. to regularly assess and consider these different capabilities in order to improve their ways of operating, coordinating and adapting. This is what produces resilience, e.g., what formal or informal strategies implement successfully when disruptions occur, but also what hinders resilience, e.g., what resources are assumed to be available in emergencies but might not be during actual events. Assessing and understanding resilience (or its absence) requires that organizations devote sufficient resources to investigating a wider range of operations: past events, both successfully or unsuccessfully managed, as well as everyday events. Such an examination of operations goes beyond the traditional focus on accidents as the main learning opportunities (Hollnager et al., 2013).
