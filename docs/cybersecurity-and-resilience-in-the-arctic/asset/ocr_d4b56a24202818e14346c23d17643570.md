Some of the Arctic countries, Finland in 2008, have declared internet access as a basic right (LVM 2008). However, the recognition of the relevance of human rights in the context of cyber challenges to critical infrastructure has less apparent in the international community. Alongside security and the right to privacy, other human rights that could be at stake when the cyber security of critical infrastructure is challenged are, for instance, the right to water and sanitation, to health, freedom of movement, to life, and to food. All of which is predicated upon expectation of local publics bottom-up governance in tandem with top national or international security andor development effort (Palma-Oliveira et al., 2019)

A potential way of addressing the aforementioned interdependency between civil and political rights and economic, social, and cultural rights, as well as cyber security and critical infrastructure protection, is to approach the problem from a human security perspective. After the Cold War, when the international security agenda began to broaden (of security other than military way into security agendas) and deepen (refer objects other than the state becoming acknowledged), and international policy and moral concern for the state's occasional inability or unwillingness to protect its citizens rose, the concept of human security took ground (Bazan Hansen 2009; Tajbakesh & Chenoy 2007). On human rights, instead of focusing only on the state's obligation to provide and ensure rights, it also empowers the development of individuals and communities to look after themselves and have to say in the development of their everyday surroundings. The normative aim of the research framework is to improve human well-being. It calls for individuals' communities' “freedom from fear,” and “freedom from dignity” in their everyday life. (Hanson 2008; Tajbakesh & Chenoy 2007) In this case, it focuses on the well-being of the population living in the Arctic whose life is becoming increasingly dependent on critical infrastructure offset and controlled through or running on ICTs (Salminen & Hossain 2018).

Critical infrastructure relates to people's economic, food, health, community, political, and environmental security (these are seven aspects of human security listed in the 1994 United Nations Development Programme's Human Development Report) is thinner and more sporadic in the Arctic than, for example, in non-Arctic regions of the eight Arctic countries. In addition, great variance exists between the Arctic areas of, for example, Finland and the level of urbanization, distances, geography and climate, economic structures, business factors (as a big share of critical infrastructure is owned and operated by private companies), political and administrative decisions that provide the boundaries for infrastructure development and protection, as well as lifestyles and proximity to national decision making. In general, infrastructure tends to be more developed where there are more people and a high volume of economic activity; this principle also holds within the Arctic region. For example, in Finnish Lapland infrastructure is more developed in towns (Roovaniemi, Kemi and Tornio) and in terms (Saarisiski, Yallas, and Pyha-Luosto) than in rural areas. In terms of cyber security, this means that information infrastructure is more redundant, fault-tolerant, and resilient and, thus, more reliable around towns, as well as the main roads, in rural areas.
