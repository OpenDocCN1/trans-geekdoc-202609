## 2.1.2. The Impact of Climate Change: Environmental Challenges and Threats

Climate change, which is affecting the Arctic at a faster rate than anywhere else on earth [18], is compounding the environmental hazards inherent to ice. The speed of warming in the Arctic is twice as fast as the rest of the world, resulting in a dramatic reduction in minimum ice extent during winter and summer. This pattern is likely to continue at increasing rates due to the high albedo (surface reflectivity) of ice when compared to open water [17]. The different albedos create a positive feedback loop: as ice melts and is replaced by water, radiation is absorbed, leading to increased temperatures, which in turn cause even more ice to melt, and so on [17], [19], [20], [21]. This process is known as the ice-albedo feedback loop.

### 2.1.2.1. Melting Sea Ice

In 2018 and 2019 winter ice extent hit near-record lows; additionally, the Bering Strait was almost completely free of ice, an occurrence which hasn't happened since 1850. Meanwhile, the summer minimum ice extent in the Arctic has fallen from approximately 7.5 million km in 1979 to 3 million km in recent years [17]. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change predicts that by mid-century, the Arctic will be virtually free of ice [22]. Other models have the Arctic reaching the ice-free threshold (defined as fewer than 1 km² of ice as early as 2032). A 10-year period of consistently ice-free Summers is forecasted to begin as early as 2040; even the latest of estimates predicts consistently ice-free Septembers by at least 2064 [23]. In, the thickness of sea ice which exists has been steadily decreasing [24].

The effects of climate change, notably melting ice, are opening up the Arctic; at the same time, other effects are making this already extreme environment even more difficult to navigate. Sea ice presents one of increasingly challenging aspects of navigation in the Arctic. Drifting sea ice can cause last-minute changes in navigation routes and damage ocean vessels and maritime installations [25]. Due to climate change, sea ice drifts have been increasing over the last several decades by up to 17% per decade [25], [26], [27], compounding the threat.

It is ideal for icebreakers to proceed at as fast as possible [28]. This is because the faster the icebreaker goes, the more ice floes will be shattered, these smaller ice floes present less of a hazard to ships [28]. However, at the same time, the force of ice varies with the square of the speed, a faster icebreaker and a faster ice floe will result in an exponentially stronger force of impact, and thus a much higher risk of damage to the ship [28]. drifting ice floes are responsible for a large portion of shipping accidents and shipwrecks in the Arctic, with ice jets, a particular kind of rapid ice flow, found to be "the most dangerous phenomenon for marine transportation" on the Northern Sea Route (NSR) [29], [30], [31], [32]. Faster-moving ice sea can also transport pollutants or contaminants; as industry grows in the Arctic caused by ensuing oil spills will be made worse and harder to contain due to the increased speed of ice floes [16], [29].

### 2.1.2.2. Strong Wind and Waves

In addition to ice floes, a concordant increase in wind speed has been noticed, with winter winds in the Arctic Ocean estimated to be increasing by about 50% on average [29]. Like ice floes, rapid winds pose significant dangers to ships and marine installations. Wind velocity has a direct effect on the ability of a ship to adhere to its original or desired navigational course [33]. Higher wind velocity can increase the difficulty of aviation-related naval operations, which constitute a large element of USN operations. According to a report from the Navy Research Laboratory:

Atmospheric conditions affect naval operations in a wide variety of winds. From an aviation perspective, both fixed wing aircraft such as F/A-18s and rotorcraft as V-22s must navigate through powerful environmentally and self generated turbulent inflows as multiple airflow generated by different sources can lead to complicated and unpredictable conditions that further endanger already complicated taking off and landing operations. [34]

The most significant environmental factors that affect the safety of ships on the high sea are winds and waves [36], the increasing speed of these elements puts ships in the Arctic at additional risks. ### 2.1.2.3. Thawing Permafrost

Climate change in the Arctic region is causing problems affecting the land as well as the sea. Many of the infrastructure the Department of Defense (DoD) relies on becomes more difficult to build as permafrost melts [37]. Unfortunately, permafrost is expected to thaw 70% of the Arctic within the next years [37]. Thawing permafrost can lead to ground subsidence, instability, and erosion [38], [39]. Permafrost degradation is expected to increase in severity [40], [41], [42]. Some scholars, as Karjalen et al. (2019), note that "many recent-scale studies suggest that permafrost degradation will increase" [44]. Aalto et al. (2018) have identified that a large number of infrastructure are at high risk [48]. Given that the Arctic is already severely underdeveloped, thawing permafrost will likely spell disaster if something is not done quickly in order to bolster infrastructure.

### 2.1.2.4. Coastal Erosion

The effects of climate change -- thawing permafrost, waves, and rising sea levels due to melting ice -- are having a significant effect on the Arctic coastline, leading to substantial and accelerating erosion [49], [50], [51], [52]. The main geological processes eroding the Arctic are thermoabrasion, which brings the "combined mechanical and thermal effects of impacting wave energy at the shoreline" [50], and thermodegradation, the impact of solar insulation and heat transfer above the water line [53]. In combination, processes of thermoabrasion lead to coastal erosion rates in the Arctic reaching as high as 25 meters per year [52]. To put this into perspective, where sandy shorelines are eroding, they are doing so at rates of up to 0.5 meters per year and half of the world’s sandy shorelines are not even eroding [56]. Rates of coastal erosion in the American Arctic (which, with rates in the Canadian Arctic, is the highest in the Arctic) double since the 1950s and are accelerating [50]. In the Arctic as a whole, annual rates of coastal erosion are among the highest in the world [50].
