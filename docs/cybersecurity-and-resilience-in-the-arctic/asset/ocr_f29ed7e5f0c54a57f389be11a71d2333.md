# 1.3.1. Who's drilling?

The world's first oil field, Chibisukoye, was discovered in the Republic of Komi, in 1930. Development to retrieve this oil started in the same year, and two years later in 1932 the large Yarga oil field was discovered [18]. Drilling in the Arctic has hence been around for a less than a century, yet onshore areas in the region have already been explored, with some 40 billion barrels of oil (BBO), over 1,000 cubic feet (TCF) of natural gas, and 8 billion barrels of gas liquids have been developed (as of 2009), primarily in the Western Siberian Basin of Russia and on the North Slope of Alaska [19].

In the last decade, drilling in the Arctic has been slowing due to the shale-oil and gas revolution, which has largely reduced the immediate impact of Arctic fuels. Russian-owned "Gazprom" abandoned the Shtokman field in the Barents Sea in August 2012, one of the largest offshore Arctic projects, due to spiraling costs.

Drilling into the Arctic sea-bed is more expensive when compared to the same activity in lower latitudes; specialized equipment is necessary for operating in freezing temperatures, large supplies of parts are required to ensure greater reliability and furthermore require higher salaries [20]. Some economists estimate that oil would need to be selling for more than $70 per barrel for companies to begin even when Arctic drilling [21]. Private companies such as Shell have paused exploratory drilling in the Arctic, more notably Chukchi Sea, since suffering major setbacks in 2012. In September 2015, tests showed disappointing results and abandoned Arctic drilling and have not conducted operations in the Arctic [22]. Other companies continue to explore oil resources in the Arctic, including Chevron, ExxonMobil, Cairn Energy, BP, Statoil, Caelus Energy Alaska, Rosneft, Eni S.P.A., and ConocoPhillips Alaska [23][24][25].

The extraction of resources from the Arctic will be largely dependent on the economic landscape of the future, and whether the Arctic presents a favorable alternative will depend on energy prices from different sources. Canada, America and Denmark have no active exploration sites [27]. However, there is some activity in Norwegian and Russian waters. Russia is still actively drilling for oil, yet the impacts of economic sanctions imposed by the west restricted access to capital and offshore technology that more efficiently enabled these efforts. Sanctions combined with a declining price of oil globally has made Arctic drilling largely unprofitable, with several projects in the Russian Arctic expected to be delayed for the foreseeable future [28].

# 1.3.2. Demand, development and activity

Predicting future oil and gas production levels of the Arctic is likely to always be difficult. These predictions are based on a complex set of variables, each of which is often unpredictable themselves.

Firstly, the environment has proved to be extremely difficult to operate in. With the nature of the Arctic, changes in its environment can trigger feedback loops, impacting the rate at which they occur. For example, rising levels of CO2 enhance the greenhouse effect, this rise in temperature then thaws layers of permafrost which had previously trapped CO2 and Methane, releasing more into the atmosphere and further enhancing the greenhouse effect, in continuing the increase in temperatures [29].
