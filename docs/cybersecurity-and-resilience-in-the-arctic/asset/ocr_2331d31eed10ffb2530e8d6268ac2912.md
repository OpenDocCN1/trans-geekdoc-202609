This work explores how people act individually and as collectively to sense, understand, and in ways which contribute to resilience in the places they live; and it highlights key factors that influence effectiveness. Based upon these insights, we offer principles that could promote resilience-building action among stakeholders in their communities and enable them to contribute more richly to regional dynamics impacting Arctic regions and beyond.

Resilience generally is represented as the capacity to weather and absorb shocks and maintain or restore system functions and form (Chapin et al. 2004; Linkov & Trump 2019; Walker, Holling, Carpenter, & Kiznik, 2004). Holling (1996) distinguishes between “ecological” and “engineering” resilience. The former embodies the holistic or ecosystem view that considers resilient outcomes in broader terms, which defies presumption of a single stable or ideal state; for example, species survive through evolutionary response to changing environment. Engineering resilience, on the other hand, applies resilience principles to a particular system with pre-defined objectives—such as keeping the lights on.

Much of the emerging community and all entities describe resilience as an aspiration to restore the status quo. The National Infrastructure Protection Plan (US DHS, 2013) and a contemporary presidential policy directive on critical infrastructure security and resilience (The White House 2013), define resilience as “the ability to prepare for adapt to changing conditions and withstand and recover from disruptions. Resilience includes the ability to withstand and recover from deliberate attacks, accidents, or naturally occurring threats or incidents.” The plan describes extensive coordination among federal, state, local, and private entities to protect and restore important infrastructure capabilities—especially linear sectors of water, energy, communications, and transportation. Similarly, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, initiating a study on electric grid resilience (Ramlachtan, 2018), describes the goal fundamentally as: “(1) determine which grids we are going to protect and (2) identify the steps, if needed, to ensure those risks are addressed.” The underlying mindset is that conditions are unpredictable and well-defined; we just need to protect what we have.

Media reports and analyses indicate growth and consequences of disruptive events (Kenward & Raja, 2014). Weather is unpredictable, storms worsen, power outages increase, and conflict seemingly pervasive. Whether these observations are due to anthropogenic climate change, improved reporting, or political competition, the motivation to mitigate these situations is real. Government and relief organizations provide important top-down relief to save lives, limit property damage, and facilitate restoration of services. In 2017, the United States spent over $130B on domestic disaster relief (Leflekle). Top-down action also can address large-scale effects through regulation and agreements if they effectively address root issues, and given understanding and agreement on costs and benefits.
