Threats to permafrost infrastructure in the Arctic aren solely limited to those from permafrost thawing, however. One concerning aspect is the security of communities separated by vast across the Arctic that are heavily dependent on telecommunications infrastructure to connect with the rest of the world. Often isolated, the security and wellbeing of people in these communities greatly facilitated by communication technology, helping to secure social stability in these northernmost locations. Telecommunications in the Arctic operate over the global web of sub-sea cables. These cables can be cut or broken either or through intentional means. Britain's Military Chief, Sir Stuart Peach, previously stated that Russia could pose a major threat to NATO by attacking undersea cables for international trade and the Internet (BBC, 2017) and in the 2018 US Treasury Department accused Moscow of “tracking undersea communication cables” (Schreck, 2018), going far as to issue punitive measures on Russia. Damage to telecommunications infrastructure can occur naturally; earthquakes and other seismic events have been known to disrupt integrity, and under surveillance has also captured sea life damaging undersea cables, with sharks biting through, or rough sea vegetation and debris scraping causing damage. Accidental damage may also occur when dragging anchors or dredging in shallow waters (DHS AEP, 2017).

These challenges not entirely unique to the Arctic threat to have more isolating effects due to the sparse population density of the Arctic regions. While these systems should be hardened, it’s also necessary to create redundancies and resilience, so that if these telecommunications do fail there is a ‘bounce back’ and function is only briefly impacted.

For transient operations, changes in the Arctic furthermore constitute a scenario where terrorism can cause significant damage and extensive impacts across environmental, social and physical systems. Northern borders, becoming porous due to melting sea ice, are increasingly vulnerable if border infrastructure remains static. Meanwhile, economic activity in both land and sea is growing and expected to continue along this trend. Damage to industries and activities has the potential for abundant damage, whether it be by public opinion, economic, political or something else entirely (Loukheva, 2016). Russia has already taken steps to address these concerns, establishing a counter-terrorism center in Murmansk, in order to ‘organize use of force’ in Russian waters, its EEZ and on the continental shelf (Stalen).

Threats to transient operations over maritime regions don’t have to be from malicious human actors. The environment itself poses unpredictable threats, such as icebergs and glaciers moving throughout passes. In 2017, chunks of sea ice were flushed out of the Arctic into the Atlantic Ocean off the coast of Canada’s north-eastern shore (Kaufman, 2018). These types of threats are unpredictable and fast changing, and can significantly change from one day to the next.

Cyber threats are also a major concern for permanent and transient infrastructure and operations in northern latitudes. In recent years, cyber-attacks and cyber warfare have increased in frequency and damage. In 2017 ‘NotPetya’ caused huge service disruption, impacting industries from hospitals in Pennsylvania to Tasmania, construction companies, producers, Reuters oil extraction and Maersk’s distribution capabilities, with total damages estimated at more than $10 billion (Green, 2018).
