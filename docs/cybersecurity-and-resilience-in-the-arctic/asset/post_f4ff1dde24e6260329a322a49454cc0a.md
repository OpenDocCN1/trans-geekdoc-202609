For each element within the framework, objectives are defined (Anticipate for instance the objectives: Understand, prepare and prevent available, while Withstand has the actions: Understand, Continue and Constrain affiliated with it etc.).

Garcia et al. [23] present an architecture for a resilient condition assessment monitoring system. The approach aims to adaptively accommodate both cyber and physical anomalies to a monitored system using three layers: information, assessment, and sensor selection. The monitoring system assesses overall facility health within selected periods, and dynamically adapts and re-configures the system.

De Crespiny [16] tackles the challenge of how organisations can strike a balance between the risks and rewards, and effectively to counter the growing threats from cyberspace, without losing the potential benefits. The author outlines how an organisation has two competing forces: one that increases impact of cyber-resilience concerns (Known CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability), Non-CIA (unknown, unpredictable, undetected and unexpected malicious forces) that pull in one direction. The second, opposing force impact of cyber-resilience concerns (governance, situational awareness, resilience assessment, and security). The author proposes that a sweet spot can be identified and achieved through careful considerations of these opposing forces on an organisation.

Healey [32] discusses NATO's past, present and future, highlighting that that have emerged, ongoing and likely ones using case examples of how world cases affect NATO's position, including a DDoS and web defacement attack on SHAPE (1999), the large-scale DDoS attack in Estonia 2007, and incidents involving the hackivist group Anonymous and LuzSec. The article provides future recommendations, including mechanisms for deterrence, highlighting that the CERT-MM should be pushed by NATO, but also not NATO itself among other things. The primary article serves as a historical reflection and future views on NATO as an organisation.

Zobel & Khanna [58] attempt to characterise cyber resilience for attacks such as when a sequence of Dos attacks progressively an already weakened system. The technique considers timing and the amount of loss (availability) associated with each attack, as well as whether the loss in question is immediate or has a slow onset. An underlying model represents the relative impact of each attack and the corresponding length of time its effects persist within the system. The paper demonstrates the benefit of a Monte Carlo simulation to illustrate the method's capabilities in the context of disaster planning and mitigation.

The Arctic Council has investigated on socio-ecological resilience concerns [3,4], which has led to the Arctic Resilience Action Framework (ARAF). The ARAF presents a number of key guiding principles and priorities moving forward to make the Arctic more resilient. The work examines resilience concerns from a socio-ecological perspective. As mentioned, such aspects may also very well affect technical aspects of systems that we wish to protect. The guiding principles include [5]:

- 1. Building on the strengths of the Arctic Council and its subsidiaries as a regional mechanism for cooperation.
- 2. Valuing and damaging indigenous/traditional knowledge and local knowledge.
- 3. Building upon existing global, regional and national strategies for sustainable development, climate change adaptation and mitigation, emergency preparedness, and disaster risk reduction.
- 4. Supporting multi-stakeholder engagement.
- 5. Empowering local communities.
- 6. Addressing multiple risks and look for co-benefits.
- 7. Considering risk and resilience across temporal and spatial scales.
- 8. Encouraging innovative investments that prevent and proactively mitigate risk.
- 9. Monitoring progress and adjust strategies accordingly.

The ARAF also has the following key, high-level priorities:

- 1. Analysing and understanding risk and resilience in the Arctic.
- 2. Building resilience and adaptation capacity.
- 3. Implementing measures that build resilience through policy, planning and cooperation.
- 4. Encouraging investment to reduce risk and build resilience.

The framework breaks down each priority into sub-priorities [5]. In the interest of space, we will not review these in-depth here. Krotofil & Cardenas [37] provide an empirical analysis of the Tennessee Eastman process control challenge to gain insights into the behavior of a physical process confronted with fighter-cyber-risk attacks. The paper highlights countless process-aware cyber-risk assessments in discovering the weaknesses of process design in the presence of cyber-attacks. The authors highlight that (general) low availability of time and resources to perform such an analysis means that work needs to be addressed from a process-engineering standpoint.

Linkov et al. [41,42] provide a cyber-resilience matrix, which provides a means to consider how cyber systems can "plan and prepare for", "absorb", "recover from" and "adapt to" in four stages of the event management cycle. The authors highlight how metrics can provide the quantitative or qualitative means of recovering an attribute (e.g. system performance during disruption). Linkov et al. [40] also outline how benchmarks and thresholds for risk analysis are built into the regulations and policies of organisations and nations. They suggest early integration of resilience is needed to address the emerging issues associated with complexity and uncertainty of the world.

Eis et al. [18] assert that resilience metrics in the literature often fail due to existing concepts that reduce their use, including conflict of risk and resilience, and the necessity of reconciling engineering and ecological resilience definitions and objectives.

Bjork et al. [7] aims to define cyber resilience and compares the concept to cyber security. The authors consider five keys in their comparison between the two systems: objective, intention, approach, architecture and scope. They conclude that resilience is centered on business continuity management despite adverse cyber events.

Liu et al. [43] focus on resilience management, going from a "reactive" paradigm to a proactive one through the use of situational awareness of internal network factors and external elements of complex, dynamic and heterogeneous network environment. Their situational awareness covers three main network resilience issues: perception (metrics to measure resilience of network and external factors from environment), comprehension (pattern identification) and anticipation (threat analysis) of emerging issues before doing actions to support the network resilience (in particular remediation and recovery actions).

The Global Cyber Security Capacity Centre has a Cybersecurity Capacity Maturity Model (CMM) for Nations that aims to help nations increase their security capacity. While cybersecurity maturity is not equivalent to resilience, there is enough overlap to consider cybersecurity maturity, in particular, relates to resilience. The model considers cybersecurity maturity as being comprised of five dimensions (at five levels of maturity): 1) Cybersecurity policy and strategy, 2) Cyber culture and society, 3) Cyber security education, training and skills, 4) Legal and regulatory frameworks, and 5) Standards, organisations, and technologies. The CMM includes factors specially on cyber defence defence and infrastructure resilience, differentiating infrastructure technology under daily life and ensures that an organisation continues to function. Moreover, regular review of processes according to standards and guidelines needs to be conducted ensure infrastructure resilience. Regarding cyber defences, preparing a strategy to coordination between all organisations involved, is needed to ensure an integrated approach to confronting these threats to national security.

Hult & Svanberg [34] explore the feasibility of a paradigm shift in cyber resilience, from going information security capacity to confronting adversarial cyber security. This is done in the effort to communicate attributes of a cyber-resilient organisation. The article provides examples and to illustrate what good cyber resilience looks like today.

Vurgeon & Theoretical [56] describe a resilience assessment methodology that combines qualitative analysis techniques with performance-based metrics. The former enables identification of features that limit resilience (identification of weaknesses), while quantitative data can be used to confirm the effectiveness of mitigation options. a case study is presented to demonstrate how the approach could be applied to a hothetical Human Resources system and the ramifications of those cyber systems being affected. In the example, the system is disrupted due to human error by an employee. The framework is used to identify and evaluate options improving the resilience of the system.

## 2.1. Common Themes

The aforementioned literature provides insight into pervasive issues related to resilience. Some of the key conflicts and concerns in the literature we can identify:

- **Achieving resilience is exhaustive (comprehensive).** Resilience in systems is difficult to achieve using systematic approaches as they are intrinsically exhaustive. Existing approaches demand a significant amount of resources, to front the workload and are difficult to keep up-to-date, which can be a reason why resilience is often not prioritised in organisations, but also why very regulations, guidelines or administrative provisions exist.
- **Achieving resilience is often not practical.** Previous methods often remain tooretical/conceptual to use in practice or are static in nature that they only review resilience at a particular moment in time (akin to security audits).
