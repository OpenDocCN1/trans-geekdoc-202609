# J. Hall et al. / Framing the Arctic Part I: Actors, Communities, and the Environment

# 2.3. Social communities

In contrast to the perspective of the Arctic as a resource-rich economic frontier is the social science of the Arctic as a homeland for indigenous peoples. From the Inuit to Saami, and the Chukchi to Nenets, many different indigenous groups populate the terrestrial regions across the Arctic. These peoples have survived for thousands of years in some of the harshest conditions on the planet, adapting to the environment and exploiting resources using hunting and techniques, centred around seals, whales, moose, caribou, reindeer, salmon, pike and other Arctic animals. These conditions produce not only an economic subsistence but also the fundamental basis for social identity, cultural survival and spiritual life [8].

Colonization of the Arctic and interaction with outsiders, who initially aimed to exploit the Arctic region's resources, has been occurring since the 16th Century, however since the 1800s more frequent and sustained contact has been made. Economic philosophies and political ideologies have shaped and influenced northern communities, alongside epidemiological problems as communities were introduced to modern disease, including smallpox, measles, influenza and cholera. More recently indigenous communities are beginning to be subjugated to chronic diseases such as cardiovascular related illnesses and diabetes, as well as increases in substance abuse, violence and suicides [28]. The introduction of new stressors such as alcohol and cigarettes has had significant impacts on indigenous health and lifestyles including increases in rates of cancer [28], and violence, accidents, suicides and substance abuse are increasingly prevalent in indigenous populations [27]. More traditional values may clash with those linked to a modern lifestyle, younger generations who seek to reach an equilibrium by respecting cultural roots while engaging in practices and life in the 21st century. A diminished cultural inheritance and a widening generational gap are major sociological concerns [29] [30][31].

Currently, northern communities are among of the first to face the direct consequences of climate change, owing to their dependence on the environment and its natural resources [32]. Climate change impacts ecosystems, the availability of traditional food sources, hunting practices, the accuracy of weather predictions and the safety of traveling in transforming conditions, security and wellbeing for indigenous groups in the Arctic will be heavily influenced.

The rapid and disruptive changes being felt disproportionately by indigenous Arctic in governance and decision making, as evident with the increase in establishing many different indigenous organizations such as the Aleut International Association, Arctic Athabaskan Council, Gwich'in International Council, Inuit Circumpolar Council, Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North, and the Saami Council, all of which take an active role as permanent participants in the Arctic Council.
