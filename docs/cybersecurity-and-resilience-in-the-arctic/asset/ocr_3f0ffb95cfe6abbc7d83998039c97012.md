# 3.4. The European Union

In the context of the circumpolar North and Arctic issues, the European Union (EU) is only one among many external actors. However, in the context of the European Arctic, the EU is among the key regulators and policymakers. This is not only due to Finland and Sweden being members of the Union but also owing to the European Economic Area Agreement, which results in the majority of EU legislation – covering the free movement of goods, services, capital, and people – apply to Norway (apart from Svalbard and Iceland). It deserves to be taken into account because of the role it plays in the Arctic that the EU deserves and singled out in the discussion on Arctic governance.

The European Union’s policy towards the Arctic has been evolving since 2007. In the context of global interest in the Arctic, the EU institutions started to work on an overarching and cross-sectional approach towards the region. The Joint Communication on the EU’s Integrated Policy towards the Arctic from 2016 is the EU policy statement, the most coherent one. The document identified three priority areas: climate change and safeguarding the Arctic environment; sustainable development in and around the Arctic; and international cooperation on Arctic issues. The EU Arctic policy is composed of a diverse set of EU’s internal and external policies. This reflects the broad of EU presence in the Arctic.

In the overall Arctic context, the EU is particularly important as a funder of research and as an actor strongly Arctic monitoring, observation, and surveillance, through rigorous research funding and the EU’s satellite missions: Galileo and Copernicus (Steien 2016). The EU is active in numerous negotiations relevant for the Arctic, for instance the UNFFCC, POPs Convention, BBNJ, CBD, et. As a major fisheries actor – a policy area where the EU as a supranational entity has exclusive competence in relation to its member states – the EU is full party to the Central Arctic Ocean Fisheries.

In the European Arctic, the EU is a key player due to the regulation it creates. Hundreds of relevant regulations and directives, mentioning here are general standards for environmental impact assessments (EIA) and SEA directives, 2014/52/EU and 2001/42/EC), environmental rules for the conduct of various activities such as mining, the framework for the protection of natural habitats (Habitats and Birds directives, 92/43/EEC and 2009/147/EC), waste management, and the Water Framework Directive (2000/60/EC). The EU structural, interregional and cross-border programs are important for regional development planning, networking, and research and development activities, as well as for the strengthening of local and medium enterprises, in cooperation with Russian partners. The EU has a special relationship with Greenland based on agreements on fisheries and partnership. EU transport networks and support for digital connectivity have become part of the European Arctic policy considerations.
