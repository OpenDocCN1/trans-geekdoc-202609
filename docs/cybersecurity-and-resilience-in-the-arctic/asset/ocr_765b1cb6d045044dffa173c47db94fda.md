Concurrent with this increased reliance on software has an increased vulnerability to cyber-attacks.

This has been confirmed by the Office of Naval Research (ONR) which found that the “frenetic pace” at which both the sheer amount of software and the complexity of software have been growing and continue to grow… introduces the unfortunate side of leaving software complexity and bloat unchecked, which often leads to an increase in unexpected software execution failures and vulnerable attack surface for software exploitation” [78]. The ONR determined that “Virtually all software products are affected, from applications and libraries to low level system and even communications protocols” [78].

DiRenzo et al. (2015) and Garcia-Perez et al. (2017) have highlighted some of the specific technical vulnerabilities of ship information systems [79]. Along with GPS, already discussed above systems, such as Electronic Chart and Information Systems (ECDIS), Automatic Identification Systems (AIS), Very Small Aperture Terminals (VSAT), and Inmarsat, all crucial to the operation of modern-day ships, are vulnerable to exploitation by bad actors as described.

ECDIS provides navigation information through means of an electronic chart which integrates data from a number of sources, including GPS, speed, gyrocompass, and we [70]. ECDIS indicates a ship’s location in relation to charted objects, land, and unseen obstacles. Specifically, ECDIS updates are downloaded from an external source, whether through thumb-drives or the internet. This provides the opportunity for an adversary to access the ECDIS system and modify the maps, potentially resulting in inaccurate navigation information which in turn cause ship delays, shifts getting, and even collisions. NATO crew members become increasingly reliant on such digital mapping, they leave themselves vulnerable to cyber-attacks on ECDIS systems. In 2014 a security group found weaknesses to infiltrate the “ability to read, download, replace or delete any file on the machine hosting ECDIS” [79].

AIS are used for vessel positioning and tracking electronically connecting with other ships, bases, and satellites. Somali pirates have already used this data to gain information about the cargo loads of vessels and determines which one to take [80], [81]. Additional attacks might use AIS to misreport the position of ships or create false obstacles. Bad actors “could impersonate authorities and trick the crew into disabling their AIS, which would make the ship invisible to authorities and others (except attackers)” [79]. AIS systems are “easy… to penetrate” [79], [82].

VSAT are satellite communications which can help connect ships to the internet. In 2014, around 10,000 VSATS were found to have open ports – potentially providing a means for adversaries to access the local network.

Inmarsat provides ships with data services via mobile terminals that connect to ground stations through satellites. While hard to attack, Inmarsat and similar companies use gateways in countries that, if technologically-advanced and powerful, could intercept satellite communications. These gateways are in countries including Russia and China, both major players in cyber-attacks.

The U.S. Navy’s fleet is even more vulnerable to cyber-attacks than many other countries due to slow adoption of updated software. Up to 100,000 USN computers are still running Microsoft Windows XP, which was replaced by Windows Vista in 2007 [83].
