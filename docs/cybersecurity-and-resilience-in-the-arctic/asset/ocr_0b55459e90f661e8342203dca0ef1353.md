As described above, Kivalina's citizens recognized the need to relocate due to coastal erosion, but they had no means to do it. Individual inhabitants decided to use their artistic talents to cultivate conversation about the problems and to share ideas about important goals. Beginning with locally-posted art and social media, the conversation spread through state-wide networks and ultimately, collective groups of ethnographic artists from around the world, helped to inform a culturally-specific, locally-directed relocation process that embodied shared meaning and platforms, a nonprofit organization, Re-Locate, n.d.). Through artistic and web-based Re-Locate has been organized to curate what is becoming a global conversation about the particular challenges of Kivalina in the particular place of Alaska's Arctic coast. Telling of the Kivalina story through art visualizes the emotional and geographic challenges that the Inupiat people are facing, and has the potential to synthesize the knowledge gained in Alaska to inform resilience architecture for other needs around the globe. Notably, one of the priority projects benefiting from external funders is the renovation of a community center that will foster continued dialogue among villagers and those working with them.

Rural communities and villages may have the future opportunity to leverage resources of place, including geography, history, culture and talent, in essence, to let their stories through innovative contributions to the outer world. Rarely finely, the practice of “place branding” can provide useful to deliver both locally and to external society. Place branding relies upon local actors, collective learning and experience, and niche innovations to produce not distinct solutions to local challenges, but also rich cultural—sometimes immersive experiences—for outsiders (Garcia, Horlingas, Swemakers, Fernández, 2013). Citizens of Cagliari, Italy (described previously) manifested this phenomenon as they continue to develop their community as a culturally rich destination. An Arctic element is the community of Roovaniemi, Finland, where the citizens have helped Lapland reindeer traditions with natural beauty and a wealth of recreational opportunities to become a popular tourist destination that celebrates the ancient and the modern (Rovaniemi Tourist Information, n.d.).

# 6. Conclusions and Recommendations

Arctic cultures have rich histories of adaptation based upon generations of life shared in unique places. Their, natural and socio-economic environments are changing at unprecedented rates, driving swarms of new stakeholders in the form of immigrants, interested neighbors, and interlopers with political and economic ambitions. The complex nature of change and disruption to status quo present a fleeting window of opportunity to cultivate the strong bottom-up resilience culture that has been built over centuries of arctic life, rather than tending to centralized direction that has evolved in more heavily populated and technologically developed regions.
