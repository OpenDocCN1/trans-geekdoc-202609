Such a situation calls for exploration of creative solutions from the bottom up. Those who want to help from the top or outside could be most effective if they empower and reinforce organic growth, rather than simply pursuing un-vetted (albeit well-intentioned) objectives.

Bottom-up resilience has been studied in its context, but less often in relation to its contributions to the bigger picture; moreover, it is not prominent in models, policies, or initiatives that implement most enterprise-sponsored efforts. This chapter provides a global framework that relates principles of individual, collective, and place-based creativity and adaptation that can guide top-down action. Individuals ultimately are the agents of action. They are inspired by hope and meaning; informed by processes that temper individuality with creative feedback; and enabled through the particular nature and resources of their place. If communities are the crucible of adaptation, external support could be most valuable to the degree that it fosters dynamics.

The above cases illustrate how individuals and communities use creativity and dialogue to shape local initiatives, in each instance, the communities reject the expectation of external direction and the aspiration to emulate urban models. In the instance of Kvalina, Alaskan used art to inspire ideas and participation, and also as a channel to solicit external support. Notably, some of the earliest and most sustaining external support for the relocation effort has been through art-oriented entities. Community art and events are effective catalysts for creativity and reflection but the availability of venue and media often constrain their. External agencies—especially those focused on the power of art—can provide not only resources for community centers, online presence or art supplies, but can provide inspiration like minded people.

Moreover, the "Re-Locate Kvalina" identifies partners and collaborators, not just villagers. The village is partnered with a state agency (the Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation) and teaming among the Alaska Native Health Consortium to develop more flexible water and sanitary technologies. Such initiatives do not originate from bureaucrats at the federal level; rather, from scarcity talent (Tannenbaum, 1983) arises from need and creativity in the places of Alaska. This supports a top-down tactic of listening versus telling.

Financial trends also support this diversity of interactions. The Twentieth Century witnessed the rise of superpowers whose concentrated power built political borders. Borders became dominant forces in discretionary spending, whether related to science, arts, or wars; people come to rely upon government sponsorship for substantial advancements. This template is fading in the millennium, as financial power shifts from governments, whose budgets are already strained to meeting existing commitments, toward private entities.
