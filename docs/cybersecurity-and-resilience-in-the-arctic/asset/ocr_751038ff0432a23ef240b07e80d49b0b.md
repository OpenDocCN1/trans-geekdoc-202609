Global warming also threatens not only to decrease of sea ice but also increase the frequency and severity of extreme weather events. Kolstad and Bridgegarde [30] suggest that the increasing likelihood of polar storms in years to come worsen conditions for the oil industry. In contrast, an increase in the frequency of these storms could prove a serious risk, with the potential to disrupt drilling, production and transportation. An increased risk of oil spills could curtail developments, due to the implications of this on fragile Arctic eco-systems and environment. These concerns all promote an expected increase in the cost of oil and gas retrieval from Arctic stores.

The extreme conditions of the Arctic have meant costs of initial development are very high — several situations' governments have had to bear these costs, in order to provide business to benefit. In the case of Russia, where gas contribute to the federal budget and the sector is largely state-owned, this hasn't a large restriction on retrieving resources from their large oil and gas fields. In contrast, smaller Arctic may struggle to benefit from the resources that lie in their territories. Independence from the global economy and may prove to be unilaterally beneficial to Russia.

Worth noting, that technological developments are having positive impacts on the development in the Arctic. In Newfoundland and Alaska, platforms have been designed to withstand some of the hazards present in the Arctic, such as floating icebergs. Furthermore, in Snehvit (Norway), sub-sea installations have made for safer transportation. Future oil and gas prospects are relatively unknown, and a wide range of factors will influence the demand for these resources.

# 1.4. Infrastructure

The development of infrastructure in the Arctic, including roads, ports, railways, airports and telecommunications has been driven by resource development, although it should be noted that not infrastructure also has a role in providing and creating nautical charts, and electronic aids to navigation. Today, it is the socio-economic development of Arctic communities, combined with the needs of the state and the increasing feasibility of business activity in the Arctic that will the key drivers for investments in the Arctic. The recent growth in infrastructure development are no small or minor trend, with $300 billion in projects completed, in motion or proposed in Russia above [32]. It is also here that the Russian Federation emerges as the clear leader in Arctic infrastructure, followed by Finland, Norway, Canada and the United States. Each has proposed a significant infrastructure investment within their own Arctic territories.

As ice continues to retreat, the lack of infrastructure in the Arctic becomes more apparent. Limited nautical charts, aids to navigation, communication, emergency response, and rescue further challenge this potentially dangerous operations [33]. Furthermore, developments in physical infrastructure are also difficult to achieve; remoteness, weather, temperature and the seasonal changes of the Arctic pose challenges to construction and operational costs.
