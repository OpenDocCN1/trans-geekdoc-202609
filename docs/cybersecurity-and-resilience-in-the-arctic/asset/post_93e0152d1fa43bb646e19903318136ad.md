## 2.2.2. Cyber Threats to USN Arctic Infrastructure

There are many examples of infrastructural systems utilized by the USN and NATO in the Arctic which are vulnerable to cyber attacks, including submarine cables, energy grids [84], on-land communication networks, operations centers, medical support centers, and naval ports. Additionally, commercial and civilian infrastructure utilized by residents of the American Arctic, which includes native populations, is indeed at risk; the USN has committed to help protect this civilian infrastructure as well. The Navy’s Strategic Outlook for the Arctic includes as one of its strategic objectives “defend[ing] its sovereignty and the homeland from attack. A Primary Navy responsibility is to defend the homeland, its citizens, and critical infrastructure [2].

This section will concentrate on cyber threats to undersea cables as an example of the ways in which malicious actors can attack Arctic infrastructure. Submarine cables represent another potential vulnerability for the USN and NATO in the Arctic. The Navy uses cables for fleet underwater and training ranges, sensor systems, communications and monitoring systems, which require considerable infrastructural and technical support to monitor, utilize, and repair—making consideration of their management needs and a balancing of risk and benefit [85], such as power transmission and placement of acoustic sensors [75,000 km of different types of active seafloor cables [84]. Additionally, commercial fiber optic undersea cables provide internet access for the military and civilians alike. The Defense Information Systems Agency, “relies on commercial cables for 95 percent of strategic communications” [87].

Currently, there is a 1900 km fiber optic cable which snakes through the northern coast of Alaska, from Nome to the Prudhoe Bay in the east [88], with plans for extensions to Japan and across Beaufort Sea and northern Canada to the UK [89]. The USN and NATO become increasingly reliant on such cables to carry out operations and transport important data and information, as well as to access the internet, the consequences of a successful cyber attack on these cables are rendered more severe. While it is difficult to launch a successful cyber attack on fiber optic cables than other types of cables, cyber attacks are still possible, and the results could be devastating the extent of reliance on submarine cables [90]. Physical attacks on undersea cables are also possible, and in some ways easier to carry out, but they are not as difficult to scale up to [90]. Cyber attacks are likely to constitute a substantial portion -- if not the -- of attacks on submarine cables in the fleet. As Fitzlit (2019) “Cyberattacks are identified as the leading threat to the integrity of undersea fiber optic networks” [91].

There are two forms by which attacks on undersea cables can be taken: denial of service (DoS) attacks and espionage. In other words, attacks can either destroy or disrupt cables or can tap them. Bellovin and Gansner (2003) describe methods like dispatching routers through bugs such as CERT Advisory-CA-2002-03, and directing packets to the router to crash under high CPU loads [92]. Additionally, Bellovin and Gansner (2003) explain a method by which attackers can spy on undersea cables: the attacker destroys unsurveilled parts of the network, forcing traffic onto other cables that the attackers control and can easily monitor [92].

A 2017 report by the Office of the Director of National Intelligence confirms that cyber attackers can spy on undersea cables:

The technological access and motivation of a malicious actor to successfully "hack" or "sniff" a fiber network by tapping secretly into a fiber optic cable, under the water or at a landing station, should be recognized as a capability of cybercriminal behavior. Fiber optical networks may have link budgets of 14dB or more (equating to 96% signal loss), not all taps are immediately detected. A well designed fiber network can experience a wide variety of anomalies with no data loss or network warnings detected by network monitoring systems [93].

In other words, it is possible for bad actors to tap into undersea fiber optic cables and listen in valuable, classified communications without the managers of the cable system even there has been an infiltration.

The challenge of combating such vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure systems is massive -- in terms of scale, and the potential consequences of failure.

## 2.3. Geopolitical Threats and Disruptions

As climate change has massive impacts on the Arctic, reduced ice cover opening up new waterways and potentially making valuable resources feasible to extract, new and potential geopolitical challenges in the region are emerging. One of the major sources of conflict arises from competing claims over sovereignty.

The U.N. Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) assigns an State Economic zone to 200 nautical miles from its coastline. However, if a state can prove its continental shelf extends beyond 200 nm, it can lay claim to seabed and below-water resources. Once a state ratifies UNCLOS, it has ten years to submit a claim to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf (CLCS) [94]. Three states have claims under review in the Arctic region: Canada, Denmark, and Russia. All three are overlapping, mainly over the North Pole [95]. These three states have agreed to resolve the dispute diplomatically once CLCS makes its recommendations; however, CLCS is likely to make these determinations in cases for many years [96]. The geopolitical order could be quite different by that time.

Additional sovereignty disputes include those between Canada and the United States over the Beaufort Sea, between Canada and Denmark Hans Island, between Canada and the United States --, and more, the international order over the Northwest Passage, and between Russia and the international order over the Northern Sea Route (NSR) [95, 97, 98, 99, 100, 101, 102]. As ice melts and the Arctic opens up, disputes become more salient, with tensions likely to rise. A number of these disputes are between NATO nations, which could hinder cooperation.

Despite the potential for future conflict in the Arctic, the USN Strategic Outlook for the Arctic (2019) states, "prioritization of leadership and resources on core functions in regions of limited conflict will continue to oversee competing interests in capabilities such as the Arctic" [2]. Nevertheless, the distinct possibility remains that, as climate creates a “New Arctic,” the Arctic will become an area of competition—if not outright conflict—between the United States and its enemies. As former Danish PM and former Secretary General of NATO Anders Rasmussen has written, “Like the ice, which has dominated the Arctic for thousands of years, the regional peace of decades can no longer be granted” [129].

In accordance with Rasmussen, De Buitagro (2019) observes that “there is certain cooperation in a number of policy areas, and the solving of minor territorial disputes… there a slightly increasing tension and conflicts of interests” [128]. De Buitagro continues by noting that scholars see recent Arctic developments as having “inherent destabilizing potential,” including “geopolitical power games, expansionism, aggressive energy politics, the development of the NSR, and conflicts of interests” [128]. Scholars are now seeing “more chances for military incidents occurring, and a new level of militarization and aggression in the Arctic [128]. If the USN and NATO limit spending on the Arctic merely because it is at presently peaceful, there could be negative consequences in the future.

The DoD Arctic Strategy Report, published five months after the US Strategic Outlook for the Arctic, takes a slightly different approach than the US Strategic Outlook. It defines the Arctic as “uniquely relevant to the Navy’s ability to provide deterrent options,” with the Arctic region providing “additional maneuver room for distributed nuclear and conventional force operations” [16]. This is a more comprehensive, multi-domain viewpoint than that was in the US document; the DoD recognizes that operations in the Arctic cannot be viewed in a vacuum and rather form part of a global strategy the USN, in combination with the other military forces of the United States and NATO as a whole, will work to achieve its strategic by by cooperation and integration.
