Several researchers (Walker, Abel, Anderies, & Ryan, 2009; Folke et al. 2010; Carpenter et al. 2012) distinguish between specific resilience, relating to identified systems and predictable threats, versus general resilience, relating to a systemic approach—roughly parallel to contrasting prevention and treatment of species as opposed to cultivating health. Van der Merwe, Bigg, & Preiser (2019) suggest that specific resilience correlates to the goal of restoring pre-defined conditions, which generally may be achieved through deterministic (if complicated) approaches; general resilience, on the other hand, indicates adaptation and transformation, in calling for complex (holistic) methods. In terms of basic scientific method, analytical models are only useful when within bounds (specific); exceeding that space the needs for more open-ended inquiry (general).

Stepping beyond the goal of resilience, some (Adger, 2006; Berkes, Colding, & Folke, 2003) identify adaptive capacity as a key attribute for resilience. Others increased resilience (Amundsen, 2012), transformability (Folke et al., 2010), and antifragility (Taleb, 2012) as opportunities to capitalize upon response to change. Based on the assertion that change is inevitable and ongoing, Taleb’s explicitly designs concepts for which outcomes gain in the “upside” (when conditions change favorably) than they decline in the face of unfavorable change.
