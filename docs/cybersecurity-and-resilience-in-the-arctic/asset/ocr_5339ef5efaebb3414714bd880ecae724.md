The high practical importance of subsea cables in a world dependent on fast intercontinental communication raises the question of how subsea cables are governed under current international law. This not relates to the question of who may place subsea cables where, but given that the cables largely unprotected simply at the bottom of the sea, on the floor [1], also to the matter of responsibility – for intentional or unintentional – damages to subsea cables. This is hardly a new concern in international law [6]; as early as 1984 the protection of marine cables was the object of regulation in an international treaty [7].

While the Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea [8] contains specific rules on how subsea cables behave in different marine spaces (Article 112 [8]), as well as on questions of liability (Articles 113-115 [8]), this text is concerned with the question of security of this vital infrastructure and the ability of different actors to ensure protection against outside interference.

# 2 Interference with subsea cables

Incidents involving fishing vessels, in particular trawlers [9], show that the cables, are about the same diameter as a garden hose [1] and which run to a global total length of more than 1.2 million kilometers [1], are vulnerable to outside interference. Already in 2014, the Canadian Supreme Court had to decide on a case involving damage caused by the fishing vessel Realistic [10]. A fisherman operating the ship in question found that vessel' anchor had caught a cable but, based on his recollection of a handwritten note on a map he had seen a year before, erroneously thought that the cable was not in use [10]. The fisherman then cut the cable in order to free his anchor [10]. To contrary to his assumption that the cable was not in use, the cable in question was actually a fiber optic cable owned by some of the respondents in the appeal case decided by the Supreme Court of Canada [10]. Canada's Supreme Court had to rule on questions regarding Canada Marine Insurance Act [31] and the Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims (LLMC) [32], an international treaty which limits the liability for unintentional damages. The court had to deal with the question of whether the fisherman had caused damage (and thereby lost protection) when he intentionally cut a cable but did not have the intention to cause the large damage actually resulted from the cutting [10]. While subsea cables are located near the coast, or at sea in deeper water they are simply located on the top of the sea floor [1], which means that they are less protected against intentional or unintentional interference. Although fishing zones are to be avoided when laying cables [1] and despite the fact that locations are indicated on maritime maps, accidental damage to subsea cables is a distinct possibility. From the perspective of public shipping the international legal community has long been in the process of developing norms against protecting third parties against harmful effects of ship operations, ranging from major oil pollution incidents [11] to the damage of passengers of cruise vessels [12].
