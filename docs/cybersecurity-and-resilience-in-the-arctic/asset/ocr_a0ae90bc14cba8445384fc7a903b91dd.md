# ## 1. Introduction

A romanticized depiction of forbidding ice, Earth’s far northern latitudes have remained closed until past decades to all except indigenous peoples and a small collection of European and Russian explorers. Until the 20th Century, the geography of the Arctic was not fully mapped. National Geographic and other scientific authorities concluded the pervasive ice and difficult climate made the region capable of supporting the industrialized and complex infrastructure of Western culture.[1] However, in the past ten years, longstanding reality dramatically changed. Both civilian and military actors have competed to establish economic and political interests in the region.[2]

The Arctic is characterized by low precipitation, cold winds, and short summers. To respond to climate change, Arctic temperatures are warming twice as fast as the rest of the planet, with the past five years’ temperatures exceeding all previous records since 1900.[3] This rapid warming compounds other social, ecological, and infrastructural transformations manifesting in the region. Reduced ice sea and newly discovered energy deposits in the circumpolar north coupled with shipping routes, ushering in a new era of transportation and connectivity in the Arctic.[4] For example, an Alaska-based company has laid thousands of miles of fiber optic along Alaska’s coast to bring faster internet to its northern residents.[5]
