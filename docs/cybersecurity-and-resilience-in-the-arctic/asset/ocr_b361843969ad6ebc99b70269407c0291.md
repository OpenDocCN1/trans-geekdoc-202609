However, there are opportunities if the advantages of small societies are used, such as having the necessary formal structures in place, clear responsibilities and efficient lines of communication (and personal ties) between those who need to make decisions that be based on a common situational awareness. The part that can be played by informal social networks in small societies could, be seen during an international assessment of cyber security in an Arctic society in 2019. Representatives from the society were asked about their capability for recovering and acting upon confidential information relating to cyber security. What type of procedures and systems did they have in place for their relevant authorities to receive the information, having a joint consultation, and act upon the decisions? The answer was that these procedures were still being developed. However, it also added that the key representatives were actually working nearby and in the secure room where the information was received, have a meeting there, and act upon decisions whenever needed, neither confidential information ever have to leave the room nor it to be transmitted or held outside the room!

# 4. The effects of remoteness and a harsh environment on cyber security

The environmental conditions in the Arctic present additional challenges when it comes to cyber security. Traditionally, the Internet has built-in resilience that gives it a network of interconnected nodes and there can be no single point-of-failure in the network as one node or connection can replace another. In the Arctic, this is not necessarily the case: many communities are served by a single fiber-optic cable. If the cable breaks, high-quality broadband service is unavailable. Instead of having a resilient structure on a distributed net, the communication is on a chain that is not stronger than the weakest link. The often-host conditions complicate further, with the repair work often difficult, taking a long time, and being expensive. In December 2018 a submarine cable broke the east coast of Greenland; it took almost eight months to repair it due to conditions, including sea ice (Farice, 2019; Tele-Post), Substit for fiber connections can be found. Satellite-based communication from geostationary satellites is problematic in the Arctic since these satellites are above the equator and are just above the horizon in the Arctic.

In the Arctic there is another complicating factor, as the Arctic regions discussed are typically not independent states but part of a larger state with varying degrees of self-government (e.g. Greenland), sparsely populated areas and relatively isolated and often with a local culture shaped by the harsh environment. Large differences in conditions can mean that the general framework of the state does not fully consider the specific conditions of the Arctic regions. As goes some of the services developed by authorities or commercial vendors for the general population, which may not be well-suited for the Arctic population. Adaptation for Arctic societies may be too costly.
