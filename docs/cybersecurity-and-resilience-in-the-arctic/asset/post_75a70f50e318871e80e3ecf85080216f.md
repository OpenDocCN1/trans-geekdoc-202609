## 3.2.2. Underwater

Vessels operating underwater face distinct challenges and threats. Stevens et al. (2014) propose a neurophysiological method for evaluating the resilience of submarine navigation teams [167]. In their study, navigation team members were both connected to EEGs to monitor activity and observed by expert evaluators. By comparing these results, researchers could identify weaknesses within teams. For example, they found an “absence of stress sync during periods when the evaluators perceived a risky situation”; this could signal that the team underestimated the severity of the disruption [167]. This could be used to training team members to recognize potential situations even if they are unfamiliar to them. Trainers could use a “reverse correlation approach” to discover scenarios where team members are uncertain, but which the expert evaluators may not have noticed [167]. Stevens et al. (2014) “Rapid modeling of team sync… to provide specific and timely feedback on identified behaviors. This would also provide a pathway for using data and statistics to build better watch teams” [167]. In other words, this method for assessing the resilience of submarine navigation teams can be used to target the team's weakest points.

Stark et al. (2012) similarly focus on submarine navigation resilience [168]. Their research concerns on designing submarine decision support systems that foster the development of resilient courses of action. They find that systems that incorporate the operational insights of users, as well as represent trade-offs visually, best support resilience [168]. Such systems combine the benefits of both human insight and of automation [168].

Underwater vessels can also contribute to resilience in unique ways. Tancescu (2018), for example, advocate the implementation of autonomous underwater systems to increase the resilience of critical infrastructures at sea [169]. Kan et al. (2018) have developed a method for mitigating the impact of rotational misalignments between transmitters and receiver coils when Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) are being wirelessly charged [170]. Such misalignments “always occur” when the AUV is charging because the underwater conditions are particularly unstable [170]. The charging system proposed by Kan et al. could significantly increase the resilience of the AUV charging process.

Domingo (2011) and Toso et al. (2014) suggest ways to improve the resilience of underwater networks [171], [172]. Domingo outlines the main methods of attacks on Underwater Wireless Communication Networks, examining some proposed countermeasures and commenting on areas where further research is needed [171]. Toso et al. propose using Software Defined Networking (SDN), in which network control and intelligence is centralized, in order to enhance the resilience of Acoustic Networks (UANs) [172]. SDN can also accomplish resilience through efficient detection of an intrusion as well as rapid adaptation (Stage 2 of resilience, “maintaining functionality while repelling it”). Toso also examine the potential of SDN for three underwater scenarios which are often military in nature, “design[ing preliminary architecture solutions for UANs that cater for network performance improvements jointly with the need for security robustness” [172].

The above methods are just a few examples of the strategies the Navy can pursue to enhance the resilience of this system—both those deployed on the sea surface and those operating primarily underwater. The USN would greatly benefit from investing further research in these areas and incorporating some of the findings into its systems.

## 3.3. Enhancing Resilience Based on Potential and Predicted Disruptions

This chapter discussed some of the disruptions most likely to affect USN operations in the Arctic in the near future. The following sections examine some of the methods proposed in the literature to improve resilience in the face of these disruptions, challenges, and threats. It is important to keep in mind, however, that this is only addressing known or predictable disruptions. To be resilient, Navy vessels should be prepared to absorb, recover, and adapt to disruptions. The USN would benefit from investing further research in these areas and incorporating some of the findings into its systems.

### 3.3.1. Environmental Disruptions

The main environmental disruptions affecting coastal systems in the Arctic include climate change, melting ice, waves, and unpredictable currents. These factors can impact the resilience of infrastructure and navigation systems. The USN needs to research how these systems can be prepared to handle these challenges to maintain a strategic advantage.
