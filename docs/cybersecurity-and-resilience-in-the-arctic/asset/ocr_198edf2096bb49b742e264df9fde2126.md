The growth of smart grids evokes a complex transformation of electrical generation and electricity sharing therein is associated with modernization of flexible transmission and distribution systems. Their development is associated with modernization of the whole range of power generation, transition, and consumption by improved management, protection, and optimization of the technological elements of the electrical power system. The exploitation of renewable energy ever means fine-tuning, concurrence, and numerical regulation of energy production is necessary (Eid et al., 2016). Here, the following three main characteristics of the “electric world” can be distinguished:

+   - intelligence (smart systems, energy saving, targeted power supply) (Kabachal, 2016; Kok, 2013);
- systematic (integrated regulation technology, electric power storage technology, decentralization, direct Peer-to-Peer (P2P) energy trading, multidirectional trading within a local area)Stöcker, 2018; Merz, 2016; Zhang, 2017);
- greenish (renewable energy, alternative fuel for transport, carbon markets) (Mihaylov, 2016; Ela et al. 2016).

The concept of innovative transformation of the electric power industry provides for the construction of a fully integrated, self-regulating, and self-renewable system with network topology by all generating sources, local and separate networks, and all types of consumers of the electrical grid. For example, PowerMatch is a self-sufficient distribution system that includes both distributed generation and loads arising from appliances and physical devices and that integrated distributed energy in the operation of the electricity system meets the requirements of privacy protection, openness, and scalability (Kok, 2013). In general, there are lot of benefits with the digitization of the grid; for one, it is an opportunity to integrate renewable energy sources, making them more environmentally friendly than the traditional network. Even though the introduction of intelligence into the traditional energy supply promises significant benefits in terms of productivity and efficiency, it raises concerns about the protection of smart grids from threats of cybersecurity. Complexity with vulnerability is a serious concern. Cybersecurity is a real issue at the application, network, and physical layers of Smart Grid infrastructure (Mrabet et al., 2018).

This is further complicated by interoperability and proprietary nature of technologies, wireless interference, etc. One of the promising solutions in this area is to apply other sophisticated techniques to counter cyberattacks and support smart grid communication infrastructure. This new idea that applies Smart Grid security on Blockchain-based systems is currently under research by several researchers around the world (Aitzh and Svetinovic, 2016; Nguyen et al, 2018; Schwieters et al, 2018; Mengelkamp et al, 2017; Trump et al., 2018). Since 2008, there have been several attempts to build blockchain-green energy trading platforms and energy markets (EnergyBay, 2018; WePower). To this moment, legal institutions have not established regulations yet; however, the application of Blockchain in a Smart Grids does not seem obvious (Hawletschek et al.). However, cyber risks in Smart Grid infrastructure are the need to find a solution to one of the most pressing problems in the digital world.
