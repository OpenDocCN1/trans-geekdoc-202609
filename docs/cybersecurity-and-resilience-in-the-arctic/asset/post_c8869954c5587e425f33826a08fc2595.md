In agent-based models, actors (either individual nodes or agents controlling nodes) are given some control criteria and make decisions based upon the information and resources available to them. Agent-based modeling often utilizes some degree of randomness for actor decision making. This allows for modeling of complex systems where actors controlling different components may not have identical information or goals for modeling the potential impacts of non-deterministic decisions. Agent-based modeling also explicitly characterizes at least one component of the Command and Control network. However, it is difficult, as well often computationally expensive, to trace causality for agent-based models.

Most of the reviewed papers used optimization for decision control. About ten percent of the papers used an agent-based model. Another approximately ten percent used no Command and Control Representation. Only two papers used If-heuristics for control. Optimization tends to assume a strong centralizing force controlling all aspects of the organization, as it requires all decisions to be made according to the optimization algorithm. However, organizations do not always operate optimally. Using optimization for decision control may be appropriate where individual choices and incentivize significant portions of the supply chain, as is the case in a large number of organizations.

## 3.6 Disruptions Representation

Resilience system performance after disruption. Disruptions Representation determines the set of circumstances under which a supply chain's resilience will be maintained. One common method, used in 19 of the 52 reviewed papers, is that of case studies. A case study looks at historical or contemporary situation and uses it to model a set of conditions faced. This can be repeated for different cases and can be computationally intensive, but will ignore unprecedented disruption. Similar options include set lists, where a set of scenarios is imagined, and event trees, which describe potential combinations of factors. Sets were the most common method of generating disruptions, appearing in 27 of the reviewed papers. Monte Carlo simulation, where numerous parameters are randomized, can test a large combination of unexpected events, but may become computationally expensive. Monte Carlo Simulations were used in the review of seven studies. Additionally, modeling a rule for targeted disruptions may be appropriate when an adversary is expected to sabotage the supply chain. Targeted disruption, was extraordinarily rare, appearing in only one of the reviewed papers. This paper (Zhao et al. 2011) did not include a transport or decision model, however, and was based on distance between operation and support military units. This supply chain is not particularly useful for expansion to manufacturing processes. This leads to a limitation in understanding how supply chains may maintain resilience against adversarial actions, rather than natural, or benign intent scenarios. This presents an opportunity for further research into the effect of targeted disruptions on modeled supply chains.

## 4. Conclusion

Supply chain resilience attracts significant attention given the increased realization of inevitability of disruptions. Such interest includes considerable development in the Arctic, including telecommunications, shipping, defense, and a variety of other governmental, corporate, and social activities in the region (Milović et al., 2014; Trump et al., 2018). Given the increasing complexity of supply chains, as well as the significant harmful consequences that disruptions to such supply chains yield, resilience is a topic of growing interest to a variety of supply chain planners and policymakers worldwide.

The views on resilience is lacking and is especially important for engineering applications where a supply chain is an essential component of a larger system performance and not the end goal itself. This void in the literature is especially concerning the generic literature review shows the same themes. Approaches to supply chain resilience require that one understand how the multiple networks support supply chain operation. This study is one of the first to attempt a focused review of systems to resilience quantification.

Our review of 52 recent papers on supply chain resilience identifying methods used to model Supply Chain Networks, Transportation Networks, Decision Control (C2), and Disruptions Representation. The goal was to understand how current literature understands and the interactions of components when estimating resilience. The current body of supply chain resilience modeling literature is limited by a lack of explicit acknowledgement and understanding of systems effects.

None of the papers were from scholars from Low Human Development economies, an obvious gap needs to be rectified in the literature to display a full and comprehensive understanding of how resilience of supply chains can be affected by being based in a less-developed economy. The papers used different definitions of resilience, demonstrating that a standardized definition is still elusive. In particular, papers — even those that used the National Academy of Sciences ensured that “adapt” was adopted as a separate stage from “recover.” A third of reviewed papers assumed that all supply disruptions can occur within supply chain nodes and not between them, implicitly ignoring the possibility that transportation route segments could be shared by different links and jointly disrupted. Only 12% of reviewed papers explicitly acknowledge supply chain assets as being connected to each other via a separate and unified transportation networks.

This problem is compounded for command and control, and while only 10% of the papers use a method capable of fully simulating this network, while even fewer consider actor agency as a motivation in the choice. Over half imply that Command and Control would have no effect on supply chain performance and resilience. Investigation into the makeup of the C2 networks is not, however ever non-existent. A large field of literature characterizing these networks in a SC context. This literature, however, does not offer these characterizations into a model and were excluded from this review. Future research could attempt to leverage this field of research by transferring this into the modeling context. The potential for disruptions into supply chains is also a point of published literature. This is important given that organizations may face adversarial threats which aim to maximize this impact. These include civilian and military organizations, under threat of military, criminal or terrorist adversaries.

In short, there are both opportunities and a pressing need for future research in supply chain resilience 1) comes from developing economies 2) uses the standard four-stage definition of resilience 3) “adapt” as a stage of resilience separate from “recover” 4) acknowledges and analyzes the transportation network as inherent element of supply chains 5 explores and examines command and representations other optimization, which has limitations — especially in cases decisions are made by individual actors according to individual incentives and motivating factors 6 analyzes targeted attacks as a critical possibly affecting the resilience of supply chains. Supply chain performance and resilience are the product of the interactions of multiple networks, none of which failsafe or entirely controllable. A proper understanding of supply chain resilience will, therefore, require the ability to account for these effects. The field of supply chain literature must take steps to explicitly acknowledge and model this network inter-dependencies if the profession is to be able to accurately understand, measure, and improve supply chain resilience.
