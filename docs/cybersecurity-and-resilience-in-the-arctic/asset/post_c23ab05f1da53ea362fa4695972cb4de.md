## Domain 3: Health and well-being

Historically, Indigenous communities have been expected to adapt to Western norms of health and well-being. In the contemporary Far North, however, Indigenous research tells us they are redefining what means to be resilient and healthy. During our 2018 workshop, Elders from Huslia and Nulato in Interior Alaska told us that top concerns include individual's health, access to jobs, maintaining culture, and food. In this context, resilience is not accepting historical injustice, structural inequality, and accumulating stress. Instead, resilience is about agency and control, which emerge from social support, cultural engagement, and intergenerational exchange. It is also reliant on individual and community health and well-being [25][26]. Yet, in the Arctic, well-being and health are challenged by climate and non-climate stressors alike as poverty, land dispossession, and globalization. Indigenous researchers tell us adapting despite global change requires close relationships, dependence on the land, and livelihoods (also see [27]). One Indigenous researcher spoke of using traditional methods to adapt to hunting fish because he learned of the risks associated with frost: "We want to use less frost to be healthier, so we are using a mix of traditional and modern methods to improve our lives." Understanding stories of local community resilience in the complex interplay of well-being, culture, and rapid Arctic change can reveal pathways towards lasting health for communities [20].

Engaging with health and well-being conceptions require ethical guidelines besides Domain 1 and Tier 2. Activities that engage information around health and well-being in a tiered approach:

- (1) assessing how Indigenous and other local communities conceptualize their sources of strength and vulnerabilities to well-being,
- (2) describing how community strengths affect the ability of communities to cope with global change, including climate change,
- (3) assessing potential strategies to increase community well-being in a transforming Arctic, and
- (4) identifying the role of economics (subsistence and otherwise) in community resilience to change.

## Domain 4: Transformations of Far North ecosystems

If the North is to be secure, resilient, and desirable place for humans, it is imperative to manage ecosystem processes. During our 2018 workshops, Elders from Huslia and Nulato emphasized that the local ecosystem was rapidly transforming with thawing permafrost. They increasingly face treacherous navigation routes and changes in the nutritional content (e.g., fat) of moose and seals due to changes in vegetation. Both are tied to changes in permafrost stability and hydrology. Indeed, ecological processes provide continuity for human activities ranging from subsistence to infrastructure stability [28]. An example of this includes the need to account for environmental disruptions, like frozen landscapes interfering with hunting and fishing.

The study of nutrient and carbon flows (biogeochemistry) is one way to understand how changed elements alter social and infrastructural subsystems in the Far North. In particular, thawing permafrost is a fundamental change in Arctic geochemistry with uncertain repercussions for people, ecosystems, and the built environment. Permafrost, or ground frozen for two years or longer [29], conflicts Arctic and Sub-Arctic system features including stable ground for the built environment (i.e., infrastructure; USARC Permafrost Task Force, 2003), for carbon and other elements [30][31], and the topography underneath the movement of animals and people (Schur and Mack, 2018). Permafrost also modifies the composition of plant communities [32] that provide forage for migrating animals, including those essential to subsistence activities.

Yet predicting impacts of permafrost on the social, ecological, and built system features presents a unique challenge. Permafrost is a varied and complex substance that cannot be conceptualized or managed as a homogeneous entity. In the Western Canadian Arctic, thawing permafrost released mercury, creating threats to social and ecological health and well-being [33] and a recent metanalysis estimates that permafrost contains twice the mercury found in the atmosphere, ocean, and all soils so combined [34]. In human settlements, thawing permafrost undermines the infrastructural integrity of homes, community buildings, and community resources (e.g., wastewater treatment facilities) [35]. Plant, animal, and human pathogens may also be released from permafrost thaw [36]. Such unexpected highlight the major research challenge of predicting the impact of permafrost: community identified risk of permafrost is rarely accounted for in disciplinary models [37]. When community-identified risk is excluded from models, decision-makers could be left with partial information when they seek to manage permafrost transformations towards desirable social and ecological outcomes.

Frameworks for assessing change in the Arctic requires accepting change and transforming with it. In recent times, the debate around permafrost has shifted from "is the change happening" to "how do we manage the change?"
