Making use of digital opportunities is essential for many Arctic communities as well as the countries to which they belong. Growing up with some internet access can make all the difference whether young people feel there is a future in their home community or at least there. At the same time, opportunities must be balanced against the negative impact digital dependencies have in the Arctic, as the digital services cannot be assumed to be present. It must also be taken into consideration that the digital services may be available to the society but not to others (e.g. due to geographical, cultural, or economic reasons) and this can create or deepen a digital divide in a society. It is not only the communication supporting digital services in the Arctic to be fragile; the services themselves can be fragile. Delaunay (2017) gives the example of how Amazon changed the everyday life of some in the North-American Arctic by offering purchases (including food) from its site at a lower price than a local store could offer and to be shipped at no cost to the Arctic. However, in 2015 the free shipping service in Canada was limited to the capitals of the Arctic territories, with other communities having to pay shipping charges and in practice to go back to buying food at a much higher price from a local store. This demonstrated how vulnerable and dependent such a basic need of supply can be in an Arctic environment when the market has become to depend upon one supplier with limited backup once the services stop. With the so-called 4th Industrial Revolution, the Internet of Things (IoT), and the exponential growth of the number of devices connected to the Internet, the possible attack surface grows exponentially as well. The risks and vulnerabilities manifest themselves can be quite different in the Arctic compared to other regions, as has been mentioned previously. It is not only sufficient to minimize the risk of individuals in the system; an approach needs to be used that takes a holistic approach to the total risk, as is in the Systems-Technical Process Analysis (STPA) method (Leveson & Thomas, 2018).

Each Arctic society (and even community) must in its own end way of maximizing the possibilities the new Internet-based digital services can offer, but by increasing resilience minimizing risk and reducing digital dependencies to an acceptable level. Due to the differences between these communities there can be no general solution for all, but the communities can do benefit from increased exchange of experience and ideas. With more cooperation between the Arctic societies themselves and with non-Arctic societies as well, they will be in a better position to face a very challenging future in utilizing the new services.
