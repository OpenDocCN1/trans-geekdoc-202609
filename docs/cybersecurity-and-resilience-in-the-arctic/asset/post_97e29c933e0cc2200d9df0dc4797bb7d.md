Threats can relate to potential attacks by some malicious intent (threat actor), accidents (incidents that are not caused by malicious intent), natural forces (hazards outside human control) and finally, to wear and tear (maintenance needs that may result in an incident if not addressed at appropriate times). Common most research in resilience is that the resilient system needs to be a service-like, dynamic effort that needs to be aware of contexts, including dependency relationships and furthermore, a system that is not highly resilient is more vulnerable to threats. Harm is the detrimental effect on data and systems as a consequence of attack. Assessing and improving resilience is to limit potential harm during mission processes. This is especially the case for complex and diverse organisations where all assets may not be known, processes and vital knowledge, including workarounds, are undocumented. While there has been a significant amount of effort (related to resilience research) in the areas of standards documentation, business continuity and disaster management, many organisations are still ill-equipped and do not have the resources to combat and mitigate resilience concerns. This may be attributed to the fact that resilience research today has been investigated in many related and unrelated disciplines. Resilience is a multidisciplinary concept, irrespective of organisation discipline, any system will still be vulnerable to threats. A large body of existing research, from a business continuity and cybersecurity perspective, remains too theoretical to be used in practice. This is because resilience would consume too many resources to be adopted straightforward.

Improving resilience is a matter of identifying, knowing and managing systems with respect to known threats and the risks they pose. It is necessary to investigate properties and functional relationships of assets, controls, configurations, vulnerabilities, incidents, services, risks, external dependencies, training and situation awareness [10], in order to “plan and prepare for”, “absorb”, “recover from” and “adapt” resilience events [41,42]. Assessing threats to systems is challenging, and improving resilience becomes important moving forward to limit potential harm during mission operations and exercises. Conducting such assessments is rarely a straightforward task, especially for organisations that are active in the Arctic. ## 1.1. Chapter Contributions

In this chapter, we review works in the cyber-resilience literature, and outline a number of works that have demonstrated applications for situational awareness and decision-making support. Many of these are proof-of-concept tools and methods that can be used to improve resilience of systems. We outline how these might be applied in the arctic region, well as the challenges and possible pathways forward doing so. The purpose of this chapter is to provoke a discussion on analytics and decision-making challenges for resilience in the arctic, and how these might be addressed by current research in the academic space. The remaining sections are organised as: Section 2 presents an in-depth discussion on resilience, analytics and decision-making by summarising what methods and identifying overall challenges. Section 3 overviews how existing work might address day challenges, while Section 4 outlines possible pathways forward, with a more long-term view. Finally 5 concludes the paper.

## 2. Research Literature

We begin by reviewing key works in this resilience research space before examining how analytics can aid decision making in the combat against threats. Hai [28] discusses many of the early works on resilience, and outlines common properties of resilience to be: the ability of a system to absorb external stresses, to create foresight before adverse consequences occurs; adaptive responses to prevent, and mitigate adverse consequences. Mains continue to outline how resilience engineering is a paradigm for safety management that focuses on how help people cope with complexity under pressure to achieve success. This work assumes that a threat needs to be without intent, In this chapter, we will assume that natural forces and accidents are threats without intent, yet they have the ability to cause harm.

Gibson & Tarrant [25] present a discussion on uses of conceptual models to describe resilience, outlining the general principles of resilience shows how an organisation is able to cover over time during disruption, highlighting it it is not enough to be reactive in defence, but prepared and adaptive as the situation changes. The model is derived from common themes that emerge the comparison of resilience and is based upon six principles: resilience is an outcome, multi-dimensional, exists over a range of conditions, is not a static trait, not a single trait, and is founded on good risk management. It is proposed that changes in resilience capability, e.g. (context, effects and time) play an important role in becoming resilient. The authors discuss how an adaptive system has more capacity to handle threats in a proactive posture, rather than a reactive one. Caralli et al. [10] present the CERT Resilience Management Model (CERT-RMM): a process improvement model to help manage resilience in large environments where resilience is constantly evolving. It is to help manage the security and survivability of the assets to ensure success by considering ten domains of interest:

1) Asset Management
2) Controls Management
3) Configuration and Change Management
4) Vulnerability Management
5) Incident Management
6) Service Continuity Management
7) Risk Management
8) Dependencies Management
9) Training and Awareness
10) Situational Awareness

CERT-RMM defines several concepts that provide levels of abstraction throughout the model. One of the concepts in CERT-RMM that is improving operational resilience management has a significant, positive effect on resilience. Failure of one or more assets (due to disruptive events, realised risk etc.) has a cascading impact on the mission of related business processes, services, and the organisation can impede assurance of assured services and can translate into achieving organisational goals and objectives. A “Cyber Resilience Review” (CRR) can be performed to understand an organisation’s operational resilience and ability to manage risk by its critical services during normal operations and as times of operational stress and crisis. CRR is a questionnaire to help understand how organisations depl its assets (people, technology, and facilities) in support of specific operational missions (i.e. critical services)¹

Allen [1] suggests a set of ten strategic metrics for managing operational resilience derived from high-level objectives of the operational resilience management system defined in the CERT-RMM. These measures relate to computing percentages of resilience aspects such as activities, assets, services, controls among others. Allen et al [2] then outline cases in how the CERT-RMM has been applied and the analytical methodologies used to understand the effects of applying resilience to real-world organisations, using a comparative methodology, a predictive methodology with little data, a predictive methodology with much data and a probability example. The European Union Agency for Networks and Information Security (ENISA) has a program on Networks and Resilience and Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIIP)⁴. They have two works [19,20] towards a uniform assessment of resilience and measurement frameworks for public communications networks. Examples of the metrics [20] include: mean time to incident, mean time to reach, patch management coverage, vulnerability scan among others. They reported here a lack of a standard framework or practices for a durability measurement of resilience, and a lack of knowledge and awareness of resilience issues.

Goldman et al. [27] presents a series of proactive and reactive approaches to improving resilience and described an application scenario. The authors argue that organisations have to prepare “fighting through” cyber-attacks to ensure success in a degraded or contested environment. The paper has a technical focus, describing at a high level the actuated architectural and operational recommendations to address the advanced cyber threat and enable mission assurance for critical operations. The proactive approaches range from containment of emerging resilience to diverting attackers (among others). The reactive approaches range from dynamic reconfiguration to deception, to costly reconstruction and seeking alternative operations (among others). All techniques will not work to all systems: to begin building resilience in existing or emerging systems, designers analyse which strategies are most effective for the environments and missions.

Bodeau & Graffiti [8] discusses issues pertaining cyber resilience goals, objectives, practices, and costs. The work serves to motivate and characterise cyber resilience metrics. Their framework is intended to evolve the discipline of cyber resilience engineering measures.
