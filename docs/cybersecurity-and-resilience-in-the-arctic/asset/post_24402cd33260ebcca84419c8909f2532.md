
## 3. The Advantages of Resilience: How Resilience Can Help the USN Approach Arctic Threats, Disruptions, and Challenges

Resilience is a concept that has been widely utilized in a variety of disciplines, from psychology to ecology and socio-ecology to engineering. There are many different definitions of resilience, varying across discipline, era, and even individual researcher. Still, some key elements can be extracted from most definitions: resilience refers to the ability of a system to plan and prepare for, absorb, recover from, and adapt to disruptions [150], [9]. According to Linkov et al. (2013), these stages are expounded upon by the National Academy of Sciences follows:

- 1. Plan/Prepare: Lay the foundation to keep services available and assets during a disruptive event (malfunction or attack)
- 2. Absorb: Maintain core asset function and service availability while repelling or isolating the disruption
- 3. Recover: Restore all asset function and service availability to their pre-event functionality
- 4. Adapt: Using knowledge from the event, alter protocol, configuration of the system, personnel training, or other aspects to become more resilient [152].

Each of these stages will be referred to in greater detail in the following sections.

Resilience differs in ways from risk management, representing a “new way of thinking about safety” [151]. While both resilience and engineering seek to minimize disruptions, traditional risk management is focused on finding and rooting out vulnerabilities to known threats. Risk management is an element of resilience management, but it is not the only or best approach for addressing and minimizing disruptions. Risk management is weak when faced with unknown threats; risk can only be minimized when there is an awareness of potential threats.

Resilience, on the other hand, represents a strategy for minimizing disruptions which are not just known but also unknown—a large category when considering threats in the rapidly changing Arctic context. Resilience is “based on insight,” whereas resilience is grounded in proactive behavior [151]. Additionally, resilience “puts emphasis on speeding recovery and facilitating adaptation” as risk management [110]. This is because resilience-based thinking acknowledges that not only are there threats we do not know, but also many threats—both known and will penetrate into our systems to protect. Rather attempt the impossible (stop all intrusions), resilience management accepts that intrusions and disruptions will happen. Unlike risk management, resilience-based thinking gives us a way to cope with the aftermath of these disruptions: we can learn to recover more quickly from them and adapt to them. Risk management and resilience are separate and easily confused. But, as Linkov et al. (2013) describe,

> ...resilient systems should utilize generalizable concepts distinct from but complementary risk assessment to inform and support cooperation among diverse federal agencies responsible for critical infrastructures. [152].

This case is not just for critical infrastructures but additionally for other key U.S. Navy and NATO elements, from Systems of Systems (SoS), ships, and materiel to logistics, communication, and supply chains. The USN already utilizes risk management in order to maintain its competitive advantage. However, as the examples of potential threats and disruptions outlined in this chapter demonstrate, risk management alone will not be enough to counter a rising tide of new and unknown threats. If the USN incorporates resilience in its systems, infrastructure, and tools, it may help to retain control of the seas. Indeed, as mentioned in the introduction, the 2018 National Defense Strategy explicitly states the need for a more resilient Joint Force, which would course include a more resilient USN [5].

Given that we are considering resilient systems specifically for the military, it is possible to provide more details regarding what such systems might look like. There are several definitions in the literature regarding resilience in the military context. According to Alexeev et al. (2017), resilience requires that military networks:

- ...operate while under constant attack in cyberspace and the real world... automatically determine when a problem has occurred and reconfigure or adapt to restore services automatically or notify the human if manual intervention is required... [and] degrade gracefully to provide minimal services in the event of major failures. [153]

According to Engineered Resilient Systems (ERS) program of the DoD, resilient military systems are “dependable, easily modified to meet mission goals, and produce predictable lifecycle” [154]. In a presentation of ERS Scientific and Technical Priority Description and Roadmap, Neches (2011) goes into more detail, describing a resilient system as one which “Effective in wide range of situations, ready to adapt to others through reconfiguration or replacement, with graceful and detectable degradation of function” [155]. According to Geiger et al. (2014): The DoD perspective on resilience subsumes earlier definitions. However, the DoD is now in addition to these characteristics, DoD systems are expected to perform effectively in wide range of operations across multiple alternative futures despite disruptions. This ability or property is termed Broad Utility. In light of the foregoing, four key properties of a resilient system are: Repel/Resist/Absorb; Adapt; and Broad Utility. [156]

All definitions are roughly overlapping, although with gaps for particular stages. For example, ERS and Neches (2011) do not specifically mention absorb stage [154], [155], while Alexeev et al. (2017) fails to mention adaptation as important [153]. It is possible to map all the definitions to each other by rearranging the stages (see Table 1). By doing this, we can define the broadest or extensive definition of resilience for military systems:

Combining the definitions and descriptions of stages of resilience as given in the literature results in a broad-stage definition of resilience in the military context:

- 1. **Broad Utility**: Prepare to be able to effectively perform even during disruptions
- 2. **Absorb** the disruption, maintaining as much functionality as possible while repelling it
- 3. **Recover** to pre-disruption levels of functionality
- 4. Adapt based on knowledge gained from disruption
- 5. Degrade gracefully by maintaining diminished functionality as lifecycle comes to an end

For NATO and DoD systems—including Navy systems—to incorporate these principles and abilities, they would need to have broad utility, be able to absorb, recover from, and adapt to disruptions, whether known or unknown, and to be able to degrade gracefully. Systems designed to be resilient in these ways will strengthen NATO and the U.S. Navy, enabling them to better withstand and cope with unexpected disruptions, whether due to natural disasters or malfunctions, targeted attacks. Given that many disruptions—of which we can predict, and some of which cannot—will occur in the harsh Arctic environment, the importance of resilience is especially vital considering the Navy’s role and future in the Arctic.

But what about cost? Perhaps the biggest obstacle to the adoption of resiliency-based norms is the cost, especially the upfront costs, in the process of designing and building resilient systems [157]. However, in the long run, systems which are engineered to be resilient from the very beginning will cost less than those that are not. Given how essential resiliency is for safeguarding our national security, it may be best not to skip engineering resilient systems. Furthermore, gains have been made regarding how to streamline and improve the process of engineering resilient systems such that it is more cost-effective. Neches and Martini (2002), for instance, delineate ways to design resilient systems that are also affordable:

> ...advances in computational technology can potentially transform the system development in new and novel ways to enable fast, efficient, and inexpensive upfront engineering—the key to engineering resilient systems. These, in turn, can enable rapid development, deployment, and operation of affordable adaptable and effective systems. [157]

As Neches (2011), resilient systems are "affordable for fast engineering" and "less rework," as well as "Adaptable design & test for wider range of mission contexts" [155]. All these characteristics make resilient systems cost-effective. Adaptability is built into resilient systems, such that they do not frequently have to be reworked, re-designed, or refined. Resilient systems are explicitly designed to be able to adapt and change easily, and effectively. They are sustainable. Additionally, the adaptive nature of resilient systems makes them better equipped for a wider variety of tasks—that is, they have broad utility. Accordingly, a system-of-systems (such as the USN or NATO) which is composed of resilient systems will need to be on fewer highly specialized, inflexible, and high-cost systems or components.
