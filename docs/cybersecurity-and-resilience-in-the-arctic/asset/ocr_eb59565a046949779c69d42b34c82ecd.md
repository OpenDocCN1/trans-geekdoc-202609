Nevertheless, the distinct possibility remains that, as climate creates a “New Arctic,” the Arctic will become an area of competition—if not outright conflict—between the United States and its enemies. As former Danish PM and former Secretary General of NATO Anders Rasmussen has written, “Like the ice, which has dominated the Arctic for thousands of years, the regional peace of decades can no longer be granted” [129].

In accordance with Rasmussen, De Buitagro (2019) observes that “there is certain cooperation in a number of policy areas, and the solving of minor territorial disputes… there a slightly increasing tension and conflicts of interests” [128]. De Buitagro continues by noting that scholars see recent Arctic developments as having “inherent destabilizing potential,” including “geopolitical power games, expansionism, aggressive energy politics, the development of the NSR, and conflicts of interests” [128]. Scholars are now seeing “more chances for military incidents occurring, and a new level of militarization and aggression in the Arctic [128]. If the USN and NATO limit spending on the Arctic merely because it is at presently peaceful, there could be negative consequences in the future.

The DoD Arctic Strategy Report, published five months after the US Strategic Outlook for the Arctic, takes a slightly different approach than the US Strategic Outlook. It defines the Arctic as “uniquely relevant to the Navy’s ability to provide deterrent options,” with the Arctic region providing “additional maneuver room for distributed nuclear and conventional force operations” [16]. This is a more comprehensive, multi-domain viewpoint than that was in the US document; the DoD recognizes that operations in the Arctic cannot be viewed in a vacuum and rather form part of a global strategy in the USN, in combination with the other military forces of the United States and NATO as a whole, will work to achieve its strategic goals by close cooperation and integration across all domains.

The doctrine embraced by the U.S. Army in “U.S. Army in Multi-Domain Operations 2028” [74], while of course intended to guide the Army’s strategic outlook, also provides goals and benchmarks applicable to the Joint Forces as a whole and which can be useful for the Navy. According to the doctrine:

Future operations against a near-peer threat, however, will require the Joint Force to conduct continuous and rapid integration of multidomain capabilities to gain cross-domain overmatch at decisive spaces. Defensive spaces are locations in time and space (physical, virtual, and cognitive) where the full optimization of the employment of cross-domain capabilities generates a marked advantage over an enemy and greatly complicates the outcome of an operation. The enemy’s attempts to control and defend its center of gravity by providing the Joint Force with multiple options for attacking the enemy’s vulnerabilities at decisive spaces. [74]
