# 2.5 Arctic infrastructure

Whilst development, economic activity and interest is all increasing in the Arctic, existing infrastructure is limited, and governments are slowly beginning to realize the benefits of engaging with the High North. Several new ports, harbours, mines, oil and gas pipelines, roads, airports and guard facilities are opening to serve emerging Arctic regions (Sherwin and Bishop, 2019). Society in the Arctic will likely become highly dependent on this infrastructure, and because of network properties, damage in one location can disrupt a service in an extensive geographic area (National Research Council, 2010). Harsh conditions of the Arctic paired with a dependency on systems that could potentially large scale failures give rise to a scenario in which infrastructure resilience is necessary in order to mitigate the severe impacts that would otherwise come with infrastructure disruptions, failure or collapse.

The Arctic Resilience Report (2016) regards infrastructure as the basic facilities and services needed for a functioning society, including railways, airports, and digital networks, as well as the structures needed to supply people with energy, water, food and shelter. Typically, industrial development cannot take place without large investments into infrastructure - acting as a necessary precursor for facilitating the movement of resources to markets outside of the Arctic. As this is the case, it's likely that major developments will take place as operations in the Arctic become more economically and physically viable. The positive and negative consequences of these investments and developed infrastructure will largely depend on how developments are built and where they take place. An increase in infrastructure development will come with an increase to biodiversity, traditional practices such as herding, hunting and fishing, and public access to land and resources (CAFF, 2010).
