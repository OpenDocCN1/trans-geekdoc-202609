Last, oil spill response is a major concern for the Arctic. As politics, economics and climate change drive industrialisation and human activity in the north increases, the potential for a catastrophic oil spill is amplified. Whether it risks associated with shipping accidents, onshore pipeline leaks, well-blowouts, or cruise ships entering shallow waters, the impact of an oil spill in the Arctic could be devastating to local communities and ecosystems. As sea ice opens up waterways and access to materials and resources, extraction is expected to become more widespread. These concerns have forced Arctic states to negotiate and sign an Agreement on Cooperation on Marine and Oil Pollution Preparedness, prompting each nation to establish procedures in order to respond to oil spills in advance. Ice retreat may not be a limiting factor; in 2016, oil was shipped from Russian terminals during sea season longer than the open water season (Wilkinson et al., 2017). With the right infrastructure in place, operational challenges prompted by the Arctic could be manageable, but significant risks.

Variability in Arctic conditions is also likely to increase the difficulty in aptly responding to oil spill events. Knowledge of ecological, socio-economic and cultural resources that are likely to be damaged in the event of an oil spill will be key in determining appropriate strategies and clean-up methods. Recognizing these impending threats and issues surrounding an Arctic spill, the US Coast Guard and National Oceanic and Atmospheric Association (NOAA) have conducting scientific expeditions in which strategies for mitigating oil spills are tested, evaluating tools, processes and techniques (US Climate Resilience Toolkit, 2018).
