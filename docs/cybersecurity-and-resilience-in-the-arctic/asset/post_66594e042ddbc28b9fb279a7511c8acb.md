A combination of risk and resilience strategies are needed in policy and governance mechanisms to ensure that a “safe-to-fail” approach is possible in the long-run. Here, safe to fail means that, local failures can be revealed larger potential system weaknesses, opportunities to innovate and, ultimately, expand the resilience of the whole Arctic system. This is in contrast to traditional “hardening” that seeks to avoid and prevent all failures. This hardening often undermines system resilience by hardening a subset of highly managed feedbacks at the expense of whole-system reflectivity. The challenges concerning Arctic cybersecurity may have no straightforward solutions as they are completely addressed through a resilience framework. A robust and safe cyber system will require cooperation, universal standards, diverse perspectives, including Indigenous voices; and mutual trust among actors while simultaneously balancing geopolitical tensions and individual state and community interests [160]. Despite the difficulties, cybersecurity should be a policy priority as opportunities and threats emerge, amplify, and in a rapidly transforming circumpolar North.
