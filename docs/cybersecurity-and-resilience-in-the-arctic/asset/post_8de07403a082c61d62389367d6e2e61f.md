## 3. Summary

It's clear that the Arctic faces many challenges; driven by climate change, increasing accessibility in the region has drawn involvement from a variety of actors with a wide range of pursuits. Governments, indigenous populations and business interests each face an array of challenges for which risk assessment and management techniques are likely to prove insufficient in adequately protecting people, the environment and economic interests. While we cannot predict all threats, and cannot easily protect against all known threats, enhancing our capacity for resilience in our physical and social systems is our best method for preventing large-scale catastrophic failures.

## 4. Highlights of Analytical Approaches

### 4.1 Introduction

Used as a concept to qualify and quantify "things, forces, circumstances or hazards that endanger people or what they value" (National Research Council (NRC), 1996), various forms of risk assessment have been around for centuries, and each has its own hazards of special concern. Though risk is not an inherently quantifiable characteristic, methods of risk analysis have subsequently evolved to assume that risk can be estimated, measured and expressed in numerical terms. Using historical data, analysis can be conducted from statistical significance of the loss. This is the type of approach used in cases where data is widespread and readily available, such as the analysis of car accidents, car deaths, or the frequency and impacts of disasters. In cases where historical data is scarce and losses are not logged, the approach is to use risk analysis models, where potential risk and loss is predicted. Risk analysis attempts to measure the extent and significance of a loss, and the negatives associated with this a complex system. Moreover, analysis is typically conducted in three ways: quantitatively, qualitatively, or a mixture of the two. Each of these ways has different strengths, weaknesses and purposes.

The security of a system, by the application of methods used in an attempt to deter or prevent undesirable events from occurring, should be in proportion to its risks. There is a tradeoff between security and cost, and for decision makers to identify what controls are appropriate and cost-effective, this process is often a complicated subjective matter. Risk analysis is, therefore, a methodical process for establishing uncertainties and their impacts that could be encountered in a venture or a system. It aims to establish an objective measure for threats, vulnerabilities and consequences, establishing a foundationally empirical basis for which to make decisions upon (Merad & Trump 2020).

### 4.2 Classical Risk Assessment Constructs

Risk assessment tools and strategies are used to identify vulnerabilities and weaknesses in a system and identify potential harm that could result from exposure to a hazard or other initiating event. Throughout different stages in a system's life cycle, strategies can be used to identify the potential events.

### 4.3 Probabilistic Risk Assessment

When analysing complex systems, one useful technique is probabilistic risk assessment (PRA). In its essence, PRA aims to answer three key questions (U.S. NRC, 2019):
- (a) What can go wrong within the system?
- (b) How likely is it to occur?
- (c) If it does happen, what are the consequences?

At a fundamental level, every risk assessment requires deep knowledge. Without this information, performing an analysis will prove ineffective (Linkov et al., 2016). For PRA, the procedure for quantifying risk can be outlined in 8 steps:
1. Identification of ends of interest
2. System familiarization and data collection
3. Identification, selection, and screening of catalyst events
4. Definition and modelling of scenarios linking catalyst event to ends state
5. Modelling of pivotal events
6. Risk quantification for each event and scenario - risk aggregation for all ends states
7. Full uncertainty - sensitivity analysis as needed
8. Risk assessment ranking (Ostrom & Wilhelmsen, 2012)

In order to safeguard people and environments from consequences, identifying the potential hazards and the causes within the system is necessary. However, hazard analysis can quantify threat, not the likelihood or uncertainty of them. PRA works to first see what can happen, how likely it is to happen, and then if it does happen what the consequences are. For some applications this technique works well. In Aerospace and the nuclear sectors, analysis of this kind is extremely useful. It allows for the quantification of well-known risks and provides evaluations that can inform decision making and risk management.

However, in the case of the Arctic, where the conduct of a malicious actor or human error is indirect, analysing and quantifying such risks may prove to be increasingly challenging. PRA works as a useful decision-making tool, focusing on components that require hardening; however, for events where there is high uncertainty and a lack of historical data to reference, using this method to analyse risks is insufficient to comprehensively protect a system. Moreover, numbers taken from results of analysis using this tool can be taken as objective fact, rather than what they are: subjective evaluations of hazard level and probability (Wiggins, 1985). Ignorance of this could lead to dangerous negligence in Arctic operations where the risks are fundamentally fast-changing and unpredictable.
