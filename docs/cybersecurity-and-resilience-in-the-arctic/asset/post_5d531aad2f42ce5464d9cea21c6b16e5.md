**Finland:** The electricity sector in Finland relies on nuclear energy, the energy industry, hydro-power, and food consumption; some electricity is imported from neighboring countries. Finland is one of the first countries where all electricity users have electric meters that can be tracked, with the data used to measure the efficiency of the electric grid.

Finland does not have a policy regarding the smart grid but is pushing some initiatives both in research and development. One example is CLEAN (Cluster for Energy and Environment), which manages the SGEM (Smart Grids and Energy Markets) program and involves a range of smart grid stakeholders (The Nordic Electricity Sector, 2014)

**Greenland/Denmark:** In fact, as in most Nordic countries, energy in Greenland was generated by diesel-driven power plants. Now the main source of energy in Denmark is thermoelectric while wind energy generates 20% of the country's total energy supply (Nordregio, 2010). In 2017, the wind turbines in Denmark delivered power equivalent to 43.6% of all electricity consumption in the country (Bergreen, 2018). Greenland also has been replacing its diesel power plants with hydropower. Since 1993, Greenland has been partly self-supplying energy from hydropower. There are now solar and wind energy that were rejected. Recent experiments in Greenland and studies from Alaska show that wind turbines can work effectively in Arctic conditions. Each town in Greenland generates its own energy and distributes it via a micro-grid and local district heating network.

**Iceland:** Iceland actually meets all its electricity needs from environmentally friendly renewables. This is the first country with 99.99% of its electricity needs with renewable energy. Virtually all of them come from hydropower (71.03%) and geothermal (28.91%). Wind power generates 0.04% of the electricity. Fossil fuels combs a distant fourth, with only 0.01% of the energy production. According to a previous Nordic electricity sector report (The Nordic Electricity Sector, 2014) Iceland does not have an explicit policy on implementing smart grids. Landsnet, responsible for the transmission and power system on Iceland, mentions smart grids as the next generation transmission that it intends to be part of its strategy.

**Norway:** According to 2011 data from the Norwegian government agency on Water Resources and Energy, over 95% of the country's electricity is produced by hydropower. Approximately 97.8% of electricity needs of Norway are covered by renewables (Iceland magazine, 2019).

There is a smart grid (Smartgrid) in Norway that coordinates activities linked to smart grids and smart meters. There are ongoing discussions between stakeholders from the research, energy, and government sectors on establishing a smart grid policy in Norway (The Nordic Electricity Sector, 2014).

**Russia:** Russia is one of the top producers and consumers of electric power in the world. It produces more than 240 GW of installed generation capacity. In 2016, Russia generated 1071 billion kilowatt-hours and consumed about 900 billion kilowatt-hours.

About two-thirds of Russia's total electric generations are fossil fuels (oil, natural gas, and coal) and about one-third is hydropower and nuclear energy (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2017). Russia consistently considers the Arctic as a strategic resource base and does not consider this as a place for implementing environmental policies andor smart grids for local communities.

**Sweden:** Another country that generates and consumes much of its electricity with renewable sources. Sweden is actively involved in the development and growth of smart grids. The Swedish Coordination Council for Smart Grid has a number of stakeholders working together to coordinate and harmonize research and as well as projects linked to smart grids (The Nordic Electricity Sector, 2014).

**USA:** According to Hofmann (2015), Alaska is a microgrid frontier in Arctic region. Wind farms are controlled by SCADA (supervisor controller and data acquisition system) and at the moment, up to 50% of Nome is produced by wind power. The crucial component is that energy must be consumed where it is produced. The Power Purchase Agreements with private developers and in future, may be possible to turn off the diesel generators entirely when adequate regenerated power is available. The innovation in renewable energy systems has led Alaska to a leader in the development and operation of microgrids integrating renewable energy. Today, Alaska has 12% of the world's microgrids powered by renewable energy systems. Over 100 small businesses, as well as the Alaska Center for Energy and Power, have gained expertise in this sector and are now venturing out to test global markets for renewably-powered microgrids (Hofmann, 2015).

## Background

As is evident from the above, smart grids have become a vector of many countries. The main objectives in the implementation of smart grids are energy security, economic growth, and environmental sustainability. It is characterized by way communications between service providers and customers, enabling customers to more effectively manage power consumption. A smart grid is an advanced system for generating, transmitting, and consuming electricity that consolidates traditional power plants with virtual power plants. VPP contains three types of:

- distributed generators (solar panels, wind turbines, photovoltaic stations, minis-CHP, etc.)
- consumers load
- energy storage systems.

Household consumers (washing machines, refrigerators, televisions, microwave ovens, air conditioning systems, etc.) are the most easily controlled. The management of a load largely depends on the flexibility of industrial users.

Typically, virtual power plants are connected to a medium- or low-voltage network. Elements of a virtual power plant can be located at considerable distances from each other. Networks (electrical and communication) are combined under the term 'microgrid'.

A feature of the microgrid is that it can operate. Virtual power management is carried out remotely through the energy management system (EMS), which receives information about the state of each power plant and sends control signals to them. The EMS uses a global satellite navigation system (GPS) that synchronizes measurements of the current value and/or voltage across all plants of the virtual power plant. Devices for such measurements are Phasor Measurement Units (PMU). A virtual power plant may have a commercial purpose, technical (system) services such as frequency and active power regulation, power quality maintenance, etc.) or combine both of these functions. The functional capacities of virtual power plants can be attributed as follows:

- control of dispersed generation (optimization of network nodes)
- management of electricity consumption (comparing the load schedules of consumers and power sources)
- market management of the reserve capacity (the possibility of using reserve power).

The effect of the introduction of microgrids will manifest itself for the consumer in increasing electricity supply, reliability, and energy efficiency, and through participation in the consumption management program, there is a chance to receive revenue from the sale of surplus capacity to the network.
