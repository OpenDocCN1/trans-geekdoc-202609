## 2.4. Indigenous Peoples

There is no formal and internationally acknowledged definition of “Indigenous Peoples.” The reference to Indigenous Peoples assumes that these groups of people are the original population of a territory which they retain a non-dominant position, yet they maintain historical continuity of their existence as separate groups than that of the dominant segment who rule (see Figure 14). While the term Indigenous Peoples is generally used to identify the groups or populations pre-existed in the territories, there are different terminologies used in different regions and countries, such as native, Indians, Aboriginal, first nations, etc. Some people living in Asian countries, are reluctant to describe themselves as “indigenous.” In such countries, they are identified as ethnic minorities or “tribal” populations. The Russian legislation defines Indigenous people based on their population size. Groups with less than 50,000 people are defined as “indigenous numerically-small peoples” whereas non-Russian people with a population size of over 50,000 are denied indigenous status. Whichever term is used to identify them, these peoples can form one or several groups who exist as traditional groups despite the fact that they have been marginalized, and in many cases, forced to assimilate with the dominant segment of the population, often through cultural or violent genocide. Another description of Indigenous Peoples is given by Jose R. Martinez, in his Study on the Problem of Discrimination against Indigenous Peoples[64]:

Indigenous communities, peoples and nations who have a historical continuity with pre-invasion and pre-colonial societies that developed on their territories, consider themselves distinct from other sectors of the societies now prevailing in those territories, or parts of them. They form non-dominant segments of society and are determined to preserve, develop and transmit to future generations their ancestral territories, and their ethnic identity, as the basis of their continued existence as peoples, in accordance with their own cultural patterns, social institutions and legal systems [64].
