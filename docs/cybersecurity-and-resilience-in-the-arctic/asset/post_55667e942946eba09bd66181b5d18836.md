Warf (2011) divided the Arctic into three regions with respect to Internet usage: (a) Northern America (Canada and Alaska), (b) Russia and the Nordics* (the Nordic countries: Norway, Finland, Sweden, Iceland and Greenland as part of the Danish kingdom). The Nordic countries began to use the Internet at an early stage and have held leading positions in international assessments of the ITU. In the ITU's 2017 global ranking of the ICT Development Index, Iceland was in the 1st place and most of Arctic countries were in the top 30 places (ITU 2018). In the part of Russia and Northern America there has a rapid growth in Internet connectivity with emphasis on fiber optic broadband solutions (Delaunay 2014) and almost 90% of the population in Greenland has access to faster Internet connections. The spread of broadband solutions in the Arctic has enabled many societies to experience the Internet in a completely different way from was previously possible with low communication solutions.

Although the Internet offers many possibilities for Arctic societies, there are certain challenges which must also be considered. Despite many differences, countless Arctic societies share three factors: they (1) relatively small (if not in number them at least compared to the Non-Arctic part of the country), (2) remote and (3) situated in a harsh environment. How of smallness, remoteness and a harsh environment affect cyber security in Arctic communities and societies is discussed in the next two sections.

## 3. Effects of smallness of societies on cyber security

In recent decades the concept of "small states" has come into widespread use to explain certain differences these societies seem to share when compared to larger societies. The definition of a "small state" can differ between studies and depends upon the research topic. Furthermore, the characteristics of these small states can vary widely and differ in what the societies choose to focus on. There are examples of small states reaching far in some fields, being considered world leaders in specific fields. Areng (2014) explores this further in the Lilliputian States and Cyber Security where Estonia is taken as the main example¹. Emphasis on digital solutions in Estonia as since 1995 with the so-called Tiger-Leap program. Estonia is a small society (population 1.3 million) but it is recognized as a world leader in fields of cyber security; in 2019 it published its third cyber strategy. The document mentions the limited possibilities for specialization in a small population and the risk of fragmentation expertise. This is been hit by two cyber crises: a major attack on society in 2007 and a vulnerability discovered in 2017 of the ID card used for accessing digital services in the country.
