Infrastructure destabilization is likely to be one of result of thaw, and buildings, roads, airports, pipelines, and industrial facilities will require greater maintenance and investment. Nearly 4 million people and 70% of current infrastructure in the permafrost domain are with high potential for thaw, with one of pan-Arctic infrastructure and 45% of the hydrocarbon extraction fields in the Russian Arctic where permafrost melting causes instability with the potential for severe building damage (Hjort et al., 2018). Future development will likely require new design elements to account for ongoing warming that will add to construction and maintenance costs. These attributes of the Arctic environment hence to undermine sustainability, security and economic benefit. It is this uncertainty and lack of knowledge that makes resilience of paramount when discussing the Arctic – recovery must be prioritized as threats cannot always be predicted.

Just taking into the distinctive environment, we can identify the need for resilience strategies; yet just this is common in the uniqueness of the region. Unpredictability and ability to foster sustainability, security, economic benefit and life-threatening scenarios will rely on strategies of resilience, resilience by design is necessary when contemplating any activity in the Arctic.

## 2.5 Arctic infrastructure

Whilst development, economic activity and interest is all increasing in the Arctic, existing infrastructure is limited, and governments are slowly beginning to realize the benefits of engaging with the High North. Several new ports, harbours, mines, oil and gas pipelines, roads, airports and guard facilities are opening to serve emerging Arctic regions (Sherwin and Bishop, 2019). Society in the Arctic will likely become highly dependent on this infrastructure, and because of network properties, damage in one location can disrupt a service in an extensive geographic area (National Research Council, 2010). Harsh conditions of the Arctic paired with a dependency on systems that could potentially large scale failures give rise to a scenario in which infrastructure resilience is necessary in order to mitigate the severe impacts that would otherwise come with infrastructure disruptions, failure or collapse.

The Arctic Resilience Report (2016) regards infrastructure as the basic facilities and services needed for a functioning society, including railways, airports, and digital networks, as well as the structures needed to supply people with energy, water, food and shelter. Typically, industrial development cannot take place without large investments into infrastructure - acting as a necessary precursor for facilitating the movement of resources to markets outside of the Arctic. As this is the case, it's likely that major developments will take place as operations in the Arctic become more economically and physically viable. The positive and negative consequences of these investments and developed infrastructure will largely depend on how developments are built and where they take place. An increase in infrastructure development will come with an increase to biodiversity, traditional practices such as herding, hunting and fishing, and public access to land and resources (CAFF, 2010).

Currently, permanent settlements are growing, with facilities including air and naval bases, research stations, civilian weather stations, telecommunication infrastructure, expanding ports, industrial mining operations, search and rescue bases, and seed vaults opening up (Trump et al., 2018). The impacts of these permanent installations are also risk in both environmental and geopolitical significance, and are cause for concern and further investigation. One of the most predominant to permanent infrastructure, both civilian and military, is thawing permafrost. Hjort et al. (2018) warn that one-third of Pan-Arctic infrastructure and 45% of hydrocarbon extraction in the Russian Arctic are in regions where thaw-related ground instability can cause damage to the built environment. Infrastructure such as railways and pipelines are also risk of damage. 190 km of the Eastern Siberia-Pacific (ESPO) oil pipeline, 550 km of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS) and 1260 km of major gas pipelines beginning in the YamalNets region are in regions where near-surface permafrost is expected to occur by 2050. Another 470 km of the Qinghai-Tibet Railway, and 280 km of the Obskaya-Bovankovovo railway (the world's most northern railway) traverse areas of permafrost, expected thaw in coming years. Other infrastructure including more than 1200 settlements with a population over 5000 are in regions where permafrost thaw likely.

Threats to permafrost infrastructure in the Arctic are solely limited to those from permafrost thawing, however. One concerning aspect is the security of communities separated by vast across the Arctic that are heavily dependent on telecommunications infrastructure to connect with the rest of the world. Often isolated, the security and wellbeing of people in these communities greatly facilitated by communication technology, helping to secure social stability in these northernmost locations. Telecommunications in the Arctic operate over the global web of sub-sea cables. These cables can be cut or broken either or through intentional means. Britain's Military Chief, Sir Stuart Peach, previously stated that Russia could pose a major threat to NATO by attacking undersea cables for international trade and the Internet (BBC, 2017) and in the 2018 US Treasury Department accused Moscow of “tracking undersea communication cables” (Schreck, 2018), going far as to issue punitive measures on Russia. Damage to telecommunications infrastructure can occur naturally; earthquakes and other seismic events have been known to disrupt integrity, and under surveillance has also captured sea life damaging undersea cables, with sharks biting through, or rough sea vegetation and debris scraping causing damage. Accidental damage may also occur when dragging anchors or dredging in shallow waters (DHS AEP, 2017).

These challenges not entirely unique to the Arctic threat to have more isolating effects due to the sparse population density of the Arctic regions. While these systems should be hardened, it’s also necessary to create redundancies and resilience, so that if these telecommunications do fail there is a ‘bounce back’ and function is only briefly impacted.

For transient operations, changes in the Arctic furthermore constitute a scenario where terrorism can cause significant damage and extensive impacts across environmental, social and physical systems. Northern borders, becoming porous due to melting sea ice, are increasingly vulnerable if border infrastructure remains static. Meanwhile, economic activity in both land and sea is growing and expected to continue along this trend. Damage to industries and activities has the potential for abundant damage, whether it be by public opinion, economic, political or something else entirely (Loukheva, 2016). Russia has already taken steps to address these concerns, establishing a counter-terrorism center in Murmansk, in order to ‘organize use of force’ in Russian waters, its EEZ and on the continental shelf (Stalen).

Threats to transient operations over maritime regions don’t have to be from malicious human actors. The environment itself poses unpredictable threats, such as icebergs and glaciers moving throughout passes. In 2017, chunks of sea ice were flushed out of the Arctic into the Atlantic Ocean off the coast of Canada’s north-eastern shore (Kaufman, 2018). These types of threats are unpredictable and fast changing, and can significantly change from one day to the next.

Cyber threats are also a major concern for permanent and transient infrastructure and operations in northern latitudes. In recent years, cyber-attacks and cyber warfare have increased in frequency and damage. In 2017 ‘NotPetya’ caused huge service disruption, impacting industries from hospitals in Pennsylvania to Tasmania, construction companies, producers, Reuters oil extraction and Maersk’s distribution capabilities, with total damages estimated at more than $10 billion (Green, 2018).
