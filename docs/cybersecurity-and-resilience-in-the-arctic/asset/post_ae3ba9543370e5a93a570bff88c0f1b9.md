
## 3. Arctic Development: Emerging Challenges and the Need for Resilience

Defense, transportation, social/indigenous lifestyles, and business opportunities in the Arctic are accompanied by the danger of cyber-threats, especially to critical infrastructures in the Arctic “extra critical” because of the harsh environmental conditions and remoteness (Chapter 1 for a discussion of the growing resilience upon complex interconnected digital systems and infrastructure in the Arctic and sub-Arctic). Critical infrastructures in the Arctic are also crucial for military and security since its data hubs, significant energy resources are critical for digitalization, internet and computers. Interferences with climatic conditions, ice, and disasters, require new approaches to risk and resilience assessment and also effective legal frameworks to protect critical infrastructures and sustain both industrial and military activities.

The melting of Arctic ice presents new opportunities for growth and new challenges as various nations expand their footprint into the region. The private sector, shipping and tourism efforts benefit from access to the Northwest Passage through Canada and the Northern Sea Route above. The thaw also exposes oil and gas fields that grow up to a quarter of the Earth’s obtainable petroleum, offering an incentive for oil and gas companies to move more north. In the public sector, military transportation, defense, and search and rescue operations these interest groups, both civil and military, will need to keep pace with private sector growth. As they operate in the Arctic, they bring with them the need for expanded Arctic infrastructure, both temporary and permanent [63].

This additional infrastructure brings with it the potential for larger disasters due to cyber disruption. For example, the Kolos data center to be built in Ballangen, Norway plans to take advantage of hydroelectric power and use the cold to cool out its servers. The large amount of data stored makes it a prime target for hacking or physical attack, and damage to the facility, whether cyber or physical, will be difficult to repair due to the facility’s remote location. Even temporary infrastructure could potentially pose risks; consider the voyage of the American Crystal Serpent, which in 2016 was the largest cruise ship to navigate the Northwest Passage. While this voyage was successful, the disastrous results of the ship’s onboard computer being hacked were numerous, including loss of life of the passengers and spillage of oil and other materials due to the purposeful scattering of container and passenger ships near Arctic waters.

The traditional approach to an attack is to “harden” the system, in order to prevent it from penetrating. However, this approach to security is generally ineffective in the long term; more necessary is resilience, the ability for a system to recover from disruption or failure [33, 62]. In terms of the previous examples, resilience for the cruise ship means building checks along the ship’s path and having search-and-rescue and clean cleanup booths to minimize damage. For the data center, it means extra staff to correct errors introduced by hacking and developing evacuation plans in case of physical emergency. The challenges of Arctic infrastructure are not unique to the Arctic, but they are certainly exacerbated by harsh conditions and must be handled differently from lower latitudes.

What makes resilience critical objective for Arctic survival is that increasing scale and complexity of activity within previously uninhabitable regions, as well as the growing reliance upon complex digital systems to operate various elements of infrastructure make modern life possible. In more temperate and populated regions, disruption to telecommunications or energy systems are rarely quickly fatal, and significant resources can be quickly mobilized to mitigate hazards and address system vulnerabilities within a short time. In the Arctic, however, such disruptions can have rapid consequences that place individuals and communities in jeopardy. As such, resilience is not just a preferred characteristic of the infrastructural, and environmental systems that make modern life possible in a climatologically hazardous region, but instead it an an absolute necessity.

This is especially true for cybersecurity for interconnected digital systems. Virtually all modern equipment, buildings, and infrastructure are reliant on underlying digital systems to execute intended functions – design intended to simplify the labor requirements to achieve modern comforts and capabilities regardless of the environment or climate. While digital systems also represent a ‘single-point-of-failure’ for that, if exploited, could disrupt multiple directly related systems that have a common nest through information systems. From cruise ships, to industrial mining plants, to large buildings and facilities, a reliance on centralized or interconnected digital systems must be accompanied by resilience-based strategies for efficient recovery of services in the event of disruption. This is unpacked for further discussion in Chapter 3.

For the remainder of this chapter, we frame the governance challenges with the Arctic from a national and transnational perspective. We argue that thorough exploration of such governance issues will help unpack the policy realities within the region, as well as to highlight the development priorities that various stakeholders may have in the region. Further, such discussion highlights how resilience may be integrated into policy actions for facilities, infrastructure, missions, or overall human activity in the region.

## 4. Unpacking Arctic Governance: A Short History of the Arctic Council

The Arctic Council (AC) is a high-level intergovernmental forum established to foster international cooperation between Arctic states. Towards the end of the Cold War and the relaxation of international tensions, the Arctic was considered as a region for prospective theater of war operations between NATO and USSR countries [34]. In 1989, Finland sent a letter to the other Arctic states proposing a conference on the protection of the Arctic environment. Two years later in 1991, eight countries (Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden, and USA) signed the Arctic Environmental Protection Strategy (AEPS): a multilateral, non-binding agreement on Environmental Protection among Arctic states, three indigenous peoples’ organizations – the Saami Council, the Inuit Circumpolar Conference, and the Association of Indigenous Minorities of the North, Siberia and the Far East of the Russian Federation, were also present [35]. Major environmental problems identified at that time included persistent organic contaminants, oil pollution, heavy metals, radioactivity, and acidification. AEPS was a guide to tackling these issues through the development of Action Plans and six working groups, addressing a range of issues including conserving fauna and flora, environmental monitoring, and reducing contaminants [36]. Formally established in 1996, following the signing of the Ottawa Declaration, the Arctic Council was designed to provide a forum for discussion.

The Ottawa declaration marked a shift in focus to the Arctic states; concerns of environmental protection into broader concerns of sustainable development [37], and provides the Arctic Council as a mechanism for achieving cooperation, coordination and interaction between Arctic states. Since then, the Arctic Council has exerted greater oversight for monitoring and implementing the AEPS, with a permanent Secretariat based in Tromsø, Norway. Yet the Arctic is still a "soft-law regime". Unlike its compared to the Antarctic, there have been few intentions to create binding obligations for the states involved [38]. A number of treaties including the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL 73/78), the Polar Bear Treaty, and various other bilateral and multilateral agreements oversee most, but not all facto Arctic activity [39]. These treaties do not address all prospective issues - instead of a treaty system, the Arctic Council exists to serve as an advisory body. The first time that the Arctic Council facilitated leading to a binding agreement this treaty coordinates international search and rescue (SAR) coverage and response in the Arctic and establishes the area of SAR responsibility of each state. However, these SAR regions do not outline or relate to the disputed boundaries and territories of each state.

Since the late twentieth century, the impact of climate change has only been growing more significant. As a consequence, the regime established in the 1990s was not specifically designed in a way to address these issues. Focusing on the benefits and weaknesses of the Arctic regime, arguments for its replacement or at least shift towards a hard regime can be made, allowing enforceable policy to be made. A hard law regime is thought to be able to encourage stronger political commitments, establish firmer institutional and financial foundations, give legal "teeth" to compliance and enforcement, and establish mechanisms for settling international disputes [39]. In contrast a soft law regime provides better flexibility, are more rapidly agreed to and finalized, and can be implemented more quickly in address to urgent issues.
