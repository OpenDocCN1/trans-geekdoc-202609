
As the dependency on digital services increases, the organization of storage of critical data becomes more important. Cloud-based services offer many advantages for those who need to provide computer-based services, often being more economical and secure than alternative non-cloud based solutions. Many of the advantages of the increased use of digital solutions require data flow over national borders, and to data centers. This can lead to some legal considerations concerning (a) data residency (where data are stored, geographically), (b) data sovereignty (which country has legal jurisdiction over the data) and (c) data localization (a requirement that data created within a country's borders stay within these borders). Data localization laws can be based on national security considerations or simply specify that a government requires access to critical data to be available within the country's borders for auditing. There is a concern that such laws are used as part of national protectionism. Therefore, data localization laws face political opposition and need to be justified. The European Union has adopted Regulation (EU) 2018/1807 on a framework for the free flow of non-personal data in the European Union (EU, 2018). In the Arctic the needs for some of data localization are clear. The special conditions in the Arctic, with respect to data availability when conveyed over fragile redundant communication lines, make it preferable that storage and processing of some critical data be localized in the region. To mean legal requirements must be taken to ensure that these conditions apply for Arctic data and services.

Ensuring the resilience of local data services in the Arctic can also be a challenge due to the remoteness and unreliable transport services. Components of specialized computer and network systems are expensive to have in stock and often have to be ordered and on an as-needed basis. This can be costly in the Arctic and take some time.

## 5. Increasing resilience through digital dependencies

In recent years there has been emphasis on not only reducing related risks but also on increasing resilience (Linkov and Trump, 2019). It is important to make recovery from an incident a major part of security planning and it is dangerous to assume that a service can be well-protected that it cannot fail.

Having to face unpredictable, hostile forces of nature can encourage a culture of cooperation and when with the smallness and remoteness of a society, it can also foster a culture of trust. On the other hand, may these societies become vulnerable to various risks and based on the misuse of trust and with a level of activity that is rapidly increasing. In addition to increasing threats due to criminal activities, in this activity barrier to affect and disrupt critical services and functions in society also need careful consideration in cyber security planning.

Resilience, however, is typically a part of the culture of Arctic communities notwithstanding a high degree of variability within regions. As example, in Iceland glaciers were traditionally difficult (and dangerous) to cross and are said to have created a cultural barrier as well, with the community on the side of the river further away from a population center being more resilient and less dependent on services and resources from outside. Similar differences can be found in other Arctic societies.

Making use of digital opportunities is essential for many Arctic communities as well as the countries to which they belong. Growing up with some internet access can make all the difference whether young people feel there is a future in their home community or at least there. At the same time, opportunities must be balanced against the negative impact digital dependencies have in the Arctic, as the digital services cannot be assumed to be present. It must also be taken into consideration that the digital services may be available to the society but not to others (e.g. due to geographical, cultural, or economic reasons) and this can create or deepen a digital divide in a society. It is not only the communication supporting digital services in the Arctic to be fragile; the services themselves can be fragile. Delaunay (2017) gives the example of how Amazon changed the everyday life of some in the North-American Arctic by offering purchases (including food) from its site at a lower price than a local store could offer and to be shipped at no cost to the Arctic. However, in 2015 the free shipping service in Canada was limited to the capitals of the Arctic territories, with other communities having to pay shipping charges and in practice to go back to buying food at a much higher price from a local store. This demonstrated how vulnerable and dependent such a basic need of supply can be in an Arctic environment when the market has become to depend upon one supplier with limited backup once the services stop. With the so-called 4th Industrial Revolution, the Internet of Things (IoT), and the exponential growth of the number of devices connected to the Internet, the possible attack surface grows exponentially as well. The risks and vulnerabilities manifest themselves can be quite different in the Arctic compared to other regions, as has been mentioned previously. It is not only sufficient to minimize the risk of individuals in the system; an approach needs to be used that takes a holistic approach to the total risk, as is in the Systems-Technical Process Analysis (STPA) method (Leveson & Thomas, 2018).

Each Arctic society (and even community) must in its own end way of maximizing the possibilities the new Internet-based digital services can offer, but by increasing resilience minimizing risk and reducing digital dependencies to an acceptable level. Due to the differences between these communities there can be no general solution for all, but the communities can do benefit from increased exchange of experience and ideas. With more cooperation between the Arctic societies themselves and with non-Arctic societies as well, they will be in a better position to face a very challenging future in utilizing the new services.

## 6. Conclusion

It seems likely that though the Arctic will continue to make use of greatly increased availability of digital services, it will continue to have topographical regions suffering from fragile climates and the side-effects of being far away from most of the data centers which store cloud data and services. To meet the challenges of the digital age it is necessary Arctic communities to make use of the advantages of digital services, but at the same time to preserve the resilient culture of the Arctic societies in making them less vulnerable if the digital services fail or do so dearly or totally.
