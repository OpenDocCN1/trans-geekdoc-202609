# 2.1 Cyber Security and Safety

We briefly discuss how each of the previous systems is a potential target of an internal or external attack that may affect both the safety and the security of a 4 o ship. As an example, systems to manage and control a cargo, including hazardous cargo, interface with a variety of systems ashore. Such systems may include shipment-tracking tools available to shippers via the internet. Interfaces of these can make cargo management systems and data in manifests vulnerable to cyber-attacks. Bridge systems not connected to other networks may be equally vulnerable because they are updated using removable media from other controlled or uncontrolled networks. A cyber incident can cause service denial of propulsion, and may affect all systems associated with navigation, e.g. to display charts, to determine the ship position and transit to it ships. The use of an Industry 4.0 ship to monitor and control onboard machinery, propulsion and steering make such systems vulnerable to cyber-attacks. In a 4.0 ship, the integration of ICSs with remote condition-based monitoring and with navigation equipment surely increases the overall ship vulnerability. The systems for property management, boarding and access control have to ensure the physical safety and the safety of the people and the ship cargo. They usually store valuable passenger data and are a potential target because their control simplifies illegal access to the ship. Fixed or wireless on onboard networks are usually connected to the internet, for the benefit of passengers. These systems should be considered uncontrolled and not connected to any safety-critical system on board. Any violation of this rule increases vulnerabilities and attack opportunities. Onboard computer networks for ship administration or the crew welfare are particularly vulnerable when they provide internet access and email. Internet access extends the ship attack surface cyber attackers can exploit to gain access to onboard data. These systems should be kept uncontrolled and not connected to any safety-critical system on board. Software provided by ship management companies enables any adversary can attack the supply and hide some malware in the software. In any case, internet connectivity via satellite and/or wireless connections enlarges the vulnerability of a 4.0 ship.

While some solutions exist to assess the cyber risk due to each of the previous systems [19, 21–23], few just the ones the infrastructure running previously described [6, 13]. This interconnection of the systems provided in an environment where an attacker can target a system as a bridge to reach another one in the same infrastructure. Hence, if systems are connected, the security of each ship depends on those of the others. Furthermore, as previously anticipated, in a ship that connects a larger number of subsystems, we cannot draw a sharp distinction between the risk that affects safety and the one that affects security. As an example, consider an attacker accessing to some information about a ship. The attacker may launch a denial of service attack against the navigation system to hide some of its attacks to reach the target and the denial of service result in a safety risk.
