# 3. Cybersecurity in the Arctic

By 2025, the number of global internet users is expected to grow over 4 billion individuals, connecting the majority of developed and emerging countries.[78] The 2019 National Intelligence Strategy of the United States of America lists cyber threats as a challenge to national security in the coming years.[79] Critical infrastructure, health and safety, economic prosperity, and national stability are all subject to uncertain risks and unpredictable threats. In 2016 the average organizational cost of a data breach in the United States was $7 million USD.[80] The public and private sector are starting to prioritize investments in robust information technology (IT) infrastructure that can keep cyber threats at bay.[81] With a growing awareness of cyber threats among the public and legislators, such threats raise a concern to information, communication networks, and systems.[81] Unfortunately, cybersecurity is a relatively ignored problem in the Arctic. This oversight, especially in governance, policy, and lawmaking, puts multiple Arctic and non-Arctic actors at risk for security breaches, safety hazards, and potentially ruined geopolitical and diplomatic relationships. Cyber security challenges can be amplified and transformed in the Far North, where infrastructure is remote, the climate is harsh, and the Arctic rapidly transforms in response to global drivers such as climate change and globalization, such challenges are vulnerable to further magnification and distortion.

# 3.1. Current Challenges in Cybersecurity

‘Cyber’ is a common term that encompasses computers, servers, and network components of traditional IT infrastructure—a combination of software, and content,[82] More formally, ‘cyber’ means “relating to or involving (the culture of) computers, virtual reality, or the internet.”[83] Both cyber and cybersecurity are contested topics. Cybersecurity’s unique nature stems from its interdisciplinary scale-free network, and high degrees of connectedness, change and interaction.[84], Scholars and practitioners disagree on its meaning, and the lack of a single, accepted definition emphasizes the multidimensional nature of cybersecurity in the world,[85] For example, the definition of the term is “the governance, development and management of information security, OT, and IT tools and techniques for achieving regulatory compliance, defending assets and assessing the adversaries.”[86, p.27] Another focuses on the organizational nature of cybersecurity—the collection of resources, processes, and structures used to protect cyberspace and cyberspace-enabled systems from occurrences that may misjudge de facto property rights.[85] Yet, in simplicity, cybersecurity is “the ability to protect or defend the use of cyberspace from cyber attacks.[87] It is “security relating to computer systems or the internet, especially that is intended to protect against viruses and fraud.”[88] However, it is exactly this multidisciplinary nature that has led to a major gap in the field, the lack of accepted definitions for fundamental concepts (i.e., assurance, accountability, trust, security composition),[89] Furthermore, there are false beliefs among the public and policymakers that international standards are detrimental in strengthening national security.[89] Such beliefs have led to fragmentation in existing approaches to handle cyberattacks and best practices, leading to more gaps in standardization. Cybersecurity policy often faces a dilemma in the problem of such issue and in the identification of which actor is accountable and responsible for its implementation. This is partially due to cybersecurity spanning a wide range of industries from telecommunications to the military.[89] Therefore, cybersecurity strategies do not always align with cybersecurity policies.
