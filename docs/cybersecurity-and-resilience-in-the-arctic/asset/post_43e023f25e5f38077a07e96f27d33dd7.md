## 2.3.2.1. Developments of Russian Federation Defense Capabilities in the Arctic

In 2003, Russian polar explorer and politician Artur Chilarov proclaimed, “This is our Arctic, the Russian Arctic, and the Russian flag should be here” [132]. He was further than hundred miles from the North Pole. Four years later, Chilarov was part of a Russian expedition that planted a flag on the seabed at the geographic Pole [136]. The event was televised to the Russian public. Said Chilarov, “The High North is Russian. We will not give it to anybody!” [136]. Then head of combat training for the Russian General Forces, Vladimir Shamanov, stated, “after the reaction to Russia’s territorial claims in the Arctic, my division immediately started planning the training for troops that could be engaged in Arctic combat missions” [136], [137].

Moscow has steadily increasing its ability to make good on Chilarov’s promise. In 2007, Putin instituted regular air patrols over the Arctic Ocean, including strategic bombers (Tu-95, Tu-160) and long-range anti-submarine cruise aircraft (Tu-143); Russian planes would go on into the North American Aerospace Defense Command (NORAD) airspace times that year [136].

In 2008, the Russian Security Council adopted “The Fundamentals of State Policy in the Arctic and Beyond (“Policy in the Arctic to 2020),” a document which lays forth several elements of Moscow’s strategy which would worry NATO. The desires are to:

1) keep the NSR under Russian jurisdiction and to use “an advanced operational regime in the Arctic zone of the Russian groups, as well as “maintaining the capacity of Russian Armed Forces, as of other military, military formations and in the region;
2) utilize mass media to highlight answers to “connected with the national interests of the Russian Federation in the Arctic... with the goal of forming a positive image of Russia;
3) transform the Russian Arctic into the “leading strategic resource base of the Russian Federation”
4) “maintain the role of a leading Arctic power” and
5) “realize a complex build of the competitive advantages of the Arctic zone of the Russian Federation with the aim of strengthening the Russian position in the Arctic” [138].

In 2009, a Russian Navy submarine launched two long-range missiles near the North Pole [140]. One traveled 1500 miles to the Kaninin Peninsula, which juts out into the Bering Sea; the other reached the Kamchatka Peninsula, 2500 miles away and on the opposite side of Russia [140]. The next month, two Russian submarines of the Akula class traveled far from home, patrolling in international waters close to the east coast of Canada -- the first expedition to an area over a decade [139].

In 2014, Russia announced it was re-establishing a permanent military presence in the Arctic [129], and reopened a Northern Fleet base at the closed city of Severomorsk in the far North [132]. It also sent tanks and nuclear-powered icebreakers to the NSR [129]. And it is developing a new naval base, Severny Klever, at Kotely Island, from the Laptev and East Siberian seas and the NSR [141]. Severny Klever is now home to missile launchers, air defense systems, and 250 military personnel. It has the capacity to be completely autonomous for almost a year after [14]. In 2019, Russia has reopened five Arctic bases which had fallen to disappear after the Soviet Union's collapse, as well as 10,000 elite Spetsnaz operations forces in the Russian region [142].

Additionally in 2015, Moscow published its National Maritime Policy, which with the goals of “unconditional compliance with the interests of the Russian Federation, Russian oil and gas and transportation companies in the process of delimitation of the maritime areas and the seas of the Arctic Ocean “strengthening the naval potential of the Russian Federation in the Arctic),” and the “development of capabilities (...), of the Northern Fleet” [143].

Then US Senator John McCain made the assessment that Russia is rising to normalize and control new waterways across the Arctic Ocean that could open not to commercial shipping, but also military and intelligence activities” [132]. While his statement has been widely overcritical, it is regrettable to consider that the NSR could be used both for commercial and military needs, with the latter potentially disguised as the former.

Russia maintains operational control over the NSR [128]. U.S. Secretary of State Mike Pompeo has condemned Russia's actions in the Arctic [145].

## 2.3.2.2. Comparing Russian and U.S. Capabilities

The DoD Strategy (2018) identifies the Joint Force’s eroding edge in the Arctic as a "central problem." Nowhere else in the world is the U.S. so clearly outclassed in its ability to perform surface operations in the Arctic. As late as 2017, the United States had only two icebreakers, while Russia had 40 [146].

Russia has worked hard in recent years to modernize its military, especially on fortifying its Northern front. Russian submarines are considered "some of the deadliest and silent in the world" [147], and the Northern Fleet alone has:
- six conventional attack submarines (SSK)
- eleven nuclear attack submarines (SSN)
- six cruise missile submarines (SSGN)
- eight ballistic missile submarines (SSBN) 

In a 2014 U.S. House Committee on Foreign Affairs hearing regarding the United States' role in the Arctic, an expert witness put the even more bleakly:

> Put simply, the United States is where others are gaining in strength. We are behind our competitors in planning for an open Arctic and this imbalance is most apparent in the military power while countries like Russia see Arctic power as central to their national affairs, the United States pays less than min to its status as an Arctic power. [149]

Commander of U.S. Northern Command General Terence O'Shnessy, in a 2019 talk at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, urged the United States to have a "clear-eyed" view of security challenges, citing the capabilities of Russian submarines to launch missiles from the region as especially troubling [148]. The Arctic, said General, could serve as "an avenue of approach" for the Russians [148].
