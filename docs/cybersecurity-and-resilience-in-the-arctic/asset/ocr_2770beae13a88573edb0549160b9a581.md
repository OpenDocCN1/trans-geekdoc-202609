Risk analysis is the process of assessing the consequences, their likelihood and severity of consequences when any adverse event could have a particular domain. Domains may relate to cyber security, the environment, social systems, government, businesses, the economy or potentially any chosen area of concern. Risk analysis studies tries to measure and express risk in numerical terms. Where we have a problem of opportunity cost, it's important to understand what areas are crucial to invest money, effort and time protecting, and what areas are less vital. Risk analysis informs risk management, and by hardening the systems we can protect against risks, ensuring that the processes dependent upon them are not disrupted in the wake

What this chapter aims to illustrate, is that risk analysis is not sufficient and in certain scenarios; whether it be due to lack of accurate information, unpredictability of threats, or rapidly changing conditions. Uncertain, unforeseeable, and unknow hazards have been shown in this chapter to be inherent characteristics of the Arctic region, and so it's clear that there is a need to couple risk analysis with strategies of enhancing resilience

Resilience analysis, as defined by Francis and Bekera (2014), is understood by absorbing, adaptive, and restorative capacities. Risk and resilience analysis can be differentiated in a manner of ways; while risk analysis methods are used to determine negative outcomes in the face of undesirable events, resilience is an endowed or enriched characteristic that enables the capacity of a system to absorb, adapt and recover from adverse events. Unlike risk analysis, resilience perspectives recognize the dynamic and interconnected nature of our complex modern systems and measure the ability of these systems to respond to potential disruptions without catastrophic failures in performance, function and physical structure (Palma-Oliveira and Trump 2016).

Resilience analysis must come from an understanding of how systems are interconnected and in the ways they can be disrupted (by identifying threats, hazards, shocks, disturbances, anomalies etc.) (Trump et al., 2018). Once we have outlined an acceptable level of system functionality should not decrease, we can begin to assess such vulnerability. Vulnerability analysis is important because adverse events cannot be perfectly predicted due to epistemic or aleatory uncertainty. Integrating temporal analysis into resilience analysis alongside continuous self-evaluation encourages vulnerability assessments to be used in a proactive manner. What's most important to acknowledge, however, consistent questioning of our risk models may be necessary; we must recognize that in complex systems catastrophic failures may be inevitable. For resilience, by enhancing systems we can either absorb perturbations, adapt to them, or in last resort improve our ability to recover from negative impacts
