New actors in the Arctic are predominantly from Asian states and with an increasingly active role in the Circumpolar North decision-making and governance. They include (see Box 1 for a more comprehensive list):

+   * Non-Arctic States (i.e., Japan, People's Republic of China, Republic of Korea)
* NGOs (i.e., environmental and conservationist)
* Private corporations (i.e., shipping, gas/oil, tourism, fishing, etc.)

For example, Japan released its Arctic Policy in 2015, emphasizing their aims towards international cooperation and ensuring the power of the rule of law in the Arctic [71]. On the other hand, China's Arctic Policy in 2018 advocated the creation of a 'Polar Silk Road,' highlighting its economic power and attempting to strengthen its position as a critical actor in Arctic governance. Additionally, a few NGOs have impacted Indigenous Peoples' subsistence and livelihoods by shaping laws that curtail access to sealing, whaling, and hydrocarbon extraction. Moreover, private interests have attempted to utilize accessible sea routes and resources through international law of the seas legislation.

Nevertheless, Arctic vs. non-Arctic dichotomy remains, especially in key international laws such as the Agreement to Prevent Unregulated High Seas Fisheries in the Central Arctic Ocean, the melting polar sea ice straining existing legal and policy arrangements, particularly due to the Law of the Sea (i.e., the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea-UNCLOS). With sea ice in the Arctic Ocean, an increasing number of Arctic and non-Arctic actors will try to exercise their national freedoms for economic and political interests. Geopolitical tensions are growing with renewed strains in relations between global powers (e.g., Russia versus the A with the 2007 flag planting; the United States and Russia over the 2016 elections).[68] The existing Arctic governance structure is complex and fragile. While the Arctic Council has so far successfully as a forum for negotiations, its current structure presents challenges towards adapting to a rapidly changing Arctic. For example, Canada's treaty-based relationship with Indigenous Peoples still relevant and practical? What further governance mechanisms must be in place? Trust and goodwill are carefully measured in the Arctic Council between the A, and emerging interests from Asia and private corporations have turned regions into transnational and global issues.[72]

With such a political environment, governance challenges abound. The Arctic is mostly governed by transnational law. Law is often reactionary and conservative, and not precautionary or by nature. Law is not a good mechanism to preventatively mitigate risks. Often, an incident has to occur before a law is made. Moreover, most legal systems are not adept at dealing with issues that are poorly defined and unamenable to prediction. Multiple dichotomies only serve to complicate governance.
