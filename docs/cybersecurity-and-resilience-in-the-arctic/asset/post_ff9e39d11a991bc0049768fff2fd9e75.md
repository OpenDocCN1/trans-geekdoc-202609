
# 5. A Brief Overview of the Arctic Council

The main organ of the Arctic Council is the secretariat, which rotates every two years, with each chair determining the goals and objectives of the council and a plan through which they can be achieved. This is the highest level at which interaction within the council occurs. This presents a problem, as goals can change every few years. In response to this challenge, Norway, Sweden and Denmark made an agreement on common objectives and priorities and shared the chair for six years (2007-2013), helping to ensure and promote Arctic protection through the continuation of programmes, producing greater stability and progress in achieving objectives.

Between biennial 'ministerial meetings', the work of the Arctic Council is primarily carried out by working groups and Programs and Plans. This work is directed by Senior Arctic Officials (SAOs) – representatives appointed by each Arctic state to manage its interests in the Arctic Council. These officials oversee the work of the Arctic council subsidiary bodies, including the working groups. The six working groups cover emission and pollution (ACAP), the scientific monitoring of Arctic environment, ecosystems and human populations (AMAP), conserving Arctic biodiversity (CAFF), protecting the Arctic environment from the impact of accidental pollution/radionuclide release (EPPR), protection and promotion of sustainable use of the Arctic marine environment (PAME), and advancing sustainable development (SDWG). The Programs and Action plans include the Arctic Biodiversity Assessment, the Circular Biodiversity Monitoring Program (CBMP), the Arctic Climate Impact Assessment, and the Arctic Human Development.

The council may additionally create Task forces and expert groups to carry out more specific work; most recently under Finnish chairmanship from 2017-19, the task forces were the Task Force on Marine Cooperation (TFAMC), and the Task Force on Improved Connectivity in the Arctic (TFICA).

> “Rules of procedure” exist to detail in which meetings are run and the mechanism for which decisions should be taken across all Arctic council bodies. They cover general provisions of Arctic Council meetings, ministerial meetings, meetings of Senior Arctic Officials, the implementation of cooperative activities, and other matters. These rules were revised in 2013 and can be found as published by the Arctic Council [41]. They apply to all bodies of the council and represent an agreed upon framework for international cooperation between all parties.

## 6.1. United States (US) Arctic Policy

Domestic lobbying efforts emphasizing the need for an American Arctic agenda have been occurring since the turn of the millennia (eg. Commonwealth North). The United States are called out for their lack of participation and importance placed as Arctic Arctic, and many argue that they need to understand and embrace their identity as an Arctic Nation [44].

President Bush's administration released the "National Presidential Directive/NSPD-66" in 2009, overtaking previous directives such as the "Presidential Decision Directive/PDD; issued 1994) with respect to Arctic and Antarctic policy. The Arctic policy came late in the Bush administration's final weeks, due to its bipartite nature the document is still considered to be relevant for current and future administrations.

The Policy begins by defining US interest in the Arctic in light of several developments:

- 1. Changes in national policies of Homeland Security;
- 2. The effects and impacts of climate change and human activity;
- 3. The establishment and ongoing work of the Arctic Council; and
- 4. A growing awareness that the Arctic is both fragile and rich in resources.

Other interests denoted are those concerning the delineation of territorial and maritime boundaries, scientific research and monitoring, transportation, energy, and environmental protection. The document states that the United States is dedicated to cooperation, but also leaves room for unilateral action deemed necessary. NSPD-66 then goes to include policy deliverables, covering six keys through which said policies are likely to be organized. Based on these statements, the Arctic Region Policy of the United States is:

- 1. Meet national and homeland security needs relevant in the Arctic;
- 2. Protect the Arctic environment and safeguard its biological resources;
- 3. Ensure sustainable resource management and economic development;
- 4. Strengthen and utilize the mechanisms and institutions for cooperation with other Arctic states can be achieved;
- 5. Recognize and involve indigenous communities in Arctic policy making;
- 6. Enhance scientific research and monitoring for local, regional and global environmental issues.

The USA's policy puts a focus on solving national/homeland security issues and border concerns, particularly those relating to maritime areas through bolstering military presence in aiming of projecting power throughout the region. America's shortcomings in ratifying UNCLOS not, however, prevented them from wanting to establish continental shelves, and also come to an agreement with Russia surrounding the 1990 US-Russian boundary agreement.

## 6.2. Russian Arctic Policy

Russian Arctic policy is detailed in “Fundamentals of State policy of the Russian Federation in the Period up to 2020 and Beyond: adopted by President Medvedev in 2009 [45]. Here, a shortened translation of the document, which was published in the official government newspaper, Rossiyskaya Gazeta on the 30th of March in 2009, is referenced.

The strategic priorities of the State Policy of the Russian Federation in the Arctic (up to 2020 and beyond) are to:

- 1. Create a uniform Arctic search and rescue regime and prevention of man-caused accidents;
- 2. Ensure a uniform Arctic SAR regime in an attempt to prevent accidents caused by human error;
- 3. Strengthen bilateral relationships within the framework of regional organizations, such as the Arctic Council and the Barents Euro-Arctic Council;
- 4. Assist in the organization, management, and effective use of cross-polar air routes and the North Sea Route for international navigation;
- 5. Participate and contribute within Arctic forums through the Russian-European partnerships;
- 6. Outline regions in the Arctic Ocean, ensuring a mutually advantageous presence of Russia in the Spbergen archipelago;
- 7. Improve state management, developing the Arctic economically and socially, and increase support for scientific research and monitoring;
- 8. Improve the quality of life for indigenous peoples and their social and economic activities;
- 9. Develop the Arctic resource base using new technological advancements;
- 10. Modernize and develop the Arctic infrastructure, including that of transport and fisheries in the Russian Arctic

Russia’s policy objectives in the Arctic are:

- 1. Resource Development: An expansion of the resource base, in order to satisfy Russia’s needs in hydrocarbon resources, hydro-biological resources, and other types of strategically important resources;
- 2. Military Security: ensure the upkeep and maintenance of the required combat potential of Russia’s military groupings, maintaining favorable operative regimes in the Russian Arctic zone;
- 3. Environmental security: work to preserve and support the Arctic environment;
- 4. IT and Communication: work towards the formation of a uniform information area;
- 5. Science and Technology: enabling a sufficient level of scientific research and creation of scientific bases and management, as well as implementing the development of means for the solution of defense and security problems, in addition to functioning systems of life support and industrial activity under environmental and climatic conditions;
- 6. International Cooperation: working bilaterally and multilaterally with Arctic and sub-States on the basis of international treaties and agreements which Russia is part, ensuring mutually advantageous outcomes.
