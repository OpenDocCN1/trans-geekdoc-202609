# Digital Resilience: Navigating in the New Normal

Jordanka Tomkova, PhD
INNOVABRIGE Foundation, Switzerland

**Abstract.** This paper seeks to expand the scope of conceptualizing social resilience in the digital era. It examines emerging trends in how groups or communities, formally or informally informed, use the digital space in the recovery stages of disruptive events. While disruptive events or collective disasters include health epidemics, security or economic crises, the focus here is on collective social responses to hybrid threats. Hybrid threats combine with offline destabilizing tactics that attack targets' virtual or (physical) infrastructure, information manipulation, political subversion and social polarization. The paper argues that the anonymous character of hybrid threats seeks new responses and enhanced digital social resilience means and how it manifests practiced in reality. Following a review of literature this paper identifies how communities use digital tools to respond to hybrid threats: i) collective mobilization, crowdsourcing and toolmaking; ii) emotive solidarity, and iii) restoration of morality quests. The paper postulates that these observed forms of collective behavior characterize and define merging of social resilience in the contemporary online environment.

**Keywords.** Digital social resilience, social networks, digital coping strategies, hybrid threats

## INTRODUCTION

As new information and communication technologies rapidly permeate our realities, the offline and online spaces increasingly collide in unprecedented ways forming our new normal. As part of this reality, cyberattacks, online anonymity, the darkweb, deployment of malware and subversive social media bots, disinformation, identity thefts, trolling, astroturfing, doxxing, swatting are emerging neotypes of socially erosive behavior. In combination with hybrid threats these have been observed to deepen cognitive tribalism, exploit geopolitical manipulation, political subversion, social polarization or malicious civic disobedience threatening the social fabric of our societies. They seek to expose targets' vulnerabilities, to instill a state of permanence and to destabilize the socio-political, security and often economic order as we know it.[11]

In response to these threats what hat or ethical hacking, hashtag activism, fact-checking, debunking and unmasking are emerging as the online antidotes and implicitly, what this paper considers measures of social resilience. Reviewing pioneering research and illustrative case studies, this paper seeks to place a magnifying glass on examining social resilience from the online or digital perspective.

At its core, it asks: how do people, groups use spaces and tools to absorb, recover from adapt to external shocks, namely in response to the increasing imminence of hybrid threats? What characterizes their collective coping and adaptive strategies in online environments? Aspiring to benefit researchers as well as practitioners, in the paper, three observed tendencies in online communities' coping strategies are identified: i) collective mobilization and toolaking; ii) emotional solitude; iii) restoration of mortality quests. All of these theoretical domains for social system's performance and importance for its resilience[2]. At the same time, due to the relative novelty and complexity of these phenomena, the paper makes no illusions about its exploratory power. The paper lastly posits that future policy initiatives need to allow both informal, decentralized community-driven solutions as well as agile regulatory responses to steer toward effective preparedness to withstand future threats.

## Defining Social Resilience in the Digital Space

Research on social resilience in the offline context has amply grown over the past two decades. By means no simple concept to take, literature on offline social resilience derives from several interdisciplinary theoretical streams. The first derives from systemic social-ecological resilience theories [1][1]. It defines resilience as the capacity of a system – groups or communities – to cope with external stresses and disturbances when exposed to social, political and environmental threats[4]. In other words, social resilience is multi-dimensional, comprising communities' social coping capacities – to all kinds of adverse; adaptive capacities – exhibiting 'learning' from past experiences and to adjust to future challenges; well as transformative capacities – their ability to adapt and self reorganize while undergoing change so to retain its functions, structure and identity in order to foster human welfare and societal functions to withstand future crises.[5]. The necessity of resilience to return to equilibria has been challenged, as disruptive states are also seen to include opportunities for the recombination of evolved structures and processes, renewal of the system and emergence of new trajectories[6] – not necessarily expecting systems to return to prior functions or states.

The second theoretical stream for conceptualizing social resilience derives from social psychology and cultural anthropology. Research in this stream examines individuals, organizations' communities and networks' capacity to mobilize their social capital in order to absorb, cope and adapt to disruptive events and external.[7]. Social capital is vested in relationships with others (peers, groups but also referring to communities' intrinsic assets at their level of material trust, reciprocity, participation, collective action and collective efficacy. Protective-factors or social structures extended by family members, peers, association groups or organizations are also seen to contribute to the development of attitudes and values that ultimately shape their capacity to respond and competently mitigate adverse situations.[8]. Understanding of group think, decision-making and self-organization dynamics, collective action and crowd behavior[9]

Both socio-ecological and social theory consider social resilience to be a dynamic process and a normative concept rather than a state (ecology) or a trait (social psychology) that is subject to change over time. Moreover, as pointed out by Obris[13], when studying resilience, it is essential to ask what threat or risk is examined? Resilience to what? Is it individual or to community-based threat, is it a short-term or a long-term threat, a single hazard or to multiple hazards, recurring, chronic, or seasonal threats, to slow-onset or rapid-onset risks? This paper does not touch all these dimensions but they are important for the framing of social resilience in future research.[14]

## Uniqueness of the online space

While the noted ‘creative literature’ provides pertinent theoretical frames for conceptualizing social resilience in offline settings – it makes references to what social resilience means in the contemporary online context. As a point of departure in conceptualizing digital social resilience, it is important to identify the characteristics and functionalities of the online or digital environment and the ‘social’. By digital or online we make the use of computer or Internet information and communication technologies (ICT) and data including, email social media (Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, Reddit, YouTube), mobile and software applications, digital platforms and dashboards. Digital spaces are functionally unique in that they grant self-administered 24/7 access to sets, enabling synchronous (in real time e.g. chatrooms) but also asynchronous (with time lag – e.g. e-mails) one-to-one, but also one-to-many-many communication hence enhancing the convenience, significance[15] and speed (virtual), interaction and cost-effectiveness of disseminating information.

The online environment’s unique discursive practices and psychosocial aspects add structural, representational, and interactive dimensions to the online public sphere.[16]. Unlike traditional print and television media which are unidirectional intermediaries in mass communication and mutuality among users[17], enabling users to easily move between the roles of audience and content producers. In the virtual realm, people interact and engage in diverse forms of information exchange by posting and reposting content. Iconic features of social media is the ability to provide immediate and quantifiable social feedback in response to other people’s content (e.g. likes, shares, retweet) amplifies users’ propensity to express moral emotions in response to moradores-relevant content.[18]. The feedback delivery system of social media further amplifies norm conformity our group identities become strongly highly and hyper-latent.[19].
