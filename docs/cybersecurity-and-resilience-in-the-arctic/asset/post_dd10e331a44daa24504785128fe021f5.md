Another element of the Arctic where regime shifts are taking place is in the tundra region. The tundra is covered by permafrost - permanently frozen ground that occurs from several inches below the surface to depths of more than 330 meters. Increases in temperatures are already having adverse effects on permafrost; thawing is expected to disrupt infrastructure of several types – bogging up roads to destabilizing the ground under which buildings stand, the sinking beneath oil pipelines. Hjort et al. [21] estimate that nearly a million people and 70% of infrastructure in the permafrost domain have high potential for thaw. Development will require new devices, and construction and maintenance costs are likely to increase. Moreover, thawed in permafrost and cryosphere are greenhouse gases, and melting releases them into the atmosphere. Some estimates put the thawing of East Siberia (the northern Russian Arctic) to come with an average global price of $60 trillion alone, in the absence of mitigating action.

Lastly, ice sheets and glaciers in the Arctic are another important feature of the Arctic system. The Greenland ice sheet covers over 82% of Greenland, and if melted is expected to cause a rise of over seven meters in sea level [23]. The ice sheet, however, has long been viewed to be resistant to climate change, as it is typical for thousands of years before large sheets report changing conditions. Recent observations suggest that major changes in the dynamics of parts of the ice sheet are occurring in the time scale of just a few years [1]. Ice has thinning due to temperature increases, especially during the summer, and feedback mechanisms that play a role in stabilizing the ice sheet (ice-albedo feedback and melt-warming) are now playing a destabilizing role within the ice sheet.

## 2.2. Arctic Ecosystems

The Arctic is a home to a diverse range of flora and fauna. Organizations have adapted to a region that is often cold, exposed to prolonged periods of daylight and darkness in alternating seasons, and from a range of habitats in wetlands and deserts - from ponds to open ocean. Some of these organisms, as the polar bear are icons representing the Arctic, yet others are lesser known and it is likely that some are as uncovered. The range of biodiversity is faced by an ever increasing speed of change, with a potential for some of these change to be irreversible.

## 2.3. Social communities

The Arctic is a home to a diverse range of flora and fauna. ... These peoples have survived for thousands of years in some of the harshest conditions on the planet, adapting through hunting and techniques centred around seals, whales, moose, caribou, reindeer, salmon, not only economic subsistence but also the fundamental basis for social identity and spiritual life [8].

Colonization of the Arctic and interaction with outsiders, who initially aimed to exploit the Arctic region's resources, has been occurring since the 16th Century, however since the 1800s more frequent and sustained contact has been made. Communities were introduced to modern disease, including smallpox, measles, influenza and cholera. More recently indigenous communities are beginning to be subjugated to chronic diseases such as cardiovascular related illnesses and diabetes, as well as increases in substance abuse, violence and suicides [28]. While some of these issues are now viewed as a problem of the past, it illustrates that indigenous peoples are often unwilling to rapid, extensive and disruptive changes, in economic, social and personal daily life.
