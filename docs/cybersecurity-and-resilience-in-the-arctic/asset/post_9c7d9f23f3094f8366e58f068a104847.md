The growth of smart grids evokes a complex transformation of electrical generation and electricity sharing therein is associated with modernization of flexible transmission and distribution systems. Their development is associated with modernization of the whole range of power generation, transition, and consumption by improved management, protection, and optimization of the technological elements of the electrical power system. The exploitation of renewable energy ever means fine-tuning, concurrence, and numerical regulation of energy production is necessary (Eid et al., 2016). Here, the following three main characteristics of the “electric world” can be distinguished:

- intelligence (smart systems, energy saving, targeted power supply) (Kabachal, 2016; Kok, 2013);
- systematic (integrated regulation technology, electric power storage technology, decentralization, direct Peer-to-Peer (P2P) energy trading, multidirectional trading within a local area) (Stöcker, 2018; Merz, 2016; Zhang, 2017);
- greenish (renewable energy, alternative fuel for transport, carbon markets) (Mihaylov, 2016; Ela et al. 2016).

The concept of innovative transformation of the electric power industry provides for the construction of a fully integrated, self-regulating, and self-renewable system with network topology by all generating sources, local and separate networks, and all types of consumers of the electrical grid. For example, PowerMatch is a self-sufficient distribution system that includes both distributed generation and loads arising from appliances and physical devices and that integrated distributed energy in the operation of the electricity system meets the requirements of privacy protection, openness, and scalability (Kok, 2013). In general, there are lot of benefits with the digitization of the grid; for one, it is an opportunity to integrate renewable energy sources, making them more environmentally friendly than the traditional network. Even though the introduction of intelligence into the traditional energy supply promises significant benefits in terms of productivity and efficiency, it raises concerns about the protection of smart grids from threats of cybersecurity. Complexity with vulnerability is a serious concern. Cybersecurity is a real issue at the application, network, and physical layers of Smart Grid infrastructure (Mrabet et al., 2018).

This is further complicated by interoperability and proprietary nature of technologies, wireless interference, etc. One of the promising solutions in this area is to apply other sophisticated techniques to counter cyberattacks and support smart grid communication infrastructure. This new idea that applies Smart Grid security on Blockchain-based systems is currently under research by several researchers around the world (Aitzh and Svetinovic, 2016; Nguyen et al, 2018; Schwieters et al, 2018; Mengelkamp et al, 2017; Trump et al., 2018). Since 2008, there have been several attempts to build blockchain-green energy trading platforms and energy markets (EnergyBay, 2018; WePower). To this moment, legal institutions have not established regulations yet; however, the application of Blockchain in a Smart Grids does not seem obvious (Hawletschek et al.). However, cyber risks in Smart Grid infrastructure are the need to find a solution to one of the most pressing problems in the digital world.

## 3. Smart Grid security: goals and perspectives

Smart security requirements differ from those of other critical infrastructures. The intelligence network security goals can be divided into three groups that are considered below:

- data availability
- data integrity
- confidentiality of data

**Data availability:** This security requirement is very important and one of the main tasks of ensuring the reliable operation of the intelligent network. In general, accessibility means "timely and reliable access to information" (The Smart Grid, 2010). However, the availability delays depend on task, as shown in Table 2.

**Data Integrity:** The integrity of data means that the source and quality of data are known and authenticated. Changing or destroying output data leads to loss of data integration. Loss of data integration can cause to the intrusion into the domain of an intruder or dissatisfied employee or human error. The problem of the integrity of the vasta refers to the reliability of the system and with the increasing complexity of systems, this problem is growing rapidly.

**Confidentiality of data:** This security requirement is important to ensure the privacy of end-users. Although this component has the least impact on the reliability of intelligent powers, their importance is growing with the deployment of expanded accounting infrastructure and decentralized network management.

According to Shridhar (2012), the security issues of a smart grid include the following:

- physical components
- control centers and applications
- cyber infrastructure for smart grid network
- the relationship between cyber attacks and the effects of the physical system
- protective measures to mitigate risks from cyber threats

Its structure, network, and connections make the Smart Grid target for security attacks at all points. In addition, privacy is a major issue. Given the interconnectedness and interdependence of infrastructures, the demand for a reliable and sustainable energy system is strong; such a system must:

- be confidential
- have a high level of integrity and efficiency
- ensure a high level of authentication, reliability, and stability
- possess a high level of controllability and observability

Currently, several computer scientists and researchers around the world are researching the applying of blockchain (Bonomi et al., 2018; Achan and Svetovic, 2016; Erol-Kantarci and Moufahah, 2013). The blockchain technology implements a distributed ledger technology (DLT) in a peer-to-peer network, originally employed for the Bitcoin (Nakamoto, 2008). Therefore, our main goal is to integrate forensics with the monitoring process to ensure the integrity of the smart energy platform and to discuss the points where blockchain is the perfect technology to apply forensics.

## 4. Challenges for Security and Forensics in the Smart Grid

Currently, most energy service providers use traditional security measures, including authentication, access control, authorization, data encryption, key infrastructure, firewalls, log analysis, intrusion detection systems, and more. However, recent cyberattacks on power systems have a significant gap between the possibilities of protecting and restoring traditional systems and new security requirements, in the context of intellectualization of energy systems and technologies for distribution and transmission of energy. Through cyber attacks, the network may encounter operational crashes and loss of synchronization. Such failures can damage the physical components of the power system, which can interrupt power supply and lead to significant financial losses.

When discussing smart grids, we should take into account that attacks can arise from various parts of a power system using Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA), electric transportation infrastructure, smart meters, advanced metering infrastructure (AMI), the energy storage subsystem, and vital components of the smart grid.

To cope with these challenges in distributed networks, we need information that will enable the effective investigation of cyber crimes, the prediction of system failures, and the management of the Smart Grid. Recently, Cohen (2015) discussed a potential that cyber attackers can anonymize up to 60% of Bitcoin coins in the network and open a new way to forensics in blockchain. Erol-Kantarci M. and Moufahah H.T. (2013) have defined smart grid forensics as a scientific component of the power system and have also described the applications, obstacles, and open issues in this area.
