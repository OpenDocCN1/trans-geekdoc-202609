## 1. Introduction

The United States Navy (USN) is the most powerful navy in the world [1]. Its core functions, as expounded in the Navy’s most recent strategic document [2], its deterrence, sea control, power projection, maritime security, logistics, search and rescue, and defense support. Due to a number of factors, including the size, budget, experience, and training of the USN – and in particular its fleet of 11 nuclear-powered aircraft carriers, which in number dwarf those of any other country – has maintained its dominance and sovereignty.

Even fears China, with its three aircraft carriers and over 300 ships, challenging USN superiority in the seas are seen by experts to be inaccurate and alarmist; in fact, "The United States remains ahead" [3], [4]. While that is accurate for now, the balance of powers can court shift, as it has many times before.

Both scholars and military leaders agree: The Navy must adapt in order to maintain its competitive edge in the face of various pre-existing and emerging threats [5], [6]. These threats can cause concern from a resurgent and audacious China and Russia which can also make use of new weapons such as anti-access and area denial (A/AD) weapons, UAVs, and hypersonic anti-ship missiles and can operate in the cyber domain [1], [7]. As USN Voice Admiral and Rear Admirals Rowden, Gumatoa, and Fanta note:

> If U.S. naval power is to maintain battlespace dominance in future A/AD environments, the surface Navy must counter rapidly evolving missile, air, submarine, and surface threats that will challenge our ability to sail where we want, when we want [7]

## 2. The U.S. Navy in the Arctic: Emerging Disruptions

### 2.1. Environmental Threats and Disruptions

Both the innate nature of the Arctic -- its extreme temperatures, winds, the amount of daylight, unpredictable ice sea, and so forth -- as well as changes by climate change present threats to the Navy. This section will delineate some of the most significant of those threats.

#### 2.1.1. The Arctic Environment

There are different definitions of what exactly constitutes the Arctic. It has been defined variously as the region above the northernmost tree line, where the average temperature during the warmest month (July) is below 10 degrees Celsius, and as the region north of the Arctic Circle, which corresponds to the region experiencing midnight sun (when the sun remains for an entire day) and polar night (the sun is hidden for an entire day) [12], [13]. The Navy uses the definition set in the U.S. Code, which is as follows:

> 'Arctic' means all United States and foreign territory north of the Arctic Circle and all United States territory north and west of the boundary formed by the Porcupine, Yukon, and Kuskokvim Rivers; all contiguous seas, including the Arctic Ocean and the Beaufort, Bering, and Chukchi Seas; and the Aleutian Chain. [14]

This definition encompasses the area north of the Arctic Circle in addition to some U.S. territories, namely, a larger portion of Alaska as well as the Aleutian Islands.

Regardless of the precise definition, the Arctic presents numerous challenges to ships and those who serve on them. USN Vice Admiral Lewis has stated that the Arctic forces him to float "a little closer to the limit than we traditionally do" [15].
