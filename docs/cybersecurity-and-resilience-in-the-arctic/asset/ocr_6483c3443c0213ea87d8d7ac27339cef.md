Scientists perceive the Arctic into two main climate types: maritime and interior climates. In Arctic regions, such as those found in Alaska, Iceland, Russia's northern coastline and Scandinavia, winters are typically snowy with precipitation rearing ranging anywhere from 60-125cm. Summers tend to be generalised by coolness and cloudy skies, with temperatures average floating at 10°C (50°F) [14].

Interior regions of the Arctic have a harsher climate, distinguished by less snow in the winter and summer days. Frigid temperatures remain well below freezing and in some regions of Siberia the temperatures in January are lower than -40°C (-40°F). Long, continuous days of sunshine in summer months, can bring average temperatures above 10°C (50°F), and some weather stations reach highs of more than 30°C (86°F) during heatwaves [14].

With such climate the Arctic is hence to distinctive, atypical environmental features, including snow, ice, sea ice (and its permafrost), and glaciers. Sea ice has retreating at rapid pace, reaching its lowest extent every September, revealing a sea floor that stores approximately 13% of the world's oil reserves, 30% of undiscovered seas, and substantial deposits of valuable metals such as palladium, nickel, iron ore, and many others [15]. From 1953-2006, the extent of Arctic ice has its minimum, declined at a rate of -7.8% per decade between 1953-2006. Observations from modern satellite data (1976-2006) show an even sharper decline of 9.1% per decade [16]. For the maximum extent are also increasing, but at a later rate of 1.8% per decade, and 2.9% per decade over the aforementioned time periods.

The loss of ice cap that covers the top of the world has disrupted global climate systems. Sea ice acts as an air conditioning system that has helped and stabilize our climate system for thousands of years; yet the changes initiated by our continuous emissions of billions of tons of CO2 are integrating feedback loops that may overtake them the driver of climate change [17]. Melting sea ice allows the ocean to absorb more heat, which previously would have been reflected by the ice. This reduction in the region's albedo (the amount of solar radiation) caused the ocean to warm, further increasing the rate at which the ice melts and perpetuating and enhancing the feedback loop.
