## 4.5 Reliability Engineering

In an ideal world, systems, components, and their structures would be designed perfectly, where they remain operational and achieve their predetermined missions during their time in it. However, systems, their components and structures don't exist in a perfect world; such a deterministic view of an engineered system is impractical, unrealistic and infeasible. If knowledge were not a limiting factor, the cost of development, testing, materials and analysis may far exceed the economic prospects for such a system facilitate the use of “less-than-perfect” designs, instead of trying to remove all possibilities of failure within a system or its parts.

Minimizing the occurrence and recurrence of failures within systems demands an understanding of why failures might not occur in the first place.

First understanding failure mechanisms, we can then improve the systems to reduce the impact and occurrence of them. However, an obvious problem is that the processes and interactions by which a designed system fails are not always understood. The prediction of failures involves uncertainties, hence answering these questions is an inherently probabilistic issue in which threats can be described.

Reliability analysis, whether using historical occurrences or contemplating the “physics of failure,” is dependent on these processes, and such is reliant on the strength of the evidence and knowledge about the failure events and processes that determine the confidence about reliability of the design. For systems where this information can be gathered, for application in the Arctic is unlikely to be fully effective. As a technique for understanding risk it is useful, allowing for continual improvement and a better understanding of how systems operates is minimized. In the scenarios posed by the Arctic, failures need to be well understood and in order to reduce severe consequences and limit loss of not only monetary value but also human lives.

## 4.6 The Precautionary Principle

There is a social responsibility to protect the public from exposure to harm. Ultimately, decision makers must tackle risks from one of two epistemic perspectives; a risk assessment perspective, or a precautionary perspective. These two attitudes can be used to evaluate whether we should move forward with utilizing any technology: the first is ‘innocent until proven guilty’ and the other is ‘guilty until proven innocent’. Starting with the statement “it’s safe,” risk assessment works falsificationism when the one is on regulatory bodies and the public to produce evidence that suggests a technology or activity is unsafe; contrary the precautionary principle implies verification, when controls and protections should be in place and only relaxed once further findings can provide sound evidence that no harm will result from them (Carolan, 2007).

Criticisms of the precautionary principle, are not scarce; applications of a strongly formulated precautionary principle can be utilized to continually block innovation that may be associated with it. Advantages brought about may be banned because of the principle for the potential for negative impacts, leaving the benefits undesired and unfairly outweighed (Morris and Spillane, 2008). Moreover, in developing countries this principle has been especially scrutinized, as developed countries are accused of hypocrisy as they tell others cannot progress because of adverse effects, despite ignoring them in their own development.

Furthermore, the precautionary principle also gives to the precautionary dilemma. The wider versions of a precautionary principle are “uncontroversial” in principle and necessary in practice only to combat public confusion or the self-interested claims of groups demanding unambiguous evidence of harm, which no national society requires” (Sunstein, 2005). Yet, in a stronger sense the principle seems to rule out all courses of action. It’s to be protective and instead works to be paralyzing (Sunstein, 2005).

## 5 Summary of Current Evaluation

This working group’s dialogue during the meetings was initially directed by a set of questions. These included:

- What do ‘Arctic resilience’ mean?
- Can resilience be predicted?
- How is it validated?
- What are cyber systems and other infrastructure?

Uncertain, unforeseeable, and unknown hazards have been shown in this chapter to be inherent characteristics of the Arctic region, and so it's clear that there is need to couple risk analysis with strategies of enhancing resilience.
