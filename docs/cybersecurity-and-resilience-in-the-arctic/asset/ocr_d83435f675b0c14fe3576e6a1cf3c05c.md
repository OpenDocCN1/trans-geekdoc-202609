This includes the network of individuals making decisions and authorizing actions within a single company, from executives to line managers, but also those making decisions for that company's suppliers, shippers, et cetera. The C2 describes the informational flows the allow decision making and move decisions from management to actions.

Overall, a system of networks, rather than just one network, is necessary to model a supply chain. The supply chain's resilience can be assessed as a function of multiple interdependent networks, as shown in Figure 1. For example, transportation networks are generally run separately from the supply chain, with companies using public roads, rivers, and rail networks, maintained and used by actors outside the supply chain with their own, competing, interests. The organization controlling the supply chain may have limited influence on how the transportation network, and its components, respond to disruptive events, limiting the supply chain's ability to recover. Similar network science methods have previously been used to model operational networks for similar multi-network systems and to calculate their resilience more common in military network analysis with non-linear relationships between system parameters and resilience, and are particularly more common in military network analysis analysis (Ganin et al. 2016). Unfortunately, as discussed in the Results and Discussion section, most of the recent literature fails to consider the above methods and findings in examining supply chain resilience. Particularly critical is the systems-view of supply chains, i.e., that the supply chain consists of a system of networks.
