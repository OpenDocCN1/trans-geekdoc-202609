For simplicity, we include robustness to be a part of the resilience discussion. This is similar to how the Arctic Resilience Report (2013) defines resilience: “capacity to cope with disturbances and recover in a way that they maintain their core function and identity. It also relates to the capacity to learn and adapt to changing conditions, and when necessary” [3,4]. In this chapter, there will be a much greater emphasis placed on resilience in a socio-technical context (as opposed to a ecological perspective). However, note that resilience should not exclude ecological aspects, as these aspects may very well affect technical systems that we wish to protect.

Threats can relate to potential attacks by some malicious intent (threat actor), accidents (incidents that are not caused by malicious intent), natural forces (hazards outside human control) and finally, to wear and tear (maintenance needs that may result in an incident if not addressed at appropriate times). Common most research in resilience is that the resilient system needs to be a service-like, dynamic effort that needs to be aware of contexts, including dependency relationships and furthermore, a system that is not highly resilient is more vulnerable to threats. Harm is the detrimental effect on data and systems as a consequence of attack. Assessing and improving resilience is to limit potential harm during mission processes. This is especially the case for complex and diverse organisations where all assets may not be known, processes and vital knowledge, including workarounds, are undocumented. While there has been a significant amount of effort (related to resilience research) in the areas of standards documentation, business continuity and disaster management, many organisations are still ill-equipped and do not have the resources to combat and mitigate resilience concerns. This may be attributed to the fact that resilience research today has been investigated in many related and unrelated disciplines. Resilience is a multidisciplinary concept, irrespective of organisation discipline, any system will still be vulnerable to from threats. A large body of existing research, from a business continuity and cybersecurity perspective, remains too theoretical to be used in practice. This is because resilience would consume too many resources to be adopted straightforward.

Improving resilience is a matter of identifying, knowing and managing systems with respect to known threats and the risks they pose. It is necessary to investigate properties and functional relationships of assets, controls, configurations, vulnerabilities, incidents, services, risks, external dependencies, training and situation awareness [10], in order to “plan and prepare for”, “absorb”, “recover from” and “adapt” resilience events [41,42]. Assessing threats to systems is challenging, and improving resilience becomes important moving forward to limit potential harm during mission operations and exercises. Conducting such assessments is rarely a straightforward task, especially for organisations that are active in the Arctic. Thorrison et al.’ chapter on “Cyber Security Challenges to Arctic Critical Infrastructures” in this book provides an overview on the discussions and lessons learned from a working group at a NATO Advanced Research Workshop on Governance for Cyber Security and Resilience in the Arctic as it pertains to critical infrastructure. In this chapter, I present how analytics can support decision-making w.r.t. resilience concerns.
