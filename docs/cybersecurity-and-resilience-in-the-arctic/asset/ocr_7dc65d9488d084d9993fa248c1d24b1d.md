# 1.4.1. Land-Based Infrastructure

Road networks in the Arctic are sparse; being restricted to populated areas, Arctic
roads serve local rather than inter-regional needs. Outside northern Scandinavia, good
roads are increasingly uncommon. Unlike parts of the world, Arctic land is
'travel-friendly' in winter, when the tundra is frozen and ice bridges serve
thoroughfares for vehicles from sleds to heavy industrial equipment. In summer,
when the top layer of permafrost thaws and the terrain is buggy, overland travel is
diff.

Canadian 109, more commonly known as the Inuvik–Tukttoytuk
Highway (or ITH), is a new road link between Inuvik and Tuktoytuk in Canada's
Northwest Territories. It is the "first all-weather road to Canada's Arctic Coast".
Officially opened in November 2017, Tuktoytuk was finally opened up to traffic all
year-round. Initiatives like this in the Arctic are rare, with increasing activity
by commercial and military sectors, may become more common and necessary.

# 1.4.1.1. Airports

Many airports populate the Arctic; however, few are large enough to land large
commercial airlines and are mostly intended for smaller aviation activities.
Kangerlussaa in Greenland is one of the only civilian airports in the Arctic to receive
large airlines, as well as Longyear in Svalbard, and Sabet Airport in Russia as of 2015.
Ports of entry are places where one could legally enter the foreign country.
There are 676 airports currently maintained around the Arctic and majority of them are
situated in Alaska. There are 79 airports in Arctic Canada, 71 in Russia, 62 in Denmark
(Greenland and Faroe Islands), 56 in Norwegian Arctic including Svalbard, 55 in
Finland, 35 in Sweden and close to 20 in Iceland. Not all of them exist as ports of entries
for international traffic as many are only domestically [35].
