# 438 H. Birge et al. / An Information-Based Resilience Framework for the Changing Arctic

The first challenge to use large, diverse data sets is that researchers are usually unaware of all potential source of data and information. This can be especially challenging when research span multiple disciplines, as is the case with resilience in the Far North. Often, data sources in one discipline may not be known to another. We recommend investigators identify all relevant sources of data and information, identify gaps in data and information, and finally decide ways to fill those gaps, including through additional explicit elicitation of information and knowledge from Indigenous Peoples and other local communities. We define gaps as datasets or auxiliary information about a particular source of data. Gaps in data and information streams are a common problem when forecasting social-ecological outcomes. For example, when examining human diseases caused by zoonoses, we rarely have data on wildlife as the vector. Fortunately, it takes a relatively small piece of auxiliary information to combine datasets to generate useful knowledge (e.g., [38]). Often, expert information from a diverse set of collaborators can satisfactorily fill gaps and reduce uncertainty when sources of data are difficult, infeasible, or impossible to gather [39].

This approach is well-suited to complex systems as it allows feedbacks and learning from all sources. For example, cover data is usually used to predict animal location. If a hunter collects data on moose location she can include cover data in a model to predict the presence or absence of moose over her hunting grounds. The predictions of land used in raster layers are based on, but once the model and raster layer is made, they are considered fixed (i.e., cannot be iteratively changed). Once the moose hunter finds a model that predicts the presence or absence of moose to the data, the process is complete. By contrast, integration models "connect" models from both sources. For example, knowing that a moose was spotted in a location provides information about us to learn and predict more accurately through a one-way estimation process, especially as drivers of change, including climate change, fundamentally alters systems. Another advantage of data integration is that it enables iterative learning processes from different subsystems that would otherwise be impossible to measure (see Chapter 25 of [9]).
