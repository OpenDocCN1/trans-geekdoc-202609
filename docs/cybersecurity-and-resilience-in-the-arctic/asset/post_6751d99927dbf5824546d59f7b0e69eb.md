## 4.2. Communication

Clearly, the processes of collective-making and trust building all depend upon the voice of communication. Letter is therefore essential to present of development of creative community solutions in the face of change, which in turn lies at the core of resilience. Opposing theories present two viewpoints about what constitute collective agency in order to achieve shared meaning, and each offers perspective into what drives a collective action. The first (Smirich & Morgan, 1982; Smirich, 1983) proposes that organizations are representative of systems of shared making, whereby action is precipitated by a set of common meanings or interpretations related to a joint experience. In this case, individual and personal agency is critical to forming a collaborative system now enabled to combine skills and strategies towards a common goal. The second (Weick, 1969, 1979), argues that organizations operate predominantly on a process of exchange (e.g., expected output for pay), meaning is only derived from retrospectively interpreting an organized action in order to make sense of the collaborative engagement, which minimizes the individual agency aspect of developing shared meaning. In either case, communication plays a critical role in the relationship between meaning and action.

Focusing on the precept that organized action is indicative of shared meaning—dependent upon "sense-making communication" between individual members of the group (Donnell, et al., 1986, p. 45)—is also likely that, while agreeing and taking a single action, individuals may not have interpreted the experience in the same way. This can be explained by equivalence, a theory systems which account for the potential to arrive at a single end by of multiple paths (Beer, 1959). While disagreements exist related to the proposed actions, communication mechanisms help foster a level of sense-making that will enable numerous action: metaphor, logical argument, affect, and linguistic induction (Donnellon, et al.).

Metaphors are useful in creating a conflict-free way for individuals to view as something else; or to see it through a different lens, in order to create notions. Through metaphor, members of a group can understand another's motives and apprehensions related to a proposed action “shifting” from retrospective to sense-making” (Donnellon, et al., 1996, p. 48). Metaphors can reconcile discrepancies between interpretations, in order to reach equal support for a particular sense in context. *Logical argument* is a method by which members can incrementally broaden by offering a premise, an inference, and a conclusion within the context of the SESS, which encourages others to adopt a particular notion by appealing to the emotions to induce or alter the sense (Heise, 1977; Irvine, 1982), while *linguistic induction* helps suppress through non-confrontational, ambiguous language and behavior which encourages equitable standing between members (e.g. Barnard, 1938; Fowler & Kress, 1979; Pfeifer, 1981).

The relationship between individual adaptive actions and community resilience was the case of the study conducted in Northern Ghana, to determine whether adaptive actions in farmers led to collective agency and enhanced resilience capabilities within their communities (Otsuki, et al., 2017). The study confirmed the assertion that communication is essential component driving organized action based on a shared meaning of some kind, enhancing collective agency (Donnellon, 1996). The study also illustrated the universally important importance of the community milieu *place*.
