It is becoming increasingly clear that the abilities to avoid and detect cyber-attacks (cyber-security) and to react once cyber-attacks have been detected (cyber-resilience) have become critical for the success of smart shipping operations. IoT enabled cyber-physical systems are regularly threatened by a broad range of potential cyber-attacks from criminals, terrorists, or hacktists (He et al., 2016). Cyber-attacks use any available channel to exploit poorly secured systems for different purposes, giving way to harass, compromise espionage, extortion, stock market manipulation, the planning and carrying out of terrorist activities. In parallel, there is the risk that failing to ensure continuity of IT systems may causes loss of assurance for the enterprise (D. J. Bodeau, Graubert, & Fabian-Greene, 2010). Rand has estimated that the global cost of cyber-crime can range from $799 billion to over $2 trillion (Dreyer et al., 2018), and examples include not only disruption of industrial operations, but also lives at risk.
The modern shipping industry faces cyber-risks associated with its own data and control systems, and also to its supply chains (He et al., 2016), processes connected both with suppliers and customers through the internet part of a shared network. As a result, cyber-attacks' access and impact factors share a common network, gaining access to IT systems through the weakest link in the supply network (He et al., 2016, Khan & Sepveda Estrada, 2015). Considering their effects and the urgency of a problem that is continuing to unfold, it is essential to ask how risks derived from the use of IT systems should be managed in shipping operations to increase detection and reaction to cyber-attacks.
