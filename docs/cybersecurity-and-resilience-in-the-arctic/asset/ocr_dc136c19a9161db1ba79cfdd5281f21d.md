The macro system described here by Goerger et al. would be prepared for disruptions and attacks and better equipped to absorb them and recover from them through the use of swarm intelligence, in which multiple independent sensors interact with each other in order to form a robust global intelligence with greater insight into the data collected than the sum of the parts. In this case, the global intelligence, i.e. the macro system, could provide robust and more accurately information regarding the environmental hazards a vessel may face.

Additional research into enhancing the resilience of navigation systems operating in the Arctic is needed. In particular, research focus on the later stages of resilience -- how systems can recover from environmental disruptions, based on knowledge gained from the disruption, and degrade gracefully. The USN could fund and incorporate the results of such research.

A few (somehow) resilient systems are already in operation, as the Amphibious Bulk Liquid Transfer System (ABLTS), a system of buoyant boats which enables transfer of fuel and water to the coast [174]. By ABLTS, in case of a natural disaster or other kind of disruption, the USN could ensure critical water/or fuel to need coastal communities, whether towns and cities or infrastructure, such as refueling and repair systems [175]. Even so, there are some weaknesses of the ABLTS which degrade its resilience; in particular, that the hoses float exposure to attacks and accidental collisions causes them to break down quickly by weather conditions and UV [176].

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers has studied the resilience of coastal systems, mounting the North Atlantic Comprehensive Study (2015) to specifically examine ways to increase the resilience of local communities in coastal areas affected by Hurricane Sandy [177], [179]. The approach recommended by the Study, while of course tailored to needs of this particular region, could have benefits for Alaskan coastal communities as well. Recommendations including these three elements holistically:

> ...coastal water resource, or engineering features and activities; ecosystem features, both occurring and constructed; and community aspects including the social and economic as-sets, and critical facilities of coastal systems. [178]

The report is highly detailed and extensive ([179]); a similar specifically targeted to the needs of the Arctic U.S. communities could use.
