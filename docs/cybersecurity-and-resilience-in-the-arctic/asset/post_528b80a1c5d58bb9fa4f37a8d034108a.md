- Tool-Development Objectives. Develop tools that help predict and respond to resilience concerns in the most effective and efficient way. We need to document and continually improve decision-makers' own understanding of their systems' anatomy so they may measure, assess, improve and communicate proactive and reactive resilience for real situations, in real-time. This might be achieved through development of annotation and visual analytics tools to predict system failures (connected to a back-end system that monitors resilience KPIs).

- Conduct experiments to generate data. Academics often rely on simulations or data that may be realistic, the closer the data is to reflect the real world, the better this will be for mitigating actual incidents. There is also noise in real-world data that is simply not present in simulation – which ought to be captured from live exercises.

- Push for proof-of-concepts, new technologies and their integration. Presently, not enough empirical datasets. Many systems are not agile enough to adapt to the evolving resilience landscape. Being agile can come at two costs: (1) having a low tolerance for legacy systems, and (2) continually trying and integrating new technologies into production environments. Specifically:

- Low-tolerance for legacy systems. A resilient system is one that has redundancy built in and moves to only work with latest technologies. Legacy systems ought to be phased out after upgrades, developing strategies to straightforward migrate data and systems for continual review and improving their infrastructure in order to be able to cope with the idea of systems becoming deprecated.

- Always evolve. Continually trying and integrating new technologies may seem like a bad idea at face value when the system runs the risk of systems failing over or runs the risk of security violations. However, increasing organizational agility by being able to adopt new technologies into production environments fast, and not relying on any one piece of technology at any given time, is likely to be a better solution.

- Increased Distribution and Autonomy. Autonomy in analytics and decision making systems is important, but perhaps not well understood. Increased autonomy of machines based on automated analytics limits human error, but may also increase the digital attack surface.

- Invest in institutional memory. Documentation of systems is helpful, but documentation alone may not be enough. Proposing ways to improve institutional memory may improve ability to perform analytics. This means that no asset (machine or person) becomes essential to a mission, but instead distribute rules and allow for redundancy to cope with a variety of operational stakeholders.

- Make documentation part of job – documentation logging allows for useful information in the event of a crisis (information where it might not have been).

- Multi-stakeholder engagement. Happa and Fairclough [29] discuss the idea having many stakeholders with different perspectives of the same situation to sit down and discuss it in line with a common understanding about an incident. This is in line with the Arctic council’s recommendations: “Different stakeholders will have different perspectives to bring to the table”

**Education and Awareness.** Raising education and awareness of possible resilience issues may appear as a futile effort, but it is important to limit the risks of threats.

**Increase paper-based (or any non-computer) redundancy solutions.** The UK Department of Health and Social Care System and National Health Service suggest that “Paper processes need to exist and be implemented in the event that the incident management system is impacted by a cyber attack” [51]. One of the lessons learned from their handling of the Wannacry ransomware incident. Happa et al. [30] discuss how pragmatic-failure assessments can facilitate fast and effective decision-making during cyber-attacks. The model helps analyze relevant questions at the right time, irrespective of availability of data. Using abstraction-based reasoning enables risk-owners to achieve “some” situational awareness. The model is intended for a paper-based state of affairs.

**Stronger collaboration between military, transportation, companies, government and academia** is necessary to bring expertise to tackle the wider issues and come to agreement about how analytics can be applied.

This chapter provides a discussion on analytics and decision-making challenges for improving resilience in the arctic, and how these might be addressed by current research in the academic space. It outlined key areas for resilience management, challenges and recommendations for moving forward.
