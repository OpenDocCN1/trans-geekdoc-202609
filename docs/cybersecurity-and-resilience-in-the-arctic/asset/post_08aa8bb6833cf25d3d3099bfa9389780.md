### Arctic Infrastructure
The melting of Arctic ice presents both new opportunities for growth and new challenges to overcome as Arctic expands into this largely unexplored territory. The private sector, shipping and tourism efforts benefit greatly from the Northwest Passage through Canada and the Northeast Passage and Northern Sea Route. The thaw exposes oil and natural gas fields that could house up to a quarter of the Earth's obtainable petroleum, offering an incentive for oil and natural gas companies to move north. In the public sector, military transportation, defense, and search and rescue operations will need to keep pace with private sector growth. As these interest groups, both civil and military, seek to operate in the Arctic, they bring with them the need for expanded Arctic infrastructure, both temporary and permanent.

This additional infrastructure brings with it the potential for larger disasters due to cyber disruption. For example, the Kolos Data Center was built in Ballangen, Norway plains to take advantage of hydroelectric power and use the cold climate to cool its servers. The large amount of data stored makes it a prime target for hacking or phishing attack, and damage to the facility, whether cyber or physical, will be difficult to repair given the facility's remote location. Even temporary infrastructure could pose risks; consider the voyage of the American *Crystal Serenity*, which in 2016 was successful, the potentially disastrous result of ship's onboard computer being hacked, including loss of life of the passengers and spilling of oil and other materials into Arctic waters.

The traditional security response to an attack by an outside enemy is to "harden" the system, in order to prevent the enemy from penetrating it. However, this approach to cyber security is generally ineffective in the long run; more resilience is necessary, the ability for a system to recover from disruption or failure. ## Analytical Methods and Tools

In the modern era, all missions survive on data. A correct knowledge of environmental conditions, incoming threats, and available resources is critical to any mission. In the case of Arctic operations, where an error as innocuous as overestimating the outdoor temperature could mean the difference between life and death, preserving the integrity of critical data is especially vital. An instrument could help, a breaker could miss an incoming attack, or an adversary breach security and compromise the reliability of measuring systems. For these situations, having a data collection system in place that is both hard — able to resist attacks and resilient — able to recover from partial or total failure — is essential.

Also considering how to tell whether an Arctic operation is sufficiently resilient in these types of disaster scenarios before the operation begins. With improved modeling and resilience standards, Arctic missions could be assessed during their planning phases to account for these conditions. Additionally, as Arctic conditions become increasingly favorable to commercial activity, resilience standards could play a significant role in determining the Arctic economic environment. Unfortunately to date, there are no international standards for Arctic resilience. This lack of standards does more than complicate modeling; it leaves Arctic facilities with no guidelines for what data they need to collect to maintain operation and no way to validate that they are operating safely.

## Social Inquiry and Governance

The cyber infrastructure required to support Arctic physical infrastructure creates new questions surrounding its governance, maintenance, and effects on local peoples and environment. For example, laying undersea fiber-optic cable is widely considered appropriate for bringing internet connections to Arctic North America; one company has expressed its desire to run cable under the Pacific Ocean to Japan and under the Arctic Ocean to the UK. As such a task has never been undertaken, many questions surrounding it remains unaffected. What effects does laying the cable have on the environment, particularly given jurisdictional and national security concerns for Arctic and sub-Arctic nations? What are the implications if cable is laid when nations are at peace but peace shatters later? Who assesses and whether telecommunication can be laid under international waters? Both the construction and disruption of new Arctic infrastructure could have long-lasting effects on the environment and global policy, potentially conflicting policies and objectives by the various nations engaged within Arctic development.

Another facet to the Arctic social challenge is the known psychological toll that Arctic-like conditions take on cognitive resilience and decision making. The eight nations — Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Russia, Sweden, and the U.S. — have formed an inter-governmental high-level forum, the Arctic Council, in partnership with native groups and other observers to manage Arctic affairs. Relations have been well and peacefully so far, these eight nations have differing priorities, and care must be taken to ensure diplomacy remains intact as Arctic environment changes. International discourse is all important as the Arctic Council currently lacks a clear mandate to address security issues.

## Seeking 21st Century Solutions for 21st Century Problems

This book presents a diversity of perspectives and opinions inspired by the January 2019 NATO SPS ARW, as well as chapters from invited experts on differing topics. Grouped roughly into the categories above, these seek to fully explore the extent of cyber-development concerns within the Arctic, and/or offer possible ideas or solutions to begin to mitigate harmful consequences cyber disruptions may pose. Prior to this, Hall et al offer a framing overview of what the Arctic is, what its emerging risk and governance challenges are (with particular emphasis on cybersecurity), and what factors exacerbate or mitigate the highly uncertain threats facing actors.

Thorisson provides a thorough overview of infrastructure resilience challenges and concerns, including the characterization of cybersecurity threats within the Arctic region as well as necessary steps actors in the public and private sectors should consider to reduce their exposure and/or consequences to such threats. Their perspectives are bolstered by Humholm, Paulson, Kirchner, and Mersky et al. Bairdi and Wandji et al. respectively provide an analysis of infrastructural and mission-oriented challenges associated with shipping and naval operations in Arctic latitudes, and argue that resilience-based approaches are a necessity to (a) ensure mission continuity in the face of multiple uncertain threats, and (b) to adapt to changes in increasingly accessible Arctic waters. Humholm provides an exhaustive overview of cyber-infrastructure threats within Arctic lands and waters, while Paulson notes the unique cyber-infrastructure management and governance concerns facing Iceland and its risks and implications of undersea cables within the Arctic and sub-Arctic, where the large and growing network of such cables allows digital systems to operate with minimal disruption.
