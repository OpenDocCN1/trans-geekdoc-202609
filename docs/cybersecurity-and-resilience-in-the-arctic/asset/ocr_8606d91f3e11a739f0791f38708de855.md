The lack of prioritization of Arctic cyber security is at least attributable to a poor understanding of cybersecurity by stakeholders. Additionally, the Arctic has a multitude of actors at different levels, which may leave authority and responsibility around cyber security unclear.[156] Agreement and cooperation do not always come easily, especially in times of rapid and uncertain transformation, such as occurring in the modern Arctic. Trust is paramount, especially when trying to establish consensus around a policy that many countries view as critical to their national security. The relevant actors. Using a trust mechanism can provide an avenue for compliance and consensus (see Table 2). Additionally, better data are needed to track and assess effectiveness of solutions, to make informed policy decisions, and to implement meaningful standards and guidelines. Cyber security is not the same at every scale of governance. The type of cyber security depends on the level of a sub-system. Shared cyber principles are necessary in order to ensure safety around risks. Integrated reporting, information, and certificate programs are a few ways of ensuring that baseline standards are met.[100] Agencies around the world have proposed various standards and frameworks for cybersecurity. NIST has proposed a framework for critical infrastructure in response to United Executive Order 13636 [157] Microsoft has provided six norms that the public and private sectors could adopt to minimize conflict and ensure cyber security (see Box 3).[158] However, countries have yet to agree upon international standards that apply to the Arctic and are willing to share cybersecurity specifics with others. The process of establishing governance, frameworks, and norms for cybersecurity in the Arctic will not be a linear process. This initiative will require compromise and cooperation—from individuals to organizations and across the public and private sectors. Which falls within the scope of the Arctic Council is unclear.
