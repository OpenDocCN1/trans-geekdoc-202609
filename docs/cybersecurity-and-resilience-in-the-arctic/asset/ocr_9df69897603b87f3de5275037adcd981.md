# 14 J. Hall et al. / Framing the Arctic Part I: Actors, Communities, and the Environment

Beginning with the physical environment, the often-freezing temperatures are what sets the region apart from anywhere else on Earth. Like all places, weather in the Arctic varies day to day, month to month, and from place to place. The factors that influence the weather can be viewed as more stable; indeed perhaps most stable is the amount of sunlight that the Arctic is exposed to. Due to the tilt of the Earth on its axis, the summer months see an Arctic where the sun does not completely set. In contrast, the winter sees an Arctic where the sun does not fully rise, leaving the darkness for months on. This seasonal variation in sunlight produces large variations in temperatures across different months in the circumpolar north.

Figure 2 [13] shows seasonal patterns for near-surface air temperatures (in °C) relative to a baseline period from 1981-2010. Furthermore, Figure 3 [13] also shows temperature changes on a larger scale. It depicts the mean annual surface air temperature (SAT) anomaly for October-September for lands north of 60° N, showing that 2017-2018 was the second highest value in since dating back to 1900 (+1.7°C). It illustrates that the Arctic is warming at more than twice the rate of the rest of the world, with the measurements from the previous five years exceeding all records.
