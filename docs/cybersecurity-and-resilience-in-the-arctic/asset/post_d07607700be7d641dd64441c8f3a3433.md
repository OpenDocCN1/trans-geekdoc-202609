It is becoming increasingly clear that the abilities to avoid and detect cyber-attacks (cyber-security) and to react once cyber-attacks have been detected (cyber-resilience) have become critical for the success of smart shipping operations. IoT-enabled cyber-physical systems are regularly threatened by a broad range of potential cyber-attacks from criminals, terrorists, or hacktists (He et al., 2016). Cyber-attacks use any available channel to exploit poorly secured systems for different purposes, giving way to harass, compromise espionage, extortion, stock market manipulation, the planning and carrying out of terrorist activities. In parallel, there is the risk that failing to ensure continuity of IT systems may cause loss of assurance for the enterprise (D. J. Bodeau, Graubert, & Fabian-Greene, 2010). Rand has estimated that the global cost of cyber-crime can range from $799 billion to over $2 trillion (Dreyer et al., 2018), and examples include not only disruption of industrial operations, but also lives at risk.

The modern shipping industry faces cyber-risks associated with its own data and control systems, and also to its supply chains (He et al., 2016), processes connected both with suppliers and customers through the internet part of a shared network. As a result, cyber-attacks' access and impact factors share a common network, gaining access to IT systems through the weakest link in the supply network (He et al., 2016, Khan & Sepveda Estrada, 2015). Considering their effects and the urgency of a problem that is continuing to unfold, it is essential to ask how risks derived from the use of IT systems should be managed in shipping operations to increase detection and reaction to cyber-attacks.

## 2. A framework to understand resilience

An answer to this question was posed by our team (Guerra & Sepulveda Estay, 2018) in 2018. We performed a structured literature review (SLR), a documented process to collect and store existing knowledge to answer the question of How cyber-risks are managed along the supply chain? Their work initially considered supply chains as the unit of analysis, and here the framework is to reflect its application to the shipping industry.

The twelve most recurrent themes found in the analysis (Guerra & Sepulveda Estay, 2018) when applied to shipping operations, are the following:

- 1. **Compliance**, understood as the identification of, and conformance of shipping operations, to legislation affecting cyber risks and to the standards that have been developed for cyber-risk prevention, mitigation, and response.
- 2. **Situational Awareness**, which involves the identification of cyber threats, vulnerabilities, and risks in shipping operations, and the assessment of the probability and impacts of occurrences of cyber-risk events.
- 3. **Governance**, which defines who, where, and how decisions affecting IT are made. (Ali, Mahfouz & Arisha, 2017).
- 4. **Pre-Event Knowledge Management**, understood as making the best use of the knowledge available in order to achieve resilience (response to disruptions) and the improvement of this knowledge, through practices such as training, and the creation of a resilience/risk management centre.
- 5. **Cyber-Security**, which refers to the protection of the assets and systems (physical or digital) in the storing and processing of information in digital format, and involving proactive measures and techniques, focusing on the protection of the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information (Boyes, 2015).
- 6. **Agility**, defined as "the ability to respond to unpredictable changes in demand or supply" (Petit, Croxton & Fiksel, 2013), through visibility and velocity. Visibility refers to awareness about the current status of operational assets and environment (Patnayakuni & Patnayakuni, 2014; Christopher & Peck, 2004), as to be able to detect cyber-risk events.
- 7. **Velocity** refers to the speed of the reaction when cyber-events happen (Petit et al., 2013).
- 8. **Adapt**, understood as the ability to manage critical resources and shipping operations, and to adjust them to challenges and opportunities (Patnayakuni & Patnayakuni, 2014).
- 9. **Recovery Management**, which considers identifying vulnerabilities and executing contingency plans.
- 10. **Market Position and Financial Strength**, which refers to the status of an organization regarding market share, liquidity, and portfolio diversification.
- 11. **Post-Event Knowledge Management**, understood as the ability of learning from past events through post-event feedback, education, and gathering of cost/benefit knowledge.
- 12. **Social Capital**, which reflects the network of extending relationships with suppliers and customers.
