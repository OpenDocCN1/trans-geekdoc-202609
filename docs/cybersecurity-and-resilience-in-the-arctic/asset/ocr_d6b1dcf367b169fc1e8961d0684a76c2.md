# 2.3.1.1. Why China Wants In

China is attracted to the Arctic for three main reasons: its potential for new shipping
routes, its natural resources, and its scientific potential. China is committed to building a
"Polar Silk Road" through the Arctic, which will entail developing Arctic shipping
routes. China is also interested in the oil, gas, energy, and resources of the Arctic,
as well as its fishes. Finally, China is interested in the Arctic as a platform for valuable
scientific research on climate change, which will likely affect China’s significant coastal
population [105]. China also intends to continue to fund and build infrastructure,
esially for tourism [103]. This may be a means to gain geopolitical influence and
power in the region.

# 2.3.1.2. Shipping Routes

China sees the Northern Sea Route (NSR) as a significant element of its long-term
economic strategy. In fact, while there are doubts the potential of the NSR
a legitimate alternative to pre-existing major shipping routes, China seems to believe the
NSR will be viable for commercial transit sooner later than others. Beijing is
prioritizing investment in opening the NSR for shipping [106]. The NSR would
down on shipping times from northern Chinese ports to northern Europe and to the
coast of the United States by at least 40% compared to the current standard routes,
which go through the Suez Canal or the Panama Canal. China has deemed the
NSR and other passages through the Arctic as the "golden route" [105].
Access to the NSR is vitally important to China. Beijing’s policy "directly encourages
Chinese companies to assign priority to the construction of infrastructure linked to the
Arctic sea routes and emphasises that China is ready to cooperate with anyone interested
in the development of the "Polar Silk Road" [106], [107].

# 2.3.1.2.1. Oil and Gas

The Arctic has readily offered up significant petroleum reserves through onshore areas
in Canada, Russia, and Alaska. In total more than 400 oil and gas fields have been
discovered in the Arctic, comprising around 240 billion barrels of oil and oil-equivalent
natural gas [108]. This constitutes nearly 20% of the world’s "known conventional
petroleum resources" [108]. However, there is much oil and natural gas still to be
discovered in the Arctic, mostly in offshore areas. A study by the U.S. General
Survey (2008) estimated that "90 billion barrels of oil, 1,669 cubic feet of natural
gas, and 44 billion barrels of natural gas may remain to be found in the Arctic"
[108].

These energy sources are of highly attractive to all Arctic nations, and other
nations as well. China, though, particularly interested in them. China is the largest
importer and consumer of energy in the world [105]. Moving forward, it will require
significant energy resources in order to maintain its rate of economic growth [105].
