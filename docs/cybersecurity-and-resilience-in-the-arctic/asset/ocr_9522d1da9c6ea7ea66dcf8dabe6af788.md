Including Indigenous Knowledge holders in the process as partners and not as participants is critical to the ethical and beneficial inclusion of Indigenous Knowledge in frameworks that also use other sources of information.

There are many ethical complications associated with engaging Indigenous. Many security and resilience researchers and analysts are non-native and have had little to experience any analysis that includes Indigenous Knowledge, including Elders. Trust is core to any analysis of research that includes Indigenous Knowledge, and can be lost if what is at risk, and there is no dialogue, if researchers alone find that there is no evidence of shared values. There are a number of research guides that circumpolar Indigenous Peoples have agreed to, including Principles for Conducting Research in the Arctic [12]; Ethics in Indigenous Research [13]; Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Humans [14]; Ownership, Access and Possession (OCAP) and Negotiating Relationships with Inuit Communities: A Guide for Researchers [16]. There are also discipline-specific guidelines, such as the American Association of Geographers’ (AAG) Indigenous Peoples Special Group’s Declaration of Key Questions about Research Ethics with Indigenous Communities [17]. Many Indigenous communities have their own specific guidelines of ethical collaboration. Many Indigenous communities have their own specific guidelines of ethical collaboration.

For instance, one of our couriers worked with the Sami people to co-develop an ethical guideline for Saami-specific research. The Sami people identified and answered the questions to build the guidelines: What values should our research? What strengths can we build upon? What are the critical issues in Sami research? How do we establish relationships and share goals? Any investigators interested in engaging with Indigenous Knowledge can explore these question and with Indigenous partners and other communities. Additional questions include: What makes research ethical and useful? What are your stories and What is still needed to bolster adaptation and resilience? Any ethical research guidelines should also include research timelines that are acceptable to the Indigenous peoples.

As investigators embark on the tiered approach, it is crucial to co-identify with Indigenous partners the variables and outputs of interest. Criteria selection and weight assessment is the most subjective and delicate portion of the tiered approach to decision support and fall within Tier. Tier 1 is less rigorous than other Tiers but better acknowledges that these activities are functional and required for the tiered approach to move forward. Under this research and from other themes that engage with Indigenous Knowledge should be framed as strength, rather than deficit, based. A strength-based approach emphasizes the positive capacity that exists and can be fostered within communities. Areas of risk can be used to direct investigations, but the emphasis should be amplifying capacity and strengths to overcome and mitigate risks. Notably, if it is not possible to co-identify variables of interest, establish research guidelines, and agree upon a research timeline, then knowledge from Indigenous partners and other communities may not be included in a tiered approach. In this scenario, investigators must acknowledge that the utility of tiered approach outcomes will be curtailed, and why.
