Understanding how governance play a role in strengthening societal resilience in the Arctic is an essential pillar to addressing the cybersecurity and resilience of Arctic critical infrastructure. Threats to Arctic societies cross international boundaries and require cooperation among various governance structures operating in the Arctic because decisions taken at the international level have complex ramifications at the societal and community level. This overlap requires coordinated engagement between stakeholders to align security and resilience priorities. The Arctic Council and the agreements negotiated under it exemplify the role of governmental and diplomatic efforts to coordinate negotiations among Arctic stakeholders, including Indigenous organizations, communities, and national governments to strengthen societal resilience.

## 3.3. Human and Societal Challenges

Human challenges related to security of critical infrastructure are manifold. Most often, the human being in cyber security is treated as either the best vulnerability in the system (due to his or her limited awareness and skills) or as a potential malicious actor (a terrorist, hacktivist, criminal, or state-sponsored wrongdoer). However, when moving the object of cyber security from critical infrastructure to human beings experiencing the consequences of potential malfunctions in the infrastructure, both threat imageries and means of producing security change (Salminen 2019). Threats are then defined in terms of human well-being in particular contexts and security measures (Salminen & Hossain 2018). This section presents a perspective to the relationship between critical infrastructure protection and human well-being in the Arctic by building on human and security rights.

What do humans, a system of interrelated, interdependent, and indivisible rights of all human beings, have to do with cyber security challenges to Arctic critical infrastructure? Understanding security is understanding that “security is a condition, and an indicator of the importance of other values” (Bourne 2014). The security of critical infrastructure is not for securing the infrastructure itself, but rather the human interests behind and increasingly dependent on the infrastructure. As an example, in national cyber security strategies, the interest behind critical infrastructure protection has been identified as the functioning of security, the functioning of economy, or as the maintenance of societal values. One way of looking at the human interests and the impact of cyber security of critical infrastructure on people, is by looking at the human rights and human security implications of the threats to critical infrastructure, as a cyber attack on critical infrastructure may significantly impact the livelihood and safety of citizens (Clark and Hakim 2017).

### 3.3.1. Human Rights and Human Security

Human rights are the rights that every being has by virtue of being born human, regardless of race, sex, ethnicity, language, religion, or any other status (UN 2019). It is the sum of individual and collective rights laid down in state constitutions and international law. Human rights are manifold, pertaining to all aspects of life. Everyone is entitled to these, without discrimination. Some characteristics of human rights are universality, indivisibility, interdependency, and equality. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) is often seen as the first authoritative elaboration of the term “human rights” (UN 1948; Inter-Parliamentary Union and UN 2016). The UDHR, together with the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) (UN 1966a) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) (UN 1966b), form the International Bill of Human Rights. Besides the International Bill of Human Rights, other human rights instruments, both legally binding and non-binding, have been adopted. Yet, the UDHR, ICCPR, and ICESCR are the only general human rights instruments of the UN.

Sovereign states are central in international law and in human rights states; states are considered the primary duty-bearers, having the obligation to respect, fulfill, and protect human rights. As becoming more dependent on increasingly interconnected information and communication technologies (ICTs), as well as delivering a range of services that individuals depend upon, human rights ought to be weighed when discussing the security of critical infrastructure.

The international community is taking human rights in cyberspace. The Human Rights Council recognize that all human rights apply online and that the internet can be an important tool for developing and exercising human rights (A/HR/20/L.13 §5). The Tallinn Manual 2.0 on the International Law Applicable to Cyber Operations recognizes the applicability of international human rights in the context of cyber-elements (Schmitt 2017).
