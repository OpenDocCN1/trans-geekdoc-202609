# 3 Institutions and tools for international governance in the Arctic

### 3.1. Arctic Council

The Arctic Council (AC) is a forum of eight circular Arctic states, five of which are Arctic ocean states. Established in 1996, the primary idea of the creation of the AC was to offer a framework for promoting cooperation, coordination, and interaction among the Arctic States, with the involvement of the Arctic Indigenous communities on common Arctic issues; in particular, issues of sustainable development and environmental protection in the Arctic. The AC has a broad mandate of addressing regional issues concerning environmental protection and sustainable development, while military security aspects have been seen as outside its scope. This does not mean that the Arctic Council does not deal with security issues at all. In practice, the definition of what constitutes “security” often exceeds the narrow confines of security as a military concern. Overtime, the definition of security has been expanded to embrace issues other than military concerns: human and environmental aspects shape the security agenda to tackle important policy issues in the sphere of environmental pollution causing diverse challenges for humans and communities in the region. The Arctic Council engages with many issues that are part of a broadly understood human security – in particular in the context of adaptation, environmental change impacts and resilience – even if the word “security” is usually avoided.

The Arctic Council is not an international organization in the classic sense of the term. Instead, the Arctic states define the AC as an intergovernmental cooperation body serving as “high-level forum” (Arctic Council, 1996). The Arctic Council, opposed to any other regulatory body, has an entity that produces, through its working groups, technical recommendations and guidelines and influential scientific assessments. It offers a confidence building mechanism through which countries have been able to continue and develop to Arctic governance that helps prevent tension. In the last decade, the Arctic Council has also been used to negotiate international Arctic-specific agreements (on maritime and aeronautical search, on oil response, and on scientific cooperation) as well as attempts to agree on commitments on pollution reduction (as regards black carbon and methane emissions). Through its work, both soft and hard, through the international treaties negotiated by the member states under its auspices, the Arctic Council has successfully attracted the attention of key policymakers to the matter of specific environmental and economic developments. As a result, it has member states and policymakers outside the area (Young & Kankaampää, 2012; Young, 2013). The Arctic Council plays an important role within the Arctic governance landscape through different levels of governance, others:

- providing information for international negotiations and processes (e.g. climate change, mercury, or persistent organic pollutants), affecting the definition of problems among Arctic national and sub-national administrations through assessments and recommendations, contributing to the integration of Arctic traditional knowledge into scientific and decision-making processes (Kiovoura et al. 2015).

Today, the AC has gradually emerged as a new source of authority where both regionalism and regionalization hold a strong place. Regionalism and regionalization do not necessarily run in parallel; while regionalism is about the promotion of integration of various sectors, as environmental protection, governance, and security, regionalization, on the other hand, is a result of cohesion of social and economic interaction among private enterprises and civil societies (Ingimdanson 2014: 186). Various non-regional actors acknowledge a source of authority. The EU, for example, has declared in its number of policy documents on the Arctic that the AC is the most important cooperation forum to deal with Arctic (European Union and High Representative 2016). Acknowledging such authority of the AC, a number of actors from the Arctic region, including non-Arctic states participate in the AC’s structure. Many today observe that the AC has exceeded expectations of being a body of cooperation for shaping policies (Young & Kankaampää, 2012), and that it now serves as a policy-making and well as policy-shaping body.

Because of the complexity and dynamic nature of the Arctic environment, balancing and integrating political, security, business, environmental, and indigenous interests has become more urgent. Consequently, the AC is becoming an influential regional body in the Arctic. Given the broad scope of the AC on a growing number of interests, which the current debate has focused on or not the Council would become a potential "site of struggle" to develop a new form of governance approach, where both various sectors as well as actors from the Arctic and beyond. Despite its limited mandate in terms of building legal norms, over the AC has acting as an effective governance body in the Arctic offering input to help solve Arctic problems.

### 3.2. International Maritime Organization (IMO)

The International Maritime Organization (IMO) was set up in 1948 as a specialized agency of the United Nations (until 1982, it was known as the Inter-Governmental Maritime Council Organization). By nature, the Arctic Council has the status of an international organization with the clear mandate to regulate shipping and shipping related issues. Today 174 states are part to the Organization. The regulatory framework of the IMO includes, among others, maritime safety and security, marine environmental concerns, and issues related to regulatory and technical cooperation. Given the rudimentary nature of rules embodied in the UNCLOS in terms of marine environmental governance, the IMO provides legal arrangements for the implementation of these rules. Over time, the IMO has adopted a number of instruments, including the 1972 Convention on the Prevention of Marine Pollution by Dumping of Wastes and Other Matter (London Convention), the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollutions from Ships (MARPOL), and the International Convention on Oil Pollution Preparedness, Response and Cooperation (OPRC). The Organization developed the legally binding Polar Code effective from the beginning of 2017, which addresses stricter regulatory measures for shipping operations in Polar waters. While the IMO is a globally relevant international organization, its significance in the Arctic is particularly crucial given most IMO adopted regulations apply to the Arctic Ocean. The Polar Code, for instance, an Arctic-specific agenda.

### 3.3. Other regulatory institutions and arrangements

There are several other regulatory institutions in place which provide specific sectoral legal arrangements. Concerning fisheries, in addition to the general applicability of the US Fish Stock Agreement, there are a few bilateral or regional arrangements under the scope of a number of Regional Fisheries Management Organizations (RFMOS). Many of these organizations cover the Arctic Ocean. The Banana Hole and Loophole are, located in the area of the North East Atlantic Fisheries Commission (NAAFC). The OSPAR monitors the implementation of the Convention for the Protection of the Marine Environment of the North-East Atlantic (OSPAR Convention), which also covers a part of the Arctic Ocean. Its Annex V, in particular, provides a comprehensive legal framework for the implementation of Part II of the UNCLOS in relation to the protection and preservation of the marine environment. A small part of the Arctic Ocean high sea (the Loop Hole) falls within the coverage of the OSPAR Convention for the purpose of the protection of marine and coastal biodiversity at the regional level.
