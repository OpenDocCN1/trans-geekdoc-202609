# 2.1.2. The Impact of Climate Change: Environmental Challenges and Threats

Climate change, which is affecting the Arctic at a faster rate than anywhere else on earth [18], is compounding the environmental hazards inherent to ice. The speed of warming in the Arctic is twice as fast as the rest of the world, resulting in a dramatic reduction in minimum ice extent during winter and summer. This pattern is likely to continue at increasing rates due to the high albedo (surface reflectivity) of ice when compared to open water [17]. The different albedos create a positive feedback loop: as ice melts and is replaced by water, radiation is absorbed, leading to increased temperatures, which in turn cause even more ice to melt, and so on [17], [19], [20], [21]. This process is known as the ice-albedo feedback loop.

# 2.1.2.1. Melting Sea Ice

In 2018 and 2019 winter ice extent hit near-record lows; additionally, the Bering Strait was almost completely free of ice, an occurrence which hasn't happened since 1850. Meanwhile, the summer minimum ice extent in the Arctic has fallen from approximately 7.5 million km in 1979 to 3 km² in recent years [17]. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change predicts that by mid-century, the Arctic will be virtually free of ice [22]. Other models have the Arctic reaching the ice-free threshold (defined as fewer than 1 km² of ice as early 2032). A 10-year period of consistently ice-free Summers is forecasted to begin as early as 2040; even the latest of estimates predicts consistently ice-free Septembers by at least 2064 [23]. In, the thickness of sea ice which exists has been steadily decreasing [24].

The effects of climate change, notably melting ice, are opening up the Arctic; at the same time, other effects are making this already extreme environment even more difficult to navigate. Sea ice presents one of increasingly challenging aspects of navigation in the Arctic. Drifting sea ice can cause last-minute changes in navigation routes and damage ocean vessels and maritime installations [25]. Due to climate change, sea ice drifts have been increasing over the last several decades by up to 17% per decade [25], [26], [27], compounding the threat.
