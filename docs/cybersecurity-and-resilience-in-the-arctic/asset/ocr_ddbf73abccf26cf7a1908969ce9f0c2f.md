An important note in this method of risk assessment is that it requires from multiple agents or stressors to be combined. This doesn't necessarily mean they be 'added', but rather that some analysis should be conducted on if and how the effects or risks from the various agents or stressors interact. Ultimately, CRA aims to answer decision-relevant questions based on an organized scientific analysis; even if these answers are in-part and uncertain (Sexton, 2012).

Applying CRA to our real-world problems allows for psychological and sociological sources of stress to be considered in risk analysis, improving evaluations as they become more realistic in representing holistic and true situations and circumstances in the 'real-world'. Moreover, elements of CRA make risk analysis more reliable as an informant to risk decisions and strategies, as well as making it more relevant to the problems confronting officials and decision makers (Sexton, 2012). Other positives of CRA are that they aim to focus upon population based and/or location-based assessments of risk actually experienced by people and communities; it recognizes that the details and history of exposure to multiple stressors may be important for predicting risk, and it takes into account of background which may contribute to the cumulative risk order. It provides for the possibility, depending on the circumstances, of a semi-quantitative or qualitative analysis/result.

In spite of this, few CRA have been conducted; necessary conceptual models, theoretical frameworks and analytical procedures are in developmental stages. Integrating non-chemical stressors, while discrimination and poverty, have so far notably troublesome. Whilst these techniques are not yet perfected and data is scarce, it's arguably beneficial to implement these so that we can learn from our mistakes and shortcomings. Improvement to the processes can be made through trial and error, and it's important to remember that conducting a CRA, however imperfectly, does more than produce a risk estimate. Other benefits include the organizing of relevant scientific information, explicitly outlining underlying assumptions and uncertainties, frames important risk-related questions and structures the debate about how to tackle them.

Therefore, while CRA poses to be a useful tool in risk analysis, work still needs to be done to fully implement it in different areas. CRA would be especially disadvantaged in the Arctic, due to lack of consistent accurate data and knowledge.

# 4.5 Reliability Engineering

In an ideal world, systems, components, and their structures would be designed perfectly, where they remain operational and achieve their predetermined missions during their time in it. However, systems, their components and structures don't exist in a perfect world; such a deterministic view of an engineered system is impractical, unrealistic and infefeasible. If knowledge were not a limiting factor, the cost of development, testing, materials and analysis may far exceed the economic prospects for such a system facilitate the use of “less-than-perfect” designs, instead of trying to remove all possibilities of failure within a system or its parts.

Minimizing the occurrence and recurrence of failures and and within systems demands an understanding of why failures might not occur in the first place.
