# Governance and Government
Stefan KIRCHNER, Kamrul HOSSAIN, and Adam STEPIEN University of Lapland

## 1 Introduction

The Arctic consists of the Arctic Ocean and the surrounding land areas, which in practice means that Arctic spaces falls into two major categories: 1) the land territory of the Arctic states and 2) the sea, which is regulated by the international law of the sea (Byers, 2010: para. 2). Today’s international law is largely codified in the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), to which all Arctic nations with the exception of the United States are. To a great extent UNCLOS reflects the existing customary law, in particular with regard to coastal states, such as territorial seas. The Arctic Ocean in turn consists of spaces which are either 1) under the sovereignty of nation states every coastal state has automatically under customary international law, or marine areas where coastal states enjoy sovereign rights (to be distinguished from fully sovereignty), for example in Economic Zones or on continental shelves claimed by the coastal states, and 2) areas which are beyond the jurisdiction of nation states, such as the high seas and the deep sea. In the case of the Arctic Ocean, these areas have been nearly inaccessible—a situation which is likely to transform in the future, due to climate change. It is expected that multi-year ice will likely disappear in the coming decades (Wadhams, 2017: pp. 3 et seq.), which will mean that the Arctic Ocean of the future will resemble the Baltic sea, "where ice-strengthened ships and icebreaker-convoys can safely operate in winter" (Byers, 2010: para. 5). The abundance of natural resources, in particular hydrocarbons (Byers, 2010: para. 8) and the region’s strategic relevance, with its easier accessibility due to climate change, are also attracting widespread interest in the Arctic.

The Arctic spans marine areas (both within and outside of the state jurisdiction, sovereignty, and sovereign rights) and the territories of Arctic states, several of which are federations. The region is populated by indigenous peoples, who strive for self-determination. The Arctic has been economically exploited for centuries and various region, external, and global actors operate there. Consequently, the area is governed at multiple levels: global, national, provincial/sub-national, municipal, and community. The rules set by governments or municipalities interact with coastal regulations and international standards. In the European Arctic, European Union legislation and policies are as relevant as the distribution of power and responsibility between these governance levels varies depending on the sector, the type of human activity, and the specific location within the circumpolar North. Nevertheless, in most cases, it is the national regulatory and policy systems which are the most important frameworks. The Arctic is not only a case of interrelated governance levels but also of the coexistence of three channels of governance: hierarchical governance.
