# 6.5 Machine learning approaches for predicting in vivo efficacy

ML techniques have become very useful in semiconductor testing for evaluating the in vivo effectiveness of drug candidates and providing meaningful information about their possible therapeutic effects in the drug discovery process. This way, utilizing ML techniques, the researchers can process large biological datasets, define biomarkers for the prediction of compounds’ activity in vivo and build accurate models of compounds’ effectiveness.

Multifaceted integration of multiomics data for predicting in vivo efficacy is one of the most common ML approaches toward the discovery of molecular pharmac signatures. When preclinical data such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics are integrated into ML algorithms, researchers can decide on biomarkers that predict the efficacy of treatment as well as disease progression. These biomarkers also serve as a source of practical information about the molecular processes involved in the effectiveness of drugs and help analyze who would be most responsive to the treatment.

Machine learning can be employed to see the effect of structure-based drug-target interactions and determine the potential of compounds by their mode of action. Through the use of descriptions about drug-target binding affinities, pathway activation, and network perturbation, train ML algorithms are capable of estimating the probability of compounds regulating biological pathways that are associated with diseases. This mechanistic viewpoint of drug targeting allows selecting lead scaffolds with appropriate target interaction potential and favorable therapeutic effect among the most promising candidates for additional preclinical trials.

Complementarily, ML techniques can be applied to extracting efficacy data from preclinical animal models to make clinical predictions in humans. When generating and training efficacy-based ML models, such as dose-response curves, survival rates, tumor growth inhibition data, etc., researchers can build predictive models that can transpire the efficacy of compounds from preclinical to clinical phase. These models include pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and disease diversity to model patient response to drug treatment to gain reasonable predictions of how human patients would respond in relation to efficacy and toxicity during clinical trials.
