## Automated materials characterization using machine learning for screening biocompatible materials

**Abstract:** Currently, biomedical engineering has received a boost, largely due to the rate at which new biocompatible materials have been developed, offering innovative solutions to many biomedical application areas. This chapter focuses on the constantly growing field of biocompatible materials, characterized by the high relevance of these materials in enhancing patients’ quality of life and driving new developments in medicine. Understanding the surface characteristics, chemical properties, mechanical properties, and biological properties is beneficial in guiding the choice of materials for better clinical applications. This discussion extends to current developments in biocompatible materials, which have concentrated on advancements in several new synthetic polymers, new metals, and composite materials. It also addresses other areas of material characterization, presenting challenges such as ethical concerns and compliance with legislation, including the need to continually subject materials to rigorous testing and ethical standards. This chapter provides a comprehensive and critical review of recent advancements and prospective developments in biocompatible materials and is intended to serve as a useful reference for engineers working in the biomaterials and biomedical fields for their material selection and application.

**Keywords:** biocompatible materials, surface characteristics, chemical stability, emerging trends, ethical considerations, material characterization

### 1 Introduction

One major revolution in biomedical engineering has been achieved through the discovery of biodegradable materials, which have significantly impacted the design of various implants and diagnostic equipment [1]. The use of such materials in biocompatible applications tends to contribute substantially to patient outcomes due to the growing demand for new technologies in the healthcare sector [2]. To avoid adverse responses and to maximize the potential for therapeutic effects during the application of a material, that material must be as biocompatible as possible, which is defined as the ability of a material to function properly in an enzymatic sense at the site of administration [3, 4]. This chapter aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the biological characteristics, recent developments, and challenges associated with biocompatible materials, with a focus on their relevance to current state-of-the-art medical applications [5].

Understanding the basic attributes that define biocompatibility is crucial for the identification of appropriate materials for medical purposes [6, 7]. In clinical applications, the performance of a material is confined to basic characteristics that include mechanical capability, chemical response, biological interaction, and surface aspects [8, 9]. Surface roughness and charge influence cell behavior and tissue integration, while chemical stability sustains function [9]. Physiological forces determine the material's compatibility with the body's immune response, while mechanical properties such as strength and elasticity determine compatibility with physiological pressures [10].

It was pointed out that recent scientific developments in biocompatible materials have given rise to new opportunities that satisfy various clinical requirements [11]. Some examples of these trends include research into new synthetic polymers for improved functionality as well as the creation of new metal alloys and composite materials that offer enhanced performance and functionality [12]. New materials include synthetically produced polymers like polylactic acid and polyethylene glycol (PEG), which are biodegradable and exhibit low protein adsorption, and metals like titanium and cobalt-chromium alloys, which have high strength, high stiffness, and high corrosion resistance [13–15]. Fiber-reinforced composites, in which two distinct components are utilized, have also started being considered for their defined characteristics and increased functionality [16, 17].

Current and future developments still face significant challenges in material characterization as well as ethical problems [18, 19]. Since biocompatibility is the ability of a material to perform in the intended physiological roles and to respond to biological systems, it is essential to subject the material to detailed testing [20]. Another example of ethical considerations includes compliance with regulatory necessities, handling long-term effects on patient's well-being, and ensuring that all the materials used in clinical applications are safe and effective for the intended purpose [21]. Such challenges emphasize the need to continue the research and innovation processes to bridge gaps and improve material performance [22, 23].
