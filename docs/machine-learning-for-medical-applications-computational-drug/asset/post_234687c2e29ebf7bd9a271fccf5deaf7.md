## 3.5.5 Biodegradable composites
Biodegradable composites are defined as composite materials that are formed with a reinforcement of at least two separate components to obtain an improved mechanical and biological features. Such composites are composed of a matrix of bioresorbable polymers with natural and/or synthetic fiber reinforcements. The choice of materials offers the possibility to develop structures with desired mechanical parameters and, at the same time, biocompatibility. Biodegradable polymeric composites are in the process of being evaluated for use in orthopedic implant devices and for tissue engineering scaffolds, with the expectation of achieving enhanced performance and decreased late sequelae.

It is remarkable how advanced these biocompatible materials have made biomedical engineering. Different types of materials possess their own unique characteristics to meet certain challenges in the medical field, leading to improved healing for patients as well as more efficient treatment methods. Consequently, it is expected that further investigation and innovation in this area will offer new findings that enhance the characteristics of biocompatible materials and expand their applications in modern medicine.

## 4 Applications of biocompatible materials

### 4.1 Implants and prosthetics
Implants and prostheses, which are central components of modern medical treatments for numerous diseases and conditions, require biocompatible materials. These materials are designed to interact with the body, offer functional support, and improve patients’ quality of life. This section analyzes some of the functions of biocompatible materials in implants and prostheses, their advantages, disadvantages, and relevance.

### 4.1.1 Orthopedic implants
Orthopedic implants are essential for the management of skeletal disorders, arthritic conditions, and fractures. Some of these implants include bone plates and joint replacement. These implants are made of biocompatible ceramics, stainless steel, and titanium alloys. Due to their superior mechanical qualities and high resistance to corrosion, titanium and its alloys ensure efficiency and durability within the body. Owing to their high level of hardness and wear resistance, zirconia and alumina ceramics are used in knee and hip replacements. Prosthetics made from these materials encourage direct bonding to bone tissue and reduce the likelihood of implant loss, thereby improving patient satisfaction and locomotion.

### 4.1.2 Dental implants
As for replacement teeth, dental implants provide support. Titanium is popular due to its great strength, its ability to assimilate into the bone, and its generally good compatibility. The functional and esthetic rehabilitation that mimics the lost tooth structure is achieved by the stable anchorage of titanium implants to the bone tissue. There have been advancements in technology for the surface treatment of implants through etching techniques, such as the use of acid and plasma spraying, which have enhanced the bond and efficiency of dental implants.

### 4.1.3 Cardiovascular implants
Pacemakers, valves, and stents are other cardiovascular implant devices required to address diseases and illnesses of the cardiovascular system. Some of the naturally compatible materials used include silicone and polyurethane; the devices designed are highly compatible with the circulatory system. The newest advance in cardiovascular implants is the production of drug-eluting stents, which are coated with biocompatible polymers that release medicine to combat restenosis. Thus, the materials must exhibit minimal propensity to cause inflammation and thrombosis and be capable of withstanding the harsh and active conditions of the cardiovascular system.

### 4.1.4 Prosthetic limbs
These artificial limbs are designed to mimic lost limbs, which allow patients to experience improved mobility as well as functionality. The biocompatibility of the materials utilized in prosthetics includes lightweight polymers, carbon fiber-reinforced plastics, silicone rubber, and other flexible materials. Carbon fiber composites offer a high strength-to-weight ratio, making prosthetic limbs durable yet comfortable. Used in liners and sockets, silicone elastomers provide cushioning characteristics and a skin-simulating texture to enhance fit and mitigate skin chafing. New prosthetic developments, such as the addition of sensors and the incorporation of motorized attachments, depend on these materials to provide better performance and user experience.

### 4.1.5 Cochlear implants
Cochlear implants are electrical devices that help people who are profoundly deaf to appreciate sound in some ways. The preference for biocompatible materials for cochlear implants includes an outer casing covered with silicone material and electrodes made of platinum. These materials must be chosen carefully to avoid hazardous effects and harm to the delicate parts of the inner ear. It has also been observed that the design and materials used must be appropriate in order to avoid tissue reactions and achieve the optimal effect on the stimulation of the auditory nerve.

One of the major achievements in medical development is the use of biocompatible materials in both implants and prostheses, which restore patients’ functionality and improve their quality of life. Due to advances in patient needs, the market now offers new materials and technologies that enhance device functionality, durability, and safety for both clients and health professionals.

### 4.2 Drug delivery systems
To develop advanced drug delivery systems capable of delivering precisely controlled and highly localized therapeutic inputs, the use of biocompatible materials is fundamental. These materials enable systems that can release medication at optimal rates and target specific sites in the body to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects. This section explores emerging prospects in biocompatible drug delivery, focusing on key categories such as targeted delivery methods, controlled release systems, and intelligent delivery platforms. Figure 6 provides a schematic overview of these drug delivery strategies, emphasizing the role of biocompatibility in enhancing precision and effectiveness.

### 4.2.1 Controlled-release formulations
Pharmaceuticals reach the target site in the body slowly through the administration of therapeutic substances with defined release rates using controlled-release drug delivery systems. By doing so, it reduces the administration intervals, enhances patient compliance, and contributes to the sustenance of therapeutic concentrations of such medications in the circulatory system. Due to their degradation in a controlled manner that releases the medicine in a steady and systematic way, biodegradable polymers such as PCL and PLGA are commonly used in the construction of such devices. Depending on the requirements of the therapy, these materials can be developed into hydrogels, nanoparticles, and microspheres, among others.

### 4.2.2 Targeted drug delivery
Targeted drug delivery systems are intended to deliver therapeutic drugs to diseased tissues only, without affecting healthy ones. This precise approach reduces deleterious consequences while enhancing the curative advantages of pharmacotherapy. Such systems require biocompatibility, and these materials are often used as carriers that can potentially be functionalized with antibodies. To transport chemotherapeutic medicines specifically to the aforementioned tumor site, for instance, nanoparticles can be functionalized with coat changes that recognize receptors on cancer cells.
