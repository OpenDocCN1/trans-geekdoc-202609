## 2 Historical background of medical image processing

### 2.1 Early techniques in medical imaging

X-ray machines were discovered in 1895 by Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen. A pre-history of medical imaging can be traced back to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. This invention changed the nature of medicine and offered the first noninvasive investigation of the internal organs of the human body. During X-ray application, photographic plates used to be left exposed to X-rays in order to create images of bones and other concentrated structures.

Figure 1: Early techniques in medical imaging.

After the discovery of X-rays, a dramatic evolution of medical imaging occurred with the invention of fluoroscopy by Thomas Edison in the early twentieth century. This new technology of fluoroscopy allowed simultaneous imaging of the body’s interior structures, thus making X-ray imaging more dynamic through the study of dynamic physiological processes. This technique employed a fluorescent screen and X-ray source to generate continuous image projection, constituting the backbone of contemporary interventional radiological systems.

At the same time, in the twentieth century, the radioisotope and nuclear medicine were discovered. Hungarian-born George de Hevesy, a Nobel Prize winner in Chemistry in 1943, was credited with the development of utilizing radioactive tracers in diagnosis. The ability to monitor the distribution of substances in the human body encouraged the creation of techniques such as PET and scintigraphy. These techniques made functional imaging possible and allowed the assessment of physiological and metabolic functions apart from form.

The technology of ultrasonic imaging supplemented the use of X-rays in the 1950s. By employing higher frequency sound waves as a form of energy, ultrasound successfully penetrated and produced images of softer-bodied tissues that were difficult to see using X-rays. Originally invented for use in industry as a nondestructive testing technique, ultrasound found its first application in medicine, being specifically applied in obstetrics and cardiology. Its advantages included a noninvasive approach and the ability to provide an immediate view of a patient’s insides.

Computed tomography (CT), developed in the 1970s by Sir Godfrey Hounsfield and Allan Cormack, was a breakthrough in medical imaging. CT used a computer terminal and X-ray scans of the body and proved to have better contrast resolution as well as precise depiction of a body’s complicated structures. CT was introduced into clinical practice in the early 1970s and had a great influence on the diagnosis of the presence of disease, a significance acknowledged by the award of the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Hounsfield and Cormack.

The most powerful imaging method at the present moment, called magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), is based on the use of radio waves and strong magnetic fields to take high-contrast images of the interior of the body and was invented in the second half of the twentieth century. The 2003 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield for their efforts that advanced MRI technology. Soft tissue contrast is best served in neurology, musculoskeletal imaging, and cancer imaging, which do not use ionizing radiation as in CT scans.

The medical imaging techniques mentioned above have laid down the foundations of modern diagnostic imaging. Each invention incorporated some features that extended our current understanding of human physical appearance and function. It has been made possible by constant improvements in imaging technology to harness image processing strategies that incorporate computational processing to improve diagnostic accuracy, automated analysis, and image quality. The effects of these early approaches can still be observed in present technological tools and methods in healthcare in relation to medical imaging development.

### 2.2 Evolution of image processing methods

This chapter aims to analyze the development trends of the methods used for medical image processing based on the technological and methodological advancements in this field. It is most feasible to distill this progression into several principal stages, each of which is defined according to certain innovations that have helped to form the current state of medical imaging.

![](img/211c754cd1dd84920a171f497b33b689_556_0.png)

Figure 2: Evolution of image processing methods.

In fact, medical image processing technologies can be traced back to the emergence of basic imaging tools in the early twentieth century. Historically, medical imaging was dependent on analog techniques in which the X-ray was commonly used. It provides two-dimensional images with a low contrast ratio and low resolution. The assessment of these images was, for the most part, qualitative, depending on the skill of the radiologist to distinguish between normal and pathological tissues.

It can also be noted that digital imaging, which appeared later in the second half of the twentieth century, contributed significantly to medical image processing. Digital imaging was a convenient way for the transformation of analog signals into digital data, which could be more proactively analyzed. Medical images saw a change when, in the 1970s, an effective CT emerged, offering cross-sectional images of the human body. These imaging techniques advanced diagnostic ability to a great extent as CT scans developed comprehensive three-dimensional images based on computer algorithms and X-ray technologies.

In the 1980s, MRI helped bolster medical imaging by using radiofrequency signals and magnetic fields to take high-resolution pictures. In comparison to CT and X-ray scans, MRI provided far better contrast of soft tissues, which helped in the diagnosis of pathological disorders and in studying the detailed anatomy of the body. Other advanced imaging techniques, including ultrasound and positron emission tomography, also developed during this period, and each offered special imaging skills that enhanced the diagnostic sensitivity of imaging interventions.

As the marketplace for DI technology expanded, the need for image processing algorithms grew in significance. Ordinary image processing was a limited set of procedures including noise elimination, image contrast, and edge detection. As discussed later, these techniques were intended to enhance the quality of the image and therefore better facilitate accurate interpretation of the image information. The advances of algorithms came in the 1990s and early 2000s where region-based segmentation, texture analysis, and pattern recognition joined actively.

First developed as a diagnostic tool in the mid-twentieth century, ultrasound imaging has undergone many developments since the 1990s. Sound waves of high frequency are utilized in recent ultrasonic technology to yield real-time images of internal structures. Progress in Doppler imaging and 3D/4D ultrasound has increased the number of fields in which it is possible to apply this equipment, for the assessment of organ function, blood circulation, and fetal growth.

A major development was in imaging technologies in the 2000s with the shift from film to digital in this technology. The advancement of the diagnosis procedure has been made, radiation dosage has been reduced, and the pictures obtained have been made clearer by the use of digital radiography, computed radiography, and digital mammography. EHRs have been implemented, and there was consolidation of digital imaging, which has facilitated enhancement of data stewardship and retrieval.

In the 2010s, machine learning and artificial intelligence in medical imaging have advanced to a new level. The use of AI algorithms and machine learning models for automatic image analysis for the identification of diseases and diagnostic support was progressing. All of these technologies offer the potential for individualized therapy, enhanced diagnostic methods, and refined means to better analyze visual representations.
