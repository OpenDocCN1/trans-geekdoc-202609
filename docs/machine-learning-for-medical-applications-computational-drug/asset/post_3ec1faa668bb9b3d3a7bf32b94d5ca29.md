
Clustering: One of the most used clustering methods was k-Means. Although the approach minimizes the variance within each cluster, it ultimately categorizes the data into different clusters. The following stages are taken by the algorithm: it starts by placing centroids randomly, then it assigns every data point to the nearest centroid, computes the centroid value again in terms of the average of the points belonging to a certain centroid, and continues this process until reaching proper optimization. The choice for which k-Means was made quite successful involved employing criteria such as the Elbow Method. However, with clusters of different densities and shapes, k-Means was not so efficient despite being one of the simplest methods.

A hierarchy of clusters was created via hierarchical clustering, which can be applied in two ways: either bottom-up or top-down. In the agglomerative technique, each data point forms its individual cluster that merges according to its distance to nearby clusters until this criterion is met: there exists no pair of clusters that can be merged. In contrast, in the divisive technique, all data points initially start as a single cluster and are recursively divided into further subclusters. A dendrogram, a tree-like diagram that depicts the process of merging or splitting, was the most frequently used outcome. Nevertheless, hierarchical clustering is useful because it can demonstrate the hierarchical structure of the data, but it is computationally expensive for large datasets.

The density of data points was accordingly applied by the density-based clustering method, DBSCAN, to distinguish between clusters. The technique was based on observing that clusters are areas characterized by high density and separated by areas of low density. Figure 6 outlines the flow of a variety of clustering algorithms, detailing the approach of several of them to cluster the components of the datasets. Two parameters are needed for DBSCAN: These two important parameters are MinPts, which defines the minimum number of points to form a dense zone, and ρ, the maximum distance between the points belonging to a single cluster. The system divides the data into three categories: noise points, which are those outside the neighborhood; boundary points, which are those within the neighborhood range but not within the dense range; and finally, core points, which are within the dense neighborhood zone. As seen, its performance was dependent on the set parameters, but overall, it was excellent in finding clusters of any shape and handling noise points.

Mean Shift: Given a set of data points, clustering is an exploratory method that seeks the peaks of a probability density function. The algorithm also controls how a data point is constantly shifted toward the mean of other data points that fall within a specific range, and this is done repeatedly until the algorithm converges. Clusters are essentially sets of points that coalesce into a single point. While using the Mean Shift algorithm, there is no need to specify the number of clusters to be identified, and it is flexible in identifying clusters of different sizes and shapes. However, it can be computationally heavy at times, especially when dealing with large datasets.

Unlike k-Means clustering, which requires dimensionality reduction before implementation, Spectral Clustering makes use of eigenvalues of a similarity matrix for that purpose. This involves the calculation of a similarity matrix based on the disparity between two instances, the calculation of the Laplacian, and a resultant eigendecomposition. The resulting eigenvectors represent the data in a lower-dimensional space, for which traditional clustering algorithms can be applied. Finally, Spectral Clustering is particularly useful when working with data that includes clusters with different structures and is capable of detecting nonconvex clusters. The method is based on the use of matrix operations and, as a result, tends to be slow for very large datasets.

There are several uses of clustering algorithms in drug toxicity prediction, for example, in finding new patterns in chemical data, categorization of patient groups based on their response, and discovering latent relations between physicochemical properties of drugs and their toxicities. Just like all algorithms, there are benefits as well as disadvantages to each; hence, the approach selected should align with the specific details of the data as well as the goals of the analysis. Because clustering results often apply to specific domains, analyzing clustering findings usually requires domain knowledge and procedures often referred to as validation measures.

## 3.2 Dimensionality reduction

The process of reducing a dataset's features while preserving its core form and material is referred to as “dimensionality reduction.” This is particularly crucial when dealing with demanding datasets with many facets, which is typical across the domains WADILAC is designed to accommodate, such as drug toxicity prediction, which often entails datasets with numerous features. While reducing the impacts of dimensionality, dimensionality reduction also plays a role in improving the visualization of complex data, as well as increasing the computational efficiency and performance of a model.

Of the techniques most widespread for applying dimensionality reduction, principal component analysis is. The principal components, which are a set of new orthogonal components with the maximum variance, are derived using PCA from the selected features. Figure 7 depicts the process of dimensionality reduction, which reduces the amount of data but retains its characteristics in a certain dimension. The maximum variance is contributed by the first principal component, the second largest variance is contributed by the second principal component, and this pattern continues. Most of the variation, therefore, stays with the clustered features while reducing dimensionality by mapping data onto these components. PCA is particularly useful in cases of finding patterns and displaying high-dimensional data.

Thus, the t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) method reduces the number of dimensions in the data sample and brings it down to 2, so it can be visualized. Following the intention of other methods to preserve nearby instances close to each other in space when the space is reduced to fewer dimensions, t-SNE tries to preserve the local structure of the data. This method has proven quite useful for identifying patterns and clusters in complex and large data sets. However, while this technique is very efficient for exploratory data analysis, it is computationally demanding and performs poorly on very large data sets.

There exists a dimensionality reduction technique known as linear discriminant analysis (LDA), which has the objective of maximizing the distance between classes in the dataset. To achieve this end, the supervised LDA aims at finding another space of a lower dimension where the class separation is higher – an aim that differs from the unsupervised PCA, which focuses on variance. That is, LDA aims at the maximization of the ratio of between-class variation to within-class variance to reduce the dimensionality. This method performs well when used in classification tasks where the intention is to reduce dimensionality with the aim of improving the class structure of data to assist upcoming models.

The term "autoencoders" is used when neural networks designed for unlabeled data or dimensionality reduction tasks are being referred to. An encoder transforms the input and possibly maps it to a lower-dimensional space, while a decoder uses this result to reconstruct the original input. This process is known as an autoencoder.

In this study, ultra-short-term EGCG destryptophanogenic profiles are aimed to be derived from EEG by using an autoencoder, followed by subsequent analysis of the resulting profiles. The autoencoder becomes capable of performing effective data encoding since it is trained to minimize the reconstruction error. Extending this concept and making it more flexible and stable, probabilistic components – variational autoencoders – are introduced within this framework. Autoencoders can be very specific in dealing with complex, nonlinear data structures.

Feature extraction and feature selection are the two main approaches to dimensionality reduction. This can be done without changing the general feature space of the data set and thus is called feature selection, as it only selects the most important features from the entire set. On the other hand, feature extraction involves representing the data in a new, smaller feature space. The latter includes methods such as PCA and autoencoders that build a new feature space rather than selecting from existing feature spaces. Therefore, considering these issues, the strategies used in the study had to be distinguished to select the most appropriate methodology for the specific conditions of the investigation.

Dimensionality reduction techniques have been widely employed in drug toxicity prediction to enhance the model's accuracy, reduce the complexity of large datasets, and improve visualization. These techniques assist in reducing complexity and overfitting through the predefined reduction of dimensions in the methods mentioned above. The following are the limitations of dimensionality reduction: Despite their powerful descriptiveness, PCA and LDA, for instance, presuppose linear relationships between variables in an effort to fit complex and nonlinear relations. Since t-SNE is a nonparametric method, it may not be suitable for subsequent machine learning applications, even though it excels at visualization.
