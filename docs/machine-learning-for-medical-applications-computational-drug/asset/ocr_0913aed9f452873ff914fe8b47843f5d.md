# 7 Conclusion

In materials science, the recent incorporation of DL models has elevated the discovery and characterization of new materials to higher levels. Due to the inherent nature of such models, these issues bring severe interpretability challenges that must be resolved for these models to be fully utilized. The objective of this chapter has been twofold, all pertaining to interpretability: first, understanding the various definitions and concerns of transparency; second, exploring the methods available that provide visual insights about models.

The high accuracy of predictions, which allows DL models, is accounted for by the fact that it is often hard to track how particular input features actually affect the predicted output. In material science, interpretability is of paramount importance since it influences the validity and credibility of the model outputs. This trust-building is necessary for the use of artificial intelligence insights, more specifically in applications that pertain to material properties as well as performance. This is why it is important to ensure that the models developed yield easily understandable results that practitioners can act on.

A number of techniques have been developed to tackle these interpretability problems. Saliency maps and activation maps, which are used to show which features in the input data affected the output of a DL model, provide helpful information about how DL models work internally. Some explanation methods, such as LIME, SHAP, and similar approaches, are local explanation methods that offer an even more detailed breakdown of the instance under consideration, making them more granular. Nevertheless, this remains a major challenge today, as advancements in data science and machine learning have led to interpretability of models being seen as compromising model performance. Efforts to enhance clarity by applying simplified models can be costly in terms of loss of predictability, and vice versa.

Subsequent studies in this domain should be aimed at defining improved interpretability standards that are more applicable to material science. This, alongside transformations in the algorithms on which the interpretability models are based, has the potential to give rise to superior and more informative approaches to its employment. This improves not only the clarity of ideas where DL is applied but also guarantees its reliable applicability to material science issues. It thus always remains crucial to continue closing the gap between increased model complexity and their interpretability as the field develops, given that the practical application of these extraordinarily beneficial tools for material research depends upon it.
