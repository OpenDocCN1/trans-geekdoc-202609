# 2 Overview of biomedical sensors and devices

# 2.1 Types of biomedical sensors and devices

Wearable sensors represent a transformative advancement in personal health monitoring, offering continuous, noninvasive measurements of various physiological parameters. These devices have become integral to both personal fitness and clinical applications, providing real-time data that can significantly enhance health management and preventive care.

There are two most common forms of wearable sensors, which include smartwatches and fitness trackers and basic traditional watches with additional sophisticated health sensors. These gadgets usually entail including heart rate sensors, step counter, sleep pattern and physical activity sensors. Most of the models use GPS for the tracking of outdoor physical activities and then offer further statistics such as caloric consumption and stress. Different types of biomedical sensors and devices that are wearable, implantable, and diagnostic are depicted in Figure 1, where the various sensors and devices are designed to measure a diverse physiological parameter. These devices collect data and using some algorithms, guide the user on their general fitness and health; they are helpful to both the regular fitness fanatic as well as the chronically ill.

Skin-mounted patches and adhesive sensors are aimed for skin on-surface wear where it monitors different health parameters continually. These sensors are mainly employed for observing cardiac activity and blood glucose levels of dehydration. The commonly used adhesive patches are thin and flexible to prevent weighing the patient down and also to allow the patient to go about his/her normal business without much disturbance. For example, ECG adhesive monitors the heart rhythms for weeks, while glucose adhesive monitors the blood sugar levels in an unobtrusive manner. It is also convenient that the data from these sensors can be delivered to mobile devices for real-time analysis and display of results.

Already, we have smart clothes that have sensors placed to the cloth so that people do not need extra devices to monitor their health. These garments incorporate a range of underlining sensors to monitor factors such as heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, and muscle activity, among others. Examples of smart clothing are sportswear that help improve performance and recovery from diseases, and other apparels that help monitor long-term diseases. Technical innovations in textiles also in the sphere of sensors and the shrinking of the devices let smart clothing become much more comfortable and versatile, so they are worn not only during sports activities, but can also be used in healthcare.
