To seize sequential input, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) contain feedback, which allows information to remain active across time. Some of the problems that can be fit into this architecture include tasks that require time series data, such as predicting medication interactions given previous data. LSTMs and GRUs, which are additional developments of RNNs that address issues of vanishing and exploding gradients, are utilized. This advancement makes it possible to learn more easily over sequences that span a long time.

Neural networks are trained by adjusting the weights of connections between nodes to minimize the error function, which reflects the difference between the values estimated and those in the real world. The main approach for this optimization is backpropagation, in which gradients are calculated, and optimization methods such as Adam are used. Regularization is a method used to enhance the ability to generalize to unseen data and diminish over learning; two methods are dropout and weight decay.

That is by no means to say that employing the powers of neural networks does not present certain difficulties. Especially for comparatively small datasets, it turns out to be critical to have sufficient processing power as well as a large amount of manually labeled data. In addition, the issue of model interpretability remains a concern, as the complex architectures of neural networks often result in a limited capability to explain certain predictions. In the future, research efforts aimed at overcoming these problems will likely be driven by further advancements in algorithms and explainable artificial intelligence (AI).

Artificial neural networks are under constant enhancement, and new methodologies can revolutionize expectations for drug toxicity and side effects. As these models advance in complexity, the broad versatility of this data will also expand the usability of these models in pharmaceutical research and safety assessment.

# 3 Unsupervised learning techniques

# 3.1 Clustering algorithms

Clustering algorithms, one of the most basic unsupervised learning methods, were then employed to categorize data points that resemble one another in terms of their features. Unlike supervised learning, where data samples are classified, clustering seeks patterns within the data samples without necessarily requiring them to be classified. These algorithms can reveal latent structures that are essential for understanding medication toxicity and side effects and are highly effective in conducting exploratory hypothesis generation.
