Increasingly, people acknowledge the need for complex tools to manage the colossal amounts of information generated in modern drug design processes, as the requirements for enhanced and refined strategies for drug safety assessment continue to grow [3]. Due to their ability to model complex relationships and discover subtle interdependencies within large datasets, machine learning appears to be a plausible solution to these problems [8].

Traditionally, predicting drug toxicity has been a complex process that involved the use of rules of thumb, statistical methods, and experimental validation [5]. Drug assessment at the initial stages, especially during the lead identification and optimization stages, has been aided by SAR models, QSAR models, and other in vitro tests [6]. These analytical tools are often not effective for prediction, particularly when considering new chemical entities that challenge bioassay models [7]. Concerns including intraspecies differences, the inability to address delicate interactions occurring at the cellular level, limitations of preclinical models, and learning disadvantages are some of the obstacles encountered [8]. The analysis of tagged signals using traditional methods was inherently restricted, thereby providing compelling reasons for the development of enhanced methods that combine heightened predictive capabilities with a closer correlation to human physiological states [9].

With the entry of machine learning, the subject of drug toxicity prediction has undergone a total overhaul with powerful tools capable of learning from massive data sets and recognizing patterns that other methods might not detect [10]. Supervised and unsupervised learning approaches were employed through machine learning models, offering a framework for evaluating large and intricate data with numerous measurement features [11]. Such analysis was beneficial and could contribute to increased accuracy in prediction and the discovery of novel knowledge [12]. When learning was conducted using supervised data to categorize negative impacts and make predictions on the same, methods such as neural networks and ensemble techniques proved useful [13]. Categorization, together with methods of dimensionality reduction, was clustered for feature extraction and utilized to detect anomalies and improve the predictions made within the unsupervised learning process [26]. The use of these machine learning approaches was expected to overcome the limitations of conventional techniques and enhance the overall reliability of drug safety evaluations [15].

It has been agreed that stringency in the assessment and validation processes was central to the success of the machine learning algorithms for estimating medication toxicity [16]. In assessing the performance of these models regarding robustness and generality, external validation, especially, was crucial [17].
