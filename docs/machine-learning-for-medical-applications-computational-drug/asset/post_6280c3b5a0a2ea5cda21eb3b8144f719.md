## 2.3 AI-Driven prediction of protein-protein interactions

PPIs are essential elements for quite a number of biological processes and are commonly associated with ailment. Thus, it is crucial to predict PPIs, followed by the comprehension of the cell processes and discovery of potential therapeutic interventions. This paper shows how machine learning techniques and Big biological Data help AI methods become useful for PPI prediction.

Supervised learning methods are also used in machine learning for predicting PPIs and IE aimed at learning from past data with a given set of outputs to determine how this information can be used to predict future output. Used in the training of prediction models, we scale these techniques by means of using datasets with PPI and non-interaction pairs. SVMs, random forests, and deep neural networks use primary protein sequences and structures networks of interactions as features. These characteristics can help the models notice patterns that signal whether two particular proteins are likely to interact.

Another strategy uses networks to identify PPIs based on the topological properties of PPI networks by means of network-based algorithms. It has been reported that these algorithms employ proteome-scale graph structures of PPIs, and use network propagation approaches to rank potential interactions. They also predict proteins that, due to their functional association with the known partners, should interact, by mimicking the information flow in the network.

Integrative approaches involve the use of multiple data sets in order to fine-tune PPI prediction, including gene expression data, protein subcellular localization data, and functional annotations. Combining multiple datasets into these models allows them to include additional information about the interactions and reveal new associates of the proteins that are not evident from the analysis of individual types of data.

In silico estimation of PPIs from protein sequences has shown the possibility of using deep learning methods, specifically CNN and RNN. These models possess practical benefits for enhancing the prediction accuracy since they are capable of independently developing a hierarchical representation for the characteristics of proteins and recognizing complicated sequence-structure relationship.

## 2.4 Genomic data analysis for target prioritization

Recent drug development has come to depend greatly on genomic data, particularly for ranking potential therapeutic targets. High-throughput sequencing platforms generate vast volumes of genomics data, which contain valuable insights into the genetic basis of diseases. Artificial intelligence (AI) plays a crucial role in analyzing and prioritizing these targets by evaluating their relevance to disease pathogenesis, as illustrated in Figure 3.

Combining annotations from GWAS with other genomic functional datasets including gene expression and chromatin accessibility patterns is one approach to using genomic data analysis for target prioritization. Such integrated datasets are used to train algorithms such as logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting machines to identify specific genetic variations associated with specific disease symptoms. Those models can point to certain gene regions, which are likely to be implicated in disease causation and then prioritize these according to the patterns and interaction characterized in data.

Other strategies incorporate network analysis of genomic data for ranking potential targets regarding the connection with the known disease-associated genes. Genomic data are combined with protein-protein interactions, gene regulatory interactions, as well as pathways and networks to build models of genetic associations. Then, network propagation algorithms including random walk-based algorithms are further employed to rank genes with known disease genes in terms of functional relevance. Through mimicking the actual flow of signal within the network, these algorithms can predict currently unknown targets that are connected to disease-related signaling pathways.

In data analysis, advanced techniques can integrate data from large-scale functional genomics studies, such as CRISPR-based screens, to identify proteins essential for the survival of disease cells. ## 2.5 AI-based identification of novel drug targets from omics data

Omics technology has become an integrated strategy in analyzing molecular mechanisms underlying numerous diseases in biomedical research. An exciting opportunity in drug design was the discovery of novel targets based on omics information, and AI is a critical component of such efforts.

Another way AI can be used to identify pharmacological targets from the omics data is data integration from proteomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics, and genome data. These integrated datasets utilize machine learning procedures including ensemble in addition to deep learning concerning the mark, molecular markers linked to diverse disease conditions. It is possible to identify the genes and metabolic pathways that are under- or overexpressed in disease conditions, which can be considered potential future drug targets.

Analysis of omics data starting from complex networks offers a promising approach to identify new treatment plans within biological networks. Omics data integrated with functional annotations and pathways are used to construct gene co-regulatory networks, protein-protein interaction networks, and metabolic networks. Network propagation algorithms as well as community detection algorithms on these networks, can be used to prioritize nodes within these networks that are associated with functionally relevant disease processes. These algorithms can help identify potential drug targets relevant to disease-associated pathways and how simulated information flows through what are likely to be highly connected subnetworks.

With the help of AI, new cell type-specific targets involved in the development of illness can be identified on the basis of single-cell omics data. We have single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which provides unprecedented details on the heterogeneity of cells within tissues and tumors. Single-cell omics data is also valuable for computational analysis of clustering algorithms and trajectory inference that can pinpoint cell type-specific markers and disease networks. These markers and networks hit cell type-specific vulnerabilities, potential therapeutic targets of precision medicine.
