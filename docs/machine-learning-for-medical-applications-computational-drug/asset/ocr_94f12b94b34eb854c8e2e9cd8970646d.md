# 3.3.3 Pros and cons of stacking and blending

The first advantage of stacking and blending was the fact that the results from different models can be combined for better outcomes to emerge. Such techniques are more favorable in the field when working with nonhomogeneous data and when there are substantive predictive tasks to be met, for example, in the process of biomedical material designing and optimization. Stacking and blending within the study come with some drawbacks: overfitting tends to plague stacking if the meta-model is not trained properly using out-of-sample data. Choosing base models and designing the meta-model require extra attention with regard to predictive accuracy while preventing overfitting and bias in the ensemble. Solving these problems requires the analysis of the models of the subject field and such properties of the material science data as the relationships between them.

Everything pointed to a bright future for stacking and blending techniques in material science, as further advancements in meta-models were still continuing and new base models were being researched. With increasing computational power and the availability of more data, the prospects of developing highly accurate and sophisticated ensemble models lie in the future. Furthermore, continued improvements in AutoML should also reduce the implementation complexity in stacking and blending ensembles, which would otherwise be challenging for material scientists to develop. If advancements in these techniques continue and improve, the opportunity for increased innovation in material identification and improvements could lead to growth and development in biomedical materials and customized treatments.

# 3.4 Voting mechanisms for robust material property predictions

Voting mechanisms are a fundamental component of ensemble learning, a strategy that combines multiple machine learning models to produce more accurate and reliable predictions. In the context of material property prediction, voting methods aggregate the outputs of individual models to form a consensus, thereby reducing the risk of errors that could arise from relying on a single predictive model.
