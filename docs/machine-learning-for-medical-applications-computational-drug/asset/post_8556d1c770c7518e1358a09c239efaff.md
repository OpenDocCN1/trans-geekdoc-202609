### 3.5.2 Major issues that hinder hybrid model development

The main issues that are observed when designing efficient hybrid models are associated with the modulation of supervised and unsupervised learning components. The first item raised was the ability to guarantee that the unsupervised learning process induces relevant features and characteristics without interference from noisy patterns that are unrelated to the final learning. In addition, semisupervised learning models, in general, need more complex tuning and validation steps, as adding two learning approaches in one model can complicate it. This has the effect of making the choice of model architecture and parameters, as well as large-scale cross-validation to avoid overfitting and to obtain good performance on a range of material datasets, a complex issue.

It was likely that in the future, the biomedical materials hybrid machine learning models would apply more with other advanced methods, such as RL and transfer learning. Flexible semisupervised approaches that are capable of changing the proportion of supervised and unsupervised learning based on data stream input were one of the innovations. In addition, because of advances in computational abilities, other complex hybrid models that are able to handle larger and mixed information were developed. The result was better material designs and more profound information. Thorough exploration and enhancement of these blended approaches resulted in improved models that could enhance clinical use alongside the discovery of new knowledge within the sphere of biomedical materials.

## 4 Three Bayesian methods and probabilistic modeling

### 4.1 Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning in material models

Bayesian optimization is regarded as one of the most efficient techniques for hyperparameter tuning in machine learning models, particularly in domains where the model's performance is highly sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters. In materials science, hyperparameters play a critical role throughout the entire pipeline from modeling and feature attribution to material design and the analysis of structure–function relationships. Bayesian optimization treats hyperparameter tuning as a probabilistic search problem, typically modeling the objective function as a Gaussian process. It iteratively updates this model as new observations are made, thereby refining the search and identifying optimal hyperparameter configurations. ### 4.1.1 Application related to material science

From a material perspective, Bayesian optimization has been especially useful in model refinement for material property modeling and engineering. Since the evaluation of functions in experimental material science is generally costly and time-consuming, the benefits of Bayesian optimization in reducing the number of function evaluations are indeed critical. For instance, in contexts like developing predictive models for mechanical properties and thermal stability of materials, Bayesian optimization determines which set of hyperparameters, such as learning rates and regularization strengths, lead to improved and more reliable models. This is particularly essential where the search space is large and where grid search methods are unresponsive and computationally costly.

In this work, the following research methodology, based on Bayesian optimization, is used.

Specifically, there are several steps that need to be followed in the Bayesian optimization technique. It is typical to use a Gaussian process (GP) as a surrogate model to represent the relationship between the performance of models and the tunable parameters. Each time new data points (evaluations of the objective function) are received, the surrogate model is updated. The next set of hyperparameters to be considered for evaluation is selected using an acquisition function like the expected improvement. When the model reaches convergence, the model together with a set of hyperparameters is considered optimal. The strength of Bayesian optimization lies in its ability to balance exploration and exploitation; namely, it can efficiently navigate high-dimensional and complex spaces often observed in material science while also advancing into new regions of hyperparameter space, all the while building upon potentially promising areas.

### 4.1.2 Challenges and considerations

There are limitations inherently associated with Bayesian optimization when used in material models. The first obstacle was the problem related to the objective function to be minimized; when incorporating high-level material models, such as ab initio ones, these calculations could be time-consuming. This was the case with the objective function since its evaluation required recalculation of the misfit term each time the parameters of the material model were changed. The selection of the surrogate model, as well as the acquisition function, can affect the optimization process and the time and computational resources needed for the process. It was, therefore, important to guarantee that the surrogate model provided the correct structure of the objective function so as to optimize the process successfully. A further factor to discuss was the extension, as the BO methods' efficiency drops when many hyperparameters are involved in discrete searches.

### 4.2 Gaussian processes for predictive modeling of material properties

The GPs were one of the strong probabilistic modeling techniques used in predictive modeling, especially in the field of material science, where quantification of uncertainty was very important. Since GPs are nonparametric, they allow for a relatively free-form estimation of input variables and output material properties without imposing a specific architectural form. The strength of GPs was twofold: first, they were capable of predicting material properties like other models, and in addition, they could also estimate the level of uncertainty associated with the predictions, which makes them particularly useful in materials science where data is limited.

### 4.2.1 Application in material property prediction

In the area of material science, GPs have started to be used to make predictions regarding various characteristics of materials including elasticity, thermal conductivity, and strength. This makes GPs capable of handling uncertainties because experimental data, which is costly to obtain, is often limited. This demonstrates that the GP model is accurate in prediction even when the material data is sparse, and by extrapolation of the model, even materials whose underlying physical laws are not completely known can be evaluated for their properties. One of these capabilities is particularly useful in the search and identification of new materials with specific characteristics in the field of material science.

### 4.2.2 Methodological framework

The way GPs were implemented in predictive modeling was to specify a covariance function (or kernel) that defines the relationships between distinct material data points. The choice of the kernel was significant since it defines the smoothness of the function and the intricacies of the decision boundaries. Specific kernels more frequently used in material science are the radial basis function and other kernels that are equally effective when working with a number of material data sets. Once the kernel was chosen, the GP model was then trained with observed data, through which the GP model estimates the percentage probability density functions over the input space of material properties. The model then provides point estimates for new values of x along with intervals of uncertainty for the estimate. Due to this probabilistic nature of the GPs, there was a better chance of making the right decisions regarding material design and optimization.

### 4.2.3 Challenges and considerations

The use of GPs has many advantages, but there are also disadvantages, primarily associated with increased computational costs. The time complexity in the training of a GP model escalates with the training dataset size, and thus the model becomes less usable with very large material datasets. As you can see, selecting the kernel function also requires significant thought and expertise in the subject matter to ensure that the selected model accurately depicts the underlying physical events in the dataset. Furthermore, increased input field dimensionality brings the curse of dimensionality, which results in overfitting when extending GPs. To overcome these challenges, better approximations and algorithms need to be developed to extend the utility of GPs in material science.
