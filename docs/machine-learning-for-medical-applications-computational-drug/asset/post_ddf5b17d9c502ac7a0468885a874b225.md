### 5.5.3 Wearable sensors for internal recording
As for implantable sensors, it is possible to monitor internal processes of chronic conditions, for which accurate and long-term surveillance is needed. These are implantable sensors that can monitor parameters such as intracranial pressure, glucose, and heart function. Some of the conditions that are best monitored through implantable sensors are diabetes and heart diseases because they give real-time information that is very vital in the management of the diseases. Recent developments in biocompatibility of materials as well as the above-mentioned aspects of miniaturization have enhanced the safety and comfort of these devices, making them potential candidates for use in elderly patients.

### 5.5.4 Integrated health monitoring systems
Central monitoring systems are developed from a single patient-health-monitoring unit and include more sensors and technologies to produce an overall and detailed information of the patients’ state. Such systems comprise wearable devices, home-based sensors, and other telemonitoring methods for multiple health parameters’ monitoring. For instance, an integrated system consolidates sensors for heart rate, blood glucose, and fall to ensure that it covers all aspects. Such systems facilitate team work, in that they make it possible for several caregivers to view several facets of a patient’s condition and make decisions based on the information presented.

### 5.5.5 Fall-risk identification and mitigation systems
Many fall-detection and prevention systems are essential components of monitoring in geriatric settings for long-term care. In elderly people, fall-risks are prevalent and often result in severe consequences. Present-day automated fall-prevention devices incorporate accelerometers, gyroscopes, and pressure sensors to detect a fall and notify the caregiver services instantaneously. Further, certain systems offer features for assessing potential falls by analyzing successfully whether changes in gait can be made before a physical fall takes place. Such systems improve the level of safety and the possible level of autonomy for elderly people by offering the ability to respond to falls as soon as they happen.

## Environmental monitoring and integration of smart home
Environmental assessment and intelligent homes improve geriatric care by specifically reducing environmental hazards for the elderly. The home environment can be sensed through analyzers placed within a home to measure a variety of values such as room temperature, fresh air levels, and presence of hazardous items like gas, moisture. Health monitoring devices can be connected to home systems for smart home systems to deliver comprehensive care by adjusting the environment and notifying the caregivers about the problems. For instance, automated systems can control heating and cooling, depending on the health input and notify caretakers if an elderly person is inactive from the usual rhythm.

### 5.5.6 Challenges and considerations
Several challenges have to be considered when aiming for adopting long-term monitoring solutions in geriatric care – these include the ability of patients, their geriatric age, their comfort to use the devices, proper data privacy and security issues, and correct incorporation into the existing geriatric care practice. Also important are aspects such as possible psychological effects of constant supervision on the elderly and the necessity of caregivers’ training for the proper interpretation and utilization of the data received from the sensors.

Telemonitoring technologies for the elderly care have significant roles in the long-term management of illnesses in the elderly population. Specifically, these solutions offer enhanced, real-time, thus more effective, ongoing information and intervention, hence a positive impact on the specific group’s health.

## 6 Challenges in biomedical sensor development

### 6.1 Sensor accuracy and calibration issues
Peculiarities of biomedical sensors require close attention to issues of sensor accuracy and calibration as far as they determine the viability and efficiency of health monitoring systems. For getting more accurate health results that are credible for correct diagnosis, medical treatment, and subsequent handling of patients, it is crucial to provide high accuracy and correct calibration.

### 6.1.1 Role of precision on biomedical sensors
In this case, accuracy means how effectively the sensor has the capability to assess the physiological parameters without significant variation. Faulty sensors produce wrong information, which can contribute to wrong diagnoses and wrong treatments of health problems. For example, faulty glucose sensors cause wrong insulin measurements for diabetic patients, and so with wrong blood pressure measurements for cardiovascular disease patients. Hence, high accuracy and steady value outputs are core to the utility of biomedical sensors in clinical and personal healthcare applications.

### 6.1.2 Calibration procedures
Calibration refers to the process of aligning a sensor's output with a precise, measurable reference value. This involves using reference devices to compare and adjust the sensor's readings, ensuring continued accuracy over time. As shown in Figure 10, periodic calibration is essential to maintain sensor performance, especially in the face of environmental changes, component wear, and signal drift from original settings. The primary goal is to guarantee accurate data output, and when discrepancies occur, adjustments are made based on standard reference values. Regular calibration enhances the reliability of biomedical sensors and underscores the importance of robust, validated calibration procedures.

### 6.1.3 Sources of accuracy issues
There are various factors that can attest to accuracy problems in biomedical sensors. These include:

- Environmental factors: Fluctuations in temperature, humidity, and even electro-magnetic interference affect the sensor’s abilities. Biomedical sensors should be able to function properly, irrespective of the prevailing environmental characteristics, hence the need to design portable biomedical sensors.
- Biological variability: The sensor is influenced by the difference in physiology across individuals, be it the skin color of the tissues. There is biological variability of the human body that sensors should capture, and hence, sensors must be standardized.
- Sensor degradation: Sensors, as any other equipment, can also become damaged over time from exposure to bodily fluids and physical abrasion. This is primarily due to problems associated with ageing of the sensors and the need to recalibrate the system to reflect the changes as they occur.
- Interference from other substances: Chemical-type sensors respond not only to the quantity of interest but also to any chemical interferences in the sample. For instance, glucose sensors are affected by presence of other sugars in the blood stream. Such interference poses some challenges and it necessitates an effective calibration and correction algorithms.

### 6.1.4 The main challenges in calibration techniques
Calibration techniques for biomedical sensors can face several challenges, including:

- Complexity of calibration processes: Of course, there are sensors that need multiple-point calibration with certain equipment. These processes often tend to become complex and making them simple without compromising on the accuracy is difficult.
- Standardization issues: In order to manage heterogeneity, there is a need to agree on calibration procedures and the use of calibration metrics. A high level of variability means that sensors cannot be standardized, and this brings about differences in their operation and the interpretation of results supplied by these gadgets.
- Real-time calibration: However, in dynamic situations where reconfiguration is necessary, as in the cases of wearable sensors, calibration in real-time is a problem. Work is still underway to come up with ways of making real-time calibration without affecting the sensor functionality.
