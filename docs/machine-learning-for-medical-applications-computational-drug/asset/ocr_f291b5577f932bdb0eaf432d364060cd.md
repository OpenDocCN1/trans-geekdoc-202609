# 3.3.5 Challenges and innovations

While biocompatible polymers offer numerous advantages, they also present challenges that must be addressed through ongoing research.

Mechanical strength and durability: The challenge in the current and future development of these tissue scaffolds is to achieve both biocompatibility and the mechanical properties of the scaffold in near-equilibrium with those of the original tissue. New trends in polymer blending and reinforcement are being developed to improve the performance of biocompatible composites.

Degradation products: Polymer degradation has other by-products that should be tested to determine their effects before being used on human beings. Work on the development of polymeric materials with desirable and well-understood degradation characteristics is underway.

Customizability and control: Innovations in polymer chemistry and processing are intended to improve the control of the structure of biocompatible polymers to better match the desired functions of use.

# 3.4 Composite materials

Biomedical composites are considered to be a major improvement in the production of biocompatible materials because they bring desirable properties into the material mix, enhancing their functionality. These materials are developed by processing two different materials to produce a form of material with properties not inherent in the two separate materials.

In the biomedical field, composite materials are mainly used to merge the features of several materials such as metals, ceramics, and polymers, into a single material system. Such flexibility enables the control of mechanical, chemical, and biological properties in order to satisfy the needs of different medical uses, including deformation, strength, and toughness, as well as the relationships between processing variables and performance.

Composite materials are designed by their constituents to have physical characteristics that are fit for specific uses. For instance, in orthopedic implants and prosthetics, composites can be tailor-made with mechanical properties closer to those of human bones, thus supporting the structure without contributing to implant failure. Such applications utilize carbon fiber-reinforced polymers and hydroxyapatite-based composites, as these materials have high strength-to-weight ratios and also encourage bone formation.
