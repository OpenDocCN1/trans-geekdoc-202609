Concerns of fairness and equity are then posed by the likelihood of individuals with certain genetic makeup for particular diseases getting locked out of the job market or locked out of insurance cover. To tackle this issue there are currently many legislations and laws around the world such as the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) in United States that seeks to protect individuals from discrimination due to their genetic background.

Informed consent is another important ethical consideration in individualized medicine. Due to the sheer amount and density of genetic information, patients need to be informed of the meaning of genetic testing and the potential uses of this data. In order to achieve true informed consent, it is necessary to safeguard that population that has equal access to comprehensible information on possible advantages and disadvantages of genetic testing. Also there is need to make patients aware of incidental findings, which are genetic findings different from the intended diagnosis that has a large impact on the patient’s health.

One of the limitations is the privacy issue relating to the need to mark certain genotypes, which are deemed sensitive, so as to protect them from misuse and unauthorized access. The concern regarding the leak of sensitive information is enhanced by the rising trend of handling and storing health information in digital format and the popularity of cloud storage solutions. Certain essential security standards like grabbing, limiting the access to the genetic information, and the use of secure protocols in transferring it have to be implemented vigorously. In addition to this, to protect the individual affected person’s identity and encourage research and innovation, there are requirements to prescribe procedure for transfer of information between researchers and healthcare providers.

Autonomy means freedom in patients’ right to make choices concerning their treatment and care. Personalized medicine just contradicts this principle by presenting the patient with numerous factors, which can hardly be interpreted by the patient. The healthcare givers have the duty of keeping their expertise in mind, while recognizing that patients are tested to assist them in gaining the knowledge required in order to make choices that are congruent with their belief systems.

Another problem that has repeatedly emerged in the literature is the issue of fair distribution of personalized primary healthcare services. They also suggest that inequality in access to genetic testing and other personalized treatments actually increase existing disparities in healthcare, as underrepresented groups could be left out of some of the benefits of personalization. Making personalized care available across everyone is important not only in terms of revenue generating but also to take care of any imbalance in terms of revenue level regions predefined by a previously set goal.
