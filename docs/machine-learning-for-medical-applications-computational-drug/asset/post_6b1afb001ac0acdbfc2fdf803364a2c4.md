### 4.2.4 Biodegradable implants for drug delivery

Medication was administered directly at the affected site through resorbable depot systems, which disintegrate within the body. The most preferred material for constructing these implants was the biocompatible and bioresorbable material that includes PLA. For this reason, these systems decrease fluctuations in systemic exposure and do away with the need for multiple injections by dispersing the medication at the point of injury. Some of these uses include cancer therapy, where the implant offers continuous and local drug administration in the affected area, hormone replacement therapy, and birth control implants.

### 4.2.5 Injectable drug delivery systems

Injectable drug delivery systems involve the use of biodegradable and biocompatible polymers to administer drugs with minimal invasiveness. Such systems can include injectable hydrogels, microcapsular systems, or delivery via syringe. It is relatively easy to administer injectable formulations, fewer operations are required to carry them out, and it is possible to administer the drug continuously. These systems are especially beneficial in delivering biologics and vaccines, which require precise dosages and slow or sustained release.

Incorporation of biocompatible materials in drug delivery systems has enhanced the pharmaceutical field in various ways and opened new doors to deliver effective treatments and achieve better results. These systems ensure that many of the drawbacks and limitations of conventional delivery technologies are overcome, leading to improved health outcomes and better patient compliance.

## 4.3 Tissue engineering

In regenerative medicine, tissue engineering is an innovative approach that seeks to address this problem by reconstructing damaged or lost tissues and organs using comparable materials. This discipline is an intersection of engineering, materials science, and biology, aiming to construct functional tissue structures that can overcome the limitations of other therapies. The biocompatibility of some of these materials is crucial in tissue engineering because they serve as matrices, scaffolds, and carriers for the formation of new tissue and cell growth, respectively.

### 4.3.1 Scaffold design and function

Scaffolds are considered fundamental units in tissue engineering, providing physical support for cells to adhere, grow, and differentiate. Selection of proper biocompatible materials to possess the correct mechanical properties, porosity, and biodegradability was a major concern to be addressed during the scaffold designing phase. Due to their controlled degradation rates, structural flexibility, and selectivity for specific tissue types, polymers such as PLA, PGA, and their copolymers are commonly used. To enable native tissue formation and remodeling to progress uneventfully and for the scaffold to be effectively integrated into the host tissue, the scaffold must be ECM-mimetic.

### 4.3.2 Bioprinting and advanced fabrication techniques

Bioprinting and fabrication have advanced tissue engineering through innovation and enhanced approaches. Tissues with highly defined tissue organization can then be built by laying down compatible materials, cells, and bioprinting. Due to layer-on-layer deposition, researchers create three-dimensional structures of the scaffolds that closely resemble the natural tissue architecture. These technologies allow the biological properties of the scaffold to be altered effectively and enable the incorporation of various types of cells, which naturally promote improved tissue integration and functionality.

### 4.3.3 Cell-seeding and growth factors

The correct approach in this regard is the proper planting of cells on scaffolding and the application of growth factors that promote tissue growth, which are also requirements of tissue engineering. Biocompatible materials used in the construction of scaffolds must encourage cell adhesion and proliferation for the construct to be biologically functional. Antifibrotic molecules, such as ECM proteins, can be incorporated into the scaffold material to enhance the development of new tissue and levels of cell activity. This procedure allows for the generation of physiological tissue constructs that can be compared with real tissues.

### 4.3.4 Applications in regenerative medicine

Tissue engineering has demonstrated potential in a wide variety of applications within the regenerative medicine field. For example, engineered skin grafts and dermal substitutes have been developed for managing burn injuries and chronic wounds with better therapeutic efficacy than conventional approaches. In orthopedics, tissue-engineered bone and cartilage grafts are being created to fix defects, offering advantages over donor tissues in terms of recovery. Advances in tissue engineering also support the construction of novel functional tissues, including bioengineered heart valves and vessels, which could address challenges associated with organ donation.

### 4.3.5 Challenges and future directions

Up to date, there are some concerns that hinder advancement in the tissue engineering field. Maintaining the functionality and compatibility of the engineered tissues in the long term is a major consideration as well as their manufacturability and approval for use. Additionally, efforts are focused on achieving efficient vascularization of tissue constructs because tissue survival and function in the body depend on blood circulation. Regarding the limitations of existing work, this remains the primary area of focus in further research, as new materials have yet to be examined, fabrication methodologies need to be improved, and a variety of tissue types have yet to be successfully reconstructed.
