
Technique can be implemented with many machine learning algorithms, such as NN, SVM, and decision trees, in order to improve accuracy of the prediction and find additional patterns in the data source.

Boosting is another method of ensemble learning, which requires using a number of weak learners several times, and putting their solutions together to form one powerful model. Each one effectively increases the overall performance of the ensemble by focusing on the cases where the earlier models misclassified. Each weak learner is gradually improved. Since gradient boosting methods can support the high-order and nonlinear connections, with various data types, they are applied for hit recognition tasks. Some of these algorithms are XGBoost and LightGBM.

Stacking, often referred to as meta-learning, is a flexible method of ensemble learning that employs the outcomes of many base models integrated as input features of a meta-learner. The idea is in learning the meta-learner that combines the base models' predictions in a way that takes the advantage and mitigates the disadvantages of the base models. For further improvement of integrated hit detection performance, stacking can be used to combine results of multiple machine learning methods, models specific to the chemical domain, and other heuristics and rules based on expert knowledge.

Also, the identification of hit for the development of drugs incorporates random forest, which is an ensemble learning method generated by the decision tree. In random forests, more decision trees are created from randomly selected characteristics and samples to come up with the final result. The use of random forests is suitable to solve detection of hits with high-dimensional data and complicated relations but combined prediction of many decision trees increases prediction accuracy and decreases the probability of overfitting.

## 3.5 Feature engineering for improving hit identification models

Feature engineering works as a veritable way of enriching hit identification models in drug discovery by incorporating known feature sets from raw data and developing features that can help make a model function optimally. In hit identification, the focus mainly lies on feature quality and relevance, which influences the predictive accuracy and reliability of employing machine learning models.

This can be implemented through the extraction of molecular descriptors from chemical compounds in the case of hit identification. Optimized molecular descriptors relate to the size, shape, polar, and electronic character of compounds within the set, which are known to have an impact on their bioactivity. Molecular descriptors are some of the information handles that can be extracted from molecular data; these include molecular weight, topological indices, and pharmacophore fingerprints. When using molecular descriptors that describe the molecular structures of interested compounds in a study, the use of the features in machine learning models help in improving accuracy of predictions.

In the construction of the hit identification models, feature selection approaches are adopted to determine the most relevant and worthy features. It also means that feature selection makes the model less complex, easier to interpret, and improves its predictive abilities as well as rejects unnecessary features that clutter the set. Some of the feature selection techniques include filter methods, wrapper methods, and embedded methods that assess the features based on utilization of statistical measures – the learning algorithm of the intrinsic kind.

Fundamental knowledge and specific experiences are employed to design informative features that include the mechanisms and chemical characteristics linked with the drug activity. For example, a particular chemical molecular substructure, which is observed to bind to a particular biological receptor to produce desired pharmacological effect can be formulated as a feature. In the same way, structure alerts suggesting toxicity can also be introduced for enhancing the model selectivity and safety.

Normalization, scaling, and other operations and transformation functions are used to preprocess data in order to make it fit for use in machine learning. Data transformation increases feature interpretability and helps avoid the impact of skewed distribution of number of outliers in the learning process and the model. For example, taking logarithm of skewed continuous variables or scaling measures of the variables within 0 and 1 makes learning more effective.

## 4 AI Applications in molecular docking and binding affinity prediction

### 4.1 Deep learning models for protein-ligand binding prediction

In molecular docking and binding affinity prediction, deep learning models have emerged as potent tools in enhancing the accuracy of prediction of interactions of ligands and proteins. Conventional methods of machine learning fail to accurately estimate binding affinities as more complex interactions in protein and ligand structures can be identified by deep learning using neural networks with multiple layers of abstraction.

CNN is one of the widely used deep learning structures in protein-ligand interaction prediction. CNNs are particularly effective in detecting regional features and peculiarities in protein-ligand complexes designed for analyzing spatial information, including molecular structures. CNNs can learn the hierarchical representation of the protein-ligand interactions and extract the proper features for the binding affinity prediction as a result of applying filters for convolution to the molecular input representations. While making an accurate prediction of binding affinities and identifying these interaction residues in the binding site of the protein, the CNN-based models have been shown to be superior.

GNN was another deep learning technique in the prediction of protein-ligand interaction. Since chemical structures are graphs and GNNs are designed to work with graph structured data, GNNs are ideal for this purpose. GNNs are able to predict the binding affinities and also simultaneously capture the chemical and spatial interactions of the protein-ligand interface interaction by compounding information about atoms and bonds in the proximity. The assessment of GNN-based models shows potential in shedding light on the structural aspects of protein-ligand interactions and achieving reliable binding conformations.

The RNNs and long short-term memory (LSTM) network have been used in procedures in which predictions of interaction between protein ligand are involved. Since RNNs and especially LSTMs allow modeling time dependencies and sequence properties it is reasonable to use it to model dynamic processes such as ligand binding and unbinding. Using sequential data representations of protein-ligand complexes, RNN-based models can forecast binding affinities and the poses of ligands with increased precision when targeting flexible complexes of ligands.

The information- and data-fusion approach using a combination of CNNs, GNNs, and RNNs was used for protein-ligand binding predictions. These hybrid models use the advantages of more than one network architecture to create rich representation of protein-ligand interactions and optimize the prediction accuracy. The current work demonstrates the effectiveness of the hybrid deep learning models in achieving better accuracy and reliable binding affinity and binding pose predictions for drug discovery.

### 4.2 Structure-based virtual screening using ML algorithms

An important component of drug repurposing comprises structure-based virtual screening, which relies on the use of chemical databases to predict how well a novel candidate is expected to bind to a target protein. The machine learning techniques in structure-based virtual screening have enhanced the efficiency and accuracy to a significant level.

Support Vector Machines (SVMs) are among the most widely used machine learning techniques in structure-based virtual screening (VS). SVMs classify compounds as active or inactive based on features derived from chemical structures. In VS applications, SVMs are trained on datasets containing known active and inactive compounds to identify patterns that govern ligand binding. Using these patterns, SVMs can predict the likelihood that new compounds will interact with a target protein, based on the correlation between their molecular descriptors and bioactivity. Structure-based virtual screening also uses another machine learning approach known as the random forests. Random forests are trained for virtual screening that predicts a compound binding affinity to a target protein by categorizing it according to its molecular fingerprints. Decision tree overfitting can be prevented by the use of random forests because the results of several decision trees are combined together in order to enhance prediction rates.
