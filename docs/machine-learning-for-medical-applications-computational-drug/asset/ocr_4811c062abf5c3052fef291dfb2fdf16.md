# 6 Challenges and opportunities in implementing ML in drug delivery optimization

# 6.1 Current challenges in adopting ML-driven approaches in drug delivery

The fact is that there is much potential in using ML for medication delivery optimization. At the same time, there are also many challenges that have to be addressed to get the desired result. Some of these challenges include issues of data, model development and calibration, regulatory frameworks, and model implementation in clinical setting.

The first major challenge identified by the team relates to the quality and quantity of data required to train and validate machine learning models for drug delivery optimization. These models depend on large-scale datasets that include comprehensive information such as patient profiles, genetic markers, disease pathologies, treatment outcomes, and drug distribution patterns. Such datasets covering a full spectrum of variables across the target population are often scarce, incomplete, or fragmented across different institutions and research studies. One of the most significant hurdles is acquiring high-quality, standardized data that can be securely shared across platforms and research networks. Moreover, the sensitivity of health and personal data introduces privacy and confidentiality challenges, necessitating robust data protection frameworks and ethical guidelines for responsible use. At the same time, there are a number of issues that must be considered as critical for biopharmaceutical recovery, Prediction of drug effects and biopharmaceutical behavior, in reality, is another major problem area, because biological systems are among the most difficult ones to model, and drug interactions in the human body are highly versatile and, therefore, hard to predict. Essentially, creating an ML model for drugs requires assignment of many factors and their correlations for effectiveness and delivery. This necessitates the use of complex algorithms and great compute power, to be practical. In fact, building models intended to mimic the kinetic and complex interconnectivity of biological systems is challenging and sometimes constrained by a lack of understanding of the biological processes in diseases that are being modeled.

Model validation and model interpretability restrict the process and pose further challenges. It is important to guarantee the validity and reliability of ML-based solutions, in conjunction with patients’ generalization capabilities, for clinical implementation. Nonetheless, testing these models against real-world clinical data requires confronting variability inherent to different clinical practices, and patients’ responses are not straightforward. Many advanced ML methods, particularly the well-publicized deep learning, are opaque “black boxes,” which offer the user little understanding of how they reach given predictions or recommendations.
