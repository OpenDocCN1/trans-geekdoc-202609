## Optical sensors

Optical sensors operate by detecting the interaction between light and biological tissues. These sensors utilize various optical techniques such as absorption, fluorescence, and reflection to measure physiological parameters. As illustrated in Figure 5, each method enables the noninvasive monitoring of specific biological signals with high precision.

Pulse oximetry: Probably the most widespread example of optical sensors is pulse oximetry that assesses blood oxygen saturation and pulse frequency. In most of the cases, this system employs red and infrared light to contrast between both forms of hemoglobin within the bloodstream.

OCT: OCT is used as an imaging modality, which does not involve making contact with the tissue sample and offers quality tomographic imaging of higher resolution of the biological tissue. It is widely used in ophthalmology for imaging of retina and in cardiology for imaging the coronary arteries.

Fluorescence sensors: These sensors work by identifying particular biomarkers through the measure of the fluorescence of a sample, which was earlier exposed to a particular wavelength. In diagnostics, they are employed in assays to detect tumor markers in the patient’s body.

NIRS: NIRS employs near-infrared light to probe the tissues to assess the concentrations of tissue oxygenation and hemoglobin. It is used in tracking brain and muscle oxygenation, especially during exercise.

Optical biosensors: These sensors integrate optical structures with biological receptors that selectively recognize target biological entities, for example, for glucose measuring in diabetes mellitus; given analytes in body fluids.

## Chemical sensors

Chemical sensors are used to measure the concentration of specific chemical species in living organisms. Most importantly, they are used to measure biomarkers and other chemical indices that are concerning to health.

Electrochemical sensors: Electrochemical sensors quantitatively determine the concentration of molecules from the changes of the underlying electrical characteristics, including current. Some of the applications include glucose for diabetes monitoring and lactate sensor for metabolic monitoring.

Enzyme-based sensors: These sensors employ certain enzymes to catalyze reactions with the target analytes to form changes in signals that can be measured. Amperometric glucose sensors employing enzymes to catalyze the oxidation of glucose are well suited for ambulatory monitoring of blood glucose in diabetic patients.

Chemical implants: Devices like intracranial pressure sensor are examples of implanted sensors that rely on chemical sensing for real-time monitoring within the body. These sensors utilize free-running electrochemical sensors.

Colorimetric sensors: Qualitative sensors identify an analyte using color changes as a result of reactions between the analyte and the indicator. They are applied in different analyses nowadays, the well-known are in urine analysis and infection detection.

Chemical array sensors: These sensors employ diverse chemical sensing components to quantify and identify multiple analytes at once. Some of these are diagnostics by sensing the breath for diseases and monitoring the condition of the environment, among others.

## Mechanical sensors

Physical sensors record changes within biological systems. Previous to analysis for advanced pathophysiology, they were utilized to check parameters that are connected to physical health and mechanical operations of the human body.

Strain gauges: Strain gauges are used to measure mechanical deformation by forces applied to a specimen. In biomedical applications, they are incorporated in wearable gadgets in order to track movements of joint as well as muscle activity.

Pressure sensors: These sensors determine the pressure of liquids as well as gases in the human body. Most frequently, their reliance can be identified in cases of tracking blood pressure, intracranial pressure, and respiratory pressures.

Force sensors: Strain gauges measure the level of force applied on any biological part. They are applied in prosthetic and orthotic practice to evaluate the effectiveness and make adjustment of mechanical limb substitutes and orthoses.

Accelerometers: Accelerometers record the velocity and acceleration; the information involves a device's motion and position. Along with health monitoring, they are incorporated into wearable gadgets to track movement, gait, and falls of old people.

Tactile sensors: Tactile sensors capture the force being applied on a certain plane. For instance, they are used in surgery using robots to control prosthetic limbs to get force feedback and information on how tight a grip is.

All types of sensors, optical, chemical, and mechanical, are valuable within the biomedical monitoring discipline since they generate important information to diagnose, control, and enhance the health of the patients. These sensors function in such a way that they give a holistic view of healthcare monitoring and physiology of the human body.

## 4 Technological advancements in sensor design

### 4.1 Flexible and stretchable electronics in biomedical sensors

Biomedical sensors have come under dramatic improvement in their design with the advent of flexible and stretchable electronics. These innovations overcome the defects of conventional brittle electronics to improve sensor efficiency, as well as broaden the scope of seamless health check.

### 4.1.1 Flexible and stretchable electronics: An overview

Wearable and conformable electronics are electronic devices designed to adapt to various shapes and movements. They use materials such as organic semiconductors, conductive polymers, and flexible substrates to create fully functional electronics that can bend, twist, and fold. Integrating these materials into biomedical sensors enables the development of wearable and implantable devices that are more comfortable, versatile, and equipped with enhanced functionality.

### 4.1.2 Advantages of flexible electronics in biomedical application of sensors

Enhanced comfort and wearability: Conformal sensors are made possible through flexible electronics, eliminating irritation that hampers patient adherence with most monitors today. This is crucial in the case of wearable sensors, especially those that require the subjects to have them on for protracted durations.

Improved sensor integration: First of all, the characteristics of stretchable and flexible sensors enable end-users to incorporate them into numerous garments and accessories. By integrating these features, the user can constantly be monitored without having to wear any equipment.

Durability and longevity: Compared with a stiff sensor, flexible electronics offer more adaptability and are less easily affected by mechanical stress. The increased durability is a plus for longevity of the sensors showing potential for good health monitoring for the long term.

Adaptability to dynamic environments: Bendable sensors can continue to be useful while conforming to the shape changes in the human body, for example, when stretching. Such flexibility is important in achieving the correct measurement of the parameters like heart rate and muscle activity during exercises.

In the following sections, key technologies in flexible electronics are described as follows:

Conductive polymers: Conductive polymers, including polyaniline and PEDOT, are employed to design and fabricate stretchable and bendable electronic devices with further enhanced electric conductivity and mechanical compliance. These materials are essential for the realization of pliable sensor electrodes and interconnects.

Organic semiconductors: For similar reasons of flexibility and low weight, organic semiconductors like OLEDs and organic photovoltaic are used in flexible electronic devices. They support the fabrication of reconfigurable multipurpose sensors with electrical and optical properties.

Flexible substrates: These material types include polyimide, polyethylene terephthalate, and PDMS that form the platform for flexible electronics substrates. Some of these materials are chosen for their mechanical flexibility, some for chemical stability, and some for compatibility with electronic devices.

Hybrid materials: Composites based on inorganic and organic phases are employed in optimizing the characteristics of flexible sensors. These materials exploit the beneficial features of both types to design sensors with enhanced electric/magnetic and mechanical response characteristics.

### 4.1.3 Uses of flexible electronics for biomedical sensors

Wearable health monitoring devices: Wearable electronics include flexible smartwatches, fitness trackers, and flexible health-monitoring patches that can be worn on the skin, employing physiological parameters, include heart rate, body temperature, and sweat rates.

Flexible biosensors: A biosensor that comprises an ultralow power flex circuit can be adopted for biomarker and pollutant monitoring. Examples include glucose and lactate sensors, and electrochemical sensors for the detection of different analytes.

Implantable devices: Flexible electronics can be applied to implantable sensors in monitoring glucose level of diabetic patients and intracranial pressure of neurological disorder patients. Due to portability, these devices reduce tissue injury of the patient, which in turn improves their level of comfort.

Smart textiles: Conductive ink and flexible electronics enable creating intelligent clothing by integrating textile electronics that can measure key physiological signs and give signals to the wearers. Some of them are outfits that include athletic apparel, which contain sensors for performance tracking alongside health checks.
