Closing the digital divide is therefore a core critical success factor to achieving the goals of global health. Thus, many regions, especially LMICs, are unable to access more sophisticated biomedical technologies because of lack of amenities such as roads, very low internet access, and lack of skilled personnel. Figure 24 further elaborates the world’s health and access of people, distinguishing the demand for universal healthcare and access to products in different parts of the world. Developing such devices involves understanding the real-world constraints of the environments in which the device is to be deployed, and then developing low-cost, rugged, and easy-to-use devices that effective even in such environments. Likewise, attempts at enhancing the digital environment, such as the extension of the internet connection or proper use of information and communications technology, are critical in making certain that socially excluded populace is not left behind in profiting from the extensions of modern-day technologies in the delivery of healthcare.

Cost effectiveness is another strategic factor that would enable making biomedical sensors and devices available to the larger society. Many healthcare technologies, due to their high costs, are only limited to the cash-rich health sectors and regions. To this, new strategies such as flexibility in device design, standardization and integration, and outsourcing with local manufactures can be employed to make the phones more affordable. Solutions that can be easily extended for the given purposes are significant as well in order to achieve the implementation of technologies for different contexts of the healthcare system.

Special consideration is made for the desire to implement inclusive design to allow biomedical devices to be utilized by as many people as possible from diverse backgrounds with disabilities.
