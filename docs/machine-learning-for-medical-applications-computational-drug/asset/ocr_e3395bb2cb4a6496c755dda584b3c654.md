# 1 Introduction

Biomedical sensors are a rapidly growing subfield of the broader sensor technology domain, with advances in material science, including miniaturized and networked devices within the last few decades [1]. The first biomedical sensors that were developed were large, non-intelligent, and applicable only to narrow functions such as glucose detection and estimation of heart rate [2, 3]. Most of these sensors needed frequent calibration and maintenance; deploying these sensors into everyday healthcare practices was thus difficult [4]. Due to innovations in modern technology, there are now better and less-intrusive sensors [5, 6]. It has become possible to have “chronic tracking” of many physiological parameters in real time due to incorporation of current sensors into wearables and implants [7, 8]. Such a development advances the applicability of using health data and enhances the reliability and consistency of health data for constant health check-ups, which have now become beneficial and significant in the advanced areas of present-day healthcare services [9].

As for biomedical sensors, their application of AI has greatly enhanced both their abilities and impacts on health monitoring [10, 11]. In the context of Big Data and their processing and analysis, several AI technologies are more relevant, including machine learning, and deep learning [12]. Employing these advanced techniques, AI-based sensors can determine features and interconnections in the collected information that escape the traditional approaches [13]. This capability enables enhanced and timely assessment of a patient’s health status and subsequently the possibility to diagnose previously unidentified problems as well as shape individualized care plans [14]. It is also able to enhance the operation of sensors since they learn from patient needs and thus enhance the efficiency of health monitoring devices [15]. Consequently, artificial intelligence drives substantial progress in the development of biomedical sensors from enhancing the human body monitor for discrete, occasional checks to a method of constant health monitoring [16, 17].

Although great advancement has been achieved with biomedical sensors powered by the AI technology, there are still some challenges that need to be addressed fully to enhance the capabilities and application of biomedical sensors [18]. One of the main challenges is to guarantee the correctness and reliability of measurements provided by the sensors in a number of diverse real-life scenarios [19]. Pertinent factors like sensor calibration, the level of noise within the signal, and issues with interference can have a direct bearing on the quality of data collected and this explains why further research toward better sensor designs as well as noise reduction methods [20, 21] is needed. The practical issues arising from a multiple sensor system, which is combined into a health monitoring system, are technical challenges [22].
