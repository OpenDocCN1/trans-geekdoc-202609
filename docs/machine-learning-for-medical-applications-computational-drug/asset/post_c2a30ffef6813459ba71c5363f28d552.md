
## 5.2 Performance metrics

Evaluation measures are very important in assessing the performance of developed machine learning models, especially in drug toxicity prediction. They provide information about model quality, including accuracy, precision, and how well the given model handles imbalanced sets of data. These metrics help in selecting and improving the models and their parameters to meet the specific demands of toxicity prediction.

Figure 17: Performance metrics.

Accuracy pertains to the proportion of correctly predicted cases to all cases. It provides a measure of the overall correctness of predictions. However, accuracy by itself can be misleading, especially when there is an unequal distribution of data points across some classifications.

The accuracy of a given model was described by the number of valid positive predictions out of all positive predictions. When the cost of false positives was high, as in the case of medication toxicity where average nontoxic chemicals are erroneously classified as toxic, thereby necessitating further investigations, this was important.

Recall shows how effective the model is in identifying all the related cases. This was significant for achieving the primary goal of identifying, to the largest extent possible, true positives, even at the cost of having more false positives. In drug toxicity prediction, high recall is desirable to guarantee that all toxic effects are predicted without the suppression of any possible toxic compounds.

Specifically, both recall and accuracy are evaluated to develop the F1 measure. This means that it presents a balance between the ability to achieve high accuracy of the model with respect to true positives and the ability of the model to identify all the relevant occurrences, as it indexes this entire measurement by a single score. In Figure 17, multiple characteristics are demonstrated to assess the efficiency and effectiveness of the machine learning algorithms. When evaluating models where it was impossible to achieve high accuracy and recall at the same time, the F1 score proved rather valuable.

The true positive rate and the false positive rate at the varying thresholds were displayed by the ROC curve. The averaged accuracy of the positive class versus the negative class was used to assess the model's overall ability to classify between the two classes using AUC. Finally, AUC stands for Area Under the Curve, which means that a model that does a better job of distinguishing between harmful and nonharmful substances has a high AUC value.

Another detailed measure of a model's performance is the so-called confusion matrix, which contains the number of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. It is from this matrix that other performance measures are derived, and these can help in determining where the model went wrong.

True positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives are the four outcomes of predictions that the Matthews Correlation Coefficient considers to provide a balanced accuracy of a model. It is very useful in evaluating models when assessed on imbalanced data and provides a richer measure of how well models perform compared to accuracy alone.

In particular, logarithmic loss was used to evaluate the accuracy of probabilistic forecasts by comparing the difference between the expected probability and the realized outcomes. Lower log loss values indicate better performance of the model on various probabilistic predictions, as it brings the probability estimations closer to the actual outcomes of the model.

The Brier score assesses the accuracy of probabilistic forecasts by finding the mean square of the difference between the forecast probability and the observed outcome. Lower values indicate higher calibration of the probability estimates. It evaluates the extent to which the predicted probabilities align with the realities of the findings.

These performance evaluation metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of the machine learning models, offering a measure of the models' utility in predicting drug toxicity. The selection of these metrics, depending on the particular problem, makes it possible to assess and develop models in the most effective way required for further usage.

## 5.3 Hyperparameter tuning

The most important point in the tuning phase of an ML model is called hyperparameter tuning, which is the procedure to select the most effective hyperparameters.

Often known as hyperparameters, these values are assigned prior to model training and do not undergo modification based on learned data. For predictive tasks like medication toxicity prediction, the unavoidable settings of such parameters are an obligatory step to achieve good results, as they affect the training process and the accuracy of models.

Parameters like the strength of the regularization, learning rate, and model complexity of the machine learning algorithms are all hyperparameters. In neural networks, the number of layers and units that exist, the type and parameters of the kernel in support vector machines, and the number of trees and depth in random forests are some of the best-known hyperparameters. It can therefore be seen that even the choice of hyperparameters affects the ability of the model to fit the data “too well,” and to generalize to new datasets.

### Grid Search
Grid search is an exhaustive approach where each possibility of hyperparameters is examined in combination with each of the fixed hyperparameter values. Generally, by applying this strategy, a grid of hyperparameter values is defined, the model is trained on each of them, and the combination that provides maximal performance according to some measure is selected. It is depicted in Figure 18 how hyperparameters affect the performance of a model during the training process; in other words, hyperparameters help improve the performance of a model through optimal training tuning. Even though grid search is performed, there is high complexity involved with a lot of hyperparameters, and the process can be computationally costly and time-consuming.

The first approach to the setup of the hyperparameters was called random search, in which the values of hyperparameters are chosen randomly according to a pre-determined distribution. Random search was computationally less intensive compared to grid search, where not all possible combinations are compared. Sometimes it produces better results, in addition to being more effective than a grid search at navigating through the hyperparameter space, especially when there are many hyperparameters.

### Bayesian optimization
This technique utilized probabilistic models to guide the search for the best hyperparameters. Finally, Bayesian optimization was very efficient in searching for the best hyperparameters by progressively constructing a probabilistic model that estimates the utility of different regions of the hyperparameter space. Unlike grid and random search, this method optimizes both exploration and exploitation simultaneously in a bid to find the best hyperparameters with the least amount of evaluation.

### Gradient-Based Optimization
A selection of hyperparameters that can be optimized with gradient-based methods includes learning rates in neural networks. These include what is known as gradient computations of hyper-parameter values in relation to the performance of the model in question. Performance-based ranking is especially helpful for hyperparameters, whereby they are optimized to an optimal value selected from a continuous array.

### Automated Machine Learning (AutoML)
AutoML systems naturally integrate hyperparameter tuning algorithms into their loops. Such frameworks combine grid search and random search, as well as nonconvex functions such as Bayesian optimization, to create an automated process that performs hyperparameter tuning alongside model selection and preprocessing. AutoML has been helpful in tasks such as hyperparameter optimization with reduced intervention and the enhancement of model performance.
