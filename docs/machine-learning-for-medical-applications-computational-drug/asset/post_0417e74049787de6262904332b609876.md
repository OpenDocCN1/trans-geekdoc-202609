Stacking provides several benefits in the context of drug toxicity prediction:

Improved Accuracy: Stacking is when the output of several base models is combined in such a way that the accuracy of the predictions is higher compared to the accuracy of predictions from each model separately. This is particularly helpful in drug safety, considering the fact that precise prediction of side effects is important.

Robustness to Overfitting: Ensemble combination techniques decrease the likelihood of developing an overly complicated model, as several models with different characteristics are created and incorporated. This is a diverse approach that assists in generalizing better on unseen data.

Flexibility in Model Choice: It makes it possible to use base models from different classes so that different kinds of algorithms, which account for diverse empirical aspects, can be used. For instance, integrating models that perform especially well when applied to linear interactions and models that handle nonlinear interactions improves performance.

Despite its advantages, stacking presents several challenges:

Computational Complexity: Training a meta-learner and several base learners can require a significant amount of computational resources, especially when working with large datasets and complex algorithms. Good organization and utilization of resources, as well as putting them into practice, are essential.

Risk of Overfitting: Though stacking can control for overfitting, the meta-learner model is prone to overfitting if the validation test is not well executed. The meta-learner should, therefore, be able to generalize well to new data, and cross-validation techniques must be used to achieve this.

Model Selection: L1 base models and a meta-learner were key to the success of stacking with L2 models. The efficiency of the ensemble highly depends on the choice and placement of these models.

Stacking continues to be an effective method of combining different models in machine learning to improve basic predictions. Its use in predicting drug toxicity makes it evident that it has the capacity to enhance safety evaluation and prevent adverse drug reactions.

## 4.4 Voting classifiers

Voting classifiers offer a simple form of ensemble learning to improve classification accuracy: Instead of relying on a single model’s prediction, voting classifiers use multiple models. In Figure 14, depicting the voting classifiers, a certain number of models contribute to the final prediction in an ensemble method, either by voting or majority rule. Voting classifiers take advantage of the variety among the different models by combining the outputs of many base classifiers to arrive at a final decision.

Majority Voting (Hard Voting): In majority voting, the class with the highest vote count is chosen as the final prediction. Every classifier in the ensemble provides its opinion on which class should be assigned. This method uses a simple majority rule and was earlier most efficient when the base classifiers are diverse and effective. Majority voting is particularly important when models have distinct advantages and disadvantages, as their combined results offer a reasonable outcome.

![](img/211c754cd1dd84920a171f497b33b689_375_0.png)

Figure 14: Voting classifiers.

Averaging (Soft Voting): Finally, the last prediction was made by considering the class with the highest average probability value. Soft voting consists of averaging the predicted probabilities of all the classes for all base classifiers. This method can enhance the model's capability to handle uncertainty and was useful while working with probabilistic data. When confidence in the predictions of the base classifiers differs, “soft” voting turns out to be better than “hard” voting.

Weighted Voting: In this method, every base classifier is assigned a weight corresponding to its accuracy as well as its consistency. The sum of the weighted votes of the maximum response from each classifier gives the final result. This technique allows for every single model’s performance to be incorporated into the voting process, which in turn enhances the functionality of the entire ensemble. There is also the possibility of weighting based on measures such as the F1 score or accuracy.

Stacked Generalization (Stacking): Even though stacking is not a voting approach as such, it involves training a meta-classifier on the predictions of several base classifiers. In stacking, a higher-level model derives characteristics from base models and uses them to make the final decision. This strategy can work better than elementary voting methods by leveraging the advantages of several models.

Voting classifiers have notable applications in predicting drug toxicity and side effects:

Combining Diverse Models: When it comes to enhancing prediction capability, voting classifiers are capable of incorporating a variety of base model forms, such as decision trees, support vector machines, neural networks, among others. Voting classifiers can handle more data patterns in the voting process and improve overall toxicity prediction by utilizing multiple diverse models on the data fed to the algorithm.

Handling Class Imbalances: In drug toxicity prediction, where adverse effects are significantly less frequent than nontoxic outcomes, voting classifiers can aid in addressing class imbalance, as different voting classifiers handle them differently. This aggregation is useful to ensure that the minority classes, such as rare toxic effects, are given adequate consideration.

Enhancing Predictive Performance: Ensembling strategies can reduce bias and volatility, which stem from individual models, through the amalgamation of multiple base classifiers. The results show that using the ensemble approach in toxicity evaluation offers higher reliability and makes the overall prediction of toxicity assessment more accurate.

Despite their advantages, voting classifiers face several challenges:

Computational Complexity: With the integration of multiple classifications, the computational overhead depends on the number of classifiers, where large data sets and complex models increase the time required to execute. To address this challenge, implementation and optimization are inevitable.

Diversity of Models: This means that the number of base models available determines how well voting classifiers perform. Potential voting gains are reduced if the base classifiers are rather similar and make related errors. For the best performance of the ensemble, the model pool must cover a large variety of models.

Interpretability: Although voting classifiers enhance predictive capability, deciphering the outcomes can be difficult, especially when there is a need to understand how and to what extent the different models impact the decision. Methods for identifying and characterizing the work of individual models and the ensemble as a whole are key to increasing transparency.

Voting classifiers are a powerful ensemble learning method for enhancing predictions. Their use in drug toxicity prediction offers a way of utilizing various models and handling multifaceted data to improve the results of toxicity tests.

## 4.5 Hybrid models

Ensemble learning techniques have been formulated into various categories, one of which is known as hybrid models, considered an advanced approach in ensemble learning because it integrates and takes advantage of various categories of machine learning. As a combination of various disjointed algorithms, using a hybrid model has the main objective of increasing predictive capacity and stability while working to overcome some of the weaknesses associated with other models. Hybrid models are incorporated, as shown in Figure 15, where more than one machine learning model is used to make a prediction, and adding more than one model to the work can improve the accuracy and flexibility of the model. This approach is especially valuable for domains that involve various, and often nonlinear, data types and patterns, such as drug toxicity prediction.

Most hybrid models are made of two completely separate modules that work in parallel to interpret inputs and make predictions. These elements comprise many types of first-level models, each with special advantages – for example, decision trees, neural networks, and support vector machines. Through stacking and mixing processes, which are all parts of the hybrid methods, the model integrates the elements, making it helpful to study the data more profoundly.

Stacking: Stacking means training a number of base models on the same training sample and using their outputs to train a meta-model. The base models can vary significantly with respect to each other and, in the following, could include neural networks, decision trees, and ensemble methods. The meta-model, often a less complex model, such as logistic regression, tries to find the right proportion by which the base models can generate an output. This approach improves prediction capability because the best features of several models are harnessed.
