
On the other hand, feature extraction entails reshaping the data into a smaller feature space. The latter group includes methods such as PCA and autoencoders, which create new features instead of selecting from preexisting ones. It is essential to comprehend the differences between these techniques to choose the best methodology for the particular requirements of the investigation.

Drug toxicity prediction uses dimensionality reduction techniques extensively to improve model performance, simplify large, complicated datasets, and enhance visualization. These methods help to reduce computational complexity and overfitting by lowering the number of dimensions. However, there are restrictions associated with dimensionality reduction. For instance, to capture complex, nonlinear patterns, techniques like PCA and LDA assume linear correlations among features. Furthermore, because t-SNE is nonparametric, it may not be appropriate for downstream modeling tasks, even though it works well for visualization.

## 3.3 Anomaly detection

Anomaly detection is one of the methods of unsupervised learning that serves to determine values and trends outside the normal data distributions. These are called outliers, which are usually connected to important events and/or anomalies in the data, such as drug toxicity, fraud cases, and system failures. In the case of drug toxicity prediction, anomaly detection can be very useful when it comes to identifying rare side effects that may not be captured by the chosen predictive model.

In this context, anomaly detection techniques focus on the difference between normal and anomalous data, the nature of which was unknown in advance. Various approaches are utilized in this process:

Statistical Methods: Most of the data were assumed to follow a certain distribution by using statistical methods. Z-score and the Modified Z-score are examples of measures that determine by how much an observation deviates from the mean of a given distribution. In this work, anomalous observations are considered to be those observations that are above a particular value. These techniques are not suitable for multidimensional data types.

Distance-Based Approaches: Anomalies are identified through techniques such as k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), a distance measure that indicates the distance of a given anomalous point. The measures that define the anomalies involve the isolation of an object from its nearest neighbors at the periphery of the data space. This idea was subsequently developed into techniques including the Local Outlier Factor (LOF), which measures the local density of individual data points, as well as the Isolation Forest, which measures the isolation factor of points identified as outliers.

Density-Based Methods: Distance-based methods use distances between points to determine the level of their density in a given area to detect anomalies. Let us examine points in areas with low density. The DBSCAN algorithm (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) marks such sites as outliers. These approaches are very useful when working with datasets of different densities and shapes for the search of outliers.

Methods Based on Models: In model-based techniques, data are fitted into a statistical model, and outliers are determined from the model-fitted graphs. BCM was used in methods like Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) for estimating the probability of the data. Low-probability points are called outliers. Another method is One-Class SVM, in which outliers are identified with the help of a single decision boundary drawn through most of the data samples.

Techniques Based on Reconstruction: Using models such as autoencoders, reconstruction-based methods apply a lower-dimensional representation to reconstruct the data. In other words, anomalies are detected based on reconstruction errors, which are the differences between the original data and the data that is recreated. A large value of reconstruction errors suggests that the data point is an outlier. This method works well for detecting unstructured patterns from large storage volumes.

Structural models in drug toxicity prediction can also use anomaly detection approaches, where an important method identifies effects that are not typical for drug toxicity models. For instance:

Detecting Rare Adverse Reactions: Anomaly detection can also be applied to clinical trial information to identify patterns of rare adverse reactions that have not been identified during the pre-clinical and clinical development phases.

Identifying Novel Toxicity Patterns: When anomaly detection was applied to molecular data, entirely different patterns of toxicity were discovered, which were not detected by other methods, and led to the identification of new toxicophore groups and toxic mechanisms.

Enhancing Safety Monitoring: Data analysis, in conjunction with anomaly detection, can assist in the early detection of unfavorable side effects, thereby providing better chances of treatment and increasing patient safety.

Despite that, anomaly detection has its own set of flaws and shortcomings. Unclean datasets are magnified in Figure 8, where points outside the expected pattern are identified as anomalies. Issues related to the high-dimensionality of the data were one of the major factors that made it difficult to discover noticeable irregularities. In addition, the data distribution and parameters of the data set used in applying the anomaly detection techniques have a significant impact. The objective of future work is to develop new anomaly detection algorithms that possess increased scalability, robustness, and versatility, and work better on large, complex data. Another research topic is the enhancement of the accuracy and reliability of anomaly detection systems through integration with other machine learning techniques, including ensemble methods and deep learning.

## 3.4 Association rule learning

An effectively used type of unsupervised machine learning technique for discovering both more and less obvious relationships in rather large data sets is association rule learning. Exploratory data analysis is when this technique is very useful, as the goal of such analysis is to uncover relationships that are not likely known between variables.

Association Rule Learning was initially applied mainly to the task of finding characteristic relationships between the occurrence of one item and that of another. These are usually stated in the logical form of an “If . . . Then . . .” statement. For example, if a drug has a high binding affinity, then it MUST have caused an adverse reaction. The process of association rule mining was explained with the help of Figure 9, where frequent item sets are employed to develop rules that determine relationships between variables in a set. The core concepts in association rule learning include support, confidence, and lift:

Support: It measures how often an itemset appeared in the dataset as one of the validity measures. The relative frequency of the itemset was used in its calculation.

Confidence quantifies the probability that the consequent (or “then”) part of a rule is true given that the antecedent (or “if”) part of the rule is true. Relevance measures the rule’s reliability and is obtained by using the relative support of the itemset and that of the antecedent.

Lift: Lift first subtracts the predicted support of a rule from the existing support and then divides it by the predicted support, which is contingent on the presumption that all the components of a rule are independent of each other. It demonstrates the proportion of the outcome with regard to its likelihood of happening, provided the antecedent is given.
