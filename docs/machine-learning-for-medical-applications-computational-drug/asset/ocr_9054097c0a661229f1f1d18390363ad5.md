The next challenge is the regulating environment of using ML to improve the delivery of drugs using nanocarriers, and this is still fast developing. Current supervisory bodies need to set up principles for constructing, verifying, and implementing the ML models in healthcare. This involves setting some baseline on how suitable the data is for analysis, how accurate the models being used are, and even how well a model is performing. Moreover, the current regulation must capture the learning and adaptation characteristic of the ML models to ensure that they are safe and provide value when with new data. What is even more challenging to developers and other healthcare service providers is the need to meet these regulations and standards.

Adapting the various approaches that have been developed through machine learning into clinical practice and healthcare infrastructure is also inconceivable. Before adopting ML tools, clinicians must be well equipped with the understanding and skills in using and analyzing the tools and this incurs costs. In addition to this, there is the need for proper healthcare system integration tools such as Electronic Health Record and Data integration framework for a successful incorporation of any ML solutions. Finally, the organizational culture in that traditional practices is hard to change, which also slows down the development and implementation of new technologies.

# 6.2 Regulatory considerations and compliance with safety and efficacy standards

This is due to the fact that application of ML in medication delivery optimization necessarily involves a number of regulatory questions that must be addressed to maintain safety and efficacy. The related factors for regulatory approval and ensuring patient safety include data quality, model verity, reporting, monitoring, and controlling post-market and ethical considerations.

The technological design of automated smart medicine delivery systems must meet strict regulatory requirements to be approved by health authorities. Agencies such as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) mandate that the data used in training and modeling these systems be accurate, comprehensive, and representative of the intended patient population. To achieve this, there are rigorous protocols for data collection, standardization in data entry methods, and strict data cleansing procedures to eliminate inconsistencies. The authenticity and traceability of data must be verifiable to uphold the system’s credibility. In parallel, compliance with data privacy laws, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), is essential to safeguard patient confidentiality and ensure informed consent.
