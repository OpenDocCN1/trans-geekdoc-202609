Genetic factors associated with medication response as well as disease susceptibility are another area of use of NGS in SHP. Using genome sequencing, experts and health-care practitioners define specific changes to the DNA and differences in a person’s susceptibility to various diseases such as cancer, heart diseases, and rare inherited diseases. Such information is important for initiation of therapy that is based on the genes causing diseases, in this way being more effective and individualized.

In the case of oncology, NGS is critical to the tumor profiling, specifically studying the alterations that result from the development of cancer cells in a patient. When using such a wide set of genetic information, it is possible to define necessary mutations that can be targeted by certain medicines, maximizing the efficiency of the therapy, as well as minimizing the number of side effects. For example, mutations that occur in non-small cell lung cancer, due to changes in genes such as EGFR have been identified to have specific medications that work to precisely eliminate the cancers that are caused by these abnormal genes, thus enhancing medical outcomes.

Thus, NGS extends the pharmacogenomics field by giving an understanding of how these genetic differences influence the reaction to the drugs and their processing. With this kind of data, personalized medicine decreases unwanted reactions to medications as well as increases effectiveness of therapeutic intervention by individually selecting the right drug, the right dose, and combination of drugs based on the individual’s genetic makeup. For making therapies person-oriented and for individual gene makeup of the person, genetic tests are helpful in selecting and prescribing drugs such as antidepressants and warfarin for which the CYP450 enzyme family needs to be tested.

Health informatics is therefore an area where personalized healthcare is likely to offer the greatest benefit of applying NGS; but there are also disadvantages. The era of Big Data means that an enormous volume of data is produced in clinical practice, and their evaluation and implementation require special knowledge of bioinformatics and developed computing facilities. To achieve the goals of the NGS in personalized medicine additional challenges are described: data privacy issue, ethical dilemmas, and the emergence of specific criteria for genetic testing.
