One major advancement in the employment of ML to biological materials was transfer learning. Called transfer of concept, this method assisted in mapping current models to new problems, where the model was derived from one job but applicable to another, although different [13, 14]. To fully achieve these objectives, the integration of a machine learning approach into biological materials research has some issues that must be addressed [18]. This is especially a problem for data, as biomedical research often employs data in multiple formats and of varying quality from various sources [19]. All of these topics are reviewed in this chapter as well as possible future investigations highlighted, and the importance of ML for the biological materials science field is stressed.

## 2 Introduction to machine learning in biomedical materials

### 2.1 Historical overview of biomedical materials development

The developmental process of biomedical materials can be divided into distinct milestones, which make a substantial impact on medical science and the treatment of patients. The start was in the early part of the twentieth century with the introduction of biocompatibility, the starting point toward designing implants that could be compatible with the tissues of the body. The first biomaterials had certain requirements involving mechanical properties and the biocompatibility of stainless steel and titanium, so they were chosen. These materials lacked design flexibility for integration with body tissue, which resulted in problems such as inflammation, infection, and implant rejection.

The evolution of polymer-based materials took a significant turn in the mid-twentieth century, driven by the demand for flexible, durable, and biocompatible solutions in medical applications. Polymers such as silicone and polyethylene emerged as ideal candidates due to their low cost, mechanical strength, and biocompatibility, making them suitable for a wide range of medical devices, including valves and joint replacements. This era also marked the beginning of tissue engineering, as researchers started exploring materials that could support and stimulate tissue regeneration. The introduction of biodegradable polymers, such as polylactic acid (PLA) and polyglycolic acid (PGA), revolutionized the field by enabling the development of scaffolding materials that naturally degrade in the body, reducing the need for follow-up surgeries. These advancements are depicted in Figure 1, which illustrates the timeline and material progression in biomedical polymer development.

In the latter twentieth and early twenty-first century, the development of biomedical materials applications shifted from simply bio ceramics and biopolymers to bioactive and smart materials. Bioactive materials consist of bioactive glass and hydroxyapatite that are intended to be bioreactive with the surrounding biological systems on a molecular level, enhance bone formation, and improve the apposition of implants with the neighboring tissues. New types of materials, such as shape memory alloys and hydrogels, came into existence, offering functions that respond to stimuli, including temperature and pH impulses. These developments led to the formation of more advanced equipment and treatment plans for various diseases and illnesses, in response to the continually increasing requirement for materials that would suit the distinct identity of people.

Further advancement was brought by nanotechnology, implying the ability to design and build nanomaterials with better properties and features. The application of numerous nanostructured materials, like carbon nanotubes and nanoparticles, has been researched mainly for biological purposes and includes tissue engineering, medication delivery, and imaging techniques. Therapies can therefore be made more accurate and efficient in a clinical approach since cells can be altered by tackling materials at the nano level.

As the complexity of biological issues grew, the limitations of traditional material development approaches became clearer. The needs of present-day medicine, which imply the synthesis of different branches and the analysis of large amounts of information, are too complex for trial-and-error approaches that were the driving force behind most of the progress. This realization led to the incorporation of computational and ML approaches into the modern biomedical materials development age. This development can help researchers predict and enhance the properties of materials and accelerate the discovery of new ones; in addition, it is helpful for providing patients with targeted and faster care.

The aforementioned historical development shows that biomedical material science is a fast-growing field that has required frequent innovation to meet changing healthcare demands. As the field unfolds, the application of ML is the luminous future that presents itself as the most promising area to dramatically advance new biomedical materials in medicine.

### 2.2 The emergence of machine learning in material science

The incorporation of ML into material science signifies a shift in the long-standing approach to materials and science, especially in biomedical material science. In the past, the discovery of materials used in biomedical applications was based on the accumulation of knowledge derived from experience and from identifying materials which, upon testing, meet the desired performance criteria in the development process. Although these are the traditional approaches to new product development, they have been known to bring about long development cycles and high costs; success has depended on mere luck rather than organized ingenuity.

ML has become an exciting new tool in the world of material science, allowing the large-scale analysis of data and more accurate predictions of materials' characteristics and properties. Algorithms of the supervised as well as unsupervised learning type have been useful when it comes to the task of finding a pattern within large data that would go unnoticed. This capability is significantly valuable in biomedical materials, as the relationships involving materials and other living structures are always rich in diversity and quite complex.
