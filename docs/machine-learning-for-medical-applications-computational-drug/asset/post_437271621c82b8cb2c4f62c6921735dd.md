## Transforming healthcare with machine learning

**Abstract:** Deep learning (DL) models in material science revolutionize the design and characterization of materials, but due to their complexity and the fact that no one knows exactly how the model works, they can be hard to understand. In this chapter, we introduce a critical issue of the current DL frameworks: interpretability, with a focus on post- and intermediate processing techniques that enhance the readability of the models’ predictions. The relationship between the input and the output is sometimes unclear due to the high complexity of DL methods, which require the use of high-dimensional nonlinear spaces, presenting one of the main challenges. All of these are separately discussed in the chapter: an interpretable model element, visualization methods, and local explanation methods such as Shapley values and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME). Thirdly, it compares factors such as model accuracy and interpretability, among others, because in each case, it is impossible to achieve the maximum increase in performance by introducing more complex and sophisticated models without sacrificing the interpretability of the results for end-users. Perspectives for further studies are described, with an emphasis on the ability to combine domain knowledge and create machine learning models with domain-oriented interpretability protocols for material science fields. This proposed work should be able to close the gap between abstract DL schemes and tangible materials science applications, thus encouraging the general acceptance of AI technologies in materials science and engineering research and future application development.

**Keywords:** deep learning, interpretability, materials science, visualization techniques, local explanations, model transparency

## 1 Introduction

Deep learning (DL) has been embedded into material science in a way that deals with material discovery and characterization [1]. These models, with their capacity to analyze huge amounts of data and subtle patterns, have facilitated unparalleled developments [2]. DL algorithms often become algorithmically mystified, which creates interpretability problems [3]. Essentially, these models are often quite complex, and their formats contribute to a situation where the reasoning behind the forecasts is obscure [4]. This opacity can be problematic in terms of the utilization of the models in various sectors, especially where the models need to be sensitive, accurate as well as valid [5].

Interpretation in DL is defined as the extent to which architects are able to comprehend how a model produces certain results [6]. In material science, this capability is essential for verifying the results obtained from the model against the results expected in that particular field [7–9]. In particular, when DL models are utilized to forecast material characteristics and design, the audience must have confidence in the outcomes and comprehend the model’s outputs [10]. Inadequate interpretability can prevent the incorporation of these models into crucial applications, as it is necessary to avoid catastrophic mishaps resulting from incorrect predictions [11].

Several techniques have been designed to handle cases where interpretability issues arise in the context of DL [12]. Other visualization methods that describe the input data characteristics important for predictions include saliency maps and activation maps [13]. These bow-tie and action strategies help in understanding the model’s focused areas and the decision input data areas with significant influence on the model outputs [14]. In addition, methods called LIME and Shapley values provide a more fine-grained understanding of how certain inputs contribute to the model and offer local explanations for predictions [15]. While there has been some progress in the field, there are still several trade-offs to be expected when making models interpretable [16]. The increase in transparency often involves the use of model reduction or approximation methods, which decreases predictive capability [17]. The main challenge is how to achieve interpretability while at the same time maintaining high levels of accuracy [18]. For instance, the first models, which are much easier to understand, do not produce as accurate predictions as the more complicated structures of DL algorithms [19]. This trade-off highlights the need to assess the strengths and weaknesses of various interpretability techniques in the context of materials science applications [20].

In particular, regarding future trends in the comprehension of DL models for material science, it is probable that existing trends will focus on making processes more standardized and effective [21]. That is, improvements in algorithm design at the interpretability stage and the adoption of solution-specific knowledge could enhance the solutions [22, 23]. The aim is to propose approaches that provide more comprehensible explanations while maintaining the same predictive performance [24, 25]. Accordingly, as the field advances, enhancing the levels of openness and awareness in DL material science research will be critical for spurring development and ensuring the effective use of DL techniques.

## 2 Deep learning techniques in biomedical materials

### 2.1 Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for material property prediction

Basic notions on CNNs are another kind of powerful DL computing method that focuses on analyzing structures such as data that come in grids, for instance, images. CNNs were designed to work with images, mostly for processing them, but have been adapted for many purposes, including material science. CNNs appear relevant in material property predictions due to their ability to learn feature representation and spatial hierarchy from the inputs. This makes it possible to simulate complex relations between material properties and the structures of those materials.

Convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers are some of the building blocks of most CNN structures. In our case, pooling layers are used to down sample the input and retain the most relevant aspects. The convolutional layers, on the other hand, convolve the input data with kernels to identify local features. The final layers aggregate these features to make predictions. For material property prediction, which can be achieved through CNNs, a material’s microstructure, as captured in microscopy images, can be used to predict properties such as mechanical strength, thermal conductivity, and biocompatibility. This approach is particularly useful, thanks to CNNs ability to handle the extraction of hierarchical features from data of high and even extreme complexity.

A study of CNNs for material property prediction CNNs has proved worthy of being employed for the prediction of different properties of materials. For example, CNNs have been used for the prediction of the tensile strength of composite materials from the microstructure images of the same composite material. CNNs have also been applied to estimate thermal properties based on thermal image observations. These applications show that CNNs can extract useful predictions from images, which is very beneficial for material design and optimization.
