## 3.2 QSAR modeling for predicting compound activity

The primary reason for using QSAR modeling in the discovery of hits in drug development is that their activities can be modeled based on the structures of the compounds. Thus, biological activity measures, for example binding affinities, are related quantitatively to molecular descriptors applying QSAR models, which are based on machine learning.

QSAR (Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship) modeling begins with the identification of key molecular descriptors that characterize the chemical structure and properties of molecules. These descriptors can include molecular weight, lipophilicity, hydrogen bonding capacity and sites, as well as electronic properties. Furthermore, descriptors derived from molecular structures can be groundbreaking, particularly in understanding spatial configurations and binding patterns with biological macromolecules. This process is illustrated in Figure 6.

Next, training datasets with well-defined chemical activities are employed with chemometric techniques that include neural networks, SVMs, linear regression, and random forests for QSAR model generation. Usually, these files contain chemical structures from database tests and the corresponding activity values. The methods optimize expected measures like MSE and minimize prediction errors to find out how the characteristics of input (molecular descriptors) can be mapped to the output variable (compound activity).

These QSAR models can be employed to estimate the activity of new chemical species solely based on their molecular structures as soon as the QSAR models have been built. Quantitative activity predictions of the compounds can be made by the model once the essential molecular descriptors have been entered into the model. This procedure allows for the identification of interesting candidates for further experimental examination.

Other techniques are incorporated in QSAR modeling such as feature selection, which aims to discover the best descriptors that improve the model's interpretative ability and its predictive ability in other areas. In the accuracy of prediction and robustness to noise and outliers, various models of consensus and bagging, in which multiple QSAR models are trained on different subsets of data are used.

## 3.3 Deep learning approaches for compound similarity search

SMILES-based representations, deep learning approaches have recently garnered substantial interest in the identification of hits for data-driven rational drug design (DRDD), particularly in compound similarity search. These methods utilize multilayered neural networks to capture higher-order representations of chemical structures, enabling more accurate and real-time identification of structurally or functionally similar compounds. This advancement is illustrated in Figure 7.

![](img/211c754cd1dd84920a171f497b33b689_320_0.png)
**Figure 7:** Deep learning approaches for compound similarity search.

The most well-known realization of deep learning for compound similarity search is the Siamese neural network. It involves two neural network streams called the Siamese networks, which operate in parallel with tied parameters for feature learning and map the input compound features into fixed size vectors dubbed as embeddings. These embeddings capture the structural similarities and differences between compounds where the similarity is based on the geometry of the space while the difference is based on the distance in the space. Here, Siamese networks are able to determine structurally related compounds to query compounds simply by calculating the similarity between the respective embeddings of the query compounds and compounds present in a chemical library.

The GNN is another deep learning approach to the compound similarity search. The nature of chemical structure makes use of graphs to explain them; therefore, GNNs suit graph-structured data. To represent intricate interplays of atoms and chemistry of chemical bonds, GNNs operate based on message passing with the viewpoint that node representations are refined based on information about the neighboring data. GNNs transform molecular graphs to low-dimensional vectors allowing the efficient and accurate search for analogs among millions of compounds.

For compound similarity search tasks, CNNs have been prearranged by perceiving chemical structures as a one-dimensional sequence of atom characteristics of molecular fingerprints. CNNs find feature-abstract hierarchies in chemical structures through the convolution of filters and obtain local connections and features at different levels of abstraction. Query compounds are searched structurally similar compounds with high accuracy based on the feature maps of CNNs and SDs with compounds in a chemical library.

Deep learning methods can be integrated with conventional molecular fingerprints, for improving performance of compound similarity search. Scientists can enhance the overall rate of similarity search while using only an equal number of descriptors by proposing deep learning models as features values. This enables them to utilize the complementary aspects of the two approaches.

Decision fusion methods have emerged as valuable strategies in hit identification for drug discovery, significantly enhancing the accuracy and robustness of predictive models. These methods combine the outputs of multiple individual models in a manner that leverages the unique strengths of each, effectively outperforming any single model used in isolation.
