## 2 Definition and importance of biocompatible materials

### 2.1 Defining biocompatibility

The ability of an implanted material to perform the required task within a bioenvironment without eliciting an adverse local response in the host organism is defined as biocompatibility. In the case of implants, drug delivery systems, medical devices, and other biomedical applications where materials interact with tissues, blood is very essential. Biocompatibility can be defined as the ability of the material to support and promote cell functions, integrate into the surrounding host tissues, remain functional for the required duration, and, importantly, not cause toxic or inflammatory responses.

It was therefore necessary to evaluate the material in terms of mechanical, chemical, and physical properties, as well as its interaction with the biosphere, to establish biocompatibility. Chemical properties such as solubility, surface texture, hardness, and brittleness, among others, have a direct bearing on the absorption of the chemical, thus dictating its ability to perform its intended functions within the human body without exposing the patient to health risks. The ease with which a biocompatible material can be adapted for the host tissue, minimizing the chances of incidents such as infection, rejection, and tear, was considered a measure of the material's success.

In the creation of biocompatible materials, it therefore became necessary to take into consideration the use to which the material was going to be put and the environment in which it was to be used. For instance, tissues intended for long-term implants must be biocompatible but also wear and corrosion-resistant and mechanically stiff enough to withstand forces. Likewise, materials applied in drug-delivery systems have to include the ability to release therapeutic agents and not provoke toxic reactions.

Biocompatibility of a substance is a relative factor that depends on the purpose of its use; it is not an absolute characteristic. The same material can exhibit different levels of biocompatibility depending on conditions such as the state of the patient's body, the location of implantation, and the presence of additional additives used.

Therefore, identifying biocompatibility is an intricate and ongoing process that involves accounting for all the biological interactions and characteristics of materials. Consequently, definitions and assessments of biocompatibility remain crucial to the future advancement of biomedical engineering and the application of novel technologies in medicine.

### 2.2 The role of biocompatible materials in modern medicine

Biocompatible materials today are critical as they act as the foundation of many forms of implantation, medical instruments, and treatment technologies. These materials are specially designed to interact harmoniously with biological tissues, which means they do not elicit toxic or inflammatory responses. The integration of biocompatible materials into medical applications has revolutionized the approach taken to treating patients as well as promoting the development of solutions that effectively extend the practical functionality of medicines.

### 2.3 Historical evolution of biocompatible materials

The information discussed in the present work describes how the development of biocompatible materials has followed the advances in biomedical science and engineering. Previous efforts to create materials that would specifically and safely interface with biological systems can be regarded as less successful due to the available tools and knowledge. Ancient civilizations employed basic metals such as gold and silver in surgical instruments and dental treatments because they were observed to have moderate biocompatibility functions. At the time, there was no such thing as biocompatibility in the current definition, but these early materials were selected for their durability and resistance to corrosion.

There was gradual improvement in the succeeding centuries, particularly throughout the nineteenth and early part of the twentieth century, with the coming of modern medicine and materials science. The advancements in stainless steel and other sorts of alloys necessitated the formation of more complex and advanced medical implants and devices. These metals such as stainless steel, which are anticorrosive and could be sterilized, were used for surgical tools and implantable products. Additionally, beginning in the mid-twentieth century polymers made the products more diversified for biocompatibility. The first materials used in medical applications were polymers like polystyrene and polyethylene because of their flexibility and ease of molding. During this period, the concern was to curtail the inflammation caused by these materials in hopes of avoiding serious illness.

Metals such as gold and silver were utilized by the ancient people for surgery and dental implants due to the lack of biocompatible materials per se. Though the modern notion of biocompatibility was absent, these earliest materials were selected based on their durability and noncorrosive properties.

The last several decades have also witnessed a shift in focus from the generation of materials with enhanced property profiles to the development of smart materials that can positively interface with biological systems. New composite material technology and new nanostructured material technology, appropriately designed for specific biological functions, have been the outcomes of advancements in nanotechnology and biomaterials.
