# 5.1.1 Material composition and biological interaction

The physical properties of a material include information regarding the chemical composition of the material and its interactions with the biological tissues where it is utilized. For instance, titanium and stainless-steel materials are preferred due to their low chemical reactivity and stable oxide layers, which prevent direct contact between the metal and the surrounding tissue. On the other hand, materials that contain components like nickel may cause adverse reactions depending on the individuals exposed to them. As such, the chemical composition of the materials to be used should be carefully chosen to avoid undesirable side effects.

# 5.1.2 Surface chemistry and protein adsorption

The surface properties of any material that comes into contact with proteins define how much of it is adsorbed, an important consideration when designing biomaterials. When a material is implanted into the body, the proteins from the surrounding environment rapidly adhere to the surface of the implant, creating a layer of protein that influences cellular response. Most surface chemistries that encourage the adsorption of specific proteins are generally considered biocompatible, especially if the material chemistry is hydrophilic. For example, hydrophilic coatings decrease nonspecific protein adsorption and cell adhesion, thereby decreasing the inflammatory response and improving the integration of the device with biological tissue.

# 5.1.3 Degradation products and cytotoxicity

The biodegradation of many biocompatible materials can lead to the release of chemical by-products that significantly influence their overall biocompatibility. Polymers, composites, and other biomedical materials often produce degradation products that are absorbed by surrounding tissues during the breakdown process. The type and concentration of these by-products can directly affect cytotoxicity and inflammatory responses. Therefore, materials that degrade into non-toxic, bioresorbable, and easily metabolizable compounds are preferred, as they pose minimal risk to surrounding tissues. When designing such systems, it's crucial to simulate natural degradation pathways and select materials that maintain biocompatibility over their entire functional lifespan.
