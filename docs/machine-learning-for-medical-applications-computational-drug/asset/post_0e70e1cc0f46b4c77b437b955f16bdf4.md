## 2 Overview of biomedical sensors

### 2.1 Definition and purpose of biomedical sensors

The name “biomedical sensors” is used in the detection and measurement of several varieties of physiological variables in the body. These sensors convert biological signals to electrical signals in order that applications in medicine can process, analyze, and interpret the data. Figure 1 presents the definition and use of biomedical sensors for measuring physiological parameters for diagnosing diseases and for individualized treatment. Biomedical sensors were initially designed to deliver accurate and real-time data with significant health parameters including heart rate and rhythm, blood glucose level, body temperature, blood pressure, and neural signal.

Biomedical sensors as data acquisition tools are insufficient; also, critical to enhancing patient care and thereby medical science. These sensors can promote early condition identification and early supervision of relevant health issues, all of which help to effectively control diseases. The ability to follow up continuously is especially helpful in various chronic disorders such as diabetes, hypertension, and arrhythmias where constant attention needs to be paid to prevent complications.

Figure 1: Definition and purpose of biomedical sensors.

Because biomedical sensors provide data that is utilized to customize treatment plans to each patient’s needs, they help personalize healthcare. This individualized strategy improves patient outcomes and lowers healthcare costs by facilitating more effective and efficient healthcare delivery. Biomedical sensors offer useful data in research settings that can spur advances in medical science and the creation of novel medications and treatments.

### 2.2 Historical development and milestones

Biomedical sensors experience prominent evolution stages that have changed notions about healthcare and medical diagnostics. Biomedical sensors have their roots in the early part of the twentieth century when simple tools were used to assess fundamental physiological signs. Early sensors were large and required to be operated manually, which greatly discouraged their use and precision.

The main turning points for biomedical sensors can be traced back to the 1950s with the invention of the electrocardiogram (ECG) and the electroencephalogram (EEG). These devices marked a significant advancement in the ability to diagnose and monitor the electrical activity of the heart and brain, respectively. They highlighted the critical intersection of electronics and medicine, driving innovations in health monitoring technologies and diagnostic methods (see Figure 2).

A breakthrough in the 1970s and 1980s in the development of sensor technology includes the solid-state sensors as well as miniaturization process. The increase in sophistication of devices enabling continuous monitoring of physiological parameters is attributed to the advancement within silicon-based sensor technology. During this period, the first wearable sensors were developed allowing patients to monitor their status outside the clinic.

The advancement of wireless technology in the 1990s along with integration of microelectronics to biomedical sensors was a major step forward. With the evolution of wireless communication, data could be collected remotely and transmitted in real time; this provided further utility in fields of biomedical monitoring both in clinics and at home. A technologically related invention that perhaps originated from this time period was the development of the glucose monitoring appliances used by diabetes patients to monitor and record their blood sugar levels.

As seen, biomedical sensors went through another revolution in the early 2000s through the integration of digital technology and the Internet of Things (IoT). The available and affordable smart phones and wearable technologies expanded the possibility of developing comfortable and effective health monitoring systems, which can monitor and analyze multiple physiological properties over time. Activity monitoring devices with sensors for heart rate, physical activity, and sleep improved due to these advancements and are thus used by consumers to monitor health parameters. Such devices include fitness trackers and smartwatches.

AI broke out into biomedical sensors over the most recent decade and has actualized the new age of health checking. AI and machine learning techniques have also been applied in analysis of data received from sensors making the results much more profound to develop models and healthcare interventions. Present AI-based sensors have improved capabilities of real-time monitoring and data analysis, identification of anomalies during disease progression, and individual health management considered as a progression in biomedical sensor development.

In sum, the progress of biomedical sensors in the historical evolution can be described as consisting of constant advancement from the initial mechanical structure to the application of a modern AI system. All of these milestones have continued to build the competence of biomedical sensors by broadening their use and enhancing the effect of the technologies in the domain of health and treatment.

### 2.3 Types of biomedical sensors

Biomedical sensors are defined as multifunctional devices for tracking different organisms’ functions since every type is effective in particular contexts. It is illustrated in Figure 3 that biomedical sensors are categorized by the nature of their working process as electrochemical sensors, optical sensors, and mechanical sensors, as well as their intended uses for diagnostics. It is necessary to identify the kinds of biomedical sensors in order to comprehend their functions in constant health surveillance and improvement based on IT systems and AI.

#### 2.3.1 Electrochemical sensors

Electrochemical sensors are used to identify and quantify concentration of targeted chemical compounds using an electrochemical process. Such sensors are central in glucose monitoring for diabetic patients, which measures glucose concentrations in blood fluid. The electrical reactions that occur in the electrochemical cell are found to mimic the concentration of the targeted analyte, in this case, glucose, which is accurately measured. Such sensors are important in that they are extremely sensitive and selective and are often used in the monitoring of long-term illnesses such as diabetes.
