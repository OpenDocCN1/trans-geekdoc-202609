### 6.1 Deep learning architectures

Neural network architectures embedded through multiple layers to analyze complex data patterns are known as deep learning architectures in the field of machine learning. As these structures are simplified specifically for processing large amounts of data to find relationships between them, they have been applied in different fields such as medication toxicity prediction. Deep learning architectures are depicted in Figure 21 to display diverse structures and the various applications of deep learning approaches in solving complicated problems, as illustrated in the four domains. New deep learning models are now advancing the frontier of data interpretation and enabling the anticipation of analytical results.

Figure 21: Deep learning architectures.

CNNs are particularly designed for data that has a mesh-like property, such as photographic data. In this case, it is necessary to apply filters that capture local patterns, such as edges, and this is achieved in the convolutional layers. Then, pooling layers are utilized as a way of reducing dimensionality and extracting features. CNNs’ performance has been noted in tasks such as object identification, picture classification, and, more recently, drug interaction prediction and molecular structure analysis. CNNs are very effective at learning hierarchical features in datasets related to drugs, and the use of these networks allows for the detection of detailed spatial relationships.

Since RNNs contain feed-forward loops that preserve information across time steps, they are designed for sequential data. Chronic care points that involve time series, such as monitoring drug interactions over time or a patient's treatment history, are well suited to this architecture. Concerning the vanishing and exploding gradient issues in standard RNNs, LSTM networks and GRUs help identify long-range dependencies and improve sequential prediction results.

Autoregressive models introduce problems related to the inverse and self-attention processes to address this issue, which was quite an achievement in solving sequential data. Contrast this with the fact that, whereas transformers are able to process the whole input at once with ease, RNNs are much more sequential, and sequential data is not very parallelizable. Some existing models, such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) and GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer), are true examples of how design captures detailed relationships and contextual nuances. Hence, by using Transformers, it became possible to obtain a better contextual understanding of sequences, including patient records or chemical reactions; this can enhance medication toxicity prediction.

Applying Discriminator and Generator neural networks in a zero-sum matrix, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are produced. While the discriminator checks the authenticity of the developed artificial data, the generator creates it. This adversarial training enables GANs to offer very realistic data samples in the scientific applications of data augmentation, drug interaction modeling, and probable side effect investigation. The capability of predicting pharmacological characteristics and designing molecular structures has been observed using GANs that learn from prior information.

Probabilistic autoencoders are applicable to data compression and feature learning, while VAEs stand for probabilistic ones. VAEs take the autoencoder concept further by incorporating modeling of data distribution and resemble generative models that can generate new samples, which have not been experienced before, from the learned distribution. In the DTI prediction context, we can use VAEs to generate the manifold of chemical properties, assist with data generation, and reveal additional, unobservable factors leading to toxicity. These models are very useful in situations where new drug candidates have to be developed and their possible side effects studied.
