## 2.2 Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) in time-series analysis of material behavior

RNNs have been identified as capable of analyzing sequential data, such as time series information, since they have temporal dependency abilities. In the context of developing biomedical materials, RNNs are harnessed for the realization and prediction of time-dependent material performance. This capability is especially insightful for applications that involve transitions in material properties due to conditions like mechanical load, temperature, or environmental factors. As a result of RNNs’ corresponding sequential structures, researchers are able to build models that supply more significant temporal characteristics of distinct material properties, thereby achieving more efficient predictions and a deeper understanding of material characteristics during the material design process.

### 2.2.1 RNNs’ employment in material behavior analysis

RNNs are used with time series data to analyze the behavior of materials in material science and to predict their behavior under specific conditions. For instance, when analyzing the characteristics of biomaterials used in implants, RNNs can utilize time series stress–strain data to observe the ways in which the material deforms under test loading cycles. Likewise, RNNs can be used to monitor the wear of materials in real-world scenarios and present important data for the creation of better and stronger materials. Since RNNs have the ability to handle sequences of different lengths and with different levels of complexity, they are therefore very suitable for modeling complex time-dependent phenomena in material science.

### 2.2.2 Substituting and improving RNN architectures for materials science

It was found that traditional RNNs are quite effective but generally yield poor results due to issues such as vanishing and exploding gradients. There are other complications that hinder the ability of RNNs, and these include remote dependencies, exploding gradients, vanishing gradients, and advanced topologies in RNNs, particularly the GRUs and LSTM networks. Thus, to avoid these problems and to enhance the model’s ability to learn long sequences, LSTMs incorporate methods for retaining information across extended sequences. LSTMs should be used where there are constraints on computational capabilities, as GRUs are less complex but can offer similar performance to LSTMs. Over time, these improved RNN designs have demonstrated higher accuracy and stability in modeling material behavior, resulting in more reliable prediction and evaluation.

Numerous research studies provide evidence of realistic time series analysis of material behavior through RNNs. For example, scientists have used LSTMs to determine the fatigue life of aerospace materials by analyzing time series data obtained from cyclic tests. Another study employs GRUs for modeling the thermal degradation of polymeric materials, which proved effective in illustrating the potency of RNNs in mimicking the degradation temporal patterns of inherent materials. These cases demonstrate how RNNs can be used in various issues in material science and indicate how knowledge of material response through time can be improved using RNNs.

There are several limitations with the present approach of using RNNs for analyzing time series of material behavior. Some of the challenges that are still being experienced include the need for large volumes of data, computational problems, and the limitations of the RNN models. This is why future research aims at designing better algorithms with a closer look, enhancing model interpretability, and combining RNNs with other methods in machine learning to counter these issues. Investigating the application of RNNs for other materials and new technologies reveals fresh areas for enhancing material science and engineering.

## 2.3 Autoencoders for dimensionality reduction in material characterization

Autoencoders, a class of neural networks used primarily for unsupervised learning, have gained widespread attention due to their dimensionality reduction capabilities. These models consist of an encoder that compresses high-dimensional input data into a lower-dimensional latent space, and a decoder that reconstructs the original data from this compressed representation. In the context of material characterization (MC), autoencoders help preserve critical features while minimizing redundant information, making them highly effective for analyzing large-scale datasets derived from material science simulations and experimental results. In MC, autoencoders fill the gap in working with high-dimensional data including spectroscopy, field data, as well as other analytical measurements. Fundamentally, these properties are latent within the data; autoencoders reduce their dimensions, allowing for a better understanding of the properties themselves. The mapping can also capture details that were previously hard to grasp due to the size of the feature space. For instance, in the study of the properties of polymer composite materials and alloys, autoencoder types can deliver important features, valuable for the definition of the best material combinations in terms of performance.

### 2.3.1 Benefits and advantages

When using autoencoders for the purposes of dimensionality reduction, the most significant advantage is their ability to learn nonlinear transformations of data. That is, autoencoders do not rely on comparatively simple linear transformations to find nonlinear relationships in the data, as PCA does. This feature is highly important for MC scenarios where the relationships between numerous material parameters are significantly nonlinear. The autoencoder is also able to perform data cleaning by training the network to map noisy data points into noise-free representations, thereby improving the quality of the reduced-dimension features for further analysis.

### 2.3.2 Challenges and considerations

As useful as autoencoders are when used in MC, there are some issues to consider. Some of the most important aspects that should be adjusted in the architecture of an autoencoder include the number of layers and the size of the latent space. The choice of the number of layers and the size of the latent space can significantly affect the performance of the autoencoder. One notable drawback associated with using this model is overfitting, particularly if the size of the dataset is small compared to the size of the model. Additionally, the interpretation of the compressed representation remains problematic because the representation learned by autoencoders may not align with the physically relevant aspects of the material.

It was expected that future research on autoencoders for MC would explore ways of making those models more explainable and integrating them into other machine learning methods. These are some implications for future work: possibly, a better approach for building improved models could be to develop multilayer autoencoder models that include some type of filtering, which supports marked-up output labels to utilize the results of supervised learning methods, thereby adding more robust and interpretable results.
