Genetic factors associated with medication response as well as disease susceptibility are another area of use of NGS in SHP. Using genome sequencing, experts and health-care practitioners define specific changes to the DNA and differences in a person’s susceptibility to various diseases such as cancer, heart diseases, and rare inherited diseases. Such information is important for initiation of therapy that is based on the genes causing diseases, in this way being more effective and individualized.

In the case of oncology, NGS is critical to the tumor profiling, specifically studying the alterations that result from the development of cancer cells in a patient. When using such a wide set of genetic information, it is possible to define necessary mutations that can be targeted by certain medicines, maximizing the efficiency of the therapy, as well as minimizing the number of side effects. For example, mutations that occur in non-small cell lung cancer, due to changes in genes such as EGFR have been identified to have specific medications that work to precisely eliminate the cancers that are caused by these abnormal genes, thus enhancing medical outcomes.

Thus, NGS extends the pharmacogenomics field by giving an understanding of how these genetic differences influence the reaction to the drugs and their processing. With this kind of data, personalized medicine decreases unwanted reactions to medications as well as increases effectiveness of therapeutic intervention by individually selecting the right drug, the right dose, and combination of drugs based on the individual’s genetic makeup. For making therapies person-oriented and for individual gene makeup of the person, genetic tests are helpful in selecting and prescribing drugs such as antidepressants and warfarin for which the CYP450 enzyme family needs to be tested.

Health informatics is therefore an area where personalized healthcare is likely to offer the greatest benefit of applying NGS; but there are also disadvantages. The era of Big Data means that an enormous volume of data is produced in clinical practice, and their evaluation and implementation require special knowledge of bioinformatics and developed computing facilities. To achieve the goals of the NGS in personalized medicine additional challenges are described: data privacy issue, ethical dilemmas, and the emergence of specific criteria for genetic testing.

## 4.2 Integration of Big Data and artificial intelligence in personalized medicine

Smart treatment, which expands the ability to individualize medical treatments with reference to certain patients’ characteristics, is one of the most significant innovations in healthcare thanks to the use of Big Data and AI. All these technologies provide positive tools for analyzing big and complex data, identifying tracks and advising unique treatment strategies that boost patient outcomes.

Big Data refers to large and complex datasets in terms of the volume, velocity, and variety, which stems from various forms of information such as genomics, electronic health records, clinical trials, and patient-reported information. This data itself is enormous, and it is evident that there are both advantages and limitations when it comes to integration to personalized medicine. These large datasets call for sophisticated analysis to produce meaningful patterns and correlations that guide treatment.

Big Data research immensely relied on applying AI especially ML and DL to extract information from the large datasets. The spectrum of such AI tools is aimed at handling and analyzing vast and complex datasets, wherein conditions and trends sometimes overlooked by conventional analytical approaches are detected and defined. For example, ML algorithms analyze large swaths of genomics data to look for genetic markers associated with specific diseases. In this regard, it is possible to develop prediction models that individuals can use to predict the risk for an illness, handling of the illness, and therapy needed for the same based on machine learning skills.

In personalized medicine, Big Data and AI are enhancers in several ways: First, AI advanced analysis improves the overall accuracy of diagnostic procedures considering multiple data inputs, such as images, genetics, and health records. For instance, forms of AI can help examine medical imaging data to identify early signs of diseases, which would be missed by other approaches.

Second, establishing personalized treatment programs has also benefited from the AI and Big Data work integration. AI can predict a patient’s reaction to a specific drug because it analyzes the results of the other patients with similar genetic and medical histories. By pointing towards fewer trial-and-error instances and less side effects, this precise predictive force allows doctors to select the most successful medications with far greater accuracy.

Third, new biomarkers and treatment targets are identified through the utilization of both AI and Big Data. Big Data analysis can be used to discover new targets for drugs, as common genetic patterns can be associated with the illness prognosis that was not known earlier. This technique benefits the development of biopsy drugs for specific patients by utilizing molecular qualities of target patients.

There are still challenges to the incorporation of Big Data and AI toward personalized medicine, albeit the gains are obvious. There are such critical issues as the protection of data – own and others’, formatting of the data, and the outcome of the AI and its analysis. Collaboration is needed across disciplines to ensure that the AI applications are designed and implemented in clinically appropriate and ethically and medically sound manner.

Taken together they could be symbolic of a revolution in not only the field of medicine but also a revolution in ensuring accurate, successful treatments and even medical cures that have been tailored to each patient’s particular needs. Improved patients’ health status and increased specificity in healthcare are expected as a consequence of integration of these technological advancements in the personalized medicine field.

## 4.3 Innovations in biomarker discovery and their impact on treatment customization

Emerging trends in biomarker research have pioneered the advancement of the field of targeted therapies for patients. Biosignature biomarkers are biological molecules that offer clues to a disease condition on a certain therapy and are essential aspects of pharmacogenomics, which involves building drugs tailored to the peculiarities of each patient. The technological improvements of the recent past have gone a long way to increase the discovery and utilization of biomarkers, a major revolution in the customization of treatment.

Some of the major breakthroughs in biomarker discovery have been driven by advancements in high-throughput screening techniques. As shown in Figure 8, these technologies enable the simultaneous testing of hundreds of biological samples, significantly accelerating the identification of novel biomarkers. Methods such as mass spectrometry and next-generation sequencing (NGS) are essential for detecting biomarkers linked to a wide range of diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular conditions, and neurological disorders. These advanced tools are capable of identifying subtle molecular changes that may go undetected by conventional methods, thereby improving diagnostic precision and therapeutic targeting.

New trends in biomarker discovery, driven by omics technologies such as genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics are transforming biomedical research. Molecular genetics reveal how polymorphisms influence disease risk and drug response (pharmacogenomics), while molecular profiling and metabotyping identify relevant proteins and metabolic states, respectively. Thus, the integration of information from these various levels of omics platforms enables researchers to define biomarkers that contribute to understanding disease pathogenesis and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

These changes have left none on the treatment customization untouched. Biomarkers help in partitioning of patients in terms of molecular character and allow specific drug and therapeutic interventions to be designed to address the corresponding disease. For instance, the identification of genetic mutation in a tumor has culminated into development of drugs that specifically block the proteins by means of which these mutations manifest themselves, thus leading to development of efficient and less toxic effective therapies. Like the former, biomarkers that reflect drug metabolism can act as surrogate markers directing dose adjustments depending on patients’ benefits and risks.

Biomarkers are applied not only for therapeutic purposes, but for diagnosis as well as for the prediction of the disease. Prognostic biomarkers are used in determining the disease progression and likely outcomes, which in turn guide treatment regimens that would best fit the disease progression prognosis. Another advantage of the use of biomarkers was that they can produce a daily reflection of treatment outcomes in response to the action taken.
