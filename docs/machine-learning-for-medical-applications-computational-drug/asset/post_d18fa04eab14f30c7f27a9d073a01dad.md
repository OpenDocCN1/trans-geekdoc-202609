
The first observed drawback in the evolution of biomedical sensors was the size and intrusiveness of the sensors themselves. Some of these sensors necessitated coming into direct physical contact with the body in an invasive process that could be uncomfortable for patients. For instance, older models of glucose monitors required the use of a blood sample for evaluation; getting a blood sample was painful and inconvenient for diabetic patients who needed to take several tests every day.

The other major factor that emerged is the requirement for constant calibration and other maintenance activities. The use of the early sensors in healthcare needs was cumbersome as they had to be periodically readjusted for them to be accurate; this made them unsuitable for real-life use. This constant adjusting not only amplified the operational demand but also added some human errors and variability in the process of measurement.

The issues of accuracy and reliability of early biomedical sensors were also raised. Most of these sensors were vulnerable to noise by other influences such as variability in the environment. Unfortunately, this limited their chances of giving accurate and constant measures that help them influence effective diagnostics of many health issues.

Data transmission and integration were other issues noted by authors in the article. Sensors in the early stages of development were found to be lacking in sophisticated data transmission qualities and therefore the data was to be recorded and analyzed manually. This process was very much slow and bulky since it involved much manual work, which could have confounded the analysis on health changes and the subsequent response.

Another limitation was the small number of parameters that early sensors could measure. The initial sensor systems were designed to monitor only a limited set of physiological indicators, which restricted their ability to provide a comprehensive overview of a patient's condition. Consequently, this narrow focus required the use of multiple separate instruments to track different parameters, greatly complicating the screening process.

These limitations and challenges of early biomedical sensors highlighted the need for improvements in technology to enhance the effectiveness and interconnectivity of these devices. Many of these issues have since been addressed through advances in materials science, sensor miniaturization, and wireless communication technologies, which underpin the development of modern noninvasive healthcare sensors.

## 3.2 Advances in materials science

Nanotechnology and material engineering have played central roles in the progress of biomedical sensors by bringing qualitative changes in their functionality, performance capability, and versatility. Such advancements refer to the emergence of new able sensors that fit in wearable/implantable systems; these have been designed and produced. The advantages of miniaturized sensors include:
- 1) the patients’ comfort level is enhanced
- 2) there is reduced intrusion
- 3) convenience has improved

For instance, telemetry glucose sensors can be incorporated in the small-size continuous glucose monitoring devices that can be worn on the body in order to monitor glucose level changes in near-real time and without much discomfort.

In biomedical sensors, nanotechnology refers to the incorporation of materials and structures that are at least one dimension less than 100 nanometers in order to enhance the performance of sensors. Taking advantage of the advantageous properties of nanoparticles that include increased surface area-to-volume ratio and higher reactivity, it becomes possible to develop very sensitive and selective sensors. Nanowire, nanotube, and nanoparticle have unique properties, which enhance sensitivity and specificity of the sensors to detect various physiological parameters and biomarkers.

## 3.4 Wireless and wearable sensor technologies

The advancements in the wireless and wearable sensors systems are a step up evolutionarily in the biomedical sensors and have revolutionized how health data is collected. Such capabilities have effectively solved many weaknesses associated with conventional wired and static sensors, expanding new prospects for constant and concealed health surveillance.

Wired sensors have posed insecurities as patients can only undergo collection of their health data if connected through some wires. The data collected by these sensors is wirelessly communicated to external instruments using radiofrequency, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and other wireless technology interfaces. It also improves the feasibility measures of collecting data, real-time observation, and prompt execution of health information. The use of wireless sensors is especially seen in cases where subject mobility was an issue – in this case, during physical activity they leave their beds better monitored than when stationary, therefore giving a better picture of the overall patient health.

Wearable sensors such as smartwatches and fitness trackers are some of the essential everyday technologies normally used to manage our health. These sensors are always evaluating a profile of numerous physiological parameters and personal adornments are expected to be worn, often in clothes. Examples of biological parameters include: body temperature, respiration rate, heart rate, and physical activity. Due to enhanced surface area-to-volume ratio and higher reactivity, nanoscale materials provide an opportunity to develop highly sensitive and selective sensors, including nanowires, nanotubes, and nanoparticles; it is believed that the sensor used in detection of different physiological indicators and biomarkers can benefit from these nanomaterials. Because wearables are nonintrusive, they can be worn for extended periods and data collected more often without invading the lives of the patients.

Miniaturization of sensors along with the incorporation of advanced functional materials has continued to improve the efficiency of wireless and wearable types of sensors. Sensors today have received upgrades in the form of battery life, durability, and the comfort in using them. Some of the factors that have led to development of wearable sensor devices include enhanced physical form of the sensors and availability of microfabrication technologies, which enable manufacturing of thin flexible sensors that conform to body contours.
