
## 3.3 Noninvasive sensors

As a subfield within the broader domain of biosensors, noninvasive sensors have gained prominence due to their ability to collect multiple physiological parameters without penetrating the human body. This approach enhances patient comfort while minimizing the risk of complications typically associated with invasive procedures. As shown in Figure 4, this section provides a concise overview of the main types of non-invasive sensors, their operational principles, and their applications in continuous health monitoring.

### 3.3.1 Optical sensors

Optical sensors employ principles of operation that are founded on the use of light for the measurement of physiological characteristics. One of the most widely known optical sensors is the pulse oximeter which measures blood oxygen saturation levels and pulse rates using light. This is done using the principle of photoplethysmography, where the amount of light absorbed by the oxygenated and deoxygenated blood hemoglobin clearly differs. Other optical sensors include NIRS sensors, used in tissue oxygenation and blood flow measurements and reflectance, as well as transmittance sensors, utilized in glucose monitoring of diabetic patients. Optical sensors can be used in a quasi-continuous and real-time mode because they are noninvasive in most cases.

### 3.3.2 Electrochemical sensors

Electrochemical sensors monitor physical characteristics by ascertaining the electrical activity of a chemical process. An example that is often used is the non-invasive sweat sensing, which consists of determining the composition of sweat so as to check the concentration of electrolytes. These sensors routinely use microelectrode arrays that help to record and measure the electrochemical activity with metabolic and hydration indicators. New technologies in electrochemical sensor systems enable wearable devices for the determination of biomarkers in sweat instead of regular invasive blood sampling.

### 3.3.3 Acoustic sensors

Ultrasound and acoustic wave sensors are used to monitor a number of physiological variables using sound waves. Ultrasound sensors for instance are used in imaging systems for examining internal organs, blood flow, and any pathology. Other biomechanical properties that can be measured with high-frequency acoustic sensors include tissue stiffness. These sensors offer highly specific knowledge on the inner state of the body without the interference of surgeries and are used both for diagnostics and prognosis.

### 3.3.4 Bioimpedance sensors

Bioimpedance sensors identify how much tissue resists an electrical current by using an electric current that passes through the body. These sensors can measure changes in impedance and the data that is fed back can reflect tissue composition, fluid status, and the general health state. In body composition analysis, bioimpedance techniques are applied in applications such as body composition determination, cardiac recovery, and fluid status determination. Optical and electrical bioimpedance measurements can be noninvasive and are ideal for constant supervision of changes in the body's physiological conditions, and can be incorporated into wearable health monitors.

### 3.3.5 Thermal sensors

Thermal sensors record changes in skin temperature of the body. Examples of thermal sensors include infrared thermometers and temperature-sensitive patches, by which temperatures are measured from the body without touching the patient. These sensors are suitable for use in detecting fever, observing the body temperature while people are physically active, and for evaluating thermal balance. Recent advancements in thermal sensors have made it possible to produce wearable devices that track temperatures consistently, and provide useful information for diseases like fever and hypothermia.

### Vibration sensors

Vibration sensors encompass mechanical vibrations and movements in the body with certain physiological conditions and activities. Vibration sensors for physical activity, posture, and movements include accelerometers and gyroscopes found in most fitness trackers and smartwatches. These sensors help to determine and quantify the amount of physical activity in daily life, characterize gait, and perform fall detection, which improves the accessibility of musculoskeletal and neurological condition supervision.

### PPG sensors

Two PPG sensors operating at different wavelengths measure light absorption by tissues and blood in different ways to image the microvascular bed of the skin. Due to the alterations in light absorbed by the pulsatile blood flow, PPG sensors can inform estimates of the HR, BP, and vascular profiles. PPG technology has been often implemented in wearable devices, such as fitness trackers and smartwatches, which allow detecting various cardiovascular indicators in a non-contact way.

## 3.4 Biosensors

Biosensors are defined as a type of biomedical sensors, specifically developed to recognize biological substances and transform the interaction between these substances into an electric signal. They play an essential part in disease detection as well as treatment by giving accurate, selective, and rapid quantitative analysis of multiplex biological markers. This section aims at discussing the different categories of biosensors, the operation mechanisms of biosensors, and their practical utilization in the health sector.

### Principles of biosensor operation

The working of biosensors can be explained by means of a biological element interacting with a transducer element. The biological component has to bind with a target analyte, which is glucose. The transducer transforms this interaction into an electrical signal subsequently. Electrochemical, optical, and piezoelectric transducers can be classified as the commonly used transducers of biosensors, in which each type has its own advantage based on the situation. For instance, electrochemical biosensors are relatively preferred for glucose sensing because of their high sensitivity and user friendliness, while optical biosensors are preferred because they are label-free and facilitate biolayer sensing.

### Electrochemical biosensors

Electrochemical biosensors represent the largest and most popular biosensors because they are sensitive, easy to use, and inexpensive. These sensors quantify the variation in electrical current that accompanies the biochemical interaction of the bioreceptor with the target analyte. Some examples are blood glucose meters for diabetes, regulated by glucose oxidase as the bioreceptor and electrochemical transducers, which record the current change. Finally, electrochemical biosensors are employed for the identification of biomarkers that relate to different diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular disorders, for their early screening and tailored therapy.

### Optical biosensors

Optical biosensors work by using light to identify any variation in the biological processes. These sensors generally use surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and fluorescence instrumental methods for evaluating the bioreceptor’s analyte binding events. Some features of optical biosensors include high sensitivity, so there is no need for a label and thus the possibility to monitor the process in real-time, for example, in the case of pathogen detection, where changes in the optical property show the presence of certain bacteria. Optical biosensors are widely used in genomics and proteomics for the identification of targeted nucleic acid in a heterogeneous background.

### Piezoelectric biosensors

Piezoelectric biosensors utilize the piezoelectric effect, which occurs when an electrical charge is generated in a piezoelectric material due to mechanical strain. In these biosensors, the binding of a biological analyte to the bioreceptor causes a change in mass on the surface of the piezoelectric material, leading to a shift in its resonant frequency. This type of biosensor allows biomolecular interactions to be monitored in real time with high sensitivity. For example, it can be used to identify biomolecules such as proteins, where the frequency shift is proportional to the analyte concentration, enabling quantitative measurement of biological fluids.

### Biosensors in healthcare

The demand for biosensors in healthcare remains immense because they allow for accurate, fast, and highly localized measurement. They are used in point-of-care diagnostics as a medical instrument for regular and at-home screening, including glucose monitoring for diabetes, cholesterol tests, and some screening for infections. Biosensors are also used in the field of personalized medicine, so that clinicians can monitor particular biomarkers to address targeted patients. Besides, biosensors are used in drug development and clinical research to give information on drug reactions and drug effectiveness. The continuous innovation in biosensor technology such as advances in multi-biosensor and biosensors integrated with digital health technologies is anticipated to continue to increase their use in the health sector.

Biosensors are a promising and developing category of biomedical sensors, where improvements in the sensors’ performance and potential uses are continually being pursued.
