# # 1 Introduction

Deep learning (DL) has been embedded into material science in a way that deals with material discovery and characterization [1]. These models, with their capacity to analyze huge amounts of data and subtle patterns, have facilitated unparalleled developments [2]. DL algorithms often become algorithmically mystified, which creates interpretability problems [3]. Essentially, these models are often quite complex, and their formats contribute to a situation where the reasoning behind the forecasts is obscure [4]. This opacity can be problematic in terms of the utilization of the models in various sectors, especially where the models need to be sensitive, accurate as well as valid [5].

Interpretation in DL is defined as the extent to which architects are able to comprehend how a model produces certain results [6]. In material science, this capability is essential for verifying the results obtained from the model against the results expected in that particular field [7–9]. In particular, when DL models are utilized to forecast material characteristics and design, the audience must have confidence in the outcomes and comprehend the model’s outputs [10]. Inadequate interpretability can prevent the incorporation of these models into crucial applications, as it is necessary to avoid catastrophic mishaps resulting from incorrect predictions [11].

Several techniques have been designed to handle cases where interpretability issues arise in the context of DL [12]. Other visualization methods that describe the input data characteristics important for predictions include saliency maps and activation maps [13]. These bow-tie and action strategies help in understanding the model’s focused areas and the decision input data areas with significant influence on the model outputs [14]. In addition, methods called LIME and Shapley values provide a more fine-grained understanding of how certain inputs contribute to the model and offer local explanations for predictions [15]. While there has been some progress in the field, there are still several trade-offs to be expected when making models interpretable [16]. The increase in transparency often involves the use of model reduction or approximation methods, which decreases predictive capability [17]. The main challenge is how to achieve interpretability while at the same time maintaining high levels of accuracy [18]. For instance, the first models, which are much easier to understand, do not produce as accurate predictions as the more complicated structures of DL algorithms [19]. This trade-off highlights the need to assess the strengths and weaknesses of various interpretability techniques in the context of materials science applications [20].

In particular, regarding future trends in the comprehension of DL models for material science, it is probable that existing trends will focus on making processes more standardized and effective [21]. That is, improvements in algorithm design at the interpretability stage and the adoption of solution-specific knowledge could enhance the solutions [22, 23].
