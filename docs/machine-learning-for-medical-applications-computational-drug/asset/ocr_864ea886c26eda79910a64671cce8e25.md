The material design space was the environment in material property optimization, in which every design decision affected a material with certain constant properties as well as performance and other characteristics. The RL agent self-consistently explored this space in order to learn the parameter setting that gives the desired properties of the material.

Among the benefits derived from RL in material property optimization was that the technique fit well for complicated multiobjective optimization challenges. It can often mean that biomedical materials have to have several properties which can counteract each other. For instance, a material that is intended for use in a drug delivery system should meet characteristics of controlled release as well as biocompatibility. It is important to note that RL algorithms can easily extend the capability to achieve multiple goals in parallel by outlining proper reward functions that incorporate those trade-offs and would prove more useful for material design.

In the case of applying RL in this domain, a reward function is usually chosen to optimize exactly these material properties. The RL agent engages with simulation to judge the utility of different material designs. In the course of the interaction, the agent finds out which design features contribute to improved performance based on the reward signals accumulated. There are also more optimistic approaches to RL that include deep RL, which helps take advantage of a neural network in approximating reward functions and decision policies.

There are a number of significant case studies in biomedical material optimization, and one amongst them was in the designing of drug delivery systems. RL algorithms have been implemented to find the rate of drug release for drug delivery, the stability of the delivery system, and the compatibility of the system with biological tissues. Indeed, this decision-making process can be formulated as iteratively trying out different formulations and configurations through RL models and assessing their outcomes to determine the best configurations that would help achieve particular therapeutic objectives.

Nonetheless, RL faces certain challenges in optimizing material properties based on its application. The specificity of the RL models and the demand for a high amount of computational backbone can also be a disadvantage. The simulation of an environment sufficiently faithful to the real world and the assembling of suitable data to train RL models are crucial for advancing optimization work. To deal with these challenges, there is constant improvement of computation techniques, more data acquisition, and the creation of new models.
