
Conventional methods for processing data from structure-based virtual screening include gradient boosting algorithms which include the XGBoost tree and LightGBM tree. Gradient boosting methods eliminate the prediction errors that are associated with a model and increase the value of the model by training a series of weak learner models. Gradient boosting methods can be applied to virtual screening and other tasks in which compounds can be ranked based on their expected binding affinity score to interact with a specific protein. It is possible for gradient boosting algorithms to accurately rank possible medication candidates and generate good predictions of overall performance by aggregating numerous predominantly weak learners.

CNNs and GNNs are two of the deep learning algorithms that have shown promise in structure-based virtual screening. Whereas GNNs can effectively model molecular graphs and encode complex relationships between atomic elements and chemical functional groups at the same time, CNNs can provide 3D analysis of chemical structures and learn multi-scale representations of ligand-protein interactions. By using deep learning, it becomes possible to accurately predict binding affinities and identify potential treatments in large chemical databases.

## 4.3 Molecular dynamics simulations guided by AI

Molecular dynamics simulations are powerful tools of computational biology for studying the interactions between atoms in biomolecular systems. MD simulations can reveal the kinetic properties of proteins, ligands, and solvent molecules step-by-step by solving Newton's equation of motion of atoms and molecules. The extension of MD investigations has recently revealed much potential, particularly when combined with the use of AI strategies in the simulations.

A sample use of AI in molecular dynamics simulation is the speeding up of what is known as sampling methods. Typically used MD simulations are highly inefficient at sampling the conformational space especially when dealing with large biomolecular systems that have complex energy surfaces. It is also possible to utilize artificial neural network reinforcement and GAN to steer the MD simulations of interest-specific conformational states of molecules. Based upon the previous examples and dynamical modification of sampling techniques in real situations, AI-driven MD simulations can sample the states that are difficult to reach efficiently, resulting in enhanced sampling and faster convergence.

Another area of application of AI in MD simulation is the formulation of better sampling techniques. Modified sampling procedures, which include metadynamics, umbrella sampling, and replica exchange are useful in countering energy barriers and sampling more conformations of biomolecules with ease. AI can complement traditional sampling through deep learning metadynamics and Gaussian mixture model-based enhanced sampling, which can introduce knowledge-based biasing potentials into sampling protocols. Discussed here is the potential of incorporating principles of efficient sampling schemes with the ability of AI to learn from dynamics of the system and predict appropriate sampling directions to speed up the conformational sampling and improve sampling to subsequently refine the free energy computations.

The force field parameters and the molecular models required for MD simulations could be improved by employing AI techniques. Empirical force fields commonly applied in interactions of atoms and molecules rely on factors that are incapable of capturing the structures observed in biomolecular systems. It is found that AI methods can improve the accuracy of the molecular models as well as the force field parameters. Some of these strategies include machine learning-based force field parameterization, QM/MM hybrid simulations aided by neural networks. It can be noted that incorporation of AI in force field optimization increases the effectiveness of MD simulation in providing probable molecular interaction and attributes based on quantum mechanical and experimental data.

## 4.4 Machine learning approaches for predicting binding free energies

Both proteins and ligands are treatments for diseases, and the binding free energies that determine the strength of molecular interactions are critical in selecting potential drugs. ML techniques in particular have garnered considerable interest in this context because the modeling of a relationship between such features and binding affinities can be intricate. A number of approaches have been proposed for computing binding free energies reliably and in reasonable time.

The most commonly used machine learning methods for predicting binding free energies are the regression models. These models are built based on a set of protein-ligand complexes for which the corresponding binding affinities are experimentally measured. These basic models are identified as linear regression, random forest regression, and support vector regression (SVR). These models are therefore trained to equate binding free energy with molecular descriptors extracted from protein-ligand complexes. Using data on a variety of molecular characteristics, regression models can predict binding free energies for a number of new and unknown protein-ligand complexes.

Other machine learning methods for binding free energy predictions are the deep learning models. RNNs, GNNs, and CNNs reveal the ability to learn abstract representations of molecules together with the ability to incorporate sophisticated interactions within the protein and ligand complexes. In this work, we show that by training models on three-dimensional molecular representation of protein-ligand point-cloud interactions, deep learning models achieve better accuracy and generalization when predicting binding free energies.

For the purpose of the binding free energies predictions, gradient boosting machines (GBMs) and the ensemble regression techniques have been used. Result models stack up outcomes from two models like ordinary regression models for better outcomes and normalcy. The integration of predictions from multiple models constructed from the different data subsets using different algorithms in ensemble learning methods can indeed prevent overfitting, and provide more comprehensive requirements from different sources for accurately estimating binding free energies.

The use of scoring functions based on physics and molecular mechanics, and molecular dynamics, can be combined with the ML techniques and methods to improve binding free energy predictions. Although there are instances of inaccurate measure estimation, certain data, such as interaction fingerprints obtained via machine learning, can be merged with physical energy descriptors in APF architectures. The integration of the benefits from both methods has shown that hybrid models are capable of producing reliable predictions of binding free energies that incorporate the structural and interaction characteristics of protein-ligand complexes at the atomic level.
