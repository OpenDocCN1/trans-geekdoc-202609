
The creative nature of the piece, however, appealed to those more open-ended learners. This assignment allowed them to choose their own characters, setting, and conflict, while utilizing language of their choosing. In this respect, the assignment benefitted those more intuitive and abstract learners, providing them with a less rigid method for expressing their understandings of *The Raven*.

This piece was also designed to appeal to artistic students, who could create an in-depth, visual representation of their poem. Katie felt it was important to include this activity, as art is a pivotal method for expressing the meaning of written language, and yet is often overlooked in the English classroom. Furthermore, *The Raven* is largely about feeling and what you see as a reader, so it is an obvious opportunity to incorporate the skills of creative students.

Lastly, the presentation aspect of this activity engaged those students who enjoy public speaking and commanding the attention of a room. It offered them a brief spotlight to entertain and engage their classmates.

Remember that all students need to draw just as all students need to write, even those who are not “good at drawing.” As a result, each student experiences both relative success and relative struggle in various activities. Activities of this nature are consistent with the notion of multiple intelligences (Gardner 2006).

## Employing Technology to Enhance Inquiry Teaching

As we have seen in the preceding lesson examples, working with concrete materials is an important part of the exploration phase of the inquiry teaching and learning cycle. Modern technology has exponentially enhanced the possibilities of exploration and concept clarification for inquiry teaching lessons. However, technology should not take the place of students manipulating actual concrete materials, participating in role plays, interviewing community members, or working in the natural or social environment of the community. For example, once students have designed and carried out experiments in science using real materials to uncover the relationship between factors of a concept, students can go online and view animations or time-lapse photography of microscopic or molecular reactions or interactions. Students can research online primary sources for history explorations. They can interact with students in other countries around the world through websites that connect schools for all sorts of explorations in social studies. Once students in their small groups have uncovered a number of strategies to find a solution to a new math problem and students realize an elegant solution just as class ends, individual students who are still not clear in their understanding can now go to online resources such as Khan Academy (Khan 2012; http://www.khanacademy.org/) to have it clearly reviewed again.

Technology offers a range of possibilities for students to communicate their understandings to a larger community audience. They can create videos, musical recordings, photographic exhibitions, graphic illustrations, and published printed books. The power of these technologies will be expanded upon as we discuss project-based learning and service learning in Chapter 5.

Mike Farmer uses technology within the context of inquiry teaching in his classroom in Sheffield, Massachusetts where he teaches seventh- and eighth-grade social studies and tenth-grade economics. Having become tired of lecturing (just as his students did), Mike began to incorporate larger amounts of technology into his students’ classroom activities—mostly Internet research, PowerPoint creation, MS Word, online games and the occasional Skype conversations with students in Egypt. Mike comments about his use of technology below.

### Mike’s Reflection on His Use of Technology

> I began to experience the dual reality that using technology complicated matters as students commenced, progressed through, and finished activities all at different times; while they also received the benefits of being able to slow down, speed up, repeat, correct, and so on, at their own pace. In short, technology provided a very effective form of individually tailored education.
> 
> It was really from this point on that I decided it was important to completely change how students were learning in my classroom. Heidi Hayes-Jacobs (2011), Khan (2012), Prensky (2001), Shaw (2009), and the US Government (Office Technology 2010), to name a few, were all suggesting that massive amounts of technology needed to be incorporated into school curricula in order for the “digital natives” (Prensky 2001, 1) to be effective learners in American schools. Obviously, it is necessary to use technology that works. A dated computer with spotty Internet is a bad idea.

## Protocol for Transforming Lessons to Inquiry Teaching

As noted in Chapter 1, many activities teachers have traditionally used serve as confirmation for students after they have been *told about* some conceptual understanding, historical event, writing or reading skill, or strategy for problem solving. We have come to understand that students need to *construct* knowledge if they are to have any deep understanding and retention of concepts, or insight into the nature of inquiry in an academic area. They need to first explore the phenomenon through some type of inquiry exploration that allows them to negotiate ideas with their teammates, construct naïve conceptions of the phenomenon, and especially, identify and relate the factors relevant to the phenomenon. Then they are ready to hear what the larger academic community knows about the phenomenon under study. While doing this, students also acquire thinking skills and problem-solving skills that empower them as learners for a lifetime.

However, many times teachers have used lessons that have some useful elements and wish they would not have to abandon these lessons completely. These educators would like to *transform* these confirmation experiences into inquiry explorations. Unfortunately, making the change is not that simple, and the exploration portion is only one part of the process.

Educators need to take the whole didactic package of lecture and confirmation experience and transform it into an inquiry teaching and learning experience; where explorations that are focused on concepts, thinking skills, and habits of mind are commingled with negotiation, construction, and introduction of models that describe the phenomenon. These explorations by small groups of students, in which they create understandings and negotiate ideas, must come first. In science and mathematics, these almost always involve manipulating physical materials or model making. In social studies, role-playing, comparing primary source documents, or interviewing real people are typical activities. Role-playing, comparing various types of literature, and writing in various modalities for specific purposes are familiar activities in language arts classes.

Although inquiry teaching is an iterative process where when you get to a later step you often go back and modify the first step, teachers have found the progression of steps in this protocol to be useful.

Think back to the process Maria followed in developing and implementing the lessons on mitosis. The following description expands on the steps in that process.

- 1. **Identify five to ten big ideas for the whole course.**
  - What is the big concept that I expect students to have mastered to some degree during this inquiry teaching experience?
- 2. **Identify the sequence of topics, concepts, skills, and social issues for the year.**
  - Does this lesson answer some aspect of the essential, or better yet, driving question for the unit? Is it clear to students in the transition from the last lesson what question needs to be answered next?

### 3. Identify the main concepts to be learned in the lesson.

- Is this concept one expected either from national or state standards for the grade level of my students? What is it?
- What are the specific aspects of this big concept that students will learn during this particular lesson? What are the factors involved in this particular aspect of the concept?

### 4. Identify the initial idea for an exploration where students can uncover the whole concept, or at least part of the concept, without the teacher or media doing any telling about the concept.

- Can students witness a physical manifestation of the phenomenon, or perform an initial exploration from which they can design an investigation or identify factors involved in the phenomenon?

This is the key step in transforming didactic, transmission lessons to inquiry teaching lessons where students uncover the concept through an exploration, just as academics throughout history did.

- How much do students get to manipulate materials during this initial exploration phase?
- How much needs to be done as drawing visual models?
- Can students use primary sources?
- How much can be done with role plays or simulations?
- Does this exploration have the appropriate factors involved in a way that students can intuit the relationship between factors in the concept and possibly even create a generalization from their experience?

Once you as the teacher identify the factors involved in the phenomenon, look at the exploration that you have created and see if students can:

- design all or part of the investigation needed to find out the answer to the question.
- identify the factors involved in the phenomenon, concept, or skill so they can partially design the approach to finding an answer.
- hypothesize possible relationships between factors.
- begin to understand how particular approaches to problem solving work in particular situations, and can begin to see how they might be used in analogous situations.
