
The lesson that Andrea shares with us is one of the first she ever did using primary sources with her first graders. The complete lesson, “Utopia: One Great Family,” can be found on the Digital Resource CD. Previously, Andrea had planned inquiry teaching in other subject areas but not history. (See Andrea’s reflection about becoming an inquiry teacher in Chapter 3.)

## Andrea’s Social Studies Lesson

To begin the lesson, Andrea reads a picture book about the Underground Railroad. She asks students what they think it was like being a runaway slave going through Florence. She extends this idea to the bigger, yet more personal idea of what can be done if something is unfair. Students begin their exploration by creating a circle word map with the word “community” in the middle. This activity leads students to the idea of a community banding together.

Together, Andrea and students read parts of photocopies of the original constitution from the NAEI. They also have other primary sources—maps of the town with stops of the railroad on it, a newspaper article from the local paper in 1867 written by the treasurer of the NAEI, and photographs of the buildings associated with the group. After viewing these resources, students add new ideas to their circle word map. They finish by working in small groups to write one sentence about each of two words that Andrea assigned them from the circle word map. In classes Andrea previously taught, there were even some students who were living in or next to houses that were on the Underground Railroad map! Subsequently, students were able to travel along the *African Heritage Trail* in Florence led by tour guides from the local historical society.

Andrea explains how she became excited about using primary documents for the first time and how they fit so well into her inquiry-teaching format.

## What Andrea Was Thinking

Andrea had always used literature as a way to bring history to life for her students. Researching and creating this primary-source-based lesson had forever altered how she thought about and planned her history lessons. Using these primary sources can really bring to life a period in history like nothing else. Giving her students a chance to view old photographs, and even be able to see the different type of handwriting in a letter, can open their eyes to a whole world. It was even interesting for them to see how the newspaper looked so different than it does today. They compared the copies Andrea got from the microfilm to the newspaper today—wow, what a difference! Students actually began their exploration by doing history, by being historians using primary sources. They uncovered the concepts at a first-grade level and then communicated them.

When Andrea first looked at the Library of Congress website, she was a bit skeptical about using primary sources with first graders. How could she use materials with them that they could not read or even understand? After planning her lesson and community project, she felt much more confident about teaching with primary sources, even to the youngest students. Primary sources could give them a window into history in a way that literature alone could not; photos, maps, letters, articles and, most of all, walking to the actual historic site in their own neighborhood was an awesome gift. She was so excited! Of course all of this led to deep understanding at a first-grade level of some complex social studies concepts and provided great motivation for Common Core sentence-writing skills.

## Using Nationally Validated Curricula Materials in Science and Mathematics

Nationally validated curricula materials that have been created through field-tested and researched National Science Foundation (NSF) funded projects provide valuable resources for science and mathematics teachers who wish to move toward inquiry teaching. These curricula have taken decades to develop and should be considered by any district or school looking to move to inquiry teaching. They have been field tested with thousands of students including gifted, special education, and language learners in urban, rural, and suburban areas. These materials provide a carefully devised package of the whole-inquiry learning experience. Any school or district looking to move toward inquiry teaching should visit these websites for an up to date list of the materials available

- http://www.nsf.gov/pubs/2002/nsf02084/chap1_4.htm
- http://www.nsresources.org/topic.cfm?topic=IM&allh=true

In mathematics, some of these curricula have been utilized in urban schools and districts and, when teachers were provided appropriate professional development, students have experienced enviable success. You will hear from a number of teachers who have utilized some of these curricula.

Although some of the middle- and high-school curricula do have some form of student book, the elementary FOSS and STC science curricula do not have textbooks. That is intentional. Each has suggested lists of science nonfiction trade books that are more appropriate for elementary grades and fit seamlessly with language arts lessons being taught in the same classroom. If you are looking for a way to increase engagement, differentiate instruction, and incorporate more informational text and student writing into your curriculum then Teaching with Text Sets (Cappiello and Thulin-Dawes 2012) is your answer. This must-have resource walks you through the steps to create and use multi-genre, multimodal text sets for content area (including science and math) and language arts study.

However, some practitioners will find that even these nationally validated curricular activities can be opened up for more inquiry to allow for richer student experiences. Others, because of internal school politics, need to transform laboratory experiences already utilized in their schools. In either case, the suggestions in the section later in this chapter attempt to create a protocol that teachers could use to transform each of these materials into a richer inquiry experience.

## The Planning Process for Sixth-Grade Mathematics

Carolina Vila, a mathematics teacher from Andrews Middle School in Medford, Massachusetts, has modified a NSF nationally validated lesson from the Connected Math Program (CMP). She has embedded her lesson in a year of units based on social issues that matter to her students. Creating units around social issues allows students to engage in deeply creating academic understandings. (This topic is explored in detail in Chapter 5.)

The following are Carolina’s units for the year for sixth- and seventh-grade mathematics based on the Common Core State Standards (NGA and CCSSO 2010b). For a full description of Carolina’s plans, “Sixth Grade Math Year Overview and Unit Introduction,” refer to the Digital Resource CD.

### Unit 1: The Number System: Is Global Warming in Medford Real?

For this unit, students can delve into the controversial topic of global warming. This topic allows for opportunities in which students can use negative and positive numbers, decimals, fractions, percent, and so on.

### Unit 2: Expressions and Equations: How Did Carlos Slim Helu Become the Richest Man on Earth?

Here, students can use expressions and equations to create an excel sheet (or some other program) that quickly keeps track of a business’s orders, expenses, profits, and so on. They can create their own “businesses” in their small groups.

## What Carolina Was Thinking

Unit four easily lent itself to being a hands-on inquiry unit where students uncovered mathematical solutions and a given concept during their small group explorations. Whether it was in 2-D or 3-D, manipulatives could be used each step of the way. Students filled in shapes with squares to prove their areas, as well as filled in figures with blocks to prove their volumes. Working under the essential question of “Can we trust our own eyes?” students also began to develop better estimation skills as they moved through the unit. However, the importance of exact measurement was tied in through explorations into the worlds of medicine, sports, machines, and so on. Since this unit was heavy with formulas, it was essential that students worked to uncover the formulas instead of the formulas being given to them.

This particular lesson aimed to have students uncover the best way to figure out how much a rectangular prism could hold (the volume). Traditionally, teachers told students that *V=lwh* (volume = length x width x height) was the formula and students would input the right numbers to arrive at the answer. This way of teaching volume, however, leads to only a few students fully understanding why the formula looks the way it does; no less being able to apply it to unique situations at the appropriate time. By having students arrive at the formula on their own, they reach a deeper understanding of why the formula is what it is. It also empowers them to be able to create other formulas later in the unit. Lastly, the lesson called for students to apply their own formulas, instead of one found in a math book.

In previous lessons, students have looked at measuring the surface area of boxes and have made boxes with one-centimeter grids on the outside. Carolina now wants them to move their understandings from two-dimensional measurement to three-dimensional measurement: volume.
