## Inquiry Teaching in Science

In those years, we found our attention turning to other subject areas as well. One day, a first-grade teacher stopped me in the hall with a question about science. According to the district STC science curriculum, she told me, her students were supposed to study trees. The lesson in the written curriculum actually suggested, “Show the children pictures of leaves in a book to help them learn to identify trees.”

She explained, “I think the kids would learn more about trees if we went outside, but I don’t know how to manage that.”

“I completely agree,” I replied, “and I know who can help us.” I resolved to connect our teachers to the skillful educators from the Hitchcock Center for the Environment in Amherst, Massachusetts. Our PTO (Parent Teacher Organization) agreed to fund visits for each class from Hitchcock educators. I encouraged teachers to think of the visits as a way for them to learn how to do what the visiting naturalist was demonstrating, rather than as a one-time visit from an expert. As a result, Ted Watt of Hitchcock began as our mentor to understanding the world of nature and implementing inquiry science—a relationship that continues today.

These visits were so successful and welcomed by teachers that they were eager to more deeply commit to inquiry nature studies. Luckily, a perfect opportunity presented itself in the form of a new endowment grant offered by our local Northampton Education Foundation. Led by Andrea Egitto, a committed and knowledgeable primary grade teacher, we wrote and were awarded the first large grant which provided three years of grade-level-specific training by Ted Watt. The grant enabled us to transform relevant portions of our science program to experiential inquiry field study science in natural areas within walking distance of the school. These included a nearby conservation area with varied habitats from forest to rock outcroppings to vernal pools.

Every teacher committed to this work. Additionally, the paraprofessionals who supervise students during recess came to me asking, “We want to learn from Mr. Ted, too.” Together we wrote a small grant that enabled them to have a series of after-school workshops to learn inquiry science. They produced a handy recess guide offering questions that adults could ask students at recess to encourage their conjectures and experiments such as figuring out what determines the course of the rivers that are created by melting snow. The adults encouraged the students to experiment by building tiny dams with found objects like sticks, stones, and leaves to divert the playground rivers. We also began our annual “Last Surviving Snowbank Contest.” This involved recess observations and conjectures from each class about which of the plowed snow banks around the playground would melt last (and why) with the prize being an extra recess. Thus inquiry and problem-solving were encouraged—even at recess, and our students were invited to ask questions and collaborate throughout the day. It was our hope that our students would continue these investigations at home in their neighborhoods. Their sharing at classroom morning meetings indicated that this happened.

## Writing Workshop Approach

Another academic area that our teachers felt needed improvement was writing. As they shared their concerns and questions with me, I realized that they had had limited training in the workshop approach to writing, which can be based on inquiry. We began to consider ways to put in place our own writing workshop approach. I wrote a small grant through which I was able to offer teachers training under Lynn Bowmaster’s leadership of writing workshops in third-, fourth-, and fifth-grade classrooms. We set up the grant to allow each class a series of sessions with Lynn and with the teacher participating along with students in order to experience being a writer, and to learn how to manage a writing workshop. In participating with their students, teachers were able to puzzle over their own questions:

- What does it mean to be a writer?
- Why is choice so important in writing?
- What inspires writers?
- How does the audience provide feedback to the writer?
- How can a writing workshop reach each student and inspire individual creativity?

We found our staff room and faculty meetings were becoming lively with conversations about math, science, and writing that revolved around our professional questions and led to improved classroom practice in all subject areas.

## Collaborations with Students

I also began to notice that, as principal, I had lots of opportunities to encourage a culture of inquiry and collaborative problem-solving among our students. For instance, if the weather was iffy, I would go out in the hallway and invite a passing student to be my “weather consultant,” to tell me whether we should have outside or inside recess. We would grab our coats or an umbrella and head outdoors to make the decision. One time, a deliveryman was coming up the walk, and we asked him what he thought. “Oh, they should definitely go out,” he declared, and we agreed. The idea that anyone could participate in the inquiry was reinforced: These occasions also taught our students that people all around are interested in the problems we approach at school and that we can all contribute ideas to solutions.

When it was time to change the notice on the marquee in front of the school, I would invite several students to help me. We would gather up the box of letters, the padlock key, and the old broomstick to hold open the sign. As we walked out, I would ask students, “What do you think people need to know about our school? What’s coming up that would interest them? How should we tell about it on the marquee?” Some of the best ideas I heard came from students. More importantly, however, small steps like those encouraged students to realize that we solve problems all day, and that their ideas contribute to solutions that they can see and that other people notice and appreciate.

One day during spring, a fifth grader suggested, “You know, Mrs. Riddle, I think we should have a Walk and Bike to School Week.”

“Great idea,” I said. “Let’s do it the first week after vacation, and let’s put it on the marquee right now so we all can look forward to it.” That began a yearly tradition in which we encouraged everyone in the school to walk and bike to school for the last week in April. We always remembered that this was Javier’s idea, even after he had moved on to middle and high school.

The school community’s dedicated commitment to the Responsive Classroom approach fostered collaboration and inquiry in the social and emotional realm also. This approach encourages a school to develop a culture of collaboration. It acknowledges that adherence to the principles of Responsive Classroom will not make problems disappear, but, rather, will offer an effective way to approach them collaboratively.
