
## Observations About Using Physical Materials in Practice

The use of manipulatives is the norm in effective science and mathematics classrooms. However, many teachers think of physical historical materials such as small industrial tools or machines, uniforms or weapons, and such that can be borrowed from or viewed at museums as the only manipulatives that can be used in history classrooms. These are valuable materials. However, much of the time spent on a role play where students get to experience what previous generations in various circumstances had to endure is also a manipulation of materials—their own bodies and minds. We see an example of this with the bags of cotton students experienced in Mike’s class. This is followed by another manipulation of materials—interpretation of primary source documents. A textual analysis of writings related to John Brown’s raid written by contemporaries has students deeply analyze issues of point of view and writing for an audience. The following role play allows students to create their own point of view and defend it while solidifying the concept and enriching students’ Common Core skills.

The inquiry-process steps used in Mike’s history lesson closely follow the Common Core Reading Standards for Literacy in History/Social Studies for grades six to eight. Figure 1.3 presents these standards.

These strategies prepare students for any Common Core-based testing and develop the skills necessary for effective citizenry. The role-playing aspects that lead to preadolescents’ emotional reactions to past historical events deeply engage their personal sense of fairness and justice and transform “dead” history into a vibrant living experience.

## An Example of a Common Core Mathematics Requirement Demonstrating Deeper Thinking

We have seen how the steps of the inquiry teaching and learning cycle correlate with the Common Core literacy standards in Figure 1.2, and likewise, how well the steps of the cycle of Mike’s inquiry teaching history lesson correlate with the history part of the standards (see Figure 1.3).

The Common Core Standards for Mathematical Practice almost seem to have been written with the inquiry teaching and learning cycle in mind. These standards follow:

- **MP 1:** Make sense of problems and persevere in solving them.
- **MP 2:** Reason abstractly and quantitatively.
- **MP 3:** Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others.
- **MP 4:** Model with mathematics.
- **MP 5:** Use appropriate tools strategically.
- **MP 6:** Attend to precision.
- **MP 7:** Look for and make use of structure.
- **MP 8:** Look for and express regularity in repeated reasoning.

**CCSS.Math.Practice.MP 3: Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others** illustrates the nature of deeper thinking that is integral to inquiry teaching and learning.

Mathematically proficient students understand and use stated assumptions, definitions, and previously established results in constructing arguments. They make conjectures and build a logical progression of statements to explore the truth of their conjectures. They are able to analyze situations by breaking them into cases, and can recognize and use counterexamples. They justify their conclusions, communicate them to others, and respond to the arguments of others. They reason inductively about data, making plausible arguments that take into account the context from which the data arose. Mathematically proficient students are also able to compare the effectiveness of two plausible arguments, distinguish correct logic or reasoning from that which is flawed, and—if there is a flaw in an argument—explain what it is. Elementary students can construct arguments using concrete referents such as objects, drawings, diagrams, and actions. Such arguments can make sense and be correct, even though they are not generalized or made formal until later grades. Later, students learn to determine domains to which an argument applies. If students are engaged in daily explorations that include manipulation of physical materials, games, and creation of products that are reliant on their physical and thinking skills and are all carefully facilitated by the teacher, then students will stay truly engaged and learning will be successful. However, whenever possible, even the activities with these elementary school students should include applications to their home and community life so that students begin to effectively apply their newly acquired skills and conceptual understandings to decision making in their daily lives.

In addition, almost every skill and concept taught at the preschool and elementary level in every content area is something that can immediately be applied in students’ daily lives. Mathematical concepts can easily be applied in cooking, baking, building with wood, sewing, sports statistics, and calculations used in daily purchases. All of the science concepts and investigation and decision-making skills can immediately be used in their daily lives. They are all concepts that can actually be viewed and measured with even the simplest lenses and scales. The language arts skills can be taught using situations from students’ lives, literature that relates to and expands their lives, and by creating writing that is published in books, blogs, and newsletters and performed in plays and songs.

In early childhood social studies, much of the curriculum is actually studying the culture and local history of where the students live. In upper elementary social studies and in all of the middle and high school content areas, some of the topics studied do not immediately appear relevant to students’ lives, and especially to their inherent psychological needs. The study of ancient Persia or Japan does not appear immediately relevant. Greek or Medieval literature seems written in awkward language with dated characters. Calculating centrifugal force seems unimportant to almost every sixteen-year-old. Likewise, uncountable numbers of students have asked when will they ever need to use a quadratic equation.

Yet each of these topics, if embedded in the right explorations, contextualized in challenging local projects with the appropriate large conceptual objectives and effective themes, can be made engaging and deeply relevant to the learner’s life. It is important to investigate what is relevant to early and later adolescents. Perhaps a better way to ask the question is “what matters to teenagers?” Before you read on, please take one or two minutes to write down your top two answers to this question. If possible, discuss these ideas with your colleagues or students’ parents.
