Another student who studied in an inquiry classroom compared his thinking in the two types of classrooms. He states,

> Teachers I had before would just teach math and they would help you write out ways, instead of try to take your time to understand it yourself. Many students in inquiry math classrooms change their notion of problem solving. They come to see there are different ways to think about a problem; thus, there are different ways to approach the process of solving the problem.

> My problem solving strategies have changed drastically. The way I think is way different than the way I thought last year. I look at math in a different perspective since I’m seeing how the real world can relate to math in so many different ways. What we often hear from students is that mathematics is irrelevant, boring, and hard. However, teaching using an inquiry approach has the possibility of changing students’ minds and even helping them feel joy in mathematics. Students’ productive disposition changes because they begin to see that math is much more open and welcoming of different ways of thinking. Paul Lockhart states what could happen to a student if he or she studied in an inquiry classroom. So I close this presentation with these powerful words.

> So let it be hard. Try not to get discouraged or to take your failures too personally. It’s not only you that is having trouble understanding mathematical reality; it’s all of us. Don’t worry that you have no experience or that you’re not “qualified.” What makes a mathematician is not technical skill or encyclopedic knowledge, but insatiable curiosity and a desire for simple beauty. Just be yourself and go where you want to go. Instead of being tentative and fearing failure or confusion, try to embrace the awe and mystery of it all and joyfully make a mess. Yes, your ideas won’t work. Yes, your intuition will be flawed. Again, welcome to the club. I have a dozen bad ideas a day and so does every mathematician.

> —Lockhart (2012, 10)

## Challenges I Have Faced: David Nurenberg Reflects

Ever since I have adopted inquiry teaching, project-based instruction, and democratic classroom practices, my job has become much more challenging. It is also more rewarding, as I see my students more engaged, and thinking on more complex levels, which is why I persevere. A traditional classroom is definitely easier to map out and manage—you have a plan, and you stick to it. You are in control, you have set the rules, you know the parameters, and you enforce them. In an inquiry classroom, you have to make all sorts of contingencies and remain flexible because you are never entirely sure where kids might decide to take an activity or a conversation. I cannot just sit at my desk and grade as students engage in a writing activity. I have to always be up and about, listening in on their groups, offering feedback or posing questions. Students may decide to try and set norms that I do not agree with, and then it is a matter of discussion and negotiation that can swallow up half or even an entire class. Every week I make plans, and every day I have to revise them.

Cooperative work, by its very nature, is messy. I try and be upfront with the kids in advance—I tell them that there will be conflict between them, there will be struggles and frustrations because you have four or five different people with different ideas all trying to work together. Someone inevitably tries to freeload, gets bossy, or says sharp words. It is very tempting to try and swoop in as the authority figure to try to resolve a group conflict; they very often want me to do so. However, unless a student’s safety (physical or emotional) is being threatened, I almost always turn it back to them, and that is hard. I care deeply about my students, and when I see what to me seems like an easy solution, I want to propose or even unilaterally enforce it. I have to fight against my, and their, inclinations, and instead help them talk it out and work to their own resolution that they can own. It is not always perfect, and that in itself is a lesson. As they struggle, they grow from it, and by year’s end, they have grown leaps and bounds in their ability to self-regulate and manage conflicts productively—sometimes to even welcome those conflicts as learning experiences.

Along the way, though, this methodology can be a tough sell, to students and especially to their parents. I have learned to do a lot of prepping with parents in order to let them know the educative and maturational value of what I am planning to do, and to reassure them that, yes, I do intervene in intergroup conflicts unilaterally in extreme situations. I once had a parent actually pull her child from a class of mine out of fears her daughter would find her own grade jeopardized by other students’ poor performance in groups. I have learned, in my high-powered school, where parents and students are extremely grade-conscious, to make sure that every student has plenty of assessments where he or she, and only he or she, is responsible for his or her grade. I try and remind parents and students alike that learning how to manage cooperative work is a vital skill in any modern professional job, and that if students do not learn those skills here, in a safe environment, they will be at a disadvantage later on.

Although it is becoming more and more recognized for the set of best practices that it is, inquiry teaching, and those who seek to engage in it, still faces additional institutional hurdles and pushback. For all its good intentions, the outcomes-based movement in education has encouraged (or reinforced existing) conformity within schools and departments. If you are doing something different from your colleagues, that in and of itself can bring questions or even criticism. All teachers face something of a dance between innovating and adhering to required standards (as mandated by the state, your school, your department chair, or your instructional team). What I do in the classroom sometimes does not look like an English class—the kids are not all reading the same novel at the same time; they are not always writing traditional literary essays. I draw on a great deal of material from other disciplines, from history to psychology to math, and sometimes this raises eyebrows. My best defense here lies in the products my students create. If we still get to “Rome,” does it matter what road we take? Frankly, I think we get a little beyond Rome because of how we operate.

Cooperative work is not just for students! My most successful lessons and structures have come when I have been able to coordinate and cooperate with my colleagues; I have been able to learn so much from the input of my fellow teachers, and, at our best, we all share, duplicate, and modify from one another freely in the same cooperative atmosphere that we try to instill in an inquiry classroom. By that same token, my most frustrating experiences have come when this system breaks down, when colleagues get defensive or suspicious. More than once, I have been told not to engage in certain practices and projects simply because they edge in on someone else’s territory. A particularly unfortunate example of this came when several colleagues and I had successfully obtained a $15,000 grant to teach an after-school interdisciplinary, inquiry-based class, ran it successfully past the administration and even the union, only to have one influential teacher condemn it because he feared it could draw students away from another course offering. I argued, to no avail, that our programming should be based on student needs, and that if students found that one class served them better than another, then that was the class we should offer. No luck. Fearful of engendering this teacher’s ire, my cooperating colleagues jumped ship, and no one was willing to teach the course any longer. We had to return the grant, students were disappointed, and the course never even got a chance to prove its mettle. Know the politics of your school, and do what you can to navigate them well.

Ironically, teachers in low socio-economic, high-needs schools may have more freedom to teach with non-traditional methods than teachers in more well-provided, higher socio-economic environments—when students are failing to graduate or earn high scores on state tests, school administrations are often more willing to allow teachers the freedom to do whatever it takes to produce results. In wealthy suburban schools, where students’ test scores and college admissions will likely remain high regardless of the approach teachers use, it is easier for tradition or politics to dictate what pedagogies are permitted. The kind of data you will need to defend your pedagogy in a school like this will be samples of student work, through the kinds of assessments that reveal higher order thinking is going on, as well as as much feedback from students and parents as you can collect.

I have learned that, ultimately, how much you are able to incorporate inquiry structures into your classroom will depend not only upon you and your students’ comfort level but also the comfort level of your colleagues and community. You will need to develop your ability to manage those relationships, to build social and professional capital, and either to forge relationships and compromise, or, at times, to simply shrug and accept that you will always have critics. Remember that your primary duty is to your students, and let that guide you through your development as an educator.
