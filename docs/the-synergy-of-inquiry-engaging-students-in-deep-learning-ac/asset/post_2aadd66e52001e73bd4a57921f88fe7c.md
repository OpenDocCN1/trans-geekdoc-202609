## Alignment of Adult and Student Learning and Assessment

School supervision is adult education. The principal’s class is his or her faculty. As a principal, I believe the way adults learn needs to parallel the way you would like to see them work with students, and usually does. If the principal stands at the front of the auditorium once a month for a faculty meeting, and spends the time pontificating about administrivia rather than facilitating adult learning, then do not expect anything different the next day than a teacher lecturing in front of every classroom.

Intent on doing it differently myself, I knew that if I wished to see students deeply engaged in inquiry-based activities and projects, then I needed to organize teachers similarly. Early on in my tenure as principal, I realized that the core of such engagement could be to involve the faculty in shared governance. Since our school was on a college campus, and college faculty members are more involved in governance matters than teachers in K-12, it was an obvious connection to make. Our shared governance began by forming a faculty personnel committee charged with hiring new teachers. After hiring the staff for the first year of the school, teachers took over and hired their future colleagues thereafter.

Having hired their colleagues, I did not find it difficult to convince my faculty to form peer support groups so that teachers who had been at the school longer could mentor their newest colleagues. Soon peer support gave way to peer review, and we developed a formal way for teachers to evaluate one another by portfolio, thus paralleling the way teachers were evaluating students for graduation. ## Math Class

The first lesson was in a math class in which the teacher had taught the formulas for calculating the areas of a circle, rectangle, and triangle. The project assignment was to work in small groups to determine the most efficient shape. That struck me as an odd, but interestingly open-ended, question.

The group I sat with had brought in pennies from home and proceeded to see how many pennies they could fit in a circle, rectangle, and triangle of the same area. Sure enough, the triangle was able to hold more pennies. That served to remind me of something I once read about why the ancient Egyptians built pyramids. However, that is only where the learning for these students began. One of them noticed that in the circle, each successive concentric circle had six more pennies than the last. The group decided to find out why.

They took up a collection of quarters, carefully noting how much they needed to return to each contributor. Once again, each successive concentric circle had six more quarters than the previous one. Now students got really excited. They went down to the school cafeteria and came back with 6-inch paper plates. Again, each new concentric circle had six more paper plates than the previous one. Students now knew they were on to something.

They ran up to the teacher to explain their findings and ask why it occurred this way. The teacher, recertified in mathematics, confessed that he did not know. But he suggested that they go down the hall to check with the physics teacher, who was more of a mathematician.

The physics teacher listened to students’ findings and smiled. He said that each concentric circle was not exactly 6 units larger, but 6.28318530718 or 2π, with π being approximately 3.14. These students had inadvertently discovered π, and once having done so, would likely never forget. I know I have not.

## Independent Science Project

The next instance of most memorable examples of inquiry learning was an independent science project. One student had determined to study the difference in vision between carnivores and herbivores. In order to do so, she had talked her way behind the exhibits in the small mammals’ wing of the American Museum of Natural History in New York City. There she was permitted to make plaster of Paris masks of the skulls of a deer and a leopard.

The student later took these masks into a dark room at school where she had rigged portable lighting, shone the light through the mask, and measured the arc of light emanating onto the wall from the eye sockets of each mask. These data revealed that carnivores see a narrower angle right in front of them to better focus on their prey. Herbivores, however, have a much wider angle of vision, enhancing their peripheral vision, and enabling them to see attackers approaching from the sides. This work went far beyond anything I remember having studied in science class during my own high school experience. In fact, this young woman understood the scientific method far better than I did at that stage of my education.

## Combined Science and Math

The final memorable example of inquiry learning took place in a combined science and math double period class. The collaborating teachers were working with the Salvadori Institute at the City College of New York, to engage students in a bridge project. Working in small groups, students identified an existing suspension bridge to both study and build a scale model. Their studies would involve learning everything they could about the history, physics, and mathematics of the bridge they had selected; and then, build a replica to scale.

The unit took several weeks. Students were so engaged that they came early before the start of the school day, stayed for hours after school, and came in on weekends of their own volition to work on these bridges. The work spilled out of the time and space allotted and began infringing on other classes and places in the school. You can imagine my delight as principal at coming across unattended students using box cutters and power tools in the hall.

However, it was well worth these "inconveniences" when students presented their completed projects. For the first time, I had witnessed every student in a heterogeneous class present an outstanding bridge model, and as importantly, they could speak eloquently and authoritatively on the history, physics, and math of their respective bridge. It certainly left a vivid impression with me all these years later.

## Professional Development in Mathematics Teaching

One of the most pressing problems we needed to address immediately concerned our poor scores on the previous year’s state tests, the MCAS (Massachusetts Comprehensive Assessment System). I arrived at my new post from teaching fifth grade in another district school where my own students had performed well, and I expected that students at Ryan Road School could certainly match them. In particular, I knew that the mathematics program the district uses was especially aligned with the state curriculum frameworks. However, it also is a challenging inquiry-teaching program that is difficult to teach well, and I suspected that its implementation at the school was not thorough. I began meeting with teachers for half-day curriculum work, starting with math. At the testing grades (third, fourth, and fifth) we spent time analyzing our test results, looking carefully at the test questions, and talking together about how we might expect our students to be able to tackle them. I noticed teachers saying, “These questions look just like the problems in *Investigations*. We should be using it better to prepare students for these tests.”

In this way, their own analysis led teachers to a new commitment to teaching the math program using the inquiry approach it embraces. In order to rise to the task, teachers asked me to provide professional development in effectively using an inquiry approach to mathematics. Interestingly, while this request arose from their own collaboration around what might improve test scores, it eventually led to much improved teaching of mathematics across the grades as well as to resulting improved test scores. I responded to their request by offering advanced training in the *Investigations* program through TERC (https://www.terc.edu/display/HOME/Home) and also in inquiry mathematics through *Summermath for Teachers* at Mt. Holyoke College. These graduate seminars are particularly focused on inquiry mathematics.

At the same time, the district agreed to implement an unusual elementary math-coaching program that supported inquiry among teachers at our school. In this coaching model, each district elementary school identified three math coaches who remained full-time classroom teachers. They were able to coach their colleagues for an additional dedicated stipend. As they began their new roles, the coaches told their teacher colleagues, “We are doing this not because we have the answers, but because we want to work on professional questions together with you.” This cultivation of an inquiry approach to professional development—in which colleagues share professional questions with each other in a safe environment—was at the heart of the community we were developing together.
