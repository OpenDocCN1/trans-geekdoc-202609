If a teacher can meet these needs while engaging students in learning, then it is a natural, organic process. It is what students want to do, not something that they must be forced to do. They will do it even if it is not required. Let us use the humanistic psychologists’ explanation of human needs, which is supported by our current understanding of brain neurobiology, to put this in a better conceptual perspective. In the 1950s and 60s, Abraham Maslow (1968) and his colleagues studied individuals who were recognized as the most mentally healthy individuals they knew. From this study, the researchers deduced a hierarchical set of human needs. Until one need is reasonably met, the others are not of consequence to the person. After testing this with thousands of individuals around the world, researchers have come to realize that this sequence of needs is *genetically programmed* into all humans. This matches well with our knowledge of neurobiology where the amygdala, the most ancient part of our evolutionary brain, is surrounded by the frontal cortex and other reasoning parts of the brain.

Figure 1.4 illustrates the hierarchy of needs (Maslow 1968). You can see that the survival and more emotional aspects of the hierarchy are the foundation and therefore, always override the more rational aspects up the triangle.

Once people have met their physiological needs—food, clothing, and shelter—then they have to focus on their safety needs. Homeless students keep slipping back into this category and cannot readily focus on anything happening in school. Safety needs include the ability to regularly predict what will happen in your life—not just your physical safety, but also your psychological well-being. This is why students who come from unstable homes often have a difficult time focusing on academics. It is why divorces that upset family routines and create constant uncertainty can send students into a tailspin. But among teenagers who have these needs met, the focus is on two aspects of adolescence—belonging and esteem. These next two levels of *belonging* (acceptance in a group) and *esteem* (respect for competence) are where *teens must be generatively*. We keep asking them to be at the top level, self-actualization, where you can function once the other levels are sufficiently fulfilled, but of course adolescents do not have these levels filled. Nevertheless, schools generally ask them to do things because they will need it in the future, and not to worry about what their friends think. Yet, these are all things that are difficult for teenagers, and virtually impossible for others, to do.

If we can run our classrooms in a way that meets these needs while students are engaged in explorations that uncover concepts and provide practice for their thinking skills, then students will want to be there. They will put more effort into learning than most teachers have had the opportunity to experience.

## Inquiry Teaching in Practice

Thirteen years into my teaching career, I turned to Terry Born, a co-teacher, and pointed at our classroom and said, “Look at *that*. I never imagined it could be this self-sustaining. They are so intellectually and socially engaged. It is beyond anything I could imagine.” Students had engaged in two role plays about an underlying theme in a piece of literature that they were reading, and they were working in small groups to create some two-minute theater pieces that would demonstrate their current insights and understandings of the relationship between the science and social science concepts of *evolution and revolution*. What was so amazing was the complete self-motivation of each group as they first intellectually argued with one another about the intricacies of these complex concepts, and then worked equally hard to dramatically convey their intended meaning. The previous term, a number of these students were not attending their didactic transmission style classes. It boggled my mind.

## Making Inquiry Teaching Happen

So what do you need to do in order to make this happen? Find large themes that matter both in personal relationships and in the academic subjects. “The right of the individual versus the right of society” not only is at the center of teens’ lives, it also appears in most social studies, language arts, and science and engineering lessons. Similarly “power over versus power with” is something teens are facing every day of their lives. This concept shows up in not only the subjects mentioned so far, but also in various aspects of statistics and algebra. How you plan units that are built upon these themes is clearly explained in later chapters in this book.

A quick example might be an activity following up students’ role-playing of teens in 1859 and the John Brown’s raid situation. The teacher might create the following *analogous interrelationship role play* about modern times that looks at doing something “illegal,” but for the “greater good.” The teacher has students play a group of teens who have decided to use songs from each of their MP3 players to create a “Fight Carcinoma” CD that they will sell for 10 dollars to raise money for cancer research. Although this is pirating copyrighted music, students may say they paid for the download, and the benefit from the sales outweighs the legal issues. Some of their parents and friends may disagree. Because this dilemma could be one encountered in their daily lives and mirrors the dilemma from a long ago situation (John Brown’s Harpers Ferry raid), the teens can now more easily focus on this complex moral dilemma.

## Concepts, Skills, and Habits of Mind in Practice

Concepts, thinking skills, and habits of mind provide a framework for and are integral to the elements of inquiry teaching. As students participate in inquiry-based activities, they are practicing and learning about relationships between ideas, refining their thinking skills within and across disciplines, and developing habits of mind that enable them to become independent learners.

Many times I hear teachers worrying about how they will be able to teach the complex higher-order thinking, reading, and writing skills the Common Core State Standards require and also manage to teach content. Likewise, with the New Generation Science Standards (NGSS) (Achieve Inc. 2013) teachers are concerned about how there will be sufficient time to teach all the science content in addition to engineering and nature of science skills. Teachers express similar concerns about the mathematics Common Core standards; they wonder about the complexity of the approach to problem solving and how there will be time for students to practice their algorithms.

In each subject area scenario that follows, teachers using an effective inquiry approach demonstrate that all along we have been combining process and content since it is the way that inquiry learning naturally unfolds. Lessons and units have complex conceptual relationship understandings, higher-order thinking and decision-making skills, and habits of mind as their learning objectives. These skills are all acquired at the same time during the phases of the inquiry teaching and learning cycle—exploration and clarification—and are then authentically assessed during the application phases. Bill Cummiskey, who teaches middle-school science to inner-city, mostly bilingual students, says that in implementing inquiry teaching, all three objectives evolve out of the same explorations, but need to be addressed separately during the lessons. Bill observes, “In my lesson plans, I sometimes format each of these differently so that I can track which concept, skill, and habit of mind I am emphasizing and which is which within the lesson plan. I will put parts of the lesson that focus on **concepts** in bold, *skills* in italics and underline habits of mind.”
