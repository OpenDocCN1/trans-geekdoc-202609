Many teachers do not attempt inquiry teaching or try to teach students to self-manage because they fear loss of control. Rather, if teachers plan well and give students the necessary support, students will be happy to share positive, group-supportive control with the teacher. It appears to be an enigma; by giving up certain types of control, you gain so much more. It is no longer them against you but rather we're all in it together. Yes, as the adult in the room, you have ultimate responsibility for students’ mental and physical safety and the behavior of individuals supports learning, but teaching is so much more effective when the whole group has created, and is supporting, the strategies to achieve learning.

## Group Building Games

It is not surprising when students have not created a set of behaviors for sharing objects, ideas, and space if no adult has facilitated some process for them to do so. Although students engage in aspects of group process during the content exploration activities, they do not necessarily acquire facility with those processes in that context. Therefore, teachers find it useful to engage students in a set of physical, kinesthetic activities outside of these explorations that foster a group-building ethic. However, engaging students in group-building activities will not lead them anywhere unless each activity is followed by a discussion facilitated by the teacher where students make sense of the activity. The teacher is responsible for directing the discussion of how features of the activity demonstrate particular parts of the process necessary for effective team building.

Although there are some activities that students can do sitting at their desks, a majority of them require students to get up and move around the room and physically interact with other students. These kinesthetic experiences are the most successful for group building (Griss 1998; Jensen 2000). We suggest that you start students with activities that require less contact and then move to more contact as the term progresses. For example, the first days of the class you might engage students in “Lineup” activities which are virtually contact free, then move to “Mirrors,” and follow this later in the term with the “Human Knot” which has a great deal of contact, although it does not require a lot of personal trust. Eventually, when the group has come together somewhat it is possible to try out other trust activities such as “Bridges” or “Finger Lifts.”

Group-building activities are designed so that they can only be accomplished if the group works together as a team; this includes students in the class who have physical or mental disabilities. Of course, some of the activities will need to be modified so that all students can participate—including students who have physical disabilities. Most of the activities can be done with students ranging in age from Pre-kindergarten to high-school age. However, teachers must know their students and judge which activities might be inappropriate for their group at a given time.

Just as students need to build trust and understanding amongst themselves, if you have never engaged in facilitating these activities yourself, you need to trust both your students and your own abilities and do these activities on at least a weekly basis. If it is the beginning of the school year, you might want to do them more often. Virtually everyone who has engaged his or her students in these activities on a regular basis has thought the investment of time more than valuable and brought students through a metamorphosis.

## Facilitating Group Building Games

### Introducing the Activity

Sometimes you need to connect the game to something that you have done before, for example, an incident that occurred in class or an ongoing set of skills that you and the class are working on. At other times, you may just want to start the activity cold. In either case, be sure to give clear, concise instructions about what you expect students to do during the game. Have a number of students paraphrase the instructions back to you to be sure most students understand. Before you begin the activity, plan how you will efficiently break students into appropriate groups.

### Facilitating the Activity

Be certain to walk from group to group, making sure that each group is engaged appropriately. Try to interfere as little as possible. Make quick decisions about those who opt not to participate. Will they assist you in some way? Try to let activities go to fruition, but stop them if necessary.

### Debriefing

This is the most important part of the activity, and it usually takes up the most time. This is the time when learning takes place.

These suggestions will help you facilitate the debriefing discussion. Try to:

- have your questions ready so that you get to the point (or points) you wish to make about the activity;
- allow extended responses from students;
- have them respond to one another’s responses;
- ask them for themes and to connect their ideas to actual things that happened during the activity; and
- have them be as specific and operational as possible in their answers.

Answers such as “we had to cooperate” are not helpful. Ask students what they actually had to do to cooperate or what it looked like when they were cooperating.

Questions such as the following will help move the discussion:

- What did it take to be successful at this game?
- What do you mean when you say your group was a success?
- What did people do that made it harder to succeed?
- What strategies did you use in this game that you did not have to use in the game we did just before this one?
- How were you feeling when this game was going on?

All responses should be accepted and not evaluated other than asking if anyone has another way of looking at it. Having facilitating questions in your pocket, ready to use, is as necessary here as in an exploration where you want students to come to a certain realization. You should at least be able to get to the neighborhood if not the exact address!

### Add New Aspects or Redo the Original Activity

In order to address additional issues or to just practice cooperation or communication skills at a future date, you can add new dimensions (rules) to an activity, or just redo the activity with a new focus in the debriefing section.

## Creating Class-Wide Strategies and Structures for Social Interaction

The whole process of getting student buy-in to the classroom being a mutually supportive community needs to begin on the first day of class. Although many teachers will frontload the use of group-building games to begin to create community in their classroom, this will not be effective unless students are also quickly engaged in classroom meetings. As we read in David Nurenberg’s description of his classroom at the beginning of this chapter, during classroom meetings, students will be engaged in exploring what structures and strategies they would like to see in place in the classroom in order for them to feel respected so that the classroom is a place where they can learn.

### Classroom Meetings

One of the first class meetings should focus on what strategies and structures students would like to see in place when whole-class discussions occur. The teacher first asks students to individually write down at least five things they think make whole-class discussions pleasant and fruitful for them. They can also write down things that they do not like to see occur during these discussions. Next, students are asked to see if they can come to consensus in their small groups about how they would like to see whole-class discussions occur. After a reasonable amount of time, the teacher calls the whole class together so that each group can put forth one idea at a time and see if the rest of the class can come to agreement about each idea. These ideas usually include features such as the following:

- listening when someone else is speaking
- not interrupting someone else when he or she is speaking
- expecting people to look at the person who is speaking
- not making derogatory remarks about what someone has said
- expecting everybody to participate, although not necessarily in every discussion

Usually there are long discussions and negotiations about whether students need to raise their hand in order to speak or whether this action takes away from the flow of discussion. This consideration usually occurs in middle and high school classrooms, although some elementary classrooms have dealt with this issue as well. If hand-raising does not occur, then many times strategies such as “the person who just finished speaking will call on the next speaker” are suggested.

Once the class has come to its best consensus on a list of strategies and structures that it can agree to, the teacher posts the list. In lower-grade classes, many times students all sign this post, so it is clear that they understand their responsibilities. In upper-grade classes, students verbally agree to abide by the list. No matter what the list, within a couple of weeks, students will encounter situations that arise where the ideas on the list do not make for the best student interaction. At this point, either the teacher or students in the class can ask for a class meeting to be called to negotiate modifications. Once there is buy-in by students, and academic activities are engaging, the teacher no longer needs to be the police officer, and students will begin to self-manage one another. This process is the basis for a democratic classroom. It sets up an experiential structure that leads students to become effective participatory citizens in their local, state, and national communities.

Of course all of these devised approaches need to be in compliance with whole-school regulations, unless exceptions are negotiated in advance with the principal. You may already have noticed that I have avoided the use of the word *rules*. It is a word that comes with a world of connotations and baggage, and some students who think something is a good strategy, may defy the idea just because it is a rule. There generally needs to be some bottom line, unacceptable behaviors that have an immediate consequence.
