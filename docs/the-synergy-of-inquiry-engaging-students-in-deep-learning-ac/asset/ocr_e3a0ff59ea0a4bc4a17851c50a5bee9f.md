Inquiry teaching is at the core of effective early childhood, elementary, and middle school practice, and is endemic to the hundreds of *critical small high schools* being created throughout our cities and towns. These schools, with a gestalt of inquiry teaching culture, have been demonstrated to work more effectively in urban areas to close the achievement gap than alternative models (Ancess 2000, 2003; Ancess and Darling-Hammond 2000).

Inquiry teaching creates a culture where students, teachers, and leaders have choices. These new models have begun to lead how effective educational leaders are looking at the future of schools.

# The Culture and Curriculum of an Inquiry-Based School

An inquiry-based school is characterized by a culture and curriculum of creativity, empowerment, and collaboration.

# Creativity

An inquiry teaching approach engenders a school culture where failure is expected and is viewed as simply a step on the way to success. In a school imbued in inquiry, students figure out concepts, negotiate ideas, and practice skills. They are encouraged to use classic ideas and practices that they have uncovered to solve problems. Then, they are expected to be risk-takers and create new, untried approaches and out-of-the-box ideas. They utilize multiple ways to communicate their understandings by:

+   - Writing fictional stories, allegories, plays, poems, movie story lines, and scripts
+   - Writing nonfiction evidence-supported letters to the editor and op-ed pieces for local papers and online blogs
+   - Writing scientific reports and explanations of math solutions and strategies
+   - Producing videos for school and community broadcast and creating lyrics and melodies for songs that they perform and record
+   - Painting, drawing, sculpting, and creating ceramic pieces

They make carefully planned presentations to their town or city’s elected officials. They create physical historical models of their local areas for various time periods over the past centuries and display these at town halls, banks, and other public spaces. They write oral histories combined with careful primary document research that presents multiple points of view and publish these in books, on blogs, and in video format.
