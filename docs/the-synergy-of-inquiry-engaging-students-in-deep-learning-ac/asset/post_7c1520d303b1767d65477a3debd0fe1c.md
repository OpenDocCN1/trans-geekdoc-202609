Whatever the project you choose for your students, design it such that it is group dependent. This helps create a positive peer group that organically becomes dependent upon one another’s input and output. Students talk about it as creating a family who assist each member with issues relating to school and themselves. When students wrote and produced plays, the whole class chose and created the message and dramatic plots. Once the complete plot was agreed upon, the class was broken into groups who were assigned to write the dialogue for each act and scene. Every group was dependent upon every other group to not only complete their scene but to work out the continuous compatibility of dialogue. Once the play was written, students either acted in the play, or designed and built sets, costumes, or lighting elements. Everyone helped with publicity and selling tickets. If one person did not do their part, then the whole project faltered. Students felt they were needed and wanted at school by their peers.

## A Previously Disengaged Student Engages in a Final Public Project: A Slice of School Life

For Steve, even inquiry-based subject classes were not enough. He tried different science, English, history, physical education, and math classes a number of times and each time he walked away—sometimes literally, sometimes just mentally. Now, here he was rehearsing dialogue for a play he had helped write that focused on gene pools and ecosystems. He had to laugh to himself. Last night he tried on the gray suit that his mom had bought him to go to his uncle’s funeral to see if it still fit him. It had been a little tight, but it would work when he walked on stage as the defense attorney defending the young couple that was challenging the theoretical new law requiring amniocentesis for all pregnancies. The class had argued for weeks about the ethics of the law they devised, and ironically, he was in the center of this debate. This was the same Steve, who only seven months before, the police had picked up on suspicion of assaulting another teen and putting him in the hospital. The friends who were with him that night had not considered the ethics of anything. They knew they had each other and thought surely they were right.

All that seemed so far away now. He still saw them regularly when he was in his neighborhood, but only Manny, Raphael, and Nadine were also part of this life at this school. This was not the first public high school that they had attended. Actually, he had not attended the other one all that much, easily getting lost amongst the other 4,000 students. It was not that his teachers were not dedicated. He could not articulate what he needed, but he knew it was not enough, so he cut out, just walked away. The city was big enough to swallow him up and keep him somewhat occupied. Until this past September, he had not really attended school for over six months.

## Thinking Back on the BONGO Program

Steve and his peers are actual students that Terry and I taught at Middle College High School, a New York City public school, where we taught for over 19 years. This *slice of school life* described from a student perspective is a narrative derived from actual interviews with these students done as part of the 10-year research study. Steve’s journey to success is similar to the pedagogical evolution that I underwent as my colleagues and I created structures that were effective in reengaging at-risk, urban students in learning. The BONGO Program in which Steve was enrolled was the result of a long journey where each of the components was uncovered through a mixture of learning from other teachers, studying at university, and mostly by listening to students. The program was three periods of a seven-class day where students studied rigorous subject matter in three state-mandated subjects. Students who completed whole class dependent projects with public presentations—many with social advocacy themes—were enrolled for one semester. However, since the mix of subjects changed each semester, many students re-enrolled during a subsequent year for a semester when the classes they needed were available as they realized their need for the *positive alternative peer group* they had previously experienced.

Students in Steve’s semester were enrolled in a Biology I, physical education, and English III class as part of BONGO. During the term, they read Orwell’s *1984* (1949), Huxley’s *Brave New World* (2004), and More’s *Utopia* (2005). Students also studied a bit about American government structures and engaged in physical education where small bicycling experiences twice a week led to an understanding of bicycle safety and to each student completing a 35-mile bike trip by the end of the term. Simultaneously, students were engaging in *doing* biology by designing and completing daily lab investigations related to genetics, cellular mechanisms, and ecological relationships. Genetic engineering, and its related ethical issues, became a lens through which students studied various biological concepts and connected these to the novels they were reading. Students also employed many of these biological concepts to make sense of the physiological demands on their bodies during bicycling.

For the first two-thirds of the term, small cooperative teams completed many small interdisciplinary projects and daily *analogous interrelationship role plays* that used and demonstrated mastery of science, English, and social studies knowledge and skills. These role plays always involved adolescent characters interacting with other teens, their parents, teachers or other significant adults in their lives. The personal relationships in the role plays were analogous to the larger relationships being studied in an academic perspective in history or literature. Students had an opportunity to simultaneously confront and discuss the adolescent issues that made their lives so unpredictable and themselves so needy while studying important concepts in academics.

During the final six weeks, students began to synthesize some of the larger concepts they had identified as being *vital driving questions* that they themselves were wrestling with during the term and that they found of great significance. As adolescents, they identified what was the most important social conscience issue they encountered as the term progressed so that they could then engage their peers, parents, and others who would see the play they were about to write together as a whole class. Like the little projects all term long, the final project they created was group dependent for success. As a class, they outlined, wrote, revised, and produced a two-act play that synthesized complex ethical, scientific, and legal issues into a compelling drama that spanned four decades in present and hypothetical future history. The first act of the play written by students in 1983 took place in a hypothetical 1983 United States where a constitutional convention had been called to change the constitution so that a law could be passed that allowed the government to override the rights of individuals found in the Bill of Rights. A court scene takes place where a couple refuses to obey the law that would affect their unborn baby. The second act occurs in 2020 where students devised a culture based on the extension of the idea of the government making decisions for individuals. The baby of the original couple, now a young man who has been hidden away in the mountains, emerges into this new culture with the ethics of pre-1983 Americans. This play was toured to surrounding schools and also presented in a local college theater (Jablon and Born 1988).

During the 13 years that Terry and I facilitated the BONGO Program, we were astounded by what our previously disengaged students accomplished. It was beyond my wildest dreams about what teaching and learning could be. We got to watch one class reinvent existentialism over a two-day period. Students created photographs and sculptures that were displayed in college galleries. They won the educational award from the National Hunger Project for a play they wrote and toured about world hunger and the politics involved. Four times, they won first prize for the young adults’ playwriting contest from the Dramatist Guild in New York City. Groups of two or three BONGO students have taught over 50 classes, with adults only as observers, in other public schools before they presented their original plays the next day.

Every day, Terry and I could not wait for the last three periods of the school day when 35–50 students would have boundless energy, enthusiasm, curiosity, and creativity as they wrestled with the complexities of literature from Greek drama to current plays such as “The Zoo Story” (Albee 1958); or of scientific theories from Mendel’s pea plants to Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle. Concurrently, students were delving into every era of history, art, music, and theater, while fine tuning their bodies by running or bicycling greater and greater distances. Every afternoon or evening, they stayed endlessly arguing ideas, socializing, writing and drawing, and building and rehearsing until we “finally threw them out and sent them home.” We would have liked to have spent more afternoons and evenings working with our students, but we did have families and other lives. It was so alluring. As I sit here and write about it I close my eyes, and I am back there and as a teacher it makes me whole.
