In fact, students that struggle most with heterogeneous groups and push to move at a faster pace are almost always those with the greatest social-emotional needs, and who have the most to learn about their own deficits and the importance of collaboration in a team. These students often benefit the most and soon learn that, while they are advanced in certain areas, they have a lot to gain from the other members of their group. In the Mystery Powders Unit, this often manifests in surprising ways. For instance, the most capable scientists in the group typically take charge early on, helping to develop potential tests and hypothesizing on the identity of the different powders. At the end of the unit, however, as we work to summarize our data into investigation reports, which we call scientific papers, students with writing and organizational skills often stand out. Every year, I find that students who struggle most with vocabulary acquisition and complex concepts always bring some prior experience that is invaluable to the project. For example, students who have worked with a parent on fixing machines often know ways to use tools or develop methodological protocols that are well beyond the skill set of their more academic peers.

## Inquiry Planning Process

The natural modification of working in small groups dovetails nicely with the process of planning an inquiry-based curriculum. As new standards, particularly the Next Generation Science Standards (Achieve 2013) have made explicit, there are a myriad of components to incorporate into each lesson—from science thinking skills, to habits of mind, to crosscutting concepts, and core ideas. Consequently, I often feel overwhelmed when beginning the planning process for a year or even a unit. Inquiry-based teaching has lightened the stress level dramatically. I have seen in countless ways how important skills and ideas naturally spiral throughout the various branches of science. This has meant, for me, that I can be okay when only half of my students internalize the concept of precision in measurement and the nature of significant digits, because these ideas will crop up again when we apply them in later units on technology or biology. Each inquiry lesson is so dense with relevant standards and potential learning moments that my job becomes less *what can I get them to learn,* and more *how can I focus their attention on the central ideas* while letting some students develop only peripheral understandings that we circle back to in a later unit.

This realization has led me to add new fields to my unit-planning document. I use the backwards-planning template from *Understanding by Design* (Wiggins and McTighe 2005), but have found that the initial box for lesson goals and standards is too general. Instead, I differentiate core standards and ideas from those that I am spiraling from previous units, as well as others that I am introducing for the first time. For example, our eighth-grade science course begins with a six-week long investigation of modern industrial agriculture. We utilize our community garden plot, local partners, and do a book study of Michael Pollan’s *The Omnivore’s Dilemma: A Natural History of Four Meals* (2006) to get students to think of their personal health and nutrition as inextricably linked to global environmental issues and the health of a number of ecosystems. Within this unit, our primary concern is to develop an understanding of ecology and the natural cycles that produce and recycle food energy and organic material. Simultaneously, however, students are reviewing the concepts developed in our mystery powder chemistry unit as we track various types of elements and compounds through a food web. Also, students are being introduced to new topics such as evolution (for example, the evolution of teosinte into modern feed corn) or human anatomy. This allows me to not only push students to engage with the relevant science at multiple levels, but gives real impetus for students to create their own experiments to continue their learning on topics they were exposed to in our readings or on field trips. This year, students applied their learning from our garden trips to create experiments to explore connections between snails, basil plants, and other model organisms, and human anatomy. This led into a month-long investigation of human body systems and connected directly to our study of heredity, reproduction and evolution in the spring.

## Authenticity in School Work

Finally, as shown directly above, inquiry-style teaching and learning lends an authenticity to school that is very often lacking. When I co-taught with our seventh-grade ELA teacher, we often discussed the need for student work to be meaningful and authentic. Rather than writing book reports, this teacher assigned students to draft book reviews that mirrored reviews from websites and newspapers. He argued that when these students graduate and want to write in the future, they will never have to, or want to, write a book report that summarizes a text. Instead, real people *review* books they read, offering analysis and argument alongside any necessary summary. In my experience, students recognize and appreciate this type of work. They find it valuable and empowering to participate in the types of work the professionals in our disciplines are doing. As I previously mentioned, rather than doing lab reports, we write scientific papers modeled off of the format most professional journals require. I bring in my wife’s lab notebook and reference her publications (she is a microbiologist) to show students that *real scientists and engineers* write these papers about their findings, and so, since we are engaging in the same sort of exploration, we share our findings in a similar fashion.

Beyond authentic work, inquiry fosters authenticity in student questions and interest. Students find their own ways to connect our work to their personal lives and even to relevant topics across the curriculum. At the start of eighth grade, we produce a list of science themes that we have uncovered as nearly universal throughout our investigations over the past year. Without much prompting, students are able to create a list that closely matches the Next Generation Science Standards list of crosscutting concepts.

## Transformation in Teaching and Learning

Lastly, with months of meaningful and rewarding work behind them, students typically understand when time constraints require that some lessons be faster, more condensed versions of the inquiry investigations we would normally use. When teachers plan strategically so that topics covered quickly are fairly straightforward and manageable, students will trust that they are being prepared for future investigations where greater understanding will be uncovered. Even in brief *teach the topic* lessons, mini-inquiry activities can activate students to uncover the core ideas or most important concepts, while hinting at the complexity that awaits in a more in-depth exploration. Overall, I have found that the inquiry-teaching lessons have transformed my experience of teaching and learning even further, making discipline much easier and student learning more successful.

## Mike Farmer’s Reflections

When I first started teaching a little over 10 years ago, I had visions in my mind of Robin Williams in *Dead Poets Society*, “seize the day boys, seize the day!” That was all I needed to do, to inspire, to help students break the mold of traditional “boring” education. With very strong content knowledge, well-prepared class materials, and a large degree of confidence developed during my preceding years in the business sector, I began to address my audience. I was not a lackluster performer. In fact, my evaluators were impressed with my oratory and ability to *edutain*. Despite these endorsements, I did not end up winning, or being nominated for, any awards. Well, not by my real audience, students.

Over the next couple of years, I realized that the social studies classes I was teaching needed activities to engage students. After all, do I like sitting in a chair for 40 minutes listening to somebody? In fact, fresh from my newly acquired Master of Education experience, I decided that group work was the way to go. I continued to dispense knowledge and have students discuss aspects of it in their groups; I even added the occasional role play, drama, webquest, creative assignment, primary-source interpretation, game, poetry reading, and sing-along to pep up the whole experience. Oh yes, I was working very hard and my students were having a good time, that is, until I began to lecture again. Then, after five or ten minutes, you could just see them slump in their chairs.
