
The result of this two-month overarching inquiry project is that students come away with a deep and self-sufficient understanding of how to use a microscope, create a controlled experiment, understand the characteristics of the different kingdoms, and the diversity of life in general, know what conditions are good (and not so good) for certain types of life, and understand how to identify different organisms. An inquiry science project is the necessary tool to bring together all of the facts or book knowledge they are learning and make it real. I do my best throughout the experiment to make many connections to the community and each of their projects, so students realize there are real world implications of what they are doing. Two examples of possible extensions of this project would be to look at water quality in their towns or to compare well water to water piped in from a reservoir.

I think the takeaway message for me is that inquiry science is essential to learning, and if taught correctly, is the most effective way of teaching. Another takeaway message is that the more time a teacher has to create and refine these inquiry projects, the more students can gain from them. Creating meaningful inquiry lessons takes a lot of time and creativity, and it can be hard to come up with something from scratch, so teachers should use their resources, as well as be given time to do so. It is not necessary to reinvent the wheel, but a teacher may have to sand it down a little to fit correctly into his or her own classroom. Teacher collaboration, the Internet, and professional networks such as NSTA are excellent sources for ideas and sharing.

## First-Year Mathematics Teacher’s Reflection

*I asked a first-year mathematics teacher, an exceptional student teacher who had successfully used inquiry teaching in her placement, to speak about her experiences in her first three months in a traditional high school mathematics department. She requested that she and her school not be identified. Despite some disheartening experiences, she is committed to sustaining an inquiry approach with her students. That is her way of doing her students justice.*

As a first-year teacher in a relatively traditional mathematics department, I really struggled to find a way to bring inquiry into the classroom. I was scared to do things too differently and worried that I might be in a district that would not allow me to do the things that seemed to me to be best practice.

My first week, we had school for two days, and then it was the weekend. As I was talking to the other teachers I was told that most do not jump into the curriculum but instead set up the classroom routines. It seemed like a perfect time to bring problem solving and inquiry into my classroom, to get students used to the way we would do inquiry. I gave them some toothpick problems. The toothpicks would be arranged in a particular formation, and given a certain number of restraints, students had to rearrange them into a different formation. My students struggled. Some were really frustrated when I refused to tell them. I had them working in groups right away, which I think shocked some of them. Given that it was the first and second day, I used this as a way to build community and to see which groups worked well and which groups did not. Most of my students are upper classmen and know each other, so they quickly began to work together. It was great for me to see students engaged right away. In surveys, a lot of them had told me they disliked math but here they were problem solving and seeing patterns. The groups that worked together tended to solve the puzzles faster, and others wanted to not know the answer so that they could keep working. It was definitely encouraging.

Once the second week began, I struggled to bring in a lot of inquiry-based learning. I would try, but the pace of the curriculum was fast and I felt like there was no time. However, I also felt like I was not doing my students justice. I started small. We were starting off with quadratics in my Algebra II class. We began with just a conjecture board about what they knew, which really threw me. I was under the impression that they knew a lot about quadratics, and what became clear was that they were really unsure. During the next class, I gave a few students a tennis ball, and asked them to play catch. I think my students thought I was crazy. I asked them what they noticed. My class started talking about the shape of the ball’s path and we used that as a jumping off point. I was able to refer back to this simple demonstration as a way to keep students on the same page.

I have had the most success with incorporating inquiry in my geometry classes. The class is proof based, and with that as the cornerstone, it lends itself to asking the question “why?” all the time. I think at this point, midway through term two, my students are a little sick of hearing me ask the question. Recently, I did an activity with two of my three geometry sections to explore what we need to know in order to ensure two triangles are congruent. Doing inquiry can be a struggle. A lot of my students try to wait for me to give the answer, and some ask constantly “Am I right?” or “Is this how you do it?” I gave all students cut fettuccine with specific lengths and specific angles along with some uncut pieces that they could break or cut to fit what they needed. We walked through discussing and modeling how knowing that one side is congruent is not enough to determine that two triangles are congruent. We did the same with one angle. I then asked them to think about the next step, thinking about our goal being that we want to measure the fewest amount of things and still be able to know the two triangles are congruent. This left a lot of my students waiting for me to tell them what to do next. I asked the question “What is the next fewest amount of things that we could measure?” Luckily, one student said “two sides.”

The rest of the activity sort of floundered. This class was small with about 16 students, and they really just wanted me to tell them what to do. I spent most of the class just walking around asking questions and trying really hard not to answer theirs. By the end of the class, I could tell my students were really not sure of anything at all. They wrote down what I told them, but they could not really say why we were writing anything down. I needed to restructure the activity but I was not giving up.

I tutor after school in the library, usually math, but sometimes I will help out with other subjects. I was working with a student on her physics homework, and she turned to me and said, “I wish she (the teacher) would just tell us what to do like they do in math class. That is why math is easy for me; we just follow steps and use formulas. In physics she does not tell us how to do the problem, we have to figure it out ourselves.” I think this was the one most disheartening remarks I have heard so far. Probably worse than the million “I hate math” proclamations, is the notion that students do not realize that thinking is required in math class.

## Jonathan Katz’s Reflections

*Jonathan has been an incredibly effective inquiry mathematics teacher for decades at a number of public high schools in New York City while working with a great number of diverse students. He also allows us to hear from some math teachers from across the city with whom he works as a math coach.*

What is mathematics and why do we teach it? Many people have thought about this question, and the answers vary based on people’s beliefs and perceptions about mathematics. Is mathematics fascinating, creative, and beautiful as some people profess, or is it a discipline we have to bear and get through as many people have felt? Is it a creative discipline, or is it a rigid set of rules and procedures? Look at this description of mathematics and see how you feel about it. In the excerpt, a professor is talking to a 10 year-old boy about prime numbers.

> That’s right, a desert. No matter how far you go, you don’t find any. Just sand as far as the eye can see. The sun shines down mercilessly, your throat is parched, your eyes glaze over. Then you think you see one, a prime number at last, and you go running toward it—only to find it’s just a mirage, nothing but hot wind. Still, you refuse to give up, staggering on step by step, determined to continue the search...until you see it at last. The oasis of another prime number, a place of rest and cool, clear water (Ogawa 2009, 64).

In my first few years of teaching, I would have been classified as a conventional didactic teacher. I worked hard and had some success, but I always felt that I was fooling everybody. I was good at getting students to do well on standardized tests, but what were they really learning? As the years went on, I incorporated new ideas into my teaching. I knew that I loved mathematics, and I wanted my students to have the same emotion. I tried to bring problem solving, art, and architecture into my classroom, but I did it on a limited basis. I was viewed as a very successful teacher, but I still feared that I was a fake: good at getting students to do well on tests but feeling that student understanding was all superficial. I would not have used the terms *procedural fluency* and *conceptual understanding*, rather I felt I taught students ways to do mathematics, tricks of the trade, without real procedural fluency or deep conceptual understanding.
