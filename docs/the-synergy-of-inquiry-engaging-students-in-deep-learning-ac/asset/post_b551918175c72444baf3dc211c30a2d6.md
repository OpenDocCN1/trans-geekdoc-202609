
## Jigsaw Reading

Each teammate has part of the handout to read. Student 1 must read and teach section A to Students 2 and 3. Student 2 must teach section B, and Student 3 must teach section C. All students with section A plan lessons. Sections B and C do the same; then they return to the original group of one, two, and three and begin to teach.

For more information on Sample Cooperative Learning Structures, refer to the Digital Resource CD.

If you are not familiar with many of these structures and other aspects of facilitating lessons in small groups, I would recommend that you research cooperative learning. I have been using The Cooperative Classroom by Lynda Baloche (1998) with teachers in staff development sessions and my classes. Because Baloche’s work has been so useful to me, it is likely that some of this chapter reflects some of her ideas about creating an effective community of learners in the classroom. She also has much more detailed descriptions of cooperative group structures and details about the kinds of lessons that they are best suited for. Every time you begin to question whether the changes she suggests for your classroom are worth the trouble, she will reference studies that demonstrate their efficacy.

## A Grade So Far

If we follow on the structure of the spiraled inquiry teaching learning cycle where concepts and skills spiral through subsequent lessons, we can see that this structure enables all students to master a competent level of concept understanding or skill acquisition with the help of their peers and the teacher. However, whether this total mastery will occur within a day, or even three days, is not always guaranteed. Therefore, when students hand in their reports, videos, or authentic assessments, if they are not given a grade so far, they will usually not continue to try and master what they have not fully accomplished.

Students should always receive comments and questions back on their assignments that allow them to redo the work and master the material. Of course there is a great deal of conversation among teachers about how to tweak the redo procedures so that it does not encourage students to be lazy on their first attempts. If inquiry teaching is to succeed, whatever an inquiry teacher finally decides as the procedure, teachers need to have a grade so far policy accompanied by some level of response to the original student work. Similar to the practice of asking students to explain theirs, whereas mine was open for anyone to read and comment on (a practice I highly recommend even if you do not do cooperative-learning groups). This provided me with feedback about how these groups were operating and how individuals were, or were not dealing with the problems and successes that ensued.

Since then, I have been teaching full time in the school of education at a university. Johnson and Johnson (1999) and others have written their books, and the term *cooperative learning* is on the lips of most of those involved in education. Robert Slavin (1995) has collated data from over 20 years of research about the effectiveness of cooperative learning for the brightest, slowest, and average learners in classes throughout the country. As I run classes for undergraduate or graduate students or professional staff development workshops for educators that include a section on cooperative learning for inquiry learning, I encounter a set of questions that people do not seem to be able to find satisfactory answers for in the literature. At first I was surprised, but I soon came to realize that it had been my own years in the classroom, and discussing the results with other teachers I respected, that had uncovered most of the responses I give to such cogent questions. These questions include the following:

- If you give one grade, how do you prevent one student from doing all the work on a report while the others coast?
- If you give individual grades, do you defeat the idea of team building?
- How often do you change students in groups?
- How can you keep track of the progress of each student in each group if they are collaborating on their work?
- Is this method really effective in providing students with the skills they need to contribute to a team in the work place?

Some of the answers are straightforward and concise, while others are more dependent upon the underlying objectives of the teacher who is employing this technique and lead to other, more introspective, questions to be answered. For example, the issue of how to effectively and fairly grade students in a cooperative group motivated me to create a system that helps define a reasonably powerful answer: use three or four forms of evaluation.

## Evaluating a Single Group-Prepared Report

At times, students in a group would submit one extensive report of their findings and the inferences they drew from their findings. These reports usually turned out to be five or six handwritten pages at the secondary level, including a detailed procedure they used, along with conclusions that could be drawn from evidence they presented. Likewise, they critiqued the limits of their procedures. Students collaborated on this report over the two or three days it took to do the investigation.
