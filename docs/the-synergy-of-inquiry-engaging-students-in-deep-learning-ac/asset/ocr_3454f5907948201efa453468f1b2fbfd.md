# Transforming the School Through the Synergy of Inquiry

When more than one teacher in a school uses the inquiry approach, the effectiveness of instruction across all teachers’ classrooms increases. When more than one subject is taught using an inquiry teaching approach, cross-disciplinary thinking skills become ubiquitous for both students and faculty. When all subject areas use inquiry-based teaching processes, the positive effects are evident on the development of the following:

+   - Higher order thinking skills required by the CCSS
+   - Habits of mind that students carry throughout the rest of their lives
+   - Problem solving strategies that cross disciplines, leading to greater student creativity
+   - Student self-management that creates a safe and supportive school environment

The added value supplied as more teachers employ the inquiry approach brings a synergistic effect that goes beyond what each individual contributes. As in any synergistic relationship, the interaction or cooperation of two or more people produces a combined effect greater than the sum of its separate effects. A culture is built in the school where teachers see students as competent to solve problems, clarify concepts, and develop deep understanding. Students feel empowered as they acquire strategies to address real world problems using their synthesizing and analyzing skills to effectively apply their deep conceptual understandings.

In this culture, teachers are placed at the center of curriculum planning. They have greater leeway to plan and implement well-constructed interdisciplinary and project-based units. Students immediately apply their newly acquired problem solving skills and conceptual understandings as they confront issues in their own real communities. Parents are more integrally engaged in schools because of real life community service learning applications where they can be assets to learning in ways previously inaccessible and under used. The culture infiltrates every aspect of school life. Ineffective didactic practices that previously dominated school culture, begin to be evaluated when they no longer support the cognitive and social growth of students or faculty.
