Reuben Hersh, in his discussion of mathematical philosophy states, “It is the questions that drive mathematics. Solving problems and making up new ones are the essence of mathematical life” (1997, 18). George Polya (1945) wrote in the preface to the first edition of *How to Solve It*:

> > Thus, a teacher of mathematics has a great opportunity.
> > If he fills his allotted time with drilling his students in routine operations he kills their interest, hampers their intellectual development, and misuses his opportunity. But if he challenges the curiosity of his students by setting them problems proportionate to their knowledge, and helps them to solve their problems with stimulating questions, he may give them a taste for, and some means of, independent thinking.

(Boaler 2008, 26)

Both Polya and Hersh are describing an essential aspect of inquiry. Students are presented an interesting problem, which is challenging yet approachable; it is open ended in that it contains different roads to reach a solution or conclusion; and it will lead to other questions and problems for a student to try to solve. John McCrann, a geometry teacher in New York City agrees with Polya and Hersh. He writes,

> > “Too often, K–12 mathematics is presented as a static set of answers void of the questions that lead humans to develop those answers. Inquiry mathematics reconstitutes the mathematical process, prompting students to ask and understand the questions and the answers in problematic situations involving quantitative and logical reasoning.”

Many students of mathematics, both past and present, have come to perceive mathematics as uninteresting and irrelevant. It lacks coherence because the different content areas of mathematics have been “chopped up into small pieces which focus on the mastery of algorithmic procedures and isolated skills.”

(Schoenfeld 1988, 163)

Can pure mathematics be interesting in itself? Can the beautiful patterns and relationships that make up mathematics engage a student’s mind?
