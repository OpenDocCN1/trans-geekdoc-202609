By that same token, my most frustrating experiences have come when this system breaks down, when colleagues get defensive or suspicious. More than once, I have been told not to engage in certain practices and projects simply because they hedge in on someone else’s territory. A particularly unfortunate example of this came when several colleagues and I had successfully obtained a $15,000 grant to teach an after-school interdisciplinary, inquiry-based class, ran it successfully past the administration and even the union, only to have one influential teacher condemn it because he feared it could draw students away from another course offering. I argued, to no avail, that our programming should be based on student needs, and that if students found that one class served them better than another, then that was the class we should offer. No luck. Fearful of engendering this teacher’s ire, my cooperating colleagues jumped ship, and no one was willing to teach the course any longer. We had to return the grant, students were disappointed, and the course never even got a chance to prove its mettle. Know the politics of your school, and do what you can to navigate them well.

Ironically, teachers in low social-economic, high-needs schools may have more freedom to teach with non-traditional methods than teachers in more well-provided, higher socio-economic environments—when students are failing to graduate or earn high scores on state tests, school administrations are often more willing to allow teachers the freedom to do whatever it takes to produce results. In wealthy suburban schools, where students’ test scores and college admissions will likely remain high regardless of the approach teachers use, it is easier for tradition or politics to dictate what pedagogies are permitted. The kind of data you will need to defend your pedagogy in a school like this will be samples of student work, through the kinds of assessments that reveal higher order thinking is going on, as well as as much feedback from students and parents as you can collect.

I have learned that, ultimately, how much you are able to incorporate inquiry structures into your classroom will depend not only upon you and your students’ comfort level but also the comfort level of your colleagues and community. You will need to develop your ability to manage those relationships, to build social and professional capital, and either to forge relationships and compromise, or, at times, to simply shrug and accept that you will always have critics. Remember that your primary duty is to your students, and let that guide you through your development as an educator.
