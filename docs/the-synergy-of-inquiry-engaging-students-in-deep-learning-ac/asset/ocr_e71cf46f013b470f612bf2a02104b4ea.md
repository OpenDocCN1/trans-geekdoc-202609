At first, the most unusual thing about International High School at LaGuardia Community College was that students had to fail a test of English language proficiency to be eligible for admission. True, it was on a college campus, but by this time, so were several other schools. We thought that if eight 40-minute periods each day was good, then nine 35-minute periods would be even better, and proceeded to shave five minutes off each class so that students could enroll in an additional ninth period class. Everything would change, however, when we implemented “student-for-a-day.”

# Student for a Day

About a decade after our school began, Theodore Sizer and Nancy Sizer (1999) wrote a book entitled, Students are Watching. The implication of that title and the book is that students may not be listening, but they are watching and emulating the behavior of the adults around them. Like so many others, I had first connected to Theodore Sizer (1984) after reading his book, Horace’s Compromise, which pointed to the failures of large traditional high schools; then followed his work with an organization he founded called the Coalition of Essential Schools, which was founded to promote whole school reform; and, eventually, had International join the coalition. One of Theodore and Nancy’s most important insights was that you never knew a school until you had seen it from the viewpoint of a student.

Capitalizing on that insight, I offered every teacher the opportunity to be freed up from their daily classes and responsibilities to spend the day with a student. Their classes would be covered. To stack the deck a bit, we identified the 10 percent of students who were not as successful as we wanted them to be, and asked participating teachers to select a student partner from among these disengaged students. At the very least, this would mean that our disaffected kids got to spend an entire day with a faculty member who got to know them.
