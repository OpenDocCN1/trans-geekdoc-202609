Since then, I have been teaching full time in the school of education at a university. Johnson and Johnson (1999) and others have written their books, and the term *cooperative learning* is on the lips of most of those involved in education. Robert Slavin (1995) has collated data from over 20 years of research about the effectiveness of cooperative learning for the brightest, slowest, and average learners in classes throughout the country. As I run classes for undergraduate or graduate students or professional staff development workshops for educators that include a section on cooperative learning for inquiry learning, I encounter a set of questions that people do not seem to be able to find satisfactory answers for in the literature. At first I was surprised, but I soon came to realize that it had been my own years in the classroom, and discussing the results with other teachers I respected, that had uncovered most of the responses I give to such cogent questions. These questions include the following:

+   - If you give one grade, how do you prevent one student from doing all the work on a report while the others coast?
+   - If you give individual grades, do you defeat the idea of team building?
+   - How often do you change students in groups?
+   - How can you keep track of the progress of each student in each group if they are collaborating on their work?
+   - Is this method really effective in providing students with the skills they need to contribute to a team in the work place?

Some of the answers are straightforward and concise, while others are more dependent upon the underlying objectives of the teacher who is employing this technique and lead to other, more introspective, questions to be answered. For example, the issue of how to effectively and fairly grade students in a cooperative group motivated me to create a system that helps define a reasonably powerful answer: use three or four forms of evaluation.

# Evaluating a Single Group-Prepared Report

At times, students in a group would submit one extensive report of their findings and the inferences they drew from their findings. These reports usually turned out to be five or six handwritten pages at the secondary level, including a detailed procedure they used, along with conclusions that could be drawn from evidence they presented. Likewise, they critiqued the limits of their procedures. Students collaborated on this report over the two or three days it took to do the investigation. Sometimes they divided up the tasks of an exploration, or they all worked on it at the same time if it seemed quick and straightforward.
