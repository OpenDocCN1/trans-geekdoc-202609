
## Figure 5.1 Criteria for a Meaningful Theme

**A meaningful theme should pass the test of these questions:**

- 1. Is the big idea true over space and time?
- 2. Does it broaden students’ understanding of the world or what it means to be human?
- 3. Does it demonstrate patterns that make understanding the natural and human world less complex and more predictable?
- 4. Is the big idea interdisciplinary?
- 5. Does it relate to students’ genuine interests?
- 6. Does it support student inquiry?
- 7. Can it be seen from many points of view?

With elementary school age students, the use of themes is less important than at the middle and secondary levels. Some elementary teachers use very general themes such as *change* or *patterns* as an integrating concept throughout the year for all the subject areas while using topics, such as seasons or the American Revolution as integrating points for their small interdisciplinary units.

However, once students reach early adolescence in middle school and continue to high school, meaningful themes become powerful tools to engage students, to help integrate subject area topics, and to give students insights that will assist them throughout their lives. These themes allow students to recognize some of the underpinnings of the difficulties they are confronting in trying to form relationships with peers and negotiating new power relationships with their parents. Visit http://www.lesley.edu/middle-school/service-learning/examples/ for examples of interdisciplinary units based on meaningful themes at the middle school levels. Since novice teachers created many of these units, not all of the themes are as effective as others. However, teachers who created these units considered the themes to be important for their students’ lives.

Figure 5.2 provides an example of a theme that I (and many other teachers) consider to be effective. Think about how the theme “power over versus power with” reflects the criteria in Figure 5.1.

## Figure 5.2 Example of an Effective Meaningful Theme

### Theme: Power Over versus Power With

This theme emerges in many teachers’ units as effective and meaningful. The theme’s dilemma is something that human society and individuals have wrestled with throughout recorded history. Does one gain the most power through cooperation with those you wish to lead or through force? This theme has wide applicability across the curriculum:

- **History:** This perspective applies no matter what event is being studied. For example, power over Stalin and power with Martin Luther King.

- **Language arts:** The theme enters into the conflicts that arise between characters in novels and poems. *Animal Farm* by George Orwell (1945; 1992) embodies this conflict.

- **Science:** Much of the debate about modern society is whether our current dilemmas stem from humans trying to have power over nature versus understanding how the natural ecosystem operates and trying to operate within the system so that we gain the best outcomes for humans.

- **Mathematics:** Those who understand how to manipulate statistics, graphs, and algebraic relationships can use that understanding to have *power over* or *power with* people who have less understanding. It is so easy to mislead folks by citing the average (mean) of a set of statistics, when a clearer and more meaningful perspective of a particular situation might be understood by citing the mode.

- **The Arts:** The theme can be used to coerce or inform so that others can have the power of their own interpretation. This can be seen in the difference between propaganda posters and Picasso’s painting *Guernica*, which leaves the interpretation of war up to the viewer.

- **Physical education:** Certainly effective physical education team interactions and health issues related to dieting, exercise, preventive medicine, and the allure of drugs is all about understanding the tension between *power over* and *power with*.

In each instance, using the theme *power with versus power over* provides a lens to view the concept that the world does not create simplistic solutions. Rather, it allows students to wrestle with the complex matrix of issues that are relevant and integral to solving problems. They are called upon to use their synthesizing and analytical skills to examine the multiple perspectives available and the consequences of each. The theme not only lends itself to interdisciplinary, inquiry teaching lessons and project-based learning, but it quickly becomes apparent to teenage students that many of their daily interactions with peers, parents, teachers, and employers are focused on power. This analogous interrelationship between what they are studying in school and their daily human interactions for which they are seeking insight so that they can feel empowered, allows their emotional selves to be completely engaged in academic studies. Thus, this theme meets each of the criteria for an effective theme as defined in Figure 5.1.

## Evaluating Themes for Effectiveness

It is time for you to try and evaluate a theme on your own. Use the criteria in Figure 5.1 and see how these themes might work to engage and enrich adolescents. The themes are:

- 1. The right of the individual versus the right of society
- 2. Hitler and nationalism
- 3. School gardens and sustainability
- 4. Doing the right thing when no one is looking

Now that you have had an opportunity to think about and record your thoughts about these four themes, compare your observations with the criteria in Figure 5.3.

## Figure 5.3 Analysis of the Features of Four Possible Themes

### Theme 1: The right of the individual versus the rights of society

Much of teenage daily conflict in structured social situations such as their family and school revolves around “their right to be themselves and do what they want to do” and the responsibilities imposed on them as part of one of these groups. The theme also exists to a degree within peer groups. It certainly is at the core of most social studies issues, interaction of characters in literature, and of the uses of science and technology in society. It is timeless, universal, and lends itself to endless inquiry and interpretations.

### Theme 2: Hitler and nationalism

The larger issue of nationalism, or identification with a group, or inclusive and exclusive organizations and governments are more universal themes. However, tying the theme solely to Hitler makes it less powerful, less interdisciplinary, and less applicable in teenagers’ lives. The social studies teacher and even the language arts teacher may be focusing on life during the 1930s and 1940s in Europe; however, that focus would be a topic within the larger theme of nationalism. Tweaking this topic is necessary to make it best fit teenage interpersonal relationships. The theme of *The Power of Groups: Inclusionary or Exclusionary* would include this topic, making it more universal and especially appealing to students in schools where they are dealing with this situation on a daily basis.

### Theme 3: School gardens and sustainability

As with the previous example, sustainability by itself can be further limited and situated in a human context and it could become a theme eventually meeting all the components of an effective theme. However, connecting it to the school garden limits it too much. Similarly, a school (and community) garden can be a wonderful project in an interdisciplinary thematic service learning unit (TSLU). (This type of unit is described later in this chapter.)

### Theme 4: Doing the right thing when no one is looking

This is one of the universal issues confronting all humankind throughout history and engages adolescent students in ethical dilemmas very quickly. It fits many disciplines and quickly leads to higher order thinking. It can involve a wide range of issues from shoplifting and cheating on exams to political corruption in the local town.
