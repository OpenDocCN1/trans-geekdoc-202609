You probably saw pieces of this application of concepts and skills in lesson examples presented earlier in this chapter. For example, as follow-up to the John Brown’s raid lessons, the teacher created analogous situations by having students assume the roles of 1859 teenagers debating one another and the roles of current day teenagers dealing with the ethics of downloaded music. Likewise, after dealing with syringes, students then went home and applied their understanding of the gas laws to inflating their bicycle tires. During a poetry unit, students might be asked to compare brief poems by Robert Frost with those of E.E. Cummings (or perhaps poems of their own choosing) using the elements of poetry that they had identified as a class, and then make this into a published literary critique for the class’s poetry website.

Likewise, students who have uncovered the concept of pi in a seventh-grade mathematics class by conducting an exploration solely by using a ruler, some string, and a number of different size disks, could then try to apply this understanding. They might be given the task of working for a flashlight manufacturer and be given the challenge of ordering the appropriate amount of metal sheeting to make 12 tubes that have a diameter of two inches and a length of seven inches without having any measurement tools. The assessment is just part of the learning—an integral part of the lesson. For those students who still did not own the concept and can not “abstractly” calculate it, they could use some construction paper to actually build the tubes and again see the ratio relationship between the diameter and the circumference from the physical model. Such activity would be seamless learning and assessment.

# Achieving Success with Inquiry Teaching—The Brain and Learning

Earlier in this chapter, we discussed the essential features of the inquiry process. Now, we compare this process with information from current findings in neurobiology about the processes through which the human brain learns. If this understanding of brain structures and function related to cognition and emotion matches the inquiry-learning format of engaging learners, it can make a compelling argument for educators fully embracing inquiry teaching. It is not just a better way for students to excel in assessments, but it is also a likely way to eliminate the achievement gap and to have students succeed in their careers. Understanding how the brain works is one aspect enabling you to achieve even greater success with inquiry teaching.
