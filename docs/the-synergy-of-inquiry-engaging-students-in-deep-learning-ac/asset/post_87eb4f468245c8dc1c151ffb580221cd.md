Along the phases of your class’s evolution, many times it will feel like the class is really stuck. However, you need to visualize the steps students still need to take, and how those will look and sound when they happen. It is like an Olympic gymnast visualizing his or her perfect routine before performing. As you persevere and assist your students with all of these social skill-building activities and you observe where they are in their evolution as a class, you will see that they have already begun to self-manage. Some of them will understand the process better and be able to do it better than others. Those students can assist you with moving the rest of the class through the steps to enable everyone to participate and become self-managed learners. At this stage, the inquiry process becomes self-sustaining.

It is no longer your singular responsibility to figure out solutions for the situation that the class is presenting you and to be the only one enacting it. Rather, together you and the students have created some common goals. You have supplied students with tools—social skills and group management skills—for successful learning. You know the evolution of groups and the evolution of the needs of individuals in the groups. You can predict a lot—to a degree. At this point, you and your students are both trying to make the process work, and both of you are well-prepared.

Of course all of this is contextualized in classroom routines, routines that you have suggested and that your students have modified to work better for all involved. Routines (predictability) calm the amygdala, the center of emotions that control our brains. They calm students, and they calm you—most of these routines have been used before, and you know that they work.

You have used the same inquiry teaching and learning process for your students to learn their social interaction understandings (concepts) and the skills to implement them to make their groups functional and joyful. Most importantly, the concepts and skills become self-sustaining. These skills become part of the fabric of their thinking—true complex interactive neuronal pathways in their cortex that are Social Interpersonal Habits of Mind that they own for the rest of their lives.

Students start to become truly empowered. They begin to be metacognitive about their learning. They understand how to manage their own behavior and help others in managing theirs. They begin to see how others can manipulate them and how, as a group, they do not need to be just observers when injustice occurs before them. They practice all of these skills constantly as they build the community of their classrooms and their schools. They begin to utilize some of these same skills in their family interactions. Some of them will be able to use them as they transition into adulthood to create positive change in their communities. I believe Paulo Freire (1970, 1994) and the other proponents of critical pedagogy would approve.

## Chapter 5

### Serving Disengaged and Disenfranchised Students

The best thing about practice that engages students who were previously disengaged from school is that it also turns out to be a better way to deepen the engagement of those who already appeared to be doing fine. Students start to become truly empowered. Inquiry practices have significant positive results for students with special needs, English language learners, or students whose emotional, safety, and academic needs are not met in their homes or communities. Wider application of these practices could significantly narrow the achievement gap.

Several of my colleagues who specialize in having teachers work successfully with special needs students responded positively to the chapters of this book. Individually, each of them commented that if the inquiry approach were used in schools, it is likely that many students who are currently identified as having special needs would be served with far fewer additional services.

Likewise, those who specialize in effective practices for English language learners note how this is the context in which academic second language can best be acquired. A growing body of research suggests that English language development and inquiry academic instruction do not need to be distinct forms of instruction. Students do not need to wait until they learn English in order to engage in higher order thinking and complex content (Stoddart et al. 2002; Zwiep and Straits 2013). When using an inquiry teaching approach, a learning environment is provided where collaboration and peer-to-peer talk is a natural way for students to make meaning (Stoddart et al. 2002; Lee et al. 2006).

For those of us in urban and rural areas working with adolescents who are already disengaged from school, cutting classes, and being truant for significant amounts of time, inquiry teaching in the form previously described was not enough by itself to reengage them in academics. However, it is a number of extensions of this inquiry teaching approach that have been effective in returning many of these teens to school settings and graduation. Project-based learning (PBL) is a natural extension of inquiry teaching, a step many experienced inquiry teachers have taken. Subsequently, students developed public presentation of their ideas, which in turn led to projects that assisted their communities directly. In each context, students used skills they were learning in school and during the projects themselves.

In the rest of this chapter, you will hear descriptions of successful practices and programs, and be taken through the process of planning using templates and exemplars from other teachers. Likewise, it will become clear how inquiry teaching addresses the needs of both students with special needs and second language learners. It is an exciting and exhilarating journey, so let us get started.

### The BONGO Program

Terry Born, a teacher who used inquiry teaching in her English classroom, and I were colleagues at Middle College High School, a public high school in New York City designed for students who were previously disengaged from school. Many had dropped out of other high schools or were regularly truant in their junior high schools. The school had much academic and social success with these students. However, both Terry and I noticed a set of students who were not being completely successful in our inquiry-based subject area classes, yet responded especially well when we utilized personalized role plays. In 1977, we asked our principal if we could have back-to-back English and science classes with the same students the last two periods of the day. We would offer two required classes for graduation, students would write and produce a play based on what they studied, and it would be team-taught. Over the next 13 years, this theater workshop class evolved into the BONGO Program that added a third period, with three subjects completely integrated, with final public projects that in addition to plays also included oral history books, community newspapers, and photography literary magazines. As you will hear, the BONGO Program, not an acronym, rather just a name that started as the Bongo Theater Traveling Herd, became one of the most successful programs for those urban adolescents labeled *at-risk* within traditional school settings.
