## Bill Cummiskey's Reflection

I met Paul Jablon during the second semester of my master's program at Lesley University. In fact, that term Paul taught all three of my courses, including the science methods course that focused heavily on inquiry teaching in science classrooms. At the time that I started my master's, I had been teaching for three years at a small, very successful charter school in Massachusetts. As a charter school, we are quite a bit smaller than many typical middle schools, with a total of around 330 students in grades K–8. I have the particular pleasure to loop with my students for grades seven and eight, so that I can coordinate my standards over two years and build upon concepts over multiple units and branches of science. Our classes typically run in the 70–75 minute range (we have an extended school day). Approximately 20–25 students are in each class; occasionally, eighth-grade classes are split into two groups of 10 or 12 students, which is fantastic for inquiry projects, but unusual. The school used small student-to-teacher ratios and high standards of excellence to push students well beyond the low achievements common in the district schools. Few of the teachers at my school, however, were applying project- or inquiry-based models. Even science classes tended to use clear, direct instruction, and guided practice activities throughout grades five to eight. Beyond this, our school is located in a lower income city with a high percentage of English language learners. Finally, as our school is located in an older house, we lack the typical science lab facilities and most experiments require some creative materials use.

As I learned more about inquiry teaching at Lesley, it was clear that this would be my style of teaching. The four elements, among many, that stand out as most important in my classroom are:

- depth of the discovery (exploration) stage in the learning cycle
- value of group structures
- complexity of planning inquiry-style lessons
- authenticity that inquiry-based projects provide

To explore these four themes in greater depth, I will describe one unit, built around a lesson series called “Mystery Powders” that I first encountered at Lesley, with some quick references to other projects when applicable.

## Mystery Powders Unit

The Mystery Powders Unit is a five-to-six-week project as the introduction to our study of chemistry. Students often enter seventh grade with minimal understanding of the nature of matter, and so this unit builds their conceptual understanding upon an extensive hands-on project testing various properties of different substances. The conceit is that each group is a crime scene investigation (CSI) research division tasked with analyzing samples from a crime scene where the suspect has left traces of a white powder. Groups are given samples of five different powders (common items like sugar and corn starch) and directed to develop and perform tests upon each of the powders and then analyze their results so as to differentiate the “mystery powder” (some unknown combination of one or more of the sample powders) using just their test data. Typical tests involve heating powders with votive candles, dissolving them in water, creating reactions with them using an acid (vinegar), or using an indicator (iodine solution).

In my experience, this experiment is the perfect culmination to a challenging year for seventh graders. Entering with little experience in collaborative investigation skills, by March most students have built up the necessary skill set to work independently and in groups, keeping organized materials and detailed notes. By this time, students have also internalized the feelings of success and empowerment that they have enjoyed from earlier inquiry lessons, and they are excited to take on a more advanced, long-term experiment of their own devising. In addition, this unit combines students' pre-existing interest in crime investigation with the concrete exploration of materials that my students require given their relative lack of prior experiences.

### Discovery Stage

The discovery (exploration) stage of this project is often very taxing and time consuming, as students from different backgrounds engage with the project at very different levels. At one point, I had a focused and engaged group of students testing cornstarch with an iodine indicator. When asked to observe the most significant change in the powder once the indicator was applied, students listed observation after observation, well over 30, without ever mentioning the fact that the white powder mixed with brown liquid turned a deep, vibrant purple color! These experiences, while frustrating in the moment, underscore the importance of allowing students the time to experience and manipulate materials before they are ready to develop any meaningful understanding of the underlying scientific processes involved. I have found this to be universally true, from analyzing patterns in geological data (such as mapping earthquakes to locate tectonic plates) to observing microscopic organisms in pond water. Our students come with so many half-formed or misconceived ideas about the world that without ample time to explore and build their own set of experiences, disillusioning them of their misconceptions becomes almost impossible.

### Small Groups

To make this exploration of materials feasible, small groups with clearly delineated and rotating roles are necessary. There is no way I could control, let alone teach, a class of 25 preteens fooling around with chemicals and fire without these structures in place. Beyond classroom management—a valuable achievement in its own right—group roles serve two major functions in my classroom. In essence, the heterogeneous small group is a teacher multiplier, allowing me to check in quickly with multiple groups, while knowing that the strongest students, academically or behaviorally, will focus their groups' investigation in my absence. This allows me to quickly touch base, get a sense of where each group is and how they are progressing, and drop a few guiding questions before moving on. Starters can then run with my suggestions, and I do not need to facilitate every step of the process.

Small groups also naturally allow for modification. This year, in particular, we are struggling with a great disparity between the highest achieving students and those with the greatest struggles. While tracking is a common solution to this problem, that often limits the lower achieving students while depriving the accelerated learners of valuable chances to hone their explanatory skills and develop their ability to back up their claims with strong, supporting evidence that their peers can engage with.
