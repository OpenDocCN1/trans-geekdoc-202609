I see what to me seems like an easy solution, I want to propose or even unilaterally enforce it. I have to fight against my, and their, inclinations, and instead help them talk it out and work to their own resolution that they can own. It is not always perfect, and that in itself is a lesson. As they struggle, they grow from it, and by year’s end, they have grown leaps and bounds in their ability to self-regulate and manage conflicts productively—sometimes to even welcome those conflicts as learning experiences.

Along the way, though, this methodology can be a tough sell, to students and especially to their parents. I have learned to do a lot of prepping with parents in order to let them know the educative and maturational value of what I am planning to do, and to reassure them that, yes, I do intervene in intergroup conflicts unilaterally in extreme situations. I once had a parent actually pull her child from a class of mine out of fears her daughter would find her own grade jeopardized by other students’ poor performance in groups. I have learned, in my high-powered school, where parents and students are extremely grade-conscious, to make sure that every student has plenty of assessments where he or she, and only he or she, is responsible for his or her grade. I try and remind parents and students alike that learning how to manage cooperative work is a vital skill in any modern professional job, and that if students do not learn those skills here, in a safe environment, they will be at a disadvantage later on.

Although it is becoming more and more recognized for the set of best practices that it is, inquiry teaching, and those who seek to engage in it, still faces additional institutional hurdles and pushback. For all its good intentions, the outcomes-based movement in education has encouraged (or reinforced existing) conformity within schools and departments. If you are doing something different from your colleagues, that in and of itself can bring questions or even criticism. All teachers face something of a dance between innovating and adhering to required standards (as mandated by the state, your school, your department chair, or your instructional team). What I do in the classroom sometimes does not look like an English class—the kids are not all reading the same novel at the same time; they are not always writing traditional literary essays. I draw on a great deal of material from other disciplines, from history to psychology to math, and sometimes this raises eyebrows. My best defense here lies in the products my students create. If we still get to “Rome,” does it matter what road we take? Frankly, I think we get a little beyond Rome because of how we operate.
