I soon realized that for them to all be regularly engaged in explorations that would lead to understandings and skills in science, math, and language arts, they needed to be working in small groups of four. The prolific gerbils obliged, and we soon had enough gerbils for seven tanks with two gerbils in each so that each group had their own pair. This was still a tough class to teach, but things that they would previously resist doing in language arts and math, they would now work on, if they first could explore with the gerbils.

The natural progression of my teaching led me to be trained in the workshop model for language arts. By having hundreds of fiction and nonfiction trade books in my classroom at students’ independent reading levels, my students could choose the books they wanted to read. They worked with reading partners to analyze and discuss what they were reading. In writer’s workshop, students chose their topics and learned to critique one another’s work, like great writers do. They shared their work with the class and their partners to get feedback, and then revised and edited their work before they published it for the world to see.

Many years later, I attended some problem solving, inquiry teaching math workshops for three summers. These caught my interest right away because we used the data from swinging pendulums and many other hands-on activities as a way to figure out a solution without the instructor telling us. We also did a lot with geometry and explorations with shapes and figures that led us, as adults, to many mathematical understandings we did not get as students during our own school years.

However, if the school I have been teaching in for the past 10 years had not had the NSF nationally validated math curriculum that was already inquiry based, I could not have devised all the explorations that students can do so they own the math concept or skill even if they forget the algorithm. They know how and when to use their understandings because, “you always know something about the problem you are trying to solve.”

In NSF nationally validated math curriculum, there are inquiry lessons and the materials to go with them. Kids are so excited when they uncover that, “every time I do it this way, it seems to work out. This might be the solution.” In first grade, we want them to memorize number combinations to ten, but then they explore larger numbers on their own, first using materials, trying number combinations.
