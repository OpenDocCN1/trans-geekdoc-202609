group) and *esteem* (respect for competence) are where *teens must be generatively*. We keep asking them to be at the top level, self-actualization, where you can function once the other levels are sufficiently fulfilled, but of course adolescents do not have these levels filled. Nevertheless, schools generally ask them to do things because they will need it in the future, and not to worry about what their friends think. Yet, these are all things that are difficult for teenagers, and virtually impossible for others, to do.

If we can run our classrooms in a way that meets these needs while students are engaged in explorations that uncover concepts and provide practice for their thinking skills, then students will want to be there. They will put more effort into learning than most teachers have had the opportunity to experience.

# Inquiry Teaching in Practice

Thirteen years into my teaching career, I turned to Terry Born, a co-teacher, and pointed at our classroom and said, “Look at *that*. I never imagined it could be this self-sustaining. They are so intellectually and socially engaged. It is beyond anything I could imagine.” Students had engaged in two role plays about an underlying theme in a piece of literature that they were reading, and they were working in small groups to create some two-minute theater pieces that would demonstrate their current insights and understandings of the relationship between the science and social science concepts of *evolution and revolution*. What was so amazing was the complete self-motivation of each group as they first intellectually argued with one another about the intricacies of these complex concepts, and then worked equally hard to dramatically convey their intended meaning. The previous term, a number of these students were not attending their didactic transmission style classes. It boggled my mind.

# Making Inquiry Teaching Happen

So what do you need to do in order to make this happen? Find large themes that matter both in personal relationships and in the academic subjects. “The right of the individual versus the right of society” not only is at the center of teens’ lives, it also appears in most social studies, language arts, and science and engineering lessons. Similarly “power over versus power with” is something teens are facing every day of their lives. This concept shows up in not only the subjects mentioned so far, but also in various aspects of statistics and algebra. How you plan units that are built upon these themes is clearly explained in later chapters in this book.
