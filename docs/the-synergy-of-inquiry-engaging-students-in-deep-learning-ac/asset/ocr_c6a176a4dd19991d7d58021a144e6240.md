4 brings us to the next 10, which is 40. They soon can see the patterns on their own and generalize and realize that 26 plus 4 is 30 and so on. As they work in their groups of two or four they soon see that it is not just, “put down the zero and carry the one.” Once you have the materials and the lessons it is easy if you listen to the kids because they always have the questions.

My last frontier after 15 years of inquiry teaching has been social studies. My own experience with history instruction as a student was not a positive one. It was not until I received some professional development where we began looking at local history as the doorway into student understanding and motivation, that I began to use the same approach that I had been employing for so many years in science. About 7 years ago, I started taking my students outside on nature walks. I teach in a semi-rural area, and it has worked so well for science. It was an easy transition to do the same with local history walks, and eventually using primary source documents. Yes, it even works with first graders who are in the early stages of reading. Once you get the materials and well-planned lessons together, it starts with students’ prior knowledge. Hearing what their questions are is the most exciting part. I am now on my way to building up my repertoire of social studies inquiry lessons. Now I cannot imagine teaching it any other way.
