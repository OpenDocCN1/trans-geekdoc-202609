
In implementing this step, you need to consider how to structure the class and determine what students will do with the information they collect.

- Do I want students to go from solo thinking, then to groups of four negotiating, and finally to whole class reporting and sense making?
- What will students do with data that they collect, and how will this feed back into redesign or concept formation?

## 5. Given the exploration, identify the most applicable thinking skills and habits of mind.

- What thinking skill is best cultivated by students while engaging in this exploration? (You may only know this after you start creating the exploration in greater detail.)
- How can I build in a habit of mind by when and how I position this in the term?
- What concepts and skills are students expected to already have from previous lessons in order to accomplish and make meaning from this exploration? Be sure that they have ownership of these before you do this lesson. Many times teachers find that there are some preliminary lessons with their requisite explorations that uncover these foundational concepts or skills that need to occur first. Pause with creating this lesson and create those preliminary lessons first, if necessary.

## 7. Create a set of assessment questions to use while monitoring groups and when clarifying concepts for the whole class.

- What will the teacher be doing during the manipulation of materials and student designing of the exploration to:
  i. see what students understand at various points using formative assessments?
  ii. understand where the class overall has come, so as to best plan for the next day?

## 8. Create a series of authentic applications for unique situations.

- If students cannot apply the concept or skill to a real life unrelated issue, then they truly do not own it yet.

## 9. Create facilitating questions.

- Use questions to effectively move students in a more fruitful direction during individual or group work.
- If there are concepts in a lesson that students usually misinterpret, have questions ready to help them form a better understanding. For example, undoing naïve conceptions by having students look at the evidence another way.

## Looking into Inquiry Teachers’ Classrooms

As with any new endeavor, it takes time to transition. Although there are great benefits to inquiry teaching, it does take careful and thoughtful planning. There is an upfront investment in detailed planning. You cannot sit down and write a whole year’s worth of inquiry teaching plans. You do what you can with what you have and slowly build up a repertoire to the point that it is all inquiry all the time. This planning can be done piecemeal if necessary. However, you will find that the energy needed to invest in this will come once you have completed a whole unit with your students. You will be so pleased with the progress your students make that you will have the energy and momentum to continue planning. Of course, if you can collaborate with a colleague to create inquiry-teaching plans, you will have twice as many plans in the same amount of time. In addition, your collegial interactions and feedback will create a synergy, and even more momentum.

## Reflecting With Inquiry Teachers

In this chapter, you will meet 10 exceptional teachers with whom I have had the privilege of working. You will hear them describe their experiences with inquiry teaching—the joys, struggles, insights, fears, hopes, and dreams. I met some of these teachers while I taught at Middle College High School.

As part of a program I codirected at this high school, students wrote original plays based on subjects they studied, and at times during the year, toured them to other public schools. After 13 years of working with my colleagues at becoming an effective inquiry-and project-based teacher, I forgot the differences between classes at our school and those at most of the other schools in New York City. However, whenever we toured students’ plays, I would spend days in various classrooms at other schools and was reminded of the difference in level of engagement, understandings, creativity, and energy between those classes and ours. Since then I have taught at three universities where I have had the opportunity to visit countless numbers of classrooms and work with teachers throughout the country. Whenever I enter a classroom where teachers are moving towards inquiry teaching, I detect a certain atmosphere—that level of intellectual and emotional engagement and elation stemming from figuring out and negotiating ideas.
