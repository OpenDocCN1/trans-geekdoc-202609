# Issues of Social Conscience

Cecelia Cullen was the principal of Middle College High School for a good part of the time that Terry and I ran the BONGO Program there. Because of her ability to allow teachers to create programs in her school that met the needs of students, we were able to continually tweak what we were doing and try new components in order to respond to student insights into what worked for them. In an interview with her about what she thought contributed to the success of BONGO that appeared in a documentary about the program (Jablon and Born 1989), she noted, “One of the strongest parts of the program is that it deals with social issues. These appeal to teenagers’ wishes and needs to create a perfect world and to contribute to making that world a reality.”

In the BONGO Program, a majority of the time students wrote and toured original plays that were dramatic manifestations of the *theme* that they wanted the audience to wrestle with. Other times they created and published a community newspaper or a combined photography literary magazine through which they could express their understandings that evolved during the academic aspects of the interdisciplinary program. Adolescents, especially those who are disenfranchised from school, cannot resist becoming enthralled with issues relating to fairness, especially the distinction between equal and equitable.

# Analogous Interrelationship Role Plays

For the past 40 years I have observed adolescents in a wide range of classes. For some of these classes I was the teacher, but since 1990 I have been fortunate enough to have the opportunity to be an outside observer of a cross section of classes in various disciplines, in both urban and suburban locations, and in small non-traditional schools and large fairly traditional schools. In each of these classrooms there were at least three or four students who were completely disengaged from any learning activities no matter how much the inquiry learning and interdisciplinary format was used. If we multiply these students by the number of classes in the school, this number becomes substantial. In many urban schools this number was much higher.
