## Step 2: Identify the Sequence of Topics, Concepts, Skills, and Social Issues for the Year

### What We Were Thinking

Long before we sat down to figure out how Maria was going to teach this concept without telling students any of the steps of mitosis or even why they were needed, she had created a spiraling sequence of units for the course and topics within each of the units. This was an iterative process that she modified as the year went along, and she realized better sequences as students engaged in meaning making experiences.

### Sequence of Topics

Maria had decided that most biology textbooks were written backwards, that is, the order of the topics should be reversed and placed in the order that human beings over the centuries learned how living things worked. Humans needed to survive, and to do so, they needed to do two main things: (1) predict how other animals interacted with one another, and (2) figure out how to heal their own bodies if they got hurt or sick. All of this was done at the macro level for centuries, even millennia, before the advent of the hand lens or microscope. Once, humans could not solve a problem without looking inside of organisms—they cut them open to see how they worked. When they could not see how they worked with the naked eye, lenses and microscopes were invented to see how the smaller internal parts worked. Only in the last hundred years or so did we begin to hypothesize what was going on at the molecular level. Only over the last 30 years have we been able to see what is going on at the molecular level.

The progression of topics needs to make sense to students and should not be in a random order. A good order of topics will make transitions fluid and will cut down on the need for constant new engagement, since one topic will raise questions that the next will answer.

Maria created a sequence of topics for the school year. Figure 2.2 shows this sequence starting with ecological issues, then some health issues, which led to a need to understand growth and repair, and homeostasis. This led students to look at the cellular level for answers. As they moved into asexual and sexual reproduction and a deeper understanding of health and nutrition, they began to need understandings that could only be answered at the molecular level.

### What We Were Thinking

Although the sequence that Maria and her colleagues decided upon was logical, I pointed out to them that these topics needed to be embedded in the science, technology, and societal issues that mattered to students. Such positioning was essential if they expected to deeply engage their students in uncovering and inventing concepts. Consequently, Maria created units that not only followed the sequence shown in Figure 2.2, but that were also built around topics that dealt with societal issues that affected students’ daily lives.

### Societal Issues of Consequence to Students

Maria identified several topics of interest to students; three of those unit topics are as follows:

- 1. Garbage and recycling was the focus of the first unit.
- 2. The second unit was built around a local issue about a river. The town was trying to decide whether or not to tear down a long-standing dam on a river in order to allow migration of fish back up the river. The other side of the issue was whether or not this would allow sea lamprey into the “town beach” part of the river and whether it would cause extensive erosion.
- 3. The third unit was a nutrition and exercise unit that focused on the school cafeteria and the activities done in the physical education classes.

The rest of the units also focused on issues that mattered to students and additionally exemplified the concepts in the preplanned sequence of topics.

### What We Were Thinking

Each unit was about a different relevant topic, but each unit embodied one or more of the big ideas. These big ideas overlapped over multiple units. The unit that focused on the cafeteria and nutrition had its basis in growth and repair, homeostasis, and the transfer of energy. The unit investigating solid waste and garbage in the students’ school and homes also focused on the big ideas of transfer of energy and growth and repair, but also began to include issues of adaptation. These big ideas and synthesizing structures kept being revisited using various societal issues and the explorations inherent to them. The units were designed to address all national, state, and district standards by the end of the year.

## Step 3: Identify the Main Concepts to Be Learned in the Lesson

### What We Were Thinking

Maria had already done all of the yearlong and unit planning when we began planning the mitosis lesson. This lesson logically fit into the unit on nutrition and exercise mentioned earlier as here is where students begin to need both microscopic, and even molecular understandings of genetics to answer their questions about how growth and repair occurs. At this point in the unit, students realize that they need to understand how organisms grow at the cellular level. Similarly, they realize this study would help them understand how their skin heals from a cut.

Understanding what Maria did with students before this lesson will allow one to see how students can build on the prior knowledge they had created in previous inquiry learning cycles earlier in the term.

Students’ engagement with topics of social importance provided them with opportunities to create questions and begin to form concepts to prepare them for the study of mitosis. A summary of these activities follows:

- 1. **Garbage Unit:** During the garbage unit, students grew plants by germinating kidney bean seeds in plastic baggies filled with a moist paper towel. They designed all sorts of experiments to test their self-created questions about the need for light, lights of different colors, particular temperatures, certain gases, and the effects of each of these on root length, leaf size and color, and many other factors.

- 2. **River Unit:** During the river unit, students looked at river and pond water first with a hand lens and then with a microscope. They observed both single-celled and multicellular animals and plants. Many of the animals were darting around the water, so it was difficult to see them clearly. When students looked at the plant cells of algae in the water, they saw some of the structures in the cells but did not go beyond identifying the nucleus and the green chloroplasts. They also viewed their own cheek cells stained with a nuclear stain.

- 3. **Heredity Unit:** During the whole term, students had watched gerbils reproduce in tanks in their classroom. The offspring of a male with a white female was a mixed litter. Some were brown, some white but others were black or golden. This was true with other breeding pairs where the offspring were not always the same as their parents. The class had an ongoing discussion of how this might be happening and were seeing if they could predict with accuracy what the fur color of the next litter would be given the history of the fur color of the grandparents and great-grandparents. Students had also extracted DNA from a banana, and used the model to uncover what it was and how it worked. So the concept of heredity was purposefully unclear at this point, but it was one of great interest to them.
