
## Transforming the School Through the Synergy of Inquiry

When more than one teacher in a school uses the inquiry approach, the effectiveness of instruction across all teachers’ classrooms increases. When more than one subject is taught using an inquiry teaching approach, cross-disciplinary thinking skills become ubiquitous for both students and faculty. When all subject areas use inquiry-based teaching processes, the positive effects are evident on the development of the following:

- Higher order thinking skills required by the CCSS
- Habits of mind that students carry throughout the rest of their lives
- Problem solving strategies that cross disciplines, leading to greater student creativity
- Student self-management that creates a safe and supportive school environment

The added value supplied as more teachers employ the inquiry approach brings a synergistic effect that goes beyond what each individual contributes. As in any synergistic relationship, the interaction or cooperation of two or more people produces a combined effect greater than the sum of its separate effects. A culture is built in the school where teachers see students as competent to solve problems, clarify concepts, and develop deep understanding. Students feel empowered as they acquire strategies to address real world problems using their synthesizing and analyzing skills to effectively apply their deep conceptual understandings.

In this culture, teachers are placed at the center of curriculum planning. They have greater leeway to plan and implement well-constructed interdisciplinary and project-based units. Students immediately apply their newly acquired problem solving skills and conceptual understandings as they confront issues in their own real communities. Parents are more integrally engaged in schools because of real life community service learning applications where they can be assets to learning in ways previously inaccessible and underused. The culture infiltrates every aspect of school life. Ineffective didactic practices that previously dominated school culture, begin to be evaluated when they no longer support the cognitive and social growth of students or faculty.

Inquiry teaching is at the core of effective early childhood, elementary, and middle school practice, and is endemic to the hundreds of *critical small high schools* being created throughout our cities and towns. These schools, with a gestalt of inquiry teaching culture, have been demonstrated to work more effectively in urban areas to close the achievement gap than alternative models (Ancess 2000, 2003; Ancess and Darling-Hammond 2000).

Inquiry teaching creates a culture where students, teachers, and leaders have choices. These new models have begun to lead how effective educational leaders are looking at the future of schools.

## The Culture and Curriculum of an Inquiry-Based School

An inquiry-based school is characterized by a culture and curriculum of creativity, empowerment, and collaboration.

### Creativity

An inquiry teaching approach engenders a school culture where failure is expected and is viewed as simply a step on the way to success. In a school imbued in inquiry, students figure out concepts, negotiate ideas, and practice skills. They are encouraged to use classic ideas and practices that they have uncovered to solve problems. Then, they are expected to be risk-takers and create new, untried approaches and out-of-the-box ideas. They utilize multiple ways to communicate their understandings by:

- Writing fictional stories, allegories, plays, poems, movie story lines, and scripts
- Writing nonfiction evidence-supported letters to the editor and op-ed pieces for local papers and online blogs
- Writing scientific reports and explanations of math solutions and strategies
- Producing videos for school and community broadcast and creating lyrics and melodies for songs that they perform and record
- Painting, drawing, sculpting, and creating ceramic pieces

They make carefully planned presentations to their town or city’s elected officials. They create physical historical models of their local areas for various time periods over the past centuries and display these at town halls, banks, and other public spaces. They write oral histories combined with careful primary document research that presents multiple points of view and publish these in books, on blogs, and in video format. Every one of these actions is organic to the steps of exploration, clarification, and real-world application of the inquiry teaching learning cycle. Every one of these actions empowers the individual students, teachers, school leaders, parents and community members in their roles in maintaining effective, dynamic, self-correcting, active, productive, creative places of learning. Effective inquiry engenders creativity and diversity of perspectives, and empowerment and collaboration are the norm.

### Voices of Experience

Rather than theoretically trying to explain this complex matrix of interactive factors that leads to the vitality, endurance, and academic and social success of certain school cultures where both students and teachers succeed and thrive, I have asked the principals of two such schools to describe their experiences where these cultures were collaboratively created and continue to prosper.

Although one school is a high school in a large urban city and the other is an elementary school in the rural end of a small town, the cultures created are remarkably similar. We all have much to learn from each of these journeys into inquiry teaching and the journeys of teachers and students as they collaboratively built their constantly adaptive and supportive school communities.

We first hear from Eric Nadelstern, the founding principal of the original International High School in New York City, a public school that has demonstrated remarkable success with English language learners that Jackie Ancess and Linda Darling-Hammond (1994) have documented. The school continues with this success almost 30 years later. This is the school where Jonathan Katz, one of the math teachers you heard from in Chapter 2, honed his teaching skills. It is also a high school that created its own interdisciplinary teaching teams and units, when almost no one was doing this at the secondary level. I think you will be astounded by how inquiry learning permeated and shaped every aspect of practice at the school. I had the pleasure of watching Eric and his teachers evolve over the first four years of the school’s genesis.

Then, we hear from Margaret (Margie) Riddle, a principal at Robert K. Finn Ryan Road Elementary School in Florence, Massachusetts that has a full economic spectrum of students. When Margie became principal, the school did not rank well on standardized test scores and many parents considered sending their students to other elementary schools in the district under a school choice provision. She describes the evolution of the school community as teachers moved to inquiry teaching in various subject areas and as they, along with the students, became empowered through inquiry. It is now a school where teachers want to teach, parents want their students to attend, and where students succeed. This is the school where Andrea Egitto, a teacher whom you heard from in chapters 2 and 3, now teaches. Even though Margie retired in 2013, the school culture still continues since teachers and students created it and continue to own it.

Go on a journey with these exceptional educators, leaders, and students as they create their effective learning communities. They can help you understand how the synergy of inquiry transforms schools in a way that I cannot even pretend to do.

### Inquiry Learning at the International High School at LaGuardia Community College

### Principal Eric Nadelstern’s Reflections

I opened the International High School at LaGuardia Community College in September 1985 with a staff of 14, half of whom were teachers, and 59 recently arrived immigrant students. Within four years, 435 students were enrolled and taught by a faculty of 30 teachers. In 2014, there are 16 International High Schools in New York City and two in California, all public schools, serving thousands of English Language Learners (ELLs). The inquiry learning methodologies that my teachers and I developed together are still in practice in these schools, and considered the leading edge of ELL instruction nearly 30 years after they were first implemented.

The following mission and statement of educational philosophy is from the school’s website (http://ihsnyc.org/about/mission/):

> The mission of The International High School is to enable each of our students to develop the linguistic, cognitive, and cultural skills necessary for success in high school, college, and beyond. We are committed to the following educational principles.

1. Limited English proficient students require the ability to understand, speak, read and write English with near-native fluency to realize their full potential within an English-speaking society.

2. In an increasingly interdependent world, fluency in a language other than English must be viewed as a resource for the student, the school, and the society.

3. Language skills are most effectively learned in context and emerge most naturally in purposeful, language-rich interdisciplinary study.
