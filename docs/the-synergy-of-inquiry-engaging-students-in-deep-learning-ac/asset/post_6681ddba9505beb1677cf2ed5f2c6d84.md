
## What We Were Thinking

This listing of concept objectives was a solid beginning; however, this process was not quite over even though we thought it was. Because this planning process is iterative, you soon discover that as you create the concrete exploration in which students will engage, you will uncover additional concepts that are embedded in the topic—concepts that you did not identify at first.

We also realized that we could not write the thinking skill objectives until we created the exploration from which students were going to deduce their ideas. Typically, since most explorations have multiple thinking skills involved, teachers select the one that is used the most, is the most obvious, or has not been practiced a lot during the term. The same holds true for the habit of mind objective. Maria had to figure out how to identify these objectives.

She also quickly realized that growth and repair was the big idea that connected the three concept objectives. She would have to remember to focus on it if students did not figure out the big idea on their own. By this time in the term, it was becoming a habit for her students to figure out “what we are studying is just an example of the big idea.”

## Step 4: Identify the Initial Idea for Unaided Student Exploration

Students, without assistance from Maria, had to uncover the concept that when cells divide, they duplicate the genetic material from the primary cell. Furthermore, they had to realize that this happens in a sequence of steps. They had to arrive at this understanding by observing something.

## What We Were Thinking

We found ourselves wondering how students could uncover the concept without the teacher providing specific guidance. Maria suggested that we have them look at onion root tips. These are prepared slides that students can easily view on their school microscopes and see the cells undergoing the various stages of cell division since growth rapidly occurs in the tips of roots. It was also a good idea since they had germinated bean plants in plastic bags earlier in the term and had actually observed the roots and the rest of the plant growing for a number of weeks in the clear bag.

If we could get students to somehow look at the various stages of cell division, they might be able to figure out what was occurring. We then backtracked and realized that we needed to get students to focus on what was happening in the root tips, so we conducted a role play ourselves where I played the teacher and Maria played students. (I usually do this in my own head when I am planning, so this was even better to do it with someone else.)

## Role-Play Conversation

**Teacher:** I want you to remember when we were germinating the kidney bean seeds in the plastic bags before we planted some of them in the soil. When you were observing the roots, what did you see happening?

**Student 1:** They were growing.

**Student 2:** And they were white and squiggly.

**Teacher:** How did you know they were growing?

**Student 1:** I measured mine with a ruler, and they were getting longer.

**Student 2:** I measured the width of mine, and they got a little fatter, too.

**Teacher:** So where on the root was it growing?

**Student 1:** At the tip, because it was getting longer.

**Student 2:** But couldn’t it also be growing all through it and still get longer?

**Student 3:** If it was getting fatter everywhere, it must be growing all along it, no?

**Teacher:** (Writing some of these ideas down on chart paper) So we have some ideas and some questions.

Now I want you to all think a little on your own for a bit. You should write something down, maybe even draw a picture. If you could see inside the tip of the root, look with a microscope, and watch it for two days, what do you think you would see happening?

Following our conversation, Maria and I wrote down what we thought the student answers would be. Maria’s answers were similar to some of mine about needing more cells as the plant grew. We thought some students might say each cell “cloned” to be another exact same cell. We wondered about their thoughts on the roots moving in a direction, and if they thought the cells moved. Then I realized that we needed to stop using our previous knowledge. We realized that another explanation for roots getting bigger was that cells might just get bigger. This makes total sense and is a good model for growth at the cellular level. Therefore, we needed students to see if there was evidence for cells just getting bigger or cells dividing into two cells. We then added a new concept to our list of objectives:

- Cells need to replicate, rather than simply getting bigger, when growth occurs in organisms.

## The Idea for Student Exploration

Maria suddenly realized what needed to be done: the process crystalized for her. She became excited as she saw in her mind how students could figure it all out for themselves. When she was in school, students looked at onion roots, but the teacher had told them everything before with all the scientific names. Maria and her classmates just memorized everything. Now, she saw that students could do a real exploration first and figure out the ideas themselves, so she just wrote her plan down step by step.

It was so wonderful to watch her face as she wrote down her plan, just as I would see it unfold in her classroom two and three days later. She really did not tell students any of the concepts. Students figured them all out by themselves. She just cruised the classroom and facilitated with questions when they were in small groups, and helped clarify concepts when they were working as the whole class. It was masterful. Here is how she did it.

If you carefully follow Maria’s thinking in planning the lesson, you can gain the insights to create an inquiry teaching plan for any subject area. Travel with her on this journey and see how she taught a science topic that is usually uninteresting and not well understood by most students. By the end of the lesson, virtually every student in her class had engendered fairly deep understanding because they created the idea themselves.

Each student now had a copy of a drawing of the five or six stages. Maria asked each student to complete two things for homework:

- 1. Place the drawings of stages in a sequence that they think the cells go through in order to duplicate.
- 2. Explain what they think is happening with each structural change and why they think it occurs.

## Student Observations of Stages of Mitosis

When students returned the next day, they discussed their thoughts in small research teams of four for about 10 minutes. When they reported their ideas back to the class and used their drawings to defend their thinking, it only took about another 10 minutes of negotiations for the larger scientific community—the class—to come to consensus. They agreed that the steps were as follows:

- 1. The center part (the nucleus) transformed from a solid mass into a swirl of strands.
- 2. These strands then lined up in pairs across the center and two sets became apparent.
- 3. Then the sides of the cell pushed in next to the strands and the strands were pulled to each side of the cell and the walls kept pushing in until there were two new cells.

Students figured that each new cell now had the same “nuclear stuff” as the original cell. Some kept insisting that it cloned itself (this actually was a rather conceptually accurate description).

Since the class concluded at this point, Maria assigned students two tasks for homework:

- 1. Read the section in their text on mitosis, and compare their findings and understandings with those of the larger worldwide scientific community. What was the same between their findings and what the scientific community now accepted? They also needed to note what was different and why the rest of the world knew something they did not, if that was the case.
- 2. Write a report of their findings using the format that all scientists in the world use to have their evidence accepted as valid. Although somewhat similar to a traditional lab report, this report was unique: students designed some of the procedure, they evolved all of the theories, and in the conclusions, they compared their findings to what the larger worldwide community knows.

In their published reports, students demonstrated some deep understandings across the class population. They now had the universal names for each of the stages (prophase, anaphase, and so on) of their drawings that they got from their readings and the Internet animated simulations. More importantly, most students also appeared to own the concept objectives.
