## Determining Who Contributed to a Group Written Report

With this single method of evaluation, I was not at all sure that each student had worked on the report and had acquired the concepts and skills expected. Many times, some hard-working student would do most of the report to be sure that she or he would receive a good grade. That student had almost always tried to divide up the work but found one or more students in the group did not come through with a level of understanding anywhere near theirs. When they groused about this enough at home, parents would come visit me at school about this unfairness.

In an attempt to get the more aware students on a given topic to begin enlisting the less interested or knowledgeable students during the exploration, I implemented the following. I informed students that there would be a group written report, and they would not know whether I would collect the report or give a quiz on the report to one student in the group the day it was handed in. Occasionally, I would give a two-question quiz based on the report that I held in my hand. For this quiz, I could call on any one person from the group whose answers would represent the grade for the group. This approach encouraged the team members who had more understanding and motivation to be sure they tutored those less enthusiastic. Since they soon discovered that tutoring after the fact was more difficult than getting the student to participate in the actual doing of the exploration, they began to devise ways of motivating these less enthusiastic members. Many other times, I would collect reports or other final projects written by each student in the group. However, as they were working on the exploration, they would not know whether the final “published” document would be a group or individual document. The reports or final projects would have some parts in common, but since each student needed to do the final synthesis, analysis, or creative expression, there would always be great differentiation.

Students and parents generally saw this as fair. Much of the time they were being evaluated on their understanding. Since most parents needed to work on a team at their jobs, they understood when I explained to them that I was giving their sons and daughters group leadership skills that would serve them well throughout the rest of their lives. Even though there were singular team reports, the other parts of the repertoire, used in slightly greater proportion, orchestrated a balance between fairness to individuals and team building.

## Determining Group Membership

The questions of who to assign to groups and when to change groups require a more complex and multi-dimensional answer. I usually began the term by arbitrarily assigning students to teams, literally having them count off, so they would end up in random groups. Since I had no basis to make any judgments on, I thought this a good method of getting students who did not regularly interact to get to know one another. I found it best to let these groups run for about a month to six weeks before rearranging the groupings. This timing allowed for students to not only encounter difficulties in getting certain group members to accept particular duties, but it also gave them time to attempt to work out these difficulties utilizing the conflict resolution and group-building skills that they had acquired. Through journal writing and seeing other students’ responses as well as through regular social student interaction, various groups shared strategies that they employed.

The next regrouping depended on my objectives. Sometimes I wanted stronger students paired with weaker students in order to give assistance to those who needed it and to raise the esteem levels and comprehension levels of the stronger students that come with having to explain concepts. Other times, I wanted to place students who were not contributing their part in their original groups with students who I knew were socially attracted to as a motivation for them to be proficient. Later in the term I sometimes changed the groups so that all the most motivated people were in the same groups and those who were still trying to “coast” were together. It is amazing the effect that having four students who have been dependent on the work of others placed together in the same group for success can have on some constituents of that group. Other times, I divided groups such that there would be four “quiet” students together. Many times, leaders emerged from this mixture. Even later in the term, I let them select their own groups. It was most interesting to note, both from my own observations, and from their journal entries, that by this time in the semester they had learned not to always select their social friends. Rather, they began to use criteria they had developed during the beginning of the term regarding the attributes needed in members of a good team, to select their new teams. During a 15-week term, I would change groups at least three times, sometimes as many as five times.

Lastly, what is most important is the flexibility this structure gives you as the teacher. You are constantly doing formative evaluations of groups and individuals as you circulate the room. You hear team members trying to articulate conceptual frameworks and investigative strategies. You are there to ask the appropriate questions that will guide them to procedures that will lead to greater understanding. You are on the scene to hear of differing data, interpretations, or strategies between groups so that you know when to stop them mid-exploration and have them compare results. The discrepancy in results at this juncture will allow them to see the need to control a variable or evaluate an approach or procedure before continuing.

The picture painted is of a constructivist, student-centered inquiry classroom with the teacher assuming the role of facilitator. A deep and clear understanding of complex concepts can be built during subsequent discussions since the teacher has been integrally involved in students’ dialogues during their investigations of the preliminary aspects of the phenomena governing these concepts. Thus informed, the teacher has the necessary insight into areas of difficulty.

My own experiences demonstrated a radical improvement in all areas of student behavior, motivation, and achievement. Concept and skill achievement levels of both stronger and weaker students increased, matching what Slavin (1995) had found in his 20-year meta-analysis of cooperative learning data. Likewise, their abilities to cope with teamwork situations rose to high levels as demonstrated by students who enrolled in subsequent courses who became facilitators of their collaborative teams and had them in successful operation within the first few days. These techniques changed my life as a teacher, prompted some of my students to join real research teams, and improved the lives of many of my students.

## Designing Your Classroom’s Physical Environment

In biology, architecture and engineering design, *form best supports function*. Including a little aesthetic beauty does not hurt either. Now that you have conceptualized inquiry teaching, the next step should be to design a learning environment that best supports this mode of learning. In a later chapter we will even challenge the concept of where, in a physical sense, learning best takes place. However, for this chapter, and given that tomorrow most of us will be assigned a classroom in which to teach, let us address the simpler issue of what should be in our classroom and how it should be arranged.

During inquiry-based teaching, students are conducting explorations in their small groups 60–70 percent of the time. The rest of the time, they are in some whole-class discussion about their small group results, watching media, or listening to their teacher tell them about something from the greater academic community that expands their explorations. Therefore, since form follows function, the classroom should be set up first for small group activity, with the ability to transform it quickly into a placement where the whole class can easily see and hear each other during whole class activities.

Another important feature of inquiry teaching is that the students are at the center of the learning endeavor. The room should visually symbolize this. Once there is a “front” of the room with its teacher’s desk, it becomes a visual symbol of the teacher as the focus of learning, the fount of knowledge to be distributed. We all know that in our traditional rows-of-desks classrooms, the kids who wanted to learn and felt comfortable participating sat at the front of the room, and the farther back you sat, the more you could get away from being called on for answers.
