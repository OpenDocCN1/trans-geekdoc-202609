Teachers can build this supportive culture in their own classrooms. Students acquire these social-interactive skills and self-management skills much more quickly, and with deeper understanding, when the whole-school community works on them throughout the students’ tenure at the school. Fortunately, there are schools at all levels and in urban, rural, and suburban areas, and individuals from these schools, who have been incredibly successful creating lessons and structures that have resulted in such a classroom and school culture. These individuals have either put together whole curricula, or pieces of it that the rest of us can use to have our students supporting one another in school. By engaging in these curricular activities, your students can simultaneously acquire insights and skills that are also needed for positive interpersonal relationships in their families, communities, and in the workplace. The student can become an empowered individual in all social situations.

This chapter presents the underlying rationales and formats for how this would be best implemented in one teacher’s classroom, on a middle school team, or in a whole school. Together with inquiry teaching, a supportive classroom environment makes schools incredibly powerful places for learning and really closes the achievement gap.

# David Nurenberg’s Reflection on Classroom Management

I have been teaching English and social studies with inquiry-based methods for over half of my 14-year career. Every year I challenge myself to do it more and more, because every year I see how well students respond and to what heights they can push themselves using these models.

As a teenager, I was the rare student who could learn just fine by taking notes from a teacher who lectured for 50 minutes straight. Even then, what had I learned? I could rattle off the dates and outcomes of battles in World War I, conjugate Spanish verbs, and tell you all about Newton’s laws, but the classes that actually got me *thinking* were those that also involved *doing*—analyzing something as I was building it, designing and conducting experiments, or discussing and debating ideas with fellow students. Here was where I actually had to think critically and create new knowledge—the very skills that the Common Core State Standards are now asking of students (2010). At best, traditional, teacher-centric classroom models risk preparing students only with skills in memorization and regurgitation. Far more often, it just disengages the vast majority of kids who are not intrinsically motivated and do not see the relevance of the classroom to their *daily lives*.
