
## Interdisciplinary Thematic Service Learning Units (ITSLU)

Terry and I did not start at the points illustrated in the foregoing scenarios. Like with most interdisciplinary project-based classes, we began just trying to connect two subjects and having students create a final product. Likewise, most ITSLUs start with teachers connecting two subjects whether as an individual in an elementary classroom or with a colleague in another discipline working with adolescents. Their initial efforts are simple and manageable; as their skills and confidence build, they add more components to make the units more effective for their students. In the beginning, without many of these components as part of the unit, much of the energy to keep the unit progressing needs to come from the teacher(s). However, once a critical mass of the components is added, students become so engaged that an equal or greater amount of the energy emanates from students. This is why teachers who create *interdisciplinary thematic service learning units* (ITSLU) simply cannot revert back to less self-perpetuating ways of working together with their students. Previously disengaged students are less likely to leave school and teachers are less likely to burn out. The well-planned and executed ITSLU results in both students and teachers becoming reinvigorated.

### Components of an Effective ITSLU

The components that contribute to the effectiveness of an ITSLU appear in Figure 5.4. The components are organized in three categories: (1) Curriculum, (2) Structure, and (3) Teacher Support. This checklist will be useful as you begin to create your interdisciplinary thematic service learning units. No ITSLU has all the components, but the more there are, the more effective and self-sustaining the unit becomes.

### A Starting Point and Planning Template for ITSLU

If you want to plan an interdisciplinary unit, find someone to collaborate with you. As an elementary teacher in a self-contained classroom, designing an interdisciplinary unit will be different than what you regularly do. So, find someone to work with—another teacher at your grade level, another teacher with an interest in the inquiry approach, a paraprofessional, or a community member with particular expertise. This colleague will be someone with whom you can bounce ideas around and who can assist you in implementing the unit.

If you are a middle or high school teacher, find a teacher from another discipline who is interested in collaborating with you. Together you can search online for examples of ITSLU units where you will get a feel for how teachers are integrating subjects and creating engaging projects for students. In these examples, you will see how teachers match their state standards and use an inquiry-teaching approach. Notice how some teachers use community members as resources. Each time teachers sit down to plan a new unit, they are inspired by a different element that then becomes the starting point. Although it needs to meet subject matter standards, which aspect of the subject matter you will choose is dependent upon many other factors. Sometimes a teacher team begins with an idea for a theme, but other times it is an idea for a service-learning project. It can be an avocational or vocational skill that one of the teachers possesses that prompts the direction of the unit. Sometimes, it is a local or national event, a student issue that keeps arising in the school, or a novel that is so engaging to students that it becomes the pivotal point for the unit.
