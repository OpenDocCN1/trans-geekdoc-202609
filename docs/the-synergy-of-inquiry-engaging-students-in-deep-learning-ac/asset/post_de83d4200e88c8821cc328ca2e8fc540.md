When the brain learns, it literally changes parts of itself. New nerve cells and networks are built. Knowledge is simply the way that these nerves and nerve networks are connected and how often they are used for taking actions. Learning is physically rewiring a place in the brain and how it is connected to other pieces of the brain. Because of modern imaging techniques, we can see these networks built. We can also see which ones are lit up when we are involved with certain kinds of learning and thinking, and we can use this knowledge to better engage students in learning. ## What does this iterative process in the brain mean for instruction?

- A didactic information-delivery approach to teaching tends to focus on some of the back cortex functions.
- A solely open discovery approach, proposing and testing ideas without teacher intervention, focuses unsuccessfully on the front cortex functions.
- Inquiry teaching, where the teacher sets up the learning environment and explorations and facilitates students in their own figuring out, allows for a balanced integration and interaction between these physical entities in the brain.

If we teach to the test for memorization and other lower-level thinking, much of what students know will exist in the back integrative cortex and will not be usable for real problem solving, and our students will not develop critical thinking skills. They will not own deep conceptual understanding, cannot apply any of their knowledge well, and it will not likely stay in long-term memory (Bransford, Brown, and Cocking 1999).

Our experiences are from the past, but the concepts that we create are for actions we intend to take in the future. They are plans. We need to transform experiences from *outside* the brain to knowing *inside* the brain. However, we must do more than just organize the data in our back temporal cortex. We need to actively make decisions and create ideas and concepts in the prefrontal cortex. We move the power from external others to ourselves. That action transforms the learner from just a receiver to an active producer of knowledge (Howard 2002).

Many people think of the physical brain and learning about it as a cold, analytical look at learning devoid of many of the more human aspects of our species. It is rather the opposite. It is the interaction of the cognition structures with the emotion structures, the amygdala and the basal structures, of the brain that helps us understand reasoning, motivation, and memory without which learning is not possible. It is why as teachers we need to understand the interplay between these fear and pleasure emotional structures, and the cortical cognitive structures, in order to improve our classroom practice.

## Thinking Space

### Questioning Practices

Take a critical look at the questions you have prepared for lesson plans, assignments, and assessments.

- At what level of higher-order thinking are you challenging your students on a daily basis?
- What percentage of the questions you ask fall into each of the categories shown in Figure 1.8?
- What percentage of questions on assignments fall into each of the categories in Figure 1.8?
- What about your questions on assessments and examinations?

If possible, record your class to gain insight into your questioning practices.

- What kind of questions are you using?
- What kind of thinking are you expecting?
- Are you helping your students learn how to frame effective questions?

Questions are the linchpin tool that teachers use to move students through the dimensions of inquiry teaching. Therefore, it is vital that teachers be aware that they are using appropriate questions for the phase of the inquiry cycle that they are planning for and that such questions match the needs of the particular part of the cortex involved. As you read Chapter 2 and begin to plan inquiry units, you may find it useful to refer back to Figure 1.8. In the interim, you might want to use it to evaluate your current practice utilizing questions.

## Correlation of Thinking Skills Across Subject Areas

This book establishes the concept that inquiry teaching is an integrating framework under which fits all the best practices of our profession. When these practices are put together in a process that mimics how our brain operates, the combination is synergistic, being much greater than the sum of the individual practices. However, there is another synergy that occurs when the inquiry teaching approach is used in each of the subject areas, either by the same teacher in a self-contained elementary classroom, or by a set of subject area teachers in a school where specialists teach subjects.
