The picture painted is of a constructivist, student-centered inquiry classroom with the teacher assuming the role of facilitator. A deep and clear understanding of complex concepts can be built during subsequent discussions since the teacher has been integrally involved in students’ dialogues during their investigations of the preliminary aspects of the phenomena governing these concepts. Thus informed, the teacher has the necessary insight into areas of difficulty.

My own experiences demonstrated a radical improvement in all areas of student behavior, motivation, and achievement. Concept and skill achievement levels of both stronger and weaker students increased, matching what Slavin (1995) had found in his 20-year meta-analysis of cooperative learning data. Likewise, their abilities to cope with teamwork situations rose to high levels as demonstrated by students who enrolled in subsequent courses who became facilitators of their collaborative teams and had them in successful operation within the first few days. These techniques changed my life as a teacher, prompted some of my students to join real research teams, and improved the lives of many of my students.

# Designing Your Classroom’s Physical Environment

In biology, architecture and engineering design, *form best supports function*. Including a little aesthetic beauty does not hurt either. Now that you have conceptualized inquiry teaching, the next step should be to design a learning environment that best supports this mode of learning. In a later chapter we will even challenge the concept of where, in a physical sense, learning best takes place. However, for this chapter, and given that tomorrow most of us will be assigned a classroom in which to teach, let us address the simpler issue of what should be in our classroom and how it should be arranged.

During inquiry-based teaching, students are conducting explorations in their small groups 60–70 percent of the time. The rest of the time, they are in some whole-class discussion about their small group results, watching media, or listening to their teacher tell them about something from the greater academic community that expands their explorations. Therefore, since form follows function, the classroom should be set up first for small group activity, with the ability to transform it quickly into a placement where the whole class can easily see and hear each other during whole class activities.

Another important feature of inquiry teaching is that the students are at the center of the learning endeavor. The room should visually symbolize this. Once there is a “front” of the room with its teacher’s desk, it becomes a visual symbol of the teacher as the focus of learning, the fount of knowledge to be distributed. We all know that in our traditional rows-of-desks classrooms, the kids who wanted to learn and felt comfortable participating sat at the front of the room, and the farther back you sat, the more you could get away from being called on for answers.
