Only when I feel we have reached the limitations of their pre-existing knowledge, do I then bring in new terminology or information with any sort of direct instruction, like a lecture.

For example, before reading Studs Terkel's *Working*, I ask my students to reflect in writing or discussion upon jobs that they have held—the challenges, what they were proud of, what they hated, and how it affected their identity. Before beginning our unit on news and journalism, my students role play reporting on a breaking news story, informed only by the scenario description and their own ideas of how various parties (reporters, editors, advertisers) influence a situation. Before examining the courtroom scenes in *To Kill a Mockingbird*, I always poll my kids for what they already know about courtroom procedure from their own lives and exposure to other media, and then, as we read further, have them then test their expectations against what actually happens when Atticus and Mr. Gilmer face off before Judge Taylor in Maycomb.

# Let Students Have as Much Choice and Ownership as Possible

Now that the national standards call for schools to teach students *skills* over particular texts, facts, or products, it is easier than ever to afford students the chance to engage in a way that suits their learning styles and preferences, as well as their background knowledge.

I frequently engage student groups in literature circles, where students select their own texts (either from our class library or of their own choosing entirely). Sometimes the basis for this choice is convergence with a theme we are studying, or sometimes we are practicing a certain skill (e.g., close reading and textual analysis, or learning and applying vocabulary) where nearly any choice of text will suffice. For some students, this is a chance to read about topics they have always been interested in but have never found the time to investigate. For others, it might be the first engagement with the whole idea of reading a book they have chosen, for pleasure. Nearly always, students have opportunities to engage in discussion with their peers. In this way, they reinforce the universality of what we study and enrich all of our knowledge by drawing in more sources than I ever could have found on my own.

This is a kind of differentiated instruction, which has long been best practice for working with heterogeneous groups (as all classes inevitably are).
