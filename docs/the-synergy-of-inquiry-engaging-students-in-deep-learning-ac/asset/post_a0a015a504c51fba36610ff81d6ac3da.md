
- The teacher then reads the piece aloud, narrating his or her meaning-making strategies, as he or she moves from sentence to sentence or paragraph to paragraph.

This procedure allows students to see the thinking process that occurs, and how strategies such as activating prior knowledge, previewing, and predicting, are used in extracting meaning and connecting it to schema that you already have in your brain. If teachers have not done this before, there are extensive examples of this and other modeling strategies in Allan and Miller's *Literacy and Learning in the Content Areas* (2005), including specific examples in each subject area.

In this approach, where we are encouraging students to be thinking about their thinking, we are speaking about *metacognitive awareness*. We are asking them to create schema and strategies for learning and supplying them with tools and the opportunity to practice these in a written language form within the context of doing and learning an academic discipline. We need to be explicit with them about what the literacy strategy is and does (*declarative knowledge*), when and why to use the strategy (*conditional knowledge*), and how to use the strategy (*procedural knowledge*). There is no disconnect between the understanding that evolves from actual academic explorations and the writing and related reading that follows in communicating the synthesis of these understandings. The process is continuous.

If we follow this process, we will have students whose reading comprehension level will be *interpretive* and *applied* readers, rather than *literal* readers (Herber 1978). *Applied* readers can synthesize text information with background knowledge to extend existing schema knowledge. These students will not only do well on standardized tests, but they will truly be academically literate. Focus on being an effective inquiry teacher where students are exploring academic concepts most days. Be sure to respond to their ideas in class, respond extensively to their writings, and expect them to revise their writings and thinking. Be explicit about modeling reading and writing strategies as they apprentice to you as you all engage in doing and learning together.

## Success in School for Second Language Learners

Second language learners' development of social, instructional, and academic language, a complex and long-term process, is the foundation for their success in school. Students' access to instructional tasks requiring complex thinking is enhanced when linguistic complexity and instructional support match their levels of language proficiency. The Inquiry Teaching Learning Cycle, with its effective access to higher levels of thinking and unique applications for formative assessment while allowing for individualization of linguistic complexity, is a most effective route to success for our second language learners.

- 10. When at the application stage of the inquiry learning cycle, teachers can design unique application assessments at the appropriate level of English proficiency for the second language learner.

- 11. If teachers are doing ITSLUs, common language will be used in multiple subject areas and be reinforced with less new academic vocabulary being introduced on a daily basis. This cross-disciplinary approach provides for vocabulary and syntax depth rather than breadth. Furthermore, since ITSLU themes and social dilemmas are universal, the materials will be engaging to students no matter what their culture. However, this will allow the student to express and clarify their culture’s differences in dealing with a certain type of interpersonal or political interaction.

Zwiep and Straits (2013) report the findings from a four-year project that blended inquiry taught academics and English language development involving over 2,000 urban K–5 students. Their findings are consistent with the preceding description of how inquiry teaching supports second language learners. The authors note

> Teachers stated that after participating in the program they were more focused on how they structured their lessons (questioning strategies, concept sequence) and less on the EL label of the student. Many teachers described a new awareness of how limited English proficiency does and does not impede student learning. Teachers grew to understand that their students can have a deep knowledge of the academic subject but be limited in their ability to express that thinking by their language proficiency, and even though EL students need language scaffolds and supports, the rigor of the content should be maintained.

(Zwiep and Straits 2013, 1329)

During the unique application phase, the application can be modified for specific individuals. There is also continuous feedback from the teacher for the work of every student so that students can revise and master. All of the preceding conditions are necessary for everyone’s learning but are vital for students with special needs.

Organizing curriculum and instruction around big ideas and interdisciplinary themes can facilitate the achievement of students with learning disabilities. Big ideas are important concepts or principles that help students to organize, connect, and apply component facts and ideas (Salend 1998).

The research literature provides substantial documentation about the use and benefits of inquiry teaching with students with special needs (Mastropieri and Scruggs 1993; Winzer 1993; Scruggs and Mastropieri 1994; Dalton et al. 1997; Goldman et al. 1998; Miller and Mercer 1998; Therrien et al. 2011; Villanueva et al. 2012). Scruggs et al. (1993) found inquiry-based instruction had a more positive effect on students’ vocabulary, factual recall, and application test questions scores than text-based approaches. They found that inquiry-oriented approaches helped facilitate students’ acquisition of content knowledge. The researchers also noted that when taught through inquiry-oriented approaches, students learned and remembered more and also enjoyed learning more.

This does not mean that inquiry teaching solves all the issues that students with special needs display and that additional strategies do not need to be employed. Rather, evidence demonstrates that the structure of inquiry teaching does supply some of the strategies that counteract certain learning disabilities but also supplies the context in which other additional strategies are easily or best employed. Gore (2010) speaks about employing the appropriate *keys to the locks on the doors* to success for students with special needs. For example, a student with input locks, such as discrimination or sequencing problems, might be given a concept map as an advanced organizer (direct instruction) just before the concept clarification stage. Similarly, a student with information processing or retention locks, such as metacognition or memory problems, would be assisted by the constant overt request for many students’ thinking, including their own, but the teacher would provide much more review than normal. Students in small groups could also assist students who have a difficult time focusing on relevant ideas and ignoring irrelevant ideas as they get ready to synthesize ideas before the “reporter” reports out to the whole class.

Lecture, embedded at the appropriate time, has its place in inquiry teaching. Likewise, direct instruction, explicit feedback, review and repetition are most effective for students with special needs when used appropriately within the context of inquiry teaching.

## Effects of Inquiry Teaching for Disenfranchised Students

As noted throughout this chapter, an inquiry teaching approach will consistently raise the number and diversity of students who are engaged in learning in your school. Extending inquiry teaching to include project-based, interdisciplinary learning that occurs in the community, as well as in the school building, will diversify those succeeding at learning even more, such that many of the achievement gaps begin to disappear.

In Chapter 6, we will have an opportunity to look at the full extent of the *synergy* of inquiry. Up until this point, we have spoken about how the effectiveness multiplies as each discipline and teacher that a student encounters in the school utilizes an inquiry teaching approach. Likewise, when the social context of learning is one where students are empowered by being taught how to support one another’s learning through an inquiry approach, the effectiveness multiplies further. In the next chapter we will hear from an elementary and a high school principal where the *synergistic power of inquiry* allowed the success of their schools to grow and prosper exponentially over many years. Students and teachers were, and are, truly empowered and in control of their own learning and destinies.
