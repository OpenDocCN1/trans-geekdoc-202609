# Collaborations with Students

I also began to notice that, as principal, I had lots of opportunities to encourage a culture of inquiry and collaborative problem-solving among our students. For instance, if the weather was iffy, I would go out in the hallway and invite a passing student to be my “weather consultant,” to tell me whether we should have outside or inside recess. We would grab our coats or an umbrella and head outdoors to make the decision. One time, a deliveryman was coming up the walk, and we asked him what he thought. “Oh, they should definitely go out,” he declared, and we agreed. The idea that anyone could participate in the inquiry was reinforced: These occasions also taught our students that people all around are interested in the problems we approach at school and that we can all contribute ideas to solutions.

When it was time to change the notice on the marquee in front of the school, I would invite several students to help me. We would gather up the box of letters, the padlock key, and the old broomstick to hold open the sign. As we walked out, I would ask students, “What do you think people need to know about our school? What’s coming up that would interest them? How should we tell about it on the marquee?” Some of the best ideas I heard came from students. More importantly, however, small steps like those encouraged students to realize that we solve problems all day, and that their ideas contribute to solutions that they can see and that other people notice and appreciate.

One day during spring, a fifth grader suggested, “You know, Mrs. Riddle, I think we should have a Walk and Bike to School Week.”

“Great idea,” I said. “Let’s do it the first week after vacation, and let’s put it on the marquee right now so we all can look forward to it.” That began a yearly tradition in which we encouraged everyone in the school to walk and bike to school for the last week in April. We always remembered that this was Javier’s idea, even after he had moved on to middle and high school.

The school community’s dedicated commitment to the Responsive Classroom approach fostered collaboration and inquiry in the social and emotional realm also. This approach encourages a school to develop a culture of collaboration. It acknowledges that adherence to the principles of Responsive Classroom will not make problems disappear, but, rather, will offer an effective way to approach them collaboratively.

There were times when inviting a student who was experiencing difficulty to engage in inquiry with me helped solve a complex situation.
