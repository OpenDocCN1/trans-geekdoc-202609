I use *spiral* here in the way first described by Jerome Bruner (1960) where students begin by creating developmentally appropriate concepts by manipulating materials and then, through further explorations, create and apply iconic and symbolic understandings. Implicit in this design is that those students who have not yet fully mastered particular concepts from previous lessons can revisit them in the new explorations.

Since the language used in gothic literature can seem stilted to students in the 21st century, Katie clearly delineates her genre objectives, but also looks at her larger literature and writing objectives.

She then approaches the understandings she wishes students to uncover by having them first analyze something that is in their current lives and is universally intriguing to most adolescents—scary movies.

Each student works alone, and then in a small group, to uncover what makes for a good scary movie. Through their discussion, students figure out the elements that writers, directors, and cinematographers use to create a scary movie. The reporters from each group share their insights with the whole class and Katie facilitates a lively discussion leading to a categorized set of qualities. She gives the class what the larger academic community considers the qualities inherent in gothic literature, which students then compare and contrast for homework.

The next four lessons have students exploring the life of Edgar Allen Poe and how it might have moved him to write this type of story. They then dissect his poem *The Raven*, utilizing the elements of fear and horror that they created during their first lesson and expanding upon each of the concepts. They delve further into other poetic devices such as onomatopoeia and alliteration that can also be used to create tone.

Finally, they are given an authentic assessment in which they are challenged to write a modern day poem using much of the tone and form of gothic literature, and *The Raven* in particular. They need to illustrate it and present a dramatic reading to the class.

# What Katie Was Thinking

This assignment was designed to accommodate a variety of learning styles while fostering the linguistic intelligence of students. The homework assignments leading up to the final product were designed to facilitate the learning of more concrete sequential learners who benefit from guidelines and requirements. From these assignments, students understand the format and expectations for the piece, which appeal to the straightforward and controlled learning styles of many students.
