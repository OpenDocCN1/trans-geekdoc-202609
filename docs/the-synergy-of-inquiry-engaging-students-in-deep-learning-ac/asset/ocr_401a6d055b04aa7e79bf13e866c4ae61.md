He did not distribute the guiding worksheet, (although he did use some of the questions verbally with a few students) and the students were able to figure it out. Now I know that this does not always happen the first time, but imagine how much we have not allowed our students to learn on their own. We have deemed the work too hard so we give them the answers. We do not allow them to become problem solvers and they do not have control over their own learning.

Inquiry and letting students explore their own thoughts allows students to believe that they have the answers and shifts the ownership of learning to them. Students are more apt to remember this information if they have created their own learning, yet classrooms still mostly depend on direct instruction and memorization to deliver content.

Constructivism, differentiation, and high rigor are typical topics for education courses. But what does that look like in a classroom filled with students with a variety of abilities, experiences, and motivations? It is hard to imagine how these teaching ideals transpire reality in a classroom with so many variables. There is a gap in the education of teachers in how to effectively use these strategies to help student gain the knowledge through more effective means. The gap is not what needs to be done but rather how to do it and The Synergy of Inquiry attacks this gap. Paul Jablon has written this book to not only define inquiry, but to also give practical strategies on how to implement this in a classroom the next day. Furthermore, Jablon acknowledges the struggles that educators have with classroom management, the challenges of a classroom with a variety of students, including those that may be unmotivated in the subject, and the struggle of creating systemic change within a school. Jablon uses real teachers’ accounts to help one see that while this strategy may be difficult at first, it can produce great change and great gains in the educational growth of students.
