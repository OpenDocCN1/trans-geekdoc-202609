# Grading in Cooperative Groups

Fairness in grading in cooperative groups can be a contentious issue with two aspects: (1) the effectiveness of cooperative learning groups and (2) the assessment of individuals in those groups. As a participant in an education seminar focusing on upper elementary school through college inquiry teaching, it soon became clear to me that many members had questions about these two matters. Even more concerned about grading in cooperative groups are parents of students in them. How grading can both encourage students to do their best work and at the same time have them see that it is in their own interest to assist others who do not quite understand a concept, becomes the elephant in the room during conversations with parents, students, administrators, and other educators.

As I began to explain various aspects of this complex interaction, the evolution of my own thought and understanding of this teaching framework over the past 35 years began to clearly unfold. It was sometime in the early or mid-1970s when we first began to hear how the Russian schools had structured their classrooms such that students were placed in small groups that would be graded as a team that I began to utilize a similar methodology with my students. In addition to the positive results that the Russians were achieving, it was my own experiences with student peer coaching, running student sports teams, and my own personal work as a member of a science-research team that prompted me to reformat the structure of my classroom.

At this point in my teaching career, I had already realized that students constructed their own knowledge. They had preconceptions about most natural and human phenomena in the universe and my job was to allow them to prove this preconception right or wrong. My job was to facilitate their understanding when they wandered from a logical experimental or aesthetic design. Until I had allowed them to design, and to modify and redesign their own explorations, confronting their accurate or inaccurate preconceptions, they would not be interested in how the larger community had uncovered how nature or human systems operate. They needed to hear these academic stories contextualized within their own questions, discoveries, failures, and ideas. Since I would have students uncovering major concepts while building their problem-solving skills rather than memorizing a body of knowledge, I realized I needed some way of more closely mirroring what happened in real academic-research teams. Similarly, I had coached sports teams and had been in charge of peer tutoring and therefore realized the power of students supporting one another in a common endeavor.

Given this background, rather than tiptoeing around the idea, I jumped right in and spent the next 13 years trying to perfect the system. The system is still not perfect, but it accomplishes many of my goals. During these years, both students and I kept daily journals of how the class was going relative to our lives.
