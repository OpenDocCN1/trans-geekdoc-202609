## Middle School is Not the Name on the Outside of the Building

Middle School is an educational concept. It is a set of practices that make learning comfortable for early adolescents. Many schools across the country have the name *middle school* on the outside of the building and the grade spans are either fifth to eighth or sixth to eighth. However, many of these are not *middle schools* and instead are still junior high schools. Throughout the 1960s and 1970s, a group of early adolescent educators realized that the junior high schools in our nation were the weakest link in our educational systems. These schools, where students were encountering a developmental level imbued with great anxiety, had structures that exacerbated the situation for these students. Therefore, both their cognitive and emotional development suffered.

A number of educators began to seek out and examine junior high schools with unusually high success and began to meet and write about the promising practices that worked for these early adolescents. William Alexander, Donald Eichhorn, and John Lounsbury, to name a few, created this concept of a *middle school* that was designed differently than either elementary or high school and had a unique approach to both the social and cognitive development of these tweens.

I mention the middle school practices at this point in this book, and particularly in this chapter, because many of these promising practices are similar to management practices that are espoused in this chapter. Others are similar to practices found in the next chapter that experienced inquiry teachers begin to use as they expand their repertoire so that inquiry teaching is even more engaging for a broader spectrum of students.

The next time that you read about a middle school that is not being effective, the first question that should cross your mind is, “Is it even a true *middle school*?” Just because there are teachers from different disciplines assigned to a “team,” does not mean it is a middle school. It may well be a *faux team*—one that does not do interdisciplinary thematic units or run advisories for lack of preparation to engage in these practices or any of the other practices that follow.

Look at this list of characteristics of effective middle school practice. As one reads through the list, it is easy to see how inquiry teaching and learning can easily fit at the core of all these structures. In the next chapter, you will read about how many teachers or schools at the elementary or high school level who were using an inquiry teaching approach adopted a number of these middle school practices. True middle school teams who are defined by these practices often find that using an inquiry teaching and learning approach provides a missing piece that brings even more power to their team.

The characteristics of effective middle school practice are shown in Figure 4.5. They are organized in three categories: *Structure, Curriculum, and Teacher Support.*

**Figure 4.5 Characteristics of an Effective Middle School**

### Structure

- Small communities for learning within the larger school building
- Classroom learning communities where individuals and groups of students are self-monitoring in a constructive social environment that supports one another's construction of knowledge
- Exploratory classes during the school week where students work with an adult and other students on topics that the individual student chooses to study in depth
- Families re-engage in the education of young adolescents with active participation on the team
- School transition programs that assist students transitioning into middle school or transitioning into high school

### Curriculum

- Exploring interdisciplinary, integrated, and thematic approaches to curriculum design that include multicultural perspectives and an active, student-centered approach to learning
- Connecting school curriculum to community needs and activities
- Accommodating diversity—learning the skills necessary for working with students with a wide range of special needs and refining techniques for inclusionary practices
- Integrating technology across the curriculum
- Improving academic performance by fostering health and fitness
- Providing a rigorous, core academic program with success experiences for all learners—eliminating tracking, having skilled teachers teach heterogeneous groupings and promoting cooperative learning

### Teacher Support

- Empowering teachers and administrators to make decisions about the experiences of middle-grade students
- Organizing the school into interdisciplinary academic teams of teachers and multi-age students with shared decision making by a close-knit school community
- Staffing with teachers who are expert at teaching young adolescents
- Investigating unique roles for teachers at the middle-school level, such as team planning and teaching, building advisor/advisee programs, and facilitating community-based participation
- Providing teachers with common planning time in order to work almost daily together on making these powerful interdisciplinary thematic units

My colleague, Peter Cook, has been a staff developer for NELMS (the New England League of Middle Schools), a principal of a highly-functioning middle school for many years, and an international ambassador for middle school practices in countries as far as Australia and Macedonia. I think he sums up the spirit of it when he says, “You know you have really become a middle school teacher when you find yourself and a group of parents cooking pasta for 200-300 students and adults for some evening presentation that students on your team have created, are hosting, and carrying out throughout the evening.”

## You Really Need to Believe

When I began teaching, I taught at a school with many disciplinary issues. It was common to encounter disruptive behavior in the hallways and in most classrooms. I learned a very negative set of coping skills where I needed to get control. It was certainly them against me, and from this experience I became afraid to trust my students.
