# Step 3: Identify the Main Concepts to Be Learned in the Lesson

# What We Were Thinking

Maria had already done all of the yearlong and unit planning when we began planning the mitosis lesson. This lesson logically fit into the unit on nutrition and exercise mentioned earlier as here is where students begin to need both microscopic, and even molecular understandings of genetics to answer their questions about how growth and repair occurs. At this point in the unit, students realize that they need to understand how organisms grow at the cellular level. Similarly, they realize this study would help them understand how their skin heals from a cut.

Understanding what Maria did with students before this lesson will allow one to see how students can build on the prior knowledge they had created in previous inquiry learning cycles earlier in the term.

Students’ engagement with topics of social importance provided them with opportunities to create questions and begin to form concepts to prepare them for the study of mitosis. A summary of these activities follows:

+   1. **Garbage Unit:** During the garbage unit, students grew plants by germinating kidney bean seeds in plastic baggies filled with a moist paper towel. They designed all sorts of experiments to test their self-created questions about the need for light, lights of different colors, particular temperatures, certain gases, and the effects of each of these on root length, leaf size and color, and many other factors.
+   
+   2. **River Unit:** During the river unit, students looked at river and pond water first with a hand lens and then with a microscope. They observed both single-celled and multicellular animals and plants. Many of the animals were darting around the water, so it was difficult to see them clearly. When students looked at the plant cells of algae in the water, they saw some of the structures in the cells but did not go beyond identifying the nucleus and the green chloroplasts. They also viewed their own cheek cells stained with a nuclear stain.
+   
+   3. **Heredity Unit:** During the whole term, students had watched gerbils reproduce in tanks in their classroom. The offspring of a male with a white female was a mixed litter. Some were brown, some white but others were black or golden. This was true with other breeding pairs where the offspring were not always the same as their parents. The class had an ongoing discussion of how this might be happening and were seeing if they could predict with accuracy what the fur color of the next litter would be given the history of the fur color of the grandparents and great-grandparents. Students had also extracted DNA from a banana, and used the mouth to uncover what it was and how it worked. So the concept of heredity was purposefully unclear at this point, but it was one of great interest to them.
