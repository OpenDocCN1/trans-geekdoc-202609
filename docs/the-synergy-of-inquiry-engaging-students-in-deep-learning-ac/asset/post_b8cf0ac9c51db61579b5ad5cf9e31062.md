
## What Does the Reporter Do?
The role of reporter supports those initially unsure of their ideas. Every teacher knows those students who do not have a solid belief in their own ideas. When there is a whole class discussion, they rarely, if ever, participate with their thoughts about the topic. When they do say something, it is at such a low volume that virtually no one can hear them. However, when this same student is engaging in an exploration in their small groups during inquiry teaching and learning, they are usually more inclined to say a few of their ideas to the other three students. When the teacher warns the groups that in a few minutes the reporters will need to report their group's findings to the rest of the class—and if this low-belief student is the reporter for the day—certain events may transpire. The other students review the ideas with the reporter, thus increasing her or his belief in the ideas. This person realizes that some of the group's ideas were from him or her and that the group has validated those ideas. This reporter usually speaks the ideas loudly and with confidence. As the term goes on, the student's belief in his or her own ideas increases greatly. The whole mechanism of inquiry teaching and learning supports students’ growth and belief in their thinking.

## What Is the Impact of Group Roles on Student Behavior?
In the elementary classrooms, these roles are often kept constant throughout the year. However, if all teachers in the school are using inquiry teaching, by second or third grade, students will have taken on all of these group skills, and they will only need to look at the chart to see whose turn it is to get materials or report out on a given day. Everybody else naturally does the rest.

For middle and high school students, if nobody has done these roles before, then usually by November or December students have all taken on these responsibilities many times, and they only need to look at the chart to see whose turn it is to get materials or report out on a given day. Students stay in the same groups for about six weeks or so, evolving as an effective group. They sit at the same place each day and this place has a number. The chart with the roles posted in the classroom has the numbers moved each day so that the roles revolve throughout the group.

Utilizing these group roles is just the beginning of teaching students how to be effective working members of a team: constant practice throughout the grades enables them to develop habits of mind that support cooperative learning. **It takes a concerted effort by a teacher, a middle school team, or faculty so that students can self-manage and have a safe and secure environment in which to try out their ideas.** Let us now investigate how effective groups must evolve so that we can see what experiences we must provide our students at each stage of the evolution.

## Building Effective Groups
Over the past 50 years, sociologists have investigated the developmental stages of effective team building in varied settings from engineering or medical teams, to a team of students in an inquiry-teaching classroom. Building on these insights, we can supply our students with the appropriate supports to help them through each stage. I describe three aspects of group evolution:
- 1. Stages in the life cycle of a group
- 2. Capabilities of the group at various stages
- 3. Interpersonal needs in groups

It may be helpful to understand each aspect so you and your colleagues can provide students with the skills they will need as they confront each subsequent stage. Once teachers understand these processes, it becomes easy to assess where each class is and provide them the assistance they need to advance to more effective self-management.

## Stages in the Life Cycle of a Group
The whole class, and to a degree the smaller four-person groups in a classroom, typically move through five stages in the life cycle of a group (Tuckman and Jensen 1977). They are:

Stage 1: Forming—students look to the teacher for assurance, structure, and authority.

Stage 2: Storming—as students become comfortable, they question the authority of the teacher and enter into some interpersonal conflict.

Stage 3: Norming—students look to create greater order and some agreement on norms, and how perspectives should be shared.

Stage 4: Performing—together students effectively accomplish academic tasks, investigate how else they might solve problems, and move toward higher levels of thinking and problem solving.

Stage 5: Adjourning—as their tenure as a group nears an end, students need to transition to being a group of individuals. To do so effectively, they should have the opportunity to reflect on what they have learned from working in this group and what they appreciate about having worked together.

## Capabilities of the Group at Various Stages
As effective groups move through these various stages, they begin to build certain capabilities (Gibb and Gibb 1978). As a teacher, it will be helpful to be aware of these and assist groups in strengthening them. Likewise, congratulating them on building these
skills as their groups mature will strengthen their understanding of their capabilities and allow them to more easily transition to the next stage. These capabilities are:

### Reciprocal Trust
Each member of the group finds some level of personal adequacy and group membership.
- **Feedback:** The group builds an effective communication and decision-making system.
- **Formation of Goals:** The group establishes goals that satisfy the needs of everyone in the group.
- **Interdependence:** The group has matured to a point where it is possible to concern themselves with issues of freedom, control, and interdependence.

## Interpersonal Needs in Groups
Individuals in groups express three basic interpersonal needs (Schutz 1966). Until these are taken care of, academic learning may seem unimportant. In effectively evolving groups, students become more powerful and influential, and they tend to become more curious and effective learners. The three basic needs are:
- 1. **Inclusion:** For students to succeed in the classroom, they must feel they *fit in* and are a part of their classroom. Students need to have a safe haven, where they feel that their opinions and ideas can be shared, without fear of negative consequences.
- 2. **Control:** After a student is socially accepted, the next step is how much control and influence he or she can have in the class. This happens with both fellow students and the teacher.
- 3. **Affection:** Students must show affection toward their fellow classmates for groups to be successful. It is inevitable that conflicts in groups will arise; at these times, sensitivity and flexibility are essential.

As you are looking to assist the whole group with their needs during each life cycle transition, it is helpful to be aware of the individual student's needs as he or she moves through this evolution. - 2. Discussion follows during which each student tells how the object exemplifies her or his value.
- 3. Give examples: a rock represents friendship because of its solidity or the moon is caring due to lighting the Earth at night.
- 4. Encourage free expression. Participate yourself.

## Two Truths and a Lie
Students provide three statements, two that are true and one that is not. It works best if the two truths are unbelievable and the fib is unbelievable. All three are presented in a row. Then each statement is presented and students in the class raise their hands if they think the statement is the lie. After all three statements are voted on, the person says which one is not true. Hold off discussion until after everyone in the class has gone. Somewhere about a quarter of the way through, the teacher should participate.
