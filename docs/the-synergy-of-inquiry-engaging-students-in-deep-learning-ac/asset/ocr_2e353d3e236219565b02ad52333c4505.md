# Procedure:

+   1. Each player is told to find an object that has a special value for her or him.
+   2. Discussion follows during which each student tells how the object exemplifies her or his value.
+   3. Give examples: a rock represents friendship because of its solidity or the moon is caring due to lighting the Earth at night.
+   4. Encourage free expression. Participate yourself.

# Two Truths and a Lie

Students provide three statements, two that are true and one that is not. It works best if the two truths are unbelievable and the fib is unbelievable. All three are presented in a row. Then each statement is presented and students in the class raise their hands if they think the statement is the lie. After all three statements are voted on, the person says which one is not true. Hold off discussion until after everyone in the class has gone. Somewhere about a quarter of the way through, the teacher should participate.

# Jigsaw Problem Solving

Each teammate has part of the answer; teammates must put their information together in order to solve the problem.

When students in a group are at the *forming* stage, for example, and individuals are simultaneously looking for inclusion, it makes sense to do some of the less physically close group-building activities, such as “Lineups” or “Metaphors” so that they still need to accomplish a specific task, but not in a physically close situation. Likewise, students could do “Two Truths and a Lie” or “Jigsaw Problem Solving” during this stage to get to know some things about their classmates outside of school. Initial classes should also start with some preliminary explorations about conflict, building up to conflict resolution skills, so that the conflicts that are likely to arise during the *storming* stage can be resolved in a positive manner. In inquiry teaching of academic concepts and skills where students are in a situation where they need to uncover concepts and practice skills, there is a great deal of teacher control.
