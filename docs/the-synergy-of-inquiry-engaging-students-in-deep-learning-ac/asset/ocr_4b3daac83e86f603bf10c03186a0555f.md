# Questioning Practices

Take a critical look at the questions you have prepared for lesson plans, assignments, and assessments.

+   - At what level of higher order thinking are you challenging your students on a daily basis?
+   - What percentage of the questions you ask fall into each of the categories shown in Figure 1.8?
+   - What percentage of questions on assignments fall into each of the categories in Figure 1.8?
+   - What about your questions on assessments and examinations?

If possible, record your class to gain insight into your questioning practices.

+   - What kind of questions you are using?
+   - What kind of thinking are you expecting?
+   - Are you helping your students learn how to frame effective questions?

Questions are the linchpin tool that teachers use to move students through the dimensions of inquiry teaching. Therefore, it is vital that teachers be aware that they are using appropriate questions for the phase of the inquiry cycle that they are planning for and that such questions match the needs of the particular part of the cortex involved. As you read Chapter 2 and begin to plan inquiry units, you may find it useful to refer back to Figure 1.8. In the interim, you might want to use it to evaluate your current practice utilizing questions.

# Correlation of Thinking Skills Across Subject Areas

This book establishes the concept that inquiry teaching is an integrating framework under which fits all the best practices of our profession. When these practices are put together in a process that mimics how our brain operates, the combination is synergistic, being much greater than the sum of the individual practices. However, there is another synergy that occurs when the inquiry teaching approach is used in each of the subject areas, either by the same teacher in a self-contained elementary classroom, or by a set of subject area teachers in a school where specialists teach subjects.
