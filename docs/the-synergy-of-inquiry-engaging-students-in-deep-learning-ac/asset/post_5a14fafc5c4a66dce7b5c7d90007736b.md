The spirit of inquiry in a school must include all staff members and families, not just teachers and students. One winter day, our custodian mentioned to me that teachers were continually opening classroom windows to modulate the indoor temperature and piling materials on top of the unvented heaters, causing them to malfunction. I asked him to show me exactly how the heating system worked, and he explained so effectively that I next asked him to do a short workshop for teachers since he was the one who understood the system best. I learned that our custodian himself is a very good teacher. Later, I found occasions for him to mentor students who appreciated the specialized knowledge he could share and which also interested them.

When I went outdoors during recess times, I noticed that the paraprofessionals on duty had thoughtful insight into the students’ activities and feelings. When I observed some puzzling interactions on the playground, rather than immediately intervening myself, I learned to ask a staff member on duty, “What have you noticed about those students over there? Is everything going okay with them?” Almost always, they had been watching a situation develop and knew exactly whether or not we needed to intervene. Asking their advice indicated to them that I appreciated their knowledge and expertise and that they did not need me to be the boss. After a few years, staff members on duty regularly identified problems and shared effective ideas with each other and with me, which helped make things run much more smoothly—on the playground and in the cafeteria and also at arrival and dismissal. Although I made sure to be out and about all around the school, I was rarely needed to intervene. An inquiry approach is effective in students’ learning because it encourages students to be thoughtful independent learners, to show initiative, and to be persistent. Clearly the same thing is true of adult staff members in a school.

## Playground Safety Rules

It was the paraprofessionals themselves who identified a serious playground problem in recent years. “Students know the rules,” their spokesperson said, “but they don’t follow them. I think we made them so long ago that they don’t own them. In Responsive Classroom, the students are supposed to make the rules.” This concern led me to form a Recess Safety Rules Convention. Each class (K–5) sent one delegate to the convention with a list of the safety rules that the class had agreed needed to be followed at recess. Out of some 70 plus rules, the delegates were tasked with creating a much shorter list of rules *that we can all remember*. Among the delegates, I partnered an older student with a younger one, and they set to work identifying the most important of the rules. After just two meetings, they were able to agree upon a short set of Recess Safety Rules, which each delegate proudly presented to his or her own class. Rule following on the playground subsequently improved.

Some of the students’ decisions interested me a great deal. For instance, the delegates insisted on ‘No swearing or name calling.’ I asked them, “Can you help me understand how that is a *safety* rule?” They explained that when people use bad language it makes others mad, and they might start to fight. Swearing and bad language had been a persistent problem on the playground that the adults had not been successful in solving in spite of repeated interventions. Because we had invited students to consider safety rules, the students had addressed it themselves.

## School-Wide Celebrations

An aspect of developing our supportive community involved regular school-wide celebrations. We found a way for them to also be inquiry based. Our first was a celebration of Martin Luther King’s birthday that occurred a few weeks after I began as principal. Teachers were invited to work with their classes to present something to the whole school that acknowledged the importance of Dr. King’s life and legacy. This invitation offered students and teachers the chance to consider what they knew about Dr. King and to decide together, collaboratively, how they might present their ideas to the rest of the school. Over the years, the presentations included songs, poetry, a mural, original dramatic presentations, and personal stories, including one by a student whose father, as a baby, was comforted on an airplane by Dr. King.

Other celebrations during the year, including for Memorial Day and the start of each of the four seasons, were put together in the same collaborative spirit of learning through inquiry. For instance, when we gathered all together outdoors on the first day of fall, the autumnal equinox, each class presented through actions, dress, dance, poetry, art or song, their idea of the meaning of equality. In order to decide how to present their idea, the class had to ask themselves: What does *equal* mean? What does it have to do with the first day of fall? How can we show everyone in our school what we think equal means? The various representations (including one year when they occurred just as a rainstorm burst forth and drenched us all) were creative, mathematically conceptual, and reflective of the scientific concept of the equinox.

This approach resulted also in outreach to our wider school community as we invited families to help us celebrate Hispanic Heritage Month in October with an annual fiesta and Cambodian New Year in April with traditional foods, games and dance. We asked our families to *help us understand your culture* as we learned the dances, shared recipes and cooked food for the celebrations, and learned about their history and customs.

## Success for Teachers and Students

Over the years, the spirit of inquiry became the way we operated at the school. Adults learned that approaching learning and problem solving with elementary students through collaboration and inquiry makes for deeper and more lasting learning. It also encourages students to become independent learners who continue their explorations outside of class and school—on the playground and in their families and neighborhoods. Just as important, it makes for content students and a happy school.

Finally, it is widely agreed upon that the quality of a child’s teacher is a significant factor in the depth of learning for that student. Certainly it must be the goal of every principal to both attract and retain the very best staff members for the school. In order to do that, one must work toward a school climate that is intensely professionally satisfying to the adults who work there. In the words of a superintendent for whom I once worked, “You want your school district to work the best for the best teachers.”

I thought about those wise words every time I put together a hiring committee for a new staff member. I knew I wanted the candidates to be introduced to our teachers and think to themselves, “I would love to work with these people.” I was thrilled when exactly that happened. We were able to attract highly skilled and experienced teachers, including ones who were willing to leave another secure position. “I want colleagues with whom I can discuss the ideas that matter about teaching,” remarked one who chose to make such a move.
