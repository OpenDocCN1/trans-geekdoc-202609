In an inquiry-teaching classroom, both the teacher and the other students are interested in responses to “What is your. Before a student needs to engage in a whole class discussion, he or she has tried out a way of thinking about the issue. If I had been in this situation as a student, I would not have been tense all the time and hiding behind someone so as not to be noticed. Even back then, I would have been able to tell the teacher and the rest of the students in the class how I was approaching a problem or what my best thinking was so far, but nobody ever asked. In an inquiry-teaching classroom, that sense of tension is released. Students can safely try out their thinking and assist each other in understanding the steps needed to find the answer.

Secondly, inquiry teaching, when done effectively, is much more engaging to students than transmission. We all know that most discipline and management problems arise when students are bored and see no utility in a lesson. In inquiry teaching, and its extensions to project-based learning, interdisciplinary thematic units, and service learning, *management through engagement* exists. The way you have designed the curriculum itself is inherently interesting and students want to be there every day. With a practiced and skilled inquiry teacher, they not only learn to read well, but they begin to want to, and even, love to read. Likewise, it is the same for all of the other subject areas. They become learners. They leave most lessons with more questions they want to answer than they came in with. They can become explorers, designers, and creators, a very different ethos than exists in most schools. Now that is another kind of management all together.

# Acquiring Social Interpersonal Habits of Mind (SIHM)

In my work with new teachers, their concerns about discipline and classroom management usually emerge early in conversations. At some point during our first meeting, I usually make this statement:

> > If you are like most of us, you will be teaching for the next 25 or 30 years. You have a choice during that time of being a police officer or teaching your students how to self-manage. When I began teaching, I had problems with disruptive student behavior, so I became the police officer and enforced my rules. It was anything but fun, not particularly effective, was terribly time consuming, took time away from teaching and learning, and did not do anything to teach students to be participatory citizens, or to monitor their own behavior. Students were also not nice to one another and much less supportive of one another once I wasn’t on their case. So if you would also like to teach students how to self-manage, you need to supply them with learning experiences through which they will acquire the skills, insights, and understanding by practicing them in a real setting.
