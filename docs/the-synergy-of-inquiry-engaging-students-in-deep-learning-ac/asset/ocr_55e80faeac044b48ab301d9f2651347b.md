Individual students within a group will often choose different short stories or articles on the same theme, and then jigsaw back together to teach one another about what they read. The next day, a quiz will hold all students in the group responsible for knowing ideas from all of the stories. Sometimes I collect only one quiz from an entire group, and students do not know whose quiz will be used to grade all of them; therefore, it is in their best interests to divide up work and help everyone else out. Any one of them could end up being *the important one* for their grade. After quizzes, if one of my students performs poorly, it is the team’s job to help them correct their errors. We do peer editing of writing, particularly on simple, well-defined issues like comma usage, integration of quotations, or transition sentences.

Whenever a student gets stuck on an issue of knowledge or comprehension, they know that their first job is to ask a group mate. If the whole group is stumped, they need to ask members of another group, and/or look online. This has several benefits:

+   1. It reinforces the idea that, in a community of learners, we are all responsible for one another’s education. And it does not take up precious teacher time and resources.
+   2. It engages multiple students in the responsibility for one’s own learning, either as seeker or provider of knowledge.
+   3. Finally, it helps students develop the vital tasks of self-advocacy and independence—when out in the real world, they will not always be dependent upon a singular authority figure for answers.

As they work, I help facilitate and advise them about the *process*, rather than filling in the answers that stump them, except as an ultimate last resort, or if students are barking so far up the wrong tree that I feel they need a nudge back.

The most exciting parts of group work, however, go beyond the knowledge and comprehension levels. Because so much of the work that we do in my classroom is project based, students are frequently working in groups on a long-term task, such as providing an annotated analysis guide to a novel, creating a print or video advertising campaign, or writing and producing a modern retelling and staging of a Shakespeare play. Here it is even more vital for students to have defined roles (e.g., notetaker, discussion leader, and video editor). Sometimes I assign them these roles, but usually I give them the choice based on their interests and abilities. Sometimes I even have students define for themselves what the different roles will be, checking in to ensure the roles require equal or equitable amounts of work, or to remind them of questions they have not yet asked.
