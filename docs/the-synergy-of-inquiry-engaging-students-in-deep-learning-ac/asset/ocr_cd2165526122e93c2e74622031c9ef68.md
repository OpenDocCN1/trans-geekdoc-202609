Every one of these actions is organic to the steps of exploration, clarification, and real-world application of the inquiry teaching learning cycle. Every one of these actions empowers the individual students, teachers, school leaders, parents and community members in their roles in maintaining effective, dynamic, self-correcting, active, productive, creative places of learning. Effective inquiry engenders creativity and diversity of perspectives, and empowerment and collaboration are the norm.

# Voices of Experience

Rather than theoretically trying to explain this complex matrix of interactive factors that leads to the vitality, endurance, and academic and social success of certain school cultures where both students and teachers succeed and thrive, I have asked the principals of two such schools to describe their experiences where these cultures were collaboratively created and continue to prosper.

Although one school is a high school in a large urban city and the other is an elementary school in the rural end of a small town, the cultures created are remarkably similar. We all have much to learn from each of these journeys into inquiry teaching and the journeys of teachers and students as they collaboratively built their constantly adaptive and supportive school communities.

We first hear from Eric Nadelstern, the founding principal of the original International High School in New York City, a public school that has demonstrated remarkable success with English language learners that Jackie Ancess and Linda Darling-Hammond (1994) have documented. The school continues with this success almost 30 years later. This is the school where Jonathan Katz, one of the math teachers you heard from in Chapter 2, honed his teaching skills. It is also a high school that created its own interdisciplinary teaching teams and units, when almost no one was doing this at the secondary level. I think you will be astounded by how inquiry learning permeated and shaped every aspect of practice at the school. I had the pleasure of watching Eric and his teachers evolve over the first four years of the school’s genesis.

Then, we hear from Margaret (Margie) Riddle, a principal at Robert K. Finn Ryan Road Elementary School in Florence, Massachusetts that has a full economic spectrum of students. When Margie became principal, the school did not rank well on standardized test scores and many parents considered sending their students to other elementary schools in the district under a school choice provision. She describes the evolution of the school community as teachers moved to inquiry teaching in various subject areas and as they, along with the students, became empowered through inquiry. It is now a school where teachers want to teach, parents want their students to attend, and where students succeed. This is the school where Andrea Egitto, a teacher whom you heard from in chapters 2 and 3, now teaches. Even though Margie retired in 2013, the school culture still continues since teachers and students created it and continue to own it.
