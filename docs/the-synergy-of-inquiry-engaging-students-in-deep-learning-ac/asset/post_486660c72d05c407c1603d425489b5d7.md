| Time | Procedure | Objective |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| 5 min | **Introduction**

Include an activator, for example, something that relates this to their own lives or a question previously created by students. This will not explain any of the concepts, but it sets the context for the challenge—the exploration. |  |
| Varies from 30–60 min | **Small Group Work Exploration**

Divide the class into groups of four students.

Start with students exploring some documents, artifacts, hands-on science or math investigations, or role-playing. Provide some mechanism in the beginning of the exploration to uncover students’ prior knowledge and naïve conceptions.

- Have getters retrieve materials. Ask the individual participants to make a prediction or explanation near the beginning about the exploration. Students need to share with others in their group their predictions and explanations. They then test their predictions and explanations with others in their small group. They try to see if the group can come to consensus.
- Sometimes teachers will stop all the groups while they are still engaged in their exploration if they see that most groups are missing a main idea that some other groups are approaching. They would have groups report out their thinking so far and lead a quick discussion before sending the groups back to finish their explorations. | Concept, skill, or habit of mind |

## Detailed Learning Plan (cont.)

| Time | Procedure | Objective |
|---|---|---|
| Varies from 30–60 min | **Concept Clarification**

- When the teacher thinks that each group has had extensive time to create the concept or strategy or to hone the thinking skill, they call the class together as a whole. Reporters share each group's ideas, and the teacher leads a large class discussion that moves the participants to concept clarification.
- After this, students can search for what experts know about the concept and compare it to their naive conceptions. In order to accomplish this, they can listen to the teacher telling, read texts, or even do a web quest.
- Step-by-Step: For each of the preceding actions, be sure to note what students will be doing and creating, and what the teacher will be doing to facilitate this. Do this in great detail.
- Be sure that you have modifications for students with special needs, both in this facilitation of learning of concepts and skills, and in the assessments.

Be sure to include some synthesis homework that follows from what students did in class and leads to the starting of the next class. | Identify expected misconceptions and have these up front in your mind.

## The Synergy
of Inquiry

Engaging Students in Deep Learning Across the Content Areas

> Geared to current practitioners, this how-to handbook depicts an umbrella that encourages professional educators to keep what works. Theory, lesson plans, and classroom management strategies combine to support teachers as they implement a research-based approach with proven results.

—Brian Bates, M.A.
Teacher, Moreno Valley School District
Moreno Valley, California

Learning how to seek out answers to questions without the help of the teacher is important to students' academic success. With this resource, teachers will learn to help their students understand how to find the answers they need in an effective way. Regardless of the standard—language arts, mathematics, science, or social studies—an inquiry approach to teaching is not only effective, but is an efficient way to engage students to help them achieve the standards. This valuable and timely resource provides a synergistic set of practices that create a proven way for students to develop deep conceptual understandings, complex thinking skills, and enduring practices.

![](img/1ab167431007ad5d5eacf64bb7a71ad1_275_0.png)

Paul Jablon, Ph.D., taught in the New York City public schools for 19 years where he and his colleagues created classrooms, programs, and schools that utilized inquiry teaching and interdisciplinary, project-based learning to engage those students previously disengaged from school. As a faculty member at Brooklyn College, the University of Massachusetts, and Lesley University, he had the opportunity to work for over two decades with both preservice and practicing teachers. Leading systemic-change projects in urban and suburban school districts, he has collaborated with thousands of teachers to understand how inquiry teaching is best implemented with a diverse set of students from kindergarten through college.
