Inquiry-based mathematics teachers think about how to create meaningful experiences for their students so that their interest in mathematics grows and their experience is joyful. They try to create experiences that reflect how humans learn.

Raul Gracia, a Bronx ELL math teacher of over 15 years states, “Inquiry respects students’ innate ability to think, recognize patterns, make predictions, and make errors without fear and without judgment because that is exactly how humans learn.”

Eric Marintsch, a math teacher in Germany stresses that learning takes time and that inquiry instruction respects the process of learning. He writes,

> Allowing students time with inquiry lessons means that students are given an opportunity to unearth misconceptions and are provided the opportunity to make a discovery and actually explore mathematics. When this happens, student creativity is encouraged and classroom discussions blossom. The authentic experience students have during inquiry instruction leads to a deep and lasting understanding.

What is the role of a teacher in an inquiry classroom? Do I have my students sit passively as I present mathematical procedures and algorithms and they practice what I showed them, or do I create experiences for my students so that they can construct new knowledge within a community of learners?

Laura Mourino, a NYC teacher of over 10 years, describes how she thinks about the inquiry experiences she creates for her math students and her role in the classroom.

> My students are given the opportunity to guide their instruction by persisting on a task/activity until they discover patterns that can be generalized beyond the given task. I see teachers’ fundamental role in inquiry math instruction as not a modeler, but rather, coming up with that key activity/question/task that will not only engage students in “finding the solution” but also challenge the student to think deeply about what they are doing, why they are doing it and how they can use what they are doing in the future.
