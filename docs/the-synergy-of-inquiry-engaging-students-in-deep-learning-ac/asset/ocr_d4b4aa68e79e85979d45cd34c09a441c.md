The interdisciplinary daily or weeklong units just evolve naturally, almost by themselves. Andrea Egitto’s narrative in Chapter 3 illuminates this natural evolution.

The most effective interdisciplinary connections at the middle and high school level are not just between traditionally connected disciplines—science and math or social studies and language arts. Rather, successful units have had students experiencing the connections between language arts, science, physical education, eastern social studies, and pottery. Others connect mathematics, drawing, social studies, music, history, and computer aided design. Usually two teachers start with their two disciplines, but once a real world project becomes a component they add other disciplines by necessity.

Middle schools were designed to facilitate this practice. It easily occurs in middle schools where there are *real* teams, where all the subject area teachers are dedicated to interdisciplinary projects and are given *common planning time* during the school day to plan, revise, and administrate these units. Early adolescent needs are met and early teenage angst is greatly reduced. Students are both academically and socially more adept and mature.

By having subjects integrated, students can focus their learning. Being bombarded with six or seven disconnected classes per day, students cannot think deeply about any of them. When they have subject matter that is connected, they can focus their thinking and learning in school, on their homework, and in the community.

Success in one subject area or skill in an interdisciplinary setting can give students a *portal of entry* into one with which they are not as comfortable. Interdisciplinary activities afford students the opportunity to use strength in one of the multiple intelligences, to draw on the personal appeal of a particular subject, or to use a skill well-suited to the activities. This is particularly true when teachers use the powerful tool of analogous interrelationship role plays. These activities naturally lend themselves more to the human interaction subjects of language arts and social studies. However, by combining these with math and science, students can meet their affective needs as they work on the multidisciplinary project. Math and science are included in the human relationship aspect of school, and science and math are not experienced as the cold non-emotional subjects that make them much less accessible to many students. Likewise, the reverse is true for some students who are regularly more comfortable with the more objective and logical math and science explorations. Connecting science and math with the literature or social science issues gives students a safer *portal of entry* into exploring the human interactions embedded in these subject areas.
