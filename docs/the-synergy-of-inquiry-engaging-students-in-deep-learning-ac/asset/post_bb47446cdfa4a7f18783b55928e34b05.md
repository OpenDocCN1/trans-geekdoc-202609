- Students articulate these ideas verbally, modifying their understanding, syntactical structures, and sometimes the vocabulary as a result of verbal interaction with team members.
- The teacher cruises the room, checking levels of understanding in groups and facilitates the direction of each group through fairly open and not-too-leading questions.
- The teacher also asks individual students to verbalize their understandings and then asks them to write down these ideas in their notebooks, paying particular attention to see if students include important syntactical structures in their writing. For example, "if-then" phrases occur in everyday speech and writing but take on a special significance in academics suggest areas that need clarification or expansion, rethinking, or reorganization. This is usually done with non-leading, but carefully considered questions. However, some direct instruction can also occur in these writings.
- A current grade is given. Students then have an opportunity to edit their work, both for academic understanding and clarity of communication as well as in order to raise their grade.
- This process is repeated as many times as necessary until the student is satisfied with his or her grade (understanding and written communication ability). If students have never engaged in this process, at the beginning of the school year there are usually many extensive revisions, and exchange of papers between students and the teacher. However, as the year progresses, students begin to acquire both the academic reasoning abilities and the writing structures and strategies to clearly communicate their reasoning for each new conceptual understanding that they encounter.

## Apprenticeship in Language Learning
The apprenticeship of the *novice* subject area student to the *master* subject area teacher is missing in much of inquiry teaching. When the teacher is responding both orally and in writing to students’ reasoning about how they are uncovering a concept, the teacher is simultaneously teaching concepts and thinking skills and writing and reading strategies within the context of an academic discipline. Although students initially work in teams to share insights and negotiate meaning, the final translation of the individual student’s understandings of the conceptual understanding needs to be negotiated between the teacher and student through a *written Socratic dialogue* (Critical Thinking Community 2014). This process is a true apprenticeship: students create and communicate meaning as they synthesize their personal explorations with what is known by the larger academic community.

This apprenticeship related to academic thinking needs to continue when asking students to read materials once they are immersed in their investigations as noted in step five (academic language) and step six (translating) of the process. First, they need to be assigned a variety of materials to use in their search for understanding—nonfiction trade books, magazines and journals, newspapers, Internet resources, and subject area textbooks.
