However, whenever possible even the activities with these elementary school students should include applications to their home and community life so that students begin to effectively apply their newly acquired skills and conceptual understandings to decision making in their daily lives.

In addition, almost every skill and concept taught at the preschool and elementary level in every content area is something that can immediately be applied in students’ daily lives. Mathematical concepts can easily be applied in cooking, baking, building with wood, sewing, sports statistics, and calculations used in daily purchases. All of the science concepts and investigation and decision-making skills can immediately be used in their daily lives. They are all concepts that can actually be viewed and measured with even the simplest lenses and scales. The language arts skills can be taught using situations from students’ lives, literature that relates to and expands their lives, and by creating writing that is published in books, blogs, and newsletters and performed in plays and songs.

In early childhood social studies, much of the curriculum is actually studying the culture and local history of where the students live. In upper elementary social studies and in all of the middle and high school content areas, some of the topics studied do not immediately appear relevant to students’ lives, and especially to their inherent psychological needs. The study of ancient Persia or Japan does not appear immediately relevant. Greek or Medieval literature seems written in awkward language with dated characters. Calculating centrifugal force seems unimportant to almost every sixteen year old. Likewise, uncountable numbers of students have asked when will they ever need to use a quadratic equation.

Yet each of these topics, if embedded in the right explorations, contextualized in challenging local projects with the appropriate large conceptual objectives and effective themes, can be made engaging and deeply relevant to the learner’s life. It is important to investigate what is relevant to early and later adolescents. Perhaps a better way to ask the question is “what matters to teenagers?” Before you read on, please take one or two minutes to write down your top two answers to this question. If possible, discuss these ideas with your colleagues or students’ parents.
