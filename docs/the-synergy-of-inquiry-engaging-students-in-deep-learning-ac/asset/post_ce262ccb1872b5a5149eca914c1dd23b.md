
In an inquiry-teaching classroom, both the teacher and the other students are interested in responses to “What is your best thinking so far?” Before a student needs to engage in a whole class discussion, he or she has tried out a way of thinking about the issue. If I had been in this situation as a student, I would not have been tense all the time and hiding behind someone so as not to be noticed. Even back then, I would have been able to tell the teacher and the rest of the students in the class how I was approaching a problem or what my best thinking was so far, but nobody ever asked. In an inquiry-teaching classroom, that sense of tension is released. Students can safely try out their thinking and assist each other in understanding the steps needed to find the answer.

Secondly, inquiry teaching, when done effectively, is much more engaging to students than transmission. We all know that most discipline and management problems arise when students are bored and see no utility in a lesson. In inquiry teaching, and its extensions to project-based learning, interdisciplinary thematic units, and service learning, *management through engagement* exists. The way you have designed the curriculum itself is inherently interesting and students want to be there every day. With a practiced and skilled inquiry teacher, they not only learn to read well, but they begin to want to, and even, love to read. Likewise, it is the same for all of the other subject areas. They become learners. They leave most lessons with more questions they want to answer than they came in with. They can become explorers, designers, and creators, a very different ethos than exists in most schools. Now that is another kind of management altogether.

## Acquiring Social Interpersonal Habits of Mind (SIHM)

In my work with new teachers, their concerns about discipline and classroom management usually emerge early in conversations. At some point during our first meeting, I usually make this statement:

> If you are like most of us, you will be teaching for the next 25 or 30 years. You have a choice during that time of being a police officer or teaching your students how to self-manage. When I began teaching, I had problems with disruptive student behavior, so I became the police officer and enforced my rules. It was anything but fun, not particularly effective, was terribly time consuming, took time away from teaching and learning, and did not do anything to teach students to be participatory citizens, or to monitor their own behavior. Students were also not nice to one another and much less supportive of one another once I wasn’t on their case. So if you would also like to teach students how to self-manage, you need to supply them with learning experiences through which they will acquire the skills, insights, and understanding by practicing them in a real setting.

Teachers are not alone in their concern about interpersonal interactions. Surveys show that business leaders have similar concerns about worker interaction (Weissberg and Cascarino 2013). On surveys, they report that graduates often lack writing and math skills, but their number one concern is a lack of ability to work effectively as a member of a team. It does not matter whether it is an engineering team, a design team, a team at a fast food restaurant, or a middle-school team of teachers. Actually many schools, though not intentionally designed this way, appear to be structured to teach students to compete and *not* assist one another in learning.

In our work with teachers on facilitating conflict resolution activities with their students, we find that invariably they mention how their own interpersonal relationships with their significant others improve if they practice and utilize the conflict resolution skills themselves. The most candid often point out that the faculty and administration in their school could use some work on these skills as well.

I have come to view these abilities as Social Interpersonal Habits of Mind (SIHM). In other words, these are skills and insights about group and interpersonal interaction that need to become part of the fabric of thinking of all individuals and can be utilized every day of their lives, in all settings.

## Defining Group Roles

In earlier chapters, I made the case that students create their understandings and negotiate their ideas when they can have a voice in a small group of four while they engage in an exploration. Such group learning will not be successful unless students, whether in first or twelfth grade, learn *how* to work with one another successfully. To do so, they need structured, explicit experiences with the elements that contribute to effective cooperative interaction. When you overtly teach them how to effectively cooperate, cooperative learning occurs. Students are doing two things simultaneously: (1) learning academic content concepts and thinking skills, and (2) learning the skills necessary to be effective group members.

I keep mentioning groups of four—what is so special about four? Why not create groups of five or three? With more than four students in a group, someone will not be heard and will be left out. Many teachers have found that larger groups create discipline problems, as students’ attention wavers. In addition, many times a pair of students is all that can focus and communicate on a given exploration, so you do not need to move chairs if they are already sitting in groups of four. It is so easy to pair students up without wasting time. The two pairs at a table will overhear each other’s discussion, which is a good thing, since it allows for a richer discussion. However, just having them seated appropriately will not teach them what effective group members do. Therefore, it is necessary to assign students a role in their group. This role changes daily, so they get to practice the thinking and interpersonal skills needed to accomplish each role.

### What Does the Starter Do?

The students begin by thinking that starter means boss. They soon realize that it is not that easy. Since they are responsible for keeping all members of the group on task, they need to be clear what the task is and then come up with ways to approach and communicate with the students who are not on task. As the teacher is cruising the room, when she or he notices a student off task for a period of time, they quietly and discreetly urge the starter to address the issue. Sometimes there are students who do not respond to these nudges. The group works on strategies, with the assistance of the teacher (and sometimes other groups), to come up with strategies to get disengaged students on task. This is an invaluable skill to master and will be utilized throughout students’ lives.

### What Does the Getter Do?

The getter needs to physically get up and bring back all materials used in the activity of the day. Getters need to make checklists of materials and utilize them to be sure they have acquired everything needed by the group. They also can get up and ask the teacher questions once the activity has started. Here again students get to practice articulating what the group already understands, and subsequently clearly articulate what the group is not yet clear about. Each role gives students insights, through practice, with some of the actions and thinking that it takes for a group to be successful.

### What Does the Recorder Do?

When I ask most teachers what they think the recorder does, almost always the answer is that they write down the group’s ideas, data, or best understandings up to that point. For many this has been their past experience with a recorder in a group. When I ask what might be the problem with that, the group of teachers thinks for a bit (wait time), then there are usually a few answers such as the “recorder cannot participate much in the conversation” or “they may be the kind of person who does not take good notes.” As they think through what happens on a day-to-day basis during explorations in an inquiry-teaching classroom, the limitations of one person possessing the written history of the group’s thoughts becomes apparent. During the exploration stage, each student in a group needs to record the data or ideas. Each of them will need to analyze it and be ready for continuing the exploration the next day. If only one student, the recorder, has this information, then the rest cannot use it for homework, or worse yet, if today’s recorder is absent the next day, the group cannot continue its exploration.

Specifically, the recorder’s job is to be sure to remind everyone in the group to write down their ideas or data. If the teacher, as she or he is cruising the room, sees that one or more students are forgetting to record their ideas, then the teacher quietly says to the recorder, “Hey, what’s happening with Mike or Carmen?” Many times students in the group most involved with negotiating ideas forget to write them down. Since the roles are changed every day, each student in the group gets to practice this responsibility until little by little it becomes the fabric of thinking for everybody in the group.
