In the past 10 years, I have had the opportunity to work with many effective math teachers in grades 6 through 12 as a mathematics coach. My understanding of teaching and learning has grown immensely through this work. I have sat in hundreds of classrooms where inquiry-based mathematics was the central approach used by teachers. I observed the ways mathematics was presented and how students attempted to make sense of, and understand, mathematical ideas and problems. In the following sections, you will get to hear from some of these teachers and students on how inquiry instruction can change the way mathematics is thought about.

## Inquiry-Based Mathematics Instruction

Many students regard mathematics as a collection of procedures that allows one to answer questions in a short space of time (Schoenfeld 1988). Meanwhile, inquiry is a mode of teaching and learning that “rests on conceptual innovation” (Schwab 1962, 5). Students participating in a community of inquiry, discuss and solve new and unfamiliar problems (Kwon 2005). Problems are used to help develop mathematical understanding of concepts and procedures rather than viewed as applications of already learned procedures (Koehler and Grouws 1992; Hiebert 1999). A teacher’s job is to “encourage conjecturing and arguing and to make the environment safe for students to express their thinking” (Koehler and Grouws 1992, 122). Inquiry proceeds through uncertainty and struggle leading to knowledge that is hard to come by (Schwab 1962).

Two ideas central to inquiry-based mathematics instruction are *patterns* and *problem solving*. Mathematics has been defined as the “science of patterns” and the central role of a mathematics student is that of a pattern hunter. How can I make sense of the information presented to me? What observations can I make from the patterns? What conjectures can I make based on the observed patterns? Can I prove if this conjecture holds true for all cases? When a student understands the centrality of patterns as an underlying structure of mathematics, he or she has a greater chance of developing mathematical understanding.

Reuben Hersh, in his discussion of mathematical philosophy states, “It is the questions that drive mathematics. Solving problems and making up new ones are the essence of mathematical life” (1997, 18). George Polya (1945) wrote in the preface to the first edition of *How to Solve It*:

> Thus, a teacher of mathematics has a great opportunity.
> If he fills his allotted time with drilling his students in routine operations he kills their interest, hampers their intellectual development, and misuses his opportunity. But if he challenges the curiosity of his students by setting them problems proportionate to their knowledge, and helps them to solve their problems with stimulating questions, he may give them a taste for, and some means of, independent thinking.

(Boaler 2008, 26)

Both Polya and Hersh are describing an essential aspect of inquiry. Students are presented an interesting problem, which is challenging yet approachable; it is open ended in that it contains different roads to reach a solution or conclusion; and it will lead to other questions and problems for a student to try to solve. John McCrann, a geometry teacher in New York City agrees with Polya and Hersh. He writes,

> “Too often, K–12 mathematics is presented as a static set of answers void of the questions that lead humans to develop those answers. Inquiry mathematics reconstitutes the mathematical process, prompting students to ask and understand the questions and the answers in problematic situations involving quantitative and logical reasoning.”

Many students of mathematics, both past and present, have come to perceive mathematics as uninteresting and irrelevant. It lacks coherence because the different content areas of mathematics have been “chopped up into small pieces which focus on the mastery of algorithmic procedures and isolated skills.”

(Schoenfeld 1988, 163)

Can pure mathematics be interesting in itself? Can the beautiful patterns and relationships that make up mathematics engage a student’s mind? Can these patterns help students see the connectedness of mathematical ideas and procedures? A large goal of inquiry instruction is to help students appreciate the magnificence of mathematics while also helping them understand how mathematics can help to explain the world they live in.

Inquiry-based mathematics teachers think about how to create meaningful experiences for their students so that their interest in mathematics grows and their experience is joyful. They try to create experiences that reflect how humans learn.

Raul Gracia, a Bronx ELL math teacher of over 15 years states, “Inquiry respects students’ innate ability to think, recognize patterns, make predictions, and make errors without fear and without judgment because that is exactly how humans learn.”

Eric Marintsch, a math teacher in Germany stresses that learning takes time and that inquiry instruction respects the process of learning. He writes,

> Allowing students time with inquiry lessons means that students are given an opportunity to unearth misconceptions and are provided the opportunity to make a discovery and actually explore mathematics. When this happens, student creativity is encouraged and classroom discussions blossom. The authentic experience students have during inquiry instruction leads to a deep and lasting understanding.

What is the role of a teacher in an inquiry classroom? Do I have my students sit passively as I present mathematical procedures and algorithms and they practice what I showed them, or do I create experiences for my students so that they can construct new knowledge within a community of learners?

Laura Mourino, a NYC teacher of over 10 years, describes how she thinks about the inquiry experiences she creates for her math students and her role in the classroom.

> My students are given the opportunity to guide their instruction by persisting on a task/activity until they discover patterns that can be generalized beyond the given task. I see teachers’ fundamental role in inquiry math instruction as not a modeler, but rather, coming up with that key activity/question/task that will not only engage students in “finding the solution” but also challenge the student to think deeply about what they are doing, why they are doing it and how they can use what they are doing in the future.

An inquiry math teacher believes that students have the ability to think with complexity and are creative thinkers, thus he or she encourages students to feel free to take risks as problem solvers. Robert Messineo, a fifth-year teacher in Rockaway, Queens who began teaching after working for many years in banking, writes

> I have learned to give up control of the class and let students guide their own education. This was difficult at first, but seeing my students discover various ways to teach themselves has been very rewarding. My students have become problem solvers, and can apply their problem solving techniques to other topics in their math studies.

Many teachers think inquiry teaching in mathematics can only be done with simple arithmetic in the elementary grades, with some seeing possibilities for middle school level statistics, geometry and introductory algebra. In the section “What Does Inquiry Mathematics Look Like?”, located on the Digital Resource CD, Jonathan Katz describes inquiry mathematics explorations for complex high school mathematics lessons. These lessons reveal that inquiry teaching has application for mathematics learning throughout the school years.

## Students’ Thoughts About Inquiry Instruction in Mathematics

One of the powerful aspects of inquiry instruction is the demand it makes on students to think in different ways. Students have often viewed math as a rigid discipline in which memorizing and following rules and formulas is essential to being successful. Inquiry instruction is about helping students to develop conceptual understanding and procedural flexibility by engaging in mathematical discovery. In inquiry instruction, students are asked to think when they grapple with open-ended tasks independently or in collaboration with other students. In developing an understanding of concepts, procedures, rules and formulas students are often asked to think about patterns, make conjectures and generalizations, and attempt to prove or disprove them. The thinking can be visual, algebraic, or logical. In all cases students use their reasoning skills to develop understanding.
