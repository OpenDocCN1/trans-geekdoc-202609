
Developing an ITSLU takes some thought and planning. Figure 5.6 presents a 12-step template for developing an interdisciplinary thematic service learning unit. This template can be found on the Digital Resource CD.

## Figure 5.6 Template for Planning an Interdisciplinary Thematic Service Learning Unit (ITSLU)

1. **Introduction, Overview, and Rationale**—Make clear how inquiry teaching and learning is occurring in each of the disciplines but especially in the small products and project as well as the service learning component.
2. **Main Concepts, Skills, and Habits of Mind**—Specify how each of these will be addressed for each discipline in the unit. Include some rubrics that assess at least two small products on various aspects and one rubric that assesses the final project. It is more likely that you will do most of your assessment of academic skills on the small projects leading up to the bigger service learning project. Additional assessment may be necessary based on your particular situation.
3. **State Standards**—Identify the standards applicable to the disciplines selected for the unit. Use the standards as benchmarks for assessing development of concepts and acquisition of skills.
4. **Engaging Activity**—Plan what you will have students do physically and mentally to hook them on addressing the driving question and engage in the service-learning component.
5. **Theme and Essential/Driving Question**—Explain how your theme is effective based on the components of an effective theme (see Figure 5.1). Show the connection of the theme to your essential question and how it is transformed into a driving question that intrigues students sufficiently that they care enough to search for the answer.
6. **Timelines: Day-by-Day and Week-by-Week**—Prepare timelines for each discipline and across disciplines. Chart the timelines in a way that one can see when disciplinary classes are meeting alone and when they are meeting combined with each other. If you are doing more than 15 days of activities, then describe these 15 days in detail and the rest in a week-by-week summary.
7. **Small Projects**—These projects serve a scaffolding function to teach skills and concepts. Define the progression of small projects that lead up to or are parallel with the service learning project that they support.
8. **Service Learning Component**—Describe this component in detail, including information about any community participants, including parents with insights and skills, who will interact with students and support their learning and accomplishment of their project. Be sure that students are dependent upon one another in order to complete the project.
9. **Study Skills, Research Skills, and Habits of Mind**—Define those that will be most emphasized (learned and practiced) in the unit. Explain why you selected these and where they will be practiced.
10. **Media, Technology, and the Arts**—Identify and describe the media (books, videos, music, websites, historical documents, and so on) that students will read, watch and listen to. Additionally, and even more importantly, define what books they will publish, what videos or podcasts they will produce and air on public venues, and what plays or songs they will write and perform. Be sure to enumerate what technologies they will use to produce these or study natural phenomena and how they will acquire the necessary skills.
11. **Concept Map**—Design a detailed concept map (with connecting phrases) that shows the relationship between various components of the unit.
12. **Practical Realities**—Address the *nitty-gritty factors* that will affect the success of your unit. For example:
- sources of funds for materials, transportation, or field trips
- how to obtain administration buy-in
- when planning time will occur
- managing time—define aspects of the unit that extend beyond normal school hours
- student supervision—define how students will be supervised during after-school hours; specify permissions required and how they will be obtained

Other than using inquiry teaching in your classroom, working with at least one other teacher on an ITSLU will be one of the best decisions you make during your teaching career.

## Second Language Learners and Inquiry Teaching

Many times, second language learners are disenfranchised in our general education classrooms. However, the way that learning takes place for everyone in an inquiry teaching class benefits second language learners. The nature of the inquiry approach assists these students in more quickly learning the new language while simultaneously making the academic content accessible in a way that is not possible in a traditional didactic, transmission classroom.

In 2014, I attended a WIDA (World-class Instructional Design and Assessment) workshop on *English Language Development Teaching Skills* that focused on knowledge and skills classroom teachers need to acquire when they have second language learners in their classes. By the time the all-day session had finished, I was astonished to hear that the prescribed practices to help second language learners thrive, were almost a perfect description of the inquiry teaching classroom. We, teachers, needed to learn the linguistic complexity levels that these students could be at, and accordingly, what modifications we needed to make in our written and oral materials for them. However, the rest of the classroom dynamics presented in the workshop were the same as those described throughout this book.

Not long after the WIDA meeting, I was reading a number of articles and methods books that describe best practices for addressing the CCSS English Language Arts/Literacy. As mentioned in earlier chapters, many of the practices these Common Core methods writings were suggesting matched what inquiry teaching educators already were doing in all of the subject area classrooms. Given this context, let us first review how language acquisition occurs in a native speaker’s language in an inquiry-teaching classroom before we see its application in a second language situation. This schema that applies to Common Core language skills is useful to understand in and of itself but will later serve as an advanced organizer for how it can work with second language learners.

## Language Acquisition within the Inquiry-Teaching Classroom

The inquiry-teaching classroom provides a perfect opportunity for students to practice *relational meaning* in language since they are simultaneously doing, and translating this doing, into both oral and written language. The syntax, grammar, paragraph writing, and reading comprehension skills and strategies will naturally grow out of students’ small group explorations as they are negotiating their ideas with peers and eventually writing the reports about what they learned in these explorations. This process needs to be mediated by having the teacher consistently respond to extensive student writing and the idea creation that underlies the writing, thereby supplying scaffolding throughout the whole process. Seven steps in the process, described in the following section, reflect key features of the Inquiry Teaching Learning Cycle.

### Step 1: Exploration—Students, working in a small team of four or less, are engaged in manipulating various materials.
- Students write the question that they are exploring.
- After *messing about* with the materials and discussing ideas with team members, students revise the question to more clearly and concisely state the point of the investigation.
- Students work with others on their research team (cooperative group) to create initial procedures that the team will follow.
- Each member of the team writes and revises his or her own procedures and findings.
- Each team member also writes about the affective part of the team’s exploration that day. A student writes about what he or she was feeling during this investigation, as researchers in academia have traditionally done in their journals in addition to recording their ideas. The teacher needs to be checking that students can differentiate between evidence, inferences, emotions, and opinions. The student subsequently identifies these elements and writes about them in appropriate places.

### Step 2: Meaning Negotiation—Students discuss findings and relationships between various parts of the problem that they are investigating with other members of the exploration group.
