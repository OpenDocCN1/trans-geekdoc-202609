A number of educators began to seek out and examine junior high schools with unusually high success and began to meet and write about the promising practices that worked for these early adolescents. William Alexander, Donald Eichhorn, and John Lounsbury to name a few, created this concept of a *middle school* that was designed differently than either elementary or high school and had a unique approach to both the social and cognitive development of these tweens.

I mention the middle school practices at this point in this book, and particularly in this chapter, because many of these promising practices are similar to management practices that are espoused in this chapter. Others are similar to practices found in the next chapter that experienced inquiry teachers begin to use as they expand their repertoire so that inquiry teaching is even more engaging for a broader spectrum of students.

The next time that you read about a middle school that is not being effective, the first question that should cross your mind is, “Is it even a true *middle school*?” Just because there are teachers from different disciplines assigned to a “team,” does not mean it is a middle school. It may well be a *faux team*—one that does not do interdisciplinary thematic units or run advisories for lack of preparation to engage in these practices or any of the other practices that follow.

Look at this list of characteristics of effective middle school practice. As one reads through the list, it is easy to see how inquiry teaching and learning can easily fit at the core of all these structures. In the next chapter, you will read about how many teachers or schools at the elementary or high school level who were using an inquiry teaching approach adopted a number of these middle school practices. True middle school teams who are defined by these practices often find that using an inquiry teaching and learning approach provides a missing piece that brings even more power to their team.

The characteristics of effective middle school practice are shown in Figure 4.5. They are organized in three categories: *Structure, Curriculum, and Teacher Support.*

# Figure 4.5 Characteristics of an Effective Middle School

# Structure

+   - Small communities for learning within the larger school building
+   - Classroom learning communities where individuals and groups of students are self-monitoring in a constructive social environment that supports one another's construction of knowledge
+   - Exploratory classes during the school week where students work with an adult and other students on topics that the individual student chooses to study in depth
+   - Families re-engage in the education of young adolescents with active participation on the team
+   - School transition programs that assist students transitioning into middle school or transitioning into high school
