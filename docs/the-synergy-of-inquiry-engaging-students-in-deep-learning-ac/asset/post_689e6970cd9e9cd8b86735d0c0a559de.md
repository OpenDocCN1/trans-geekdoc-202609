
This can occur that same day. Most other examples of students not abiding by a strategy or structure are organically dealt with by the group as a whole, under the facilitation of the teacher.

## The Role of Classroom Meetings and Relationships in Dealing with Disruptive Behavior in the Classroom

When teachers ask me what can be done about disruptive behavior of students in the classroom, I say that I cannot answer that question until they let me know whether the class had created structures or norms of behavior that everyone has agreed to earlier. Similarly, I want to know if the teacher has met with each student to determine what his or her expectations are for the class. If these relationships are established, then when two students are disrupting the class, the teacher has strategies to resolve the situation. Consider the following scenario:

> The teacher simply asks each student, “**What are you doing?**”
> 
> The student starts to explain the situation, but the teacher reiterates much to the surprise of the student, “**What are you doing?**”
> 
> The student finally answers something like, “I am arguing with the other student about the pen.” The teacher then says, “Will this **get you what you want** from this class?”
> 
> The answer is usually, “No.”
> 
> The teacher continues, “Then what plan can you make for now so that you get what you want from this class?”

None of this proven effective interaction can occur if these relationships were not created earlier. We realize this still has not solved the issue of the ownership of the pen, but the class is no longer disrupted, and the two students are back on task. At the end of class the teacher might be of some assistance with the pen issue.

This method is a very simplified modification of William Glasser’s (2011) Choice Theory and Reality Therapy, applied to classrooms. It is sometimes called *Responsibility Training* and has been used successfully in many schools across the country. Just as in inquiry teaching, it is built upon students figuring out plans, designing norms for the classroom, and negotiating modifications of these in classroom meetings. A former New York City high school student of mine who participated in the BONGO program, a successful program for at-risk urban adolescents, which will be discussed in Chapter 5, addressed this issue of responsibility and its effects on teenagers. Just after his graduation, Ron Zirin pointed out the following when he was being interviewed about his experience at the school. “In the other schools I was at, they expected you to learn responsibility through discipline, and for me and many other teenagers we would just naturally try to defy the rules. Here at Middle College High School, and in BONGO in particular, it is just the opposite. You learn discipline through responsibility” (Jablon and Born 1989).

## Conflict Resolution Skills

Conflicts will always occur. Consequently, conflict-resolution activities need to be added to the mix once student engagement is evident and some group building games have occurred. The question is, can we resolve them in a positive way for everyone involved, and in some cases, even de-escalate situations before they become full-blown conflicts? There are wonderful books and manuals filled with conflict resolution activities for every grade level.

In elementary schools, the grade-level teacher usually runs these activities throughout the year. In middle schools, and even nowadays in some high schools, the optimal place for these activities to occur is in advisory class. However, if there is no advisory class, then subject-area teachers need to do these tasks in their classrooms. If you are not familiar with the concept of advisories, it is described later in this chapter.

Many times teachers who feel pressured will say they do not have time to spare to do these activities with students. When I ask them how much time they spend on discipline issues or having to reteach lessons because students are not really attending, they say that it is substantial. Doing this is an investment, letting students acquire self-management and conflict resolution skills that pay off for the rest of the year. These skills also pay off for the rest of students’ lives.

Through these activities, students come to understand some important concepts about conflict resolution skills that pay off for the rest of the year. These skills also pay off for the rest of students’ lives.

They learn:
- how conflicts escalate;
- how to de-escalate conflicts;
- how to be active listeners;
- how to use “I” statements instead of “you” statements; and
- how to avoid conflicts associated with in-school and out-of-school cultural differences.

You do not need to devise these activities. There are comprehensive whole-year programs that are specifically written and field tested for elementary, middle or high schools. Be sure that these manuals are all written by teachers and field tested in urban, suburban, and rural areas.

These conflict resolution activities need to be done before any *anti-bullying* programs are instituted. They set the groundwork for such programs. In addition to teachers using these strategies with their students in their academic classrooms, many schools utilize homerooms, advisories, and other structures within their schools to accomplish the facilitation of these skills.

Many grade-level teams in elementary schools, interdisciplinary teams in middle school, and subject-area departments find it helpful to have teachers and administrators themselves engage in the middle or high school level of these conflict resolution activities. If they are to be an effective educator team, not only does this help them better facilitate the activities with their students, but it gives their own teacher teams the ability to effectively get through the confrontations of differences that occur in the storming stage of their team’s evolution. Most teams, even in middle schools that are based around teams of teachers, are *faux teams* because those team teachers are not willing, or are not encouraged by the administration, to work together and struggle to overcome their differences until they can come to some group norms for their educational goals and methodologies. Think back to David Nurenberg’s loss of a $15,000 grant to teach an after-school interdisciplinary, inquiry-based class described in Chapter 3. Teams of teachers that have gone through this process are the teams that parents beg to have their students be assigned to.

## Whole Curricula or Programs for Building a Positive Community in your Classroom or School

If you work at the elementary level, you might consider using a research- and evidence-based approach to elementary education that leads to greater teacher effectiveness, higher student achievement, and improved school climate that goes well beyond the conflict resolution manuals listed above. This is an approach that I have seen fit seamlessly with good inquiry teaching classrooms and schools. Social and emotional learning programs focus on teaching students how to manage emotions, their behaviors, and interactions with others. Some do so through direct lessons, but others focus on teacher language and modeling expectations, describing themselves as an approach to learning rather than a program. A controlled study at the University of Virginia showed higher language arts and mathematics scores on standardized tests in classrooms of teachers who faithfully utilized one of these programs.

One of my favorites on how to create a positive middle or high school community in your classroom is *Making Learning Real* by Carol Miller Lieber (2009). This book addresses every issue a teacher can have about reaching and engaging *all* learners. If you want to know about personalized learning for yourself and your students, resilience and positive life changes, culturally responsive teaching, and social efficacy, here is the go-to manual.

Later in this book you will also hear about schools that utilized programs of adventure learning using both low and high ropes courses.

## Building Relationships

Joe Dolan, a colleague and also a principal in Cambridge, Massachusetts, emphasizes the idea that both teaching and discipline are about relationships. He says that a teacher’s greatest asset is a positive relationship with each student. To build on that idea, I will add that the relationship between all of the students in the class is equally important. That is why we do “Two Truths and a Lie” or “The Name Game” so that not only the teacher, but every student, can know the name and something about every other student in the class. Refer to the Digital Resource CD for descriptions of “Two Truths and a Lie” and “Name Game.”

In an inquiry-teaching class, the importance of the teacher communicating to students that much of the knowledge to be learned will come from themselves and other students cannot be overlooked. How the teacher facilitates the *concept clarification phase* of the inquiry teaching learning cycle provides the model for students to emulate. For example, when a student makes a statement, the teacher should *not* repeat the statement. On the contrary, a really effective whole-class discussion during an inquiry lesson occurs when a student from one group gives his or her understanding and a student from another group then responds to the statement. A third student can then question one or both on the interpretations from each of these previous students. Yet again, another student can ask for clarification of an idea. The best would be for another student to try to synthesize some of these ideas into a more generalized idea. None of this can occur if students are not listening to one another and are responding only to what the teacher repeats. Therefore, the teacher should not repeat the idea because students then see the idea either as coming from the teacher, or being validated by the teacher.
