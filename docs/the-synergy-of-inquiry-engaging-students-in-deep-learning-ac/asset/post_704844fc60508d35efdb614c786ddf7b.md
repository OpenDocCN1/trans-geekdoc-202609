
> As an undergraduate in my teacher preparation program, I was actually taught to repeat student answers because everyone in the class might not have heard what the student said. (I hope that is no longer the case, although I expect that some of you may have had a similar experience.) However, in an inquiry classroom we really need to be aware of the importance of creating an ongoing interactive dynamic among students and having them negotiate ideas. We do not want to get in the way of this process by repeating their answers.

If we really want to build positive relationships between students who are usually not friends, while also increasing academic understandings, then there is no better way than to have students out of the school on field trips that are mostly related to academics. If at least one of these can be an overnight experience, positive relationships increase exponentially.

The more assignments you create that require the use of other intelligences as outlined by Howard Gardner (2006), the more opportunities you will provide to include those students who are not strong in logical-mathematical or written linguistic areas. Since almost all school-based assignments call for these two areas, students who excel in other areas see themselves as stupid. Oral poetry, music video production, drawing, or dramatic and dance performance given as mandatory assignments, not always as alternative assignments, honor their skills and understandings and their peers realize how they need the help of these previously *dumb* classmates. When we give these assignments, we create strong relationships with some previously unreachable students.

## Distributed Counseling

For over a decade, I have worked with a group called the *Institute for Student Achievement*. This is a group of former principals and teachers who have retired from very effective small inner-city high schools throughout the country. Using only a few full-time staff to coordinate the summer institutes and on-site weekly mentoring, the group has helped launch over a hundred effective small high schools in cities such as New York, Atlanta, Buffalo, Detroit, Newark (NJ), and Union City (NJ) to name a few. In addition to strong inquiry-based curriculum and high collaboration between the faculty, staff, and administration in the design and daily operations of the school, they have created a distributed counseling model that all the schools try to completely infuse into who they are and what they do.

In this model, everyone in the school sees themselves as a counselor to students. They are not trained counselors and are not giving advice to students. However, all teachers listen to students and their issues and reflect back to the student what the student has said in a way that the student can clearly see the situation in which they are engaged. The teacher can then help the student see various responses and their possible consequences. When teachers see issues arising that they think are beyond their abilities to deal with, they can refer students to two or three well-trained counselors whom students admire and trust. It is up to the student if she or he wants to continue talking to the guidance counselor. Students almost always do, but it is not over then. The counselor reports any progress back to the teacher that he or she can without losing confidentiality and credibility with the student. Of course the teacher keeps up the original relationship with the student.

These relationships and conversations many times come about because teachers are engaged in inquiry teaching that has led to project-based learning, which many times has a public presentation product. Once students are making something real while working *with* the teacher, then real person-to-person conversations occur naturally. Other times, it is because the teacher is the advisory teacher. Before the concept of *advisories* became a nationally accepted part of effective middle school practice, my colleagues at the original Middle College High School in New York City had created a very similar concept that they called *house*.

At our school, *house* met for 20 to 30 minutes every day (it changed over the years). It consisted of about 12 to 15 students and an adult. Most of the adults were teachers, but the principal, assistant principal, the counselors, family workers, and some exceptional paraprofessionals also had a house in order to keep the student to adult ratio down. Ninth to twelfth graders were in the house, and houses stayed together for four years. If four seniors graduated, they were replaced the next September by four new freshmen. That way every student not only had an adult advocate, but they also had a dozen or so other students who were connected to him or her. They also knew each other's stories and looked out for one another. Juniors and seniors would try to stop ninth and tenth graders from messing up like they had. Sometimes conflict resolution activities or some group building games were organized in September for new student inclusion, but many times it was just checking in with one another to see if people could help with issues. The "house teacher" met with the parents each term for four years and knew much about the family life of the student. This created a positive working relationship with the family.

Many times, the student had a closer relationship with another subject-area teacher than with the house teacher. That was fine because there was an intentional network that ran like a web where, if something was going on with a student, everyone on staff soon knew with whom to speak. This web included not only the adult advocates who had a relationship with the student in question, but also other students who had a relationship with the student and were included to become another kind of help and advocate.

Once you have a school with 500 or less students, this structure is really possible. During the 13 years I worked at the school, two different principals served and each of them knew not only the name of every student but usually knew some personal information about them as well. Needless to say, they spent more time out and about in the school than in their offices. The counseling was truly distributed.

Unfortunately, not all schools are set up this way. If this is the case in your school, your classroom-community building activities become even more important.

## Building Cooperative Group Structures

It seems that whenever I ask teachers about the cooperative-group structures they employ when their students are working in small groups, they usually mention Think-Pair-Share, but they have a hard time identifying other effective interactions. Over the years, teachers have created a variety of group structures that fit the needs of particular lessons. Teachers, especially those using inquiry teaching, should take advantage of all that are available.

### Sample Cooperative Learning Structures

### Circle the Sages

Students who know about something stand to become sages. Teammates each gather around a different sage to learn. Students return to teams to compare notes.

### Find My Rule (Property)

The teacher places items in a frame (e.g., two boxes, Venn diagram, or on a line). Students induce the rule for each, that is, what is the property in common, or the rule that makes them similar: Two Box Induction, Crack my Venn, or What’s my Line?

### Fish Bowl

Have three or four students engage in a discussion, debate, role play, or other interactive activity. The rest of the students in class form a circle around them. The observers in the outer circle cannot say anything until the inner group is finished, as determined by the teacher. The outer circle can then ask questions of those in the inner circle or of one another.

As always, the teacher should have a pocketful of facilitating questions at hand to move the discussion in an appropriate direction if students seem to be having difficulty with the process.

### Formations

Students stand together as a class and form shapes. For example, base groups choose an animal for a mascot (an eagle) and re-create the image of it. One person becomes the body, two others become wings, and so on.

Taken to a higher level, have students become a statue depicting their impression of child abuse, racism, and so on.

### Gallery Walk

Students in their small groups are asked the same question, challenge, or situation. They write their answers by drawing on chart paper with markers.

After a period of time decided by the teacher, students post the chart paper in different spaces on all four walls in the classroom.

All the groups then get to amble about the gallery, stopping at each chart and reading the ideas or seeing the drawings.

As a variation, as individuals or groups find something inspiring, they can use a different color marker and add their extensions to the original idea. They can also ask thoughtful questions.

### Inside Outside Circle

Students stand in concentric circles, with the inside circle facing out and the outside circle facing in. They make a quarter turn to the right and then march or rotate past each other. The teacher defines how many people they must pass before they stop. When they stop, they turn and face the person they are paired with, and respond to teacher instructions (e.g., Share an answer, express your opinion, or come up with an explanation).
