## Jack Fidler’s Reflections

At a recent eighth-grade faculty meeting, I was struck by the high percentage of teachers who felt that their students demonstrated a state of *learned helplessness*. Teacher after teacher recounted stories where students were challenged to figure out something on their own, and said some variation of, “You are the teacher; are you not supposed to tell us?”

I have tried, sometimes successfully, sometimes not, to instill openness to curiosity in my students. That recent faculty meeting made it clear to me that it is particularly challenging to accomplish this, when in class after class, teachers spoon-feed students the answers. I am pleased that at my school we are beginning to recognize that there are certain habits of mind we should cultivate in our students, habits of mind that lead to successful inquiry-based learning. Something only becomes habituated if it is practiced with regularity, so what happens in other classrooms can help or hinder my success.

Even without a culture of inquiry learning at our school, I have seen the spark in students’ eyes when they ask a question they are genuinely interested in the answer to. I have made it a part of my practice to teach students how to question the texts that they read. They find this difficult because they are not used to being guided to formulate questions while reading.

Still, it does happen. By the time my seventh graders get to Chapter 7 of *Animal Farm* (Orwell 1992), there is always one or two who are confounded by the idea of anyone confessing to a crime they did not commit. “Why would any animal confess to treason when they have watched Napoleon’s dogs tear apart someone else who confessed?” From this question, the student is ready, because interested, to explore the show trials in Stalin’s Russia, and eventually stage scenes for the class that suggest answers they may have found.

My personal challenge as a teacher is to permit room for wonder. In teaching a unit on the play *Inherit the Wind* (Lawrence and Lee 2003), a retelling of the Scopes monkey trial in 1920s Tennessee, my culminating activity has involved students formulating, and then exploring, a question about which they are still wondering after they complete the play. *Inherit the Wind* is rich in areas where student curiosity can be triggered, from questions of law and First Amendment rights, to religion in the American South, the theory of evolution, the role of media in the early 20th century, and the differences between the play and the actual trial. I teach at an Orthodox Jewish day school, where a number of students dig far deeper than I require because they want to get to the bottom of how Judaism reconciles Genesis and evolution. For those students genuinely interested in such questions, they are off and running. Here is what I find does not work: When I present students with a long and varied list of possible questions about which they might be wondering, then suddenly the assignment mutates from one truly driven by inquiry to one where they are simply selecting a choice from a teacher-approved list. I can see the difference in their motivation, or lack of, as they either enthusiastically or dutifully track down leads.

Clearly, I can benefit from the collaborative thinking of other teachers as I seek to fine tune assignments like mine for *Inherit the Wind*. That is why I am so pleased that my school has finally recognized the problem of “learned helplessness.”

## Nora Burnett’s Reflections

Inclusion plays a significant role in any successful heterogeneous classroom. It has been my experience that choice and group work are two key strategies to use when working with students who have different levels of ability and/or motivation regarding the specific content of a course (in my case, English language arts).

Choice has several benefits:

- It allows students to take ownership of their learning.
- It enables them to express their learning in a manner which is meaningful to them.
- It provides flexibility in the way in which we, as teachers, assess student learning.

I am by no means saying that teachers should completely turn over their classrooms to their students, but by incorporating some choice, students with varying degrees of motivation or skill are more likely to become engaged in their learning. In addition, I often get a better picture of students’ abilities when I provide choices in how they demonstrate their knowledge.

Similarly, I have found that group work is an excellent way to respond to, and support, different student abilities and learning preferences. In groups, students learn from another teacher—a peer. Students have a great knack for rewording concepts that a teacher has introduced. When students reword, it not only demonstrates their own knowledge, but it also reframes the idea so that someone else, who may have missed it before, can grasp it. Group work also allows a safe environment for different kinds of students. It gives students a platform to try out ideas and gain confidence prior to whole-group discussion.

## Pause, Think, and Reflect

It is important to note that working within a group is a skill that needs to be practiced. Being able to work together so that everybody plays some role is so crucial, yet people often take it for granted. Though students can find it frustrating, it is a skill that must be taught and supported. Prior to the lessons shared below, the classes discussed group work, focusing on questions such as: What are the different roles within a group? What does respectful listening look like? Is everyone using everybody’s names in the group? Why are we doing the group work? What do you, students, expect from the group? What are your expectations for each other?
