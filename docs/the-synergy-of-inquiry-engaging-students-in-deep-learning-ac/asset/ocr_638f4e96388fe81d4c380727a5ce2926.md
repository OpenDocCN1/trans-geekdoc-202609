The lesson that Andrea shares with us is one of the first she ever did using primary sources with her first graders. The complete lesson, “Utopia: One Great Family,” can be found on the Digital Resource CD. Previously, Andrea had planned inquiry teaching in other subject areas but not history. (See Andrea’s reflection about becoming an inquiry teacher in Chapter 3.)

# Andrea’s Social Studies Lesson

To begin the lesson, Andrea reads a picture book about the Underground Railroad. She asks students what they think it was like being a runaway slave going through Florence. She extends this idea to the bigger, yet more personal idea of what can be done if something is unfair. Students begin their exploration by creating a circle word map with the word “community” in the middle. This activity leads students to the idea of a community banding together.

Together, Andrea and students read parts of photocopies of the original constitution from the NAEI. They also have other primary sources—maps of the town with stops of the railroad on it, a newspaper article from the local paper in 1867 written by the treasurer of the NAEI, and photographs of the buildings associated with the group. After viewing these resources, students add new ideas to their circle word map. They finish by working in small groups to write one sentence about each of two words that Andrea assigned them from the circle word map. In classes Andrea previously taught, there were even some students who were living in or next to houses that were on the Underground Railroad map! Subsequently, students were able to travel along the *African Heritage Trail* in Florence led by tour guides from the local historical society.

Andrea explains how she became excited about using primary documents for the first time and how they fit so well into her inquiry-teaching format.

# What Andrea Was Thinking

Andrea had always used literature as a way to bring history to life for her students. Researching and creating this primary-source-based lesson had forever altered how she thought about and planned her history lessons. Using these primary sources can really bring to life a period in history like nothing else. Giving her students a chance to view old photographs, and even be able to see the different type of handwriting in a letter, can open their eyes to a whole world. It was even interesting for them to see how the newspaper looked so different than it does today. They compared the copies Andrea got from the microfilm to the newspaper today—wow, what a difference! Students actually began their exploration by doing history, by being historians using primary sources. They uncovered the concepts at a first-grade level and then communicated them.
