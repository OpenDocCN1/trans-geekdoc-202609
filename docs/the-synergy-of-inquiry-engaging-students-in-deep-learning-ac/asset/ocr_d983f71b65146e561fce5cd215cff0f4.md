# The Motion Program

The major catalyst in moving us toward a school model based on collaborative inquiry learning began without fanfare, or even notice. Felicita Santiago, a math teacher, came into my office late one August to discuss the work she had done with Uri Triessman that summer at the University of California at Berkeley. She was so excited about what she had just learned about facilitating math learning through group work, that the sounds and laughter emanating from my office attracted David Hirschy, the school’s physics teacher, who entered, sat down, and listened. At one point, he said that the work Felicita was describing is the mathematics of motion, the underlying concept in Newtonian physics. I suggested that if they wanted to do a joint class together, I would support it (Jablon 1989b).

In the days to come and in typical International High School fashion, David and Felicita recruited Kathie Rugger, an English teacher, to teach Poetry in Motion, as well as the physical education teacher, Alan Krull, who had just launched Project Adventure, an indoor version of Outward Bound. Together, this team of teachers created the Motion Program, the first full-day, integrated, interdisciplinary community of learners that would later come to define the International approach (Jablon 1989b).

The Motion Program ran the following trimester with just one class as a pilot project. Faculty members planned together and were often in each other’s classes. Felicita and David team taught a double 70-minute period combined physics and math class. Small teams of four or five students worked on projects together over several days of work as teachers circulated from table to table supporting students and extending their thinking. It was the first time I experienced the exhilaration of multiple voices engaged in meaningful work with one another. It was not noisy but purposeful.

At the end of the first trimester, 100 percent of students passed, not just one class, but all of their classes. We tried it with another class for the next trimester with the same results. In contrast at the time, 66 percent of all students enrolled in New York City high schools failed one or more subjects; 45 percent failed two or more; and a full 33 percent failed three or more subjects each semester. By any measure that 100 percent of students enrolled in the Motion Program pilot passed all of their courses was an extraordinary outcome.
