The interdisciplinary daily or weeklong units just evolve naturally, almost by themselves. Andrea Egitto’s narrative in Chapter 3 illuminates this natural evolution.

The most effective interdisciplinary connections at the middle and high school level are not just between traditionally connected disciplines—science and math or social studies and language arts. Rather, successful units have had students experiencing the connections between language arts, science, physical education, eastern social studies, and pottery. Others connect mathematics, drawing, social studies, music, history, and computer aided design. Usually two teachers start with their two disciplines, but once a real world project becomes a component they add other disciplines by necessity.

Middle schools were designed to facilitate this practice. It easily occurs in middle schools where there are *real* teams, where all the subject area teachers are dedicated to interdisciplinary projects and are given *common planning time* during the school day to plan, revise, and administrate these units. Early adolescent needs are met and early teenage angst is greatly reduced. Students are both academically and socially more adept and mature.

By having subjects integrated, students can focus their learning. Being bombarded with six or seven disconnected classes per day, students cannot think deeply about any of them. When they have subject matter that is connected, they can focus their thinking and learning in school, on their homework, and in the community.

Success in one subject area or skill in an interdisciplinary setting can give students a *portal of entry* into one with which they are not as comfortable. Interdisciplinary activities afford students the opportunity to use strength in one of the multiple intelligences, to draw on the personal appeal of a particular subject, or to use a skill well-suited to the activities. This is particularly true when teachers use the powerful tool of analogous interrelationship role plays. These activities naturally lend themselves more to the human interaction subjects of language arts and social studies. However, by combining these with math and science, students can meet their affective needs as they work on the multidisciplinary project. Math and science are included in the human relationship aspect of school, and science and math are not experienced as the cold non-emotional subjects that make them much less accessible to many students. Likewise, the reverse is true for some students who are regularly more comfortable with the more objective and logical math and science explorations. Connecting science and math with the literature or social science issues gives students a safer *portal of entry* into exploring the human interactions embedded in these subject areas.

Simultaneously, the Control Theory work of William Glasser (Glasser 1986; Gough 1987) supported the group project orientation of cooperative learning that was emerging.

Since 1993, the Buck Institute at the University of Wisconsin has taken a lead role in assisting teachers and school systems in creating meaningful and effective standards-based, problem-based learning (PBL) units (Markham 1997; Hallerman, Larmer, and Mergendoller 2011). Larmer (2014, 1) sees PBL as a broad category, which, as long as there is an extended project at the heart of it, could take several forms to be a combination of the following:

- Designing and/or creating a tangible product, performance, or event
- Solving a real-world problem (may be simulated or fully authentic)
- Investigating a topic or issue to develop an answer to an open-ended question

As in the 1930s, evidence for the success of PBL is beginning to amass during the current standards-based environment (Ravitz 2009; Strobel and van Barneveld 2009; Walker and Leary 2009; Vega 2012a; Ravitz, Hixson, English, and Mergendoller 2012). Studies that compare and contrast learning outcomes for students taught via project-based learning versus traditional instruction show that PBL (when implemented effectively):

- increases long-term retention of content;
- helps students perform as well as or better than traditional learners in high-stakes standardized tests; and
- improves problem-solving and collaboration skills, and improves students’ attitudes towards learning (Strobel and van Barneveld 2009; Walker and Leary 2009).

As we will see in Chapter 6, PBL can also provide an effective model for whole-school reform (National Clearinghouse for Comprehensive School Reform 2004; Newmann and Wehlage 1995). These inquiry-based teaching methods engage students in creating, questioning, and revising knowledge while developing their skills in critical thinking, collaboration, communication, reasoning, synthesis, and resilience (Barron and Darling-Hammond et al. 2008).

## Meaningful Themes

In candid conversations with teachers about what prompted them to enter the profession, I frequently observe that they share a common concern: to have students acquire some central core understandings about life that will enable them to lead happy, fruitful, and productive lives. These understandings tend to be some big, universal ideas. If teachers are working with adolescents, these ideas tend to be at the core of the complex issues that we all deal with as adults. They also are the ideas that are at the core of teenagers’ dealings with one another, with their parents, and with other adults in authority. Thinking back to Maslow and what adolescents care about, these are ideas that will help them deal with the complexity of life from their perspective. Teachers will mention such ideas as, “many times you cannot control what happens to you, but you can control how you react to it.”

Yet these same teachers note that they rarely get to teach about these ever so important insights into dealing productively in our complex human societies. Occasionally language arts and social studies teachers can touch upon them, but they never really get to visit the issues in any depth. When students get to explore one of these understandings from multiple disciplinary perspectives and then use this understanding in creating something that has an impact on their community, the theme becomes a powerful component of school success.

This view of what constitutes a theme is different than what you find in educational books and in discussions with some teachers. In many cases, teachers are urged to integrate their lessons around a topic. Take for example ancient Egypt, the fishing industry in New England, recycling plastics, or small family-owned farms. It is surely possible to create interdisciplinary units around these, but these are topics—not effective themes—in the way that we are addressing them here. Teachers find that using topics as the integrating issue limits what they can do in each of the subject areas in addition to not allowing the opportunity to engage students in these large generalizable life lesson ideas. Many very effective units will have these and other topics as part of the unit, but they are not the theme. A theme is a complex universal idea with certain qualities that allows for a meaningful integration of the various disciplines.

Over three decades of working with teachers on creating effective interdisciplinary thematic units, my colleagues and I have added to an initial set of criteria first compiled by Martinello and Cook (2000) and created components for an effective theme. Such units have engaged many previously disengaged students in their schools.
