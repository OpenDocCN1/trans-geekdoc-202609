Students acquire these social-interactive skills and self-management skills much more quickly, and with deeper understanding, when the whole-school community works on them throughout the students’ tenure at the school. ## David Nurenberg’s Reflection on Classroom Management

I have been teaching English and social studies with inquiry-based methods for over half of my 14-year career. Every year I challenge myself to do it more and more, because every year I see how well students respond and to what heights they can push themselves using these models.

As a teenager, I was the rare student who could learn just fine by taking notes from a teacher who lectured for 50 minutes straight. Even then, what had I learned? I could rattle off the dates and outcomes of battles in World War I, conjugate Spanish verbs, and tell you all about Newton’s laws, but the classes that actually got me *thinking* were those that also involved *doing*—analyzing something as I was building it, designing and conducting experiments, or discussing and debating ideas with fellow students. Here was where I actually had to think critically and create new knowledge—the very skills that the Common Core State Standards are now asking of students (2010). At best, traditional, teacher-centric classroom models risk preparing students only with skills in memorization and regurgitation. Far more often, it just disengages the vast majority of kids who are not intrinsically motivated and do not see the relevance of the classroom to their *daily lives*.

For me, inquiry-based methods have slain the proverbial two birds with one stone: how to engage reluctant learners and how to help all students develop and hone their higher-order thinking faculties. I have worked with students for years in co-creative classroom environments that demand and reward student independence and interdependence. Students in my classroom work cooperatively, as a class and in small groups; it is rare that all of us are pursuing the same task in the same fashion all at once. Within their groups, students have tasks for which they are individually accountable—differentiated by interest and ability level. At every turn, I attempt to give my students as much opportunity as I can to direct the course of what they learn, and to actively learn from and teach one another, with the teacher serving as guide or facilitator.

From the beginning, I emphasize with students that we are a community—this is not a classroom of 27 students and 1 teacher, but of 28 students and 28 teachers. While I hold a role of authority in the classroom, I am not the sole repository of knowledge. Rather, I try to reinforce how it is our job to teach one another, and we cannot do that unless we have a climate of mutual respect and openness. I am very explicit with these values. During the first few weeks of class, we practice routines such as “how to have a class discussion.” We mutually negotiate rules—I have a few non-negotiables, like *no put-downs*, but I have almost never had to be the one to articulate them, as the kids come up with them on their own. We also mutually design how to transition between activities, how to pass out new materials, and so on. We do the same process for setting up norms for group work, norms for when a substitute teacher is present, and then revisit these norms on occasion as they need clarification or revision.

Activities like our daily check-in (when we go around the room and students give a 10-word or less summary of how they are feeling) or “Don’t Drop the Ball” (where students have to collectively create a sentence in round-robin style) help the kids get to know one another, and practice how to work independently. Although they seem non-content related, they in fact demand of the kids that they develop and use skills in listening to one another, working together towards a common goal, and so on. “Don’t Drop the Ball,” for example, helps students practice listening, paying attention, anticipating, and cooperative goals (the group cannot succeed unless all members pull their weight). As the year goes on and the groups bond more, I vary the type of the games to be less about *get to know you* and more about *how to self-evaluate meaningfully*, and eventually, *how to say goodbye*.

Group games like these, and especially the conflict-resolution activities and simulations we engage in a few weeks later, develop students’ skills in being able to work together in meaningful ways, and to productively address the inevitable conflicts that come up. Students often find them fun because they do not feel like schoolwork, even though they are some of the most important schoolwork we ever engage in. I always tell my kids that I hated group work in high school, and it was only years later that I realized this was because no one ever taught me how to do it; teachers just plunked us in groups and told us to work. Would just plunking someone in the cockpit of a jet airplane and telling them to fly result in a successful experience?

Although these activities might at first seem to threaten precious time in content-area learning, I have found that activities like this eventually enable successful cooperative and inquiry-based work. Besides, it is seldom an *either/or*, as these activities can often be paired with relevant content. I have had my students practice “I” statements and address root causes of conflict, applying them both to scenarios from their own school and to the feud between the Montagues and the Capulets in *Romeo and Juliet*. When practicing empathy and perspective-taking to analyze our own personal conflicts, we are then explicit in talking about how these are the same skills that let us analyze whatever character or theme we are looking at in the novel or short story we are reading. The humanities are not the only disciplines with easy cross-applicability between skills for cooperative work and skills for learning content. The hypothesis you develop and defend in a science class, the pattern you attempt to construct through inferential logic in math class, all such draw upon that ability to look at data (behavior), search out root causes, and arrive at an explanation or solution that works for a given problem. A teacher who can help students find those links can, simultaneously, help students develop the skills to work together effectively in their immediate lives, cover required content, and, most importantly, help students develop links between what school asks of them and what will help them in their everyday experiences. Common Core has helped this process immensely by asking schools to no longer privilege students learning specific names, dates, and formulas as a goal in themselves, but to limit that kind of declarative knowledge to its applicability in helping students develop a core set of skills.

## Making the Classroom a Nicer Place

I spent most of my school career hiding behind the student sitting in the seat in front of me. Luckily I was small in stature, so it was not too difficult a task. I constantly felt like I was a contestant on a game show where I needed an answer at all times. At any moment, the teacher might call on me for the answer that I might or might not have. It was embarrassing for me to be wrong or worse yet, not have an answer.
