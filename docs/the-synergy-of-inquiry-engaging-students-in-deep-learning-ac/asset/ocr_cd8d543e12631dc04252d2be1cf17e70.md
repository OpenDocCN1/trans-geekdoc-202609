# What Does the Starter Do?

The students begin by thinking that starter means boss. They soon realize that it is not that easy. Since they are responsible for keeping all members of the group on task, they need to be clear what the task is and then come up with ways to approach and communicate with the students who are not on task. As the teacher is cruising the room, when she or he notices a student off task for a period of time, they quietly and discreetly urge the starter to address the issue. Sometimes there are students who do not respond to these nudges. The group works on strategies, with the assistance of the teacher (and sometimes other groups), to come up with strategies to get disengaged students on task. This is an invaluable skill to master and will be utilized throughout students’ lives.

# What Does the Getter Do?

The getter needs to physically get up and bring back all materials used in the activity of the day. Getters need to make checklists of materials and utilize them to be sure they have acquired everything needed by the group. They also can get up and ask the teacher questions once the activity has started. Here again students get to practice articulating what the group already understands, and subsequently clearly articulate what the group is not yet clear about. Each role gives students insights, through practice, with some of the actions and thinking that it takes for a group to be successful.

# What Does the Recorder Do?

When I ask most teachers what they think the recorder does, almost always the answer is that they write down the group’s ideas, data, or best understandings up to that point. For many this has been their past experience with a recorder in a group. When I ask what might be the problem with that, the group of teachers thinks for a bit (wait time), then there are usually a few answers such as the “recorder cannot participate much in the conversation” or “they may be the kind of person who does not take good notes.” As they think through what happens on a day-to-day basis during explorations in an inquiry-teaching classroom, the limitations of one person possessing the written history of the group’s thoughts becomes apparent. During the exploration stage, each student in a group needs to record the data or ideas. Each of them will need to analyze it and be ready for continuing the exploration the next day. If only one student, the recorder, has this information, then the rest cannot use it for homework, or worse yet, if today’s recorder is absent the next day, the group cannot continue its exploration.

Specifically, the recorder’s job is to be sure to remind everyone in the group to write down their ideas or data. If the teacher, as she or he is cruising the room, sees that one or more students are forgetting to record their ideas, then the teacher quietly says to the recorder, “Hey, what’s happening with Mike or Carmen?” Many times students in the group most involved with negotiating ideas forget to write them down. Since the roles are changed every day, each student in the group gets to practice this responsibility until little by little it becomes the fabric of thinking for everybody in the group.
