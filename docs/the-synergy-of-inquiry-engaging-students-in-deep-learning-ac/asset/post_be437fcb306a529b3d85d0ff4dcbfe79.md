Let us take a minute here and go back to Maria’s planning stage. Once she created and planned this sequence of exploration and concept clarification, she realized that three thinking skills were most prominent during the exploration:

- **Observation**—close and thoughtful scrutiny of materials
- **Inference**—based on evidence from careful observations
- **Model making**—using careful data collection and inference making

Likewise, she realized she could push students to realize that they were using two particular habits of mind. She could then encourage them to continue to use these habits of mind in their daily lives and give them examples of where they could be used as they were watching television during the past week. These were:

- Be curious. Be careful and accurate in observations.
- Know that often different explanations can be given for the same evidence, and it is not always possible to tell which one is correct.

Given the focus for subject area teachers to work on literacy skills, she also included an essay-writing assignment later in the lesson. She did not originally have an art subject area objective listed, but when she reflected on the lesson after she taught it, she modified her plan so that the next year she taught the lesson she could focus on building the skills of her artistically talented students, and could encourage all of her students to be as detailed and accurate as possible in their renderings. The objectives for the other subject areas were:

- Recognize not only careful and accurate drawings but also the true artistic abilities of gifted students.
- Have students create a well-reasoned and clearly-written three paragraph persuasive essay to defend their conclusions about how cell division occurs based on their research team's evidence.

## Step 6: Create a Rubric that Defines Specific Levels of Student Achievement

Once Maria created the objectives and plan for the exploration and concept clarification stages, I challenged her to create a rubric for her own use in assessing where each student was on their continuum to mastery. After a few back and forth iterations between us, she created a three-level rubric. Figure 2.7 shows the rubric for three levels of student performance—novice, competent, and expert.

### Figure 2.7 Rubric for Assessing Student Use of Thinking Skills, Habits of Mind, and Concept Understanding

|  | Novice | Competent | Expert |
|---|---|---|---|
| Process Skill | Makes quick observations and draws without great detail. Places drawings in a sequence without careful thought to what is occurring in each sequence. | Uses careful observations, makes detailed drawings, and infers a sequence (model) based on explanations of what they think is occurring with the nuclear material. | In addition to skills in the competent category, is able to provide a different explanation of why the same or a different sequence can be explained using the same evidence. |
| Habit of Mind | Fails to see that the inferential explanation that they hold to support their sequence of stages is only one possible explanation of the same evidence. | Realizes that more than one inferential explanation can be given for the same sequence and that another sequence might be explained by another interpretation of the same evidence. | In addition to understanding various interpretations for the same evidence, also understands that this is a major component of the nature of science and the scientific enterprise. |
| Concept #1 | Recognizes that the nucleus looks different in various cells near one another, but does not have an explanation for the various layouts. | Recognizes when cells replicate, nuclear material appears in strands that differ from non-replicating cells. ## The Utility of Rubrics for Teachers: They Keep You Honest

The most effective teachers I have observed and spoken with have all expressed their deeply held understanding that they teach students, not subject areas. They also understand that they need to have a deep mastery of whatever subject area they are teaching so that they know how to create appropriate explorations that allow students to own the understandings and skills in that subject. Teachers are so invested in each individual student that sometimes it becomes difficult on a daily basis to separate out the personalities of students from their current mastery of a concept or skill.

By creating rubrics for each lesson with very rich entries in each cell, teachers can remind themselves of the difference between a student who is competent and has truly mastered a concept or skill and a novice student who, despite being a wonderful kid, is still working toward mastery. Likewise, it allows teachers to look with some objectivity at the somewhat difficult child and realize that he or she has mastered the material despite possibly having the teacher at wits end.

Rubrics keep teachers focused on what level of mastery every student should be aiming for. Some students enter the class having mastered some or all of a concept or skill that is the focus of the lesson. These students need to be enriched with a higher or tangential investigation using the same exploration that the rest of the students are doing that day. The teacher has planned this extension ahead of time and created the expert category in the rubric. This assures parents that although their child is in a heterogeneous group, the teacher is differentiating appropriately for each level of mastery. In the mitosis lesson, Maria had questions ready for her expert students about spindle fibers and cell size that could be answered with the same materials but were not required understandings of all students.

Another issue that can arise is that once teachers hear the expert-level students speaking about their insights, it becomes too easy to unintentionally expect the rest of the students to get to this level. This is true despite the original appropriate objective the teacher wrote in her or his plan. By having the competent and expert categories next to one another in the rubric, the teacher does not frustrate students who are not yet ready for expert understandings. It also allows the teacher to create appropriate assessments for the competent objectives expectations, and not to think of themselves as ineffective because their students have not all mastered the expert level.

Just as with all the other parts of a plan, the cells in rubrics get richer and more useful as they are revised because teachers are paying close attention to student dialogue. Each time teachers better understand the intricacies that differentiate the categories of mastery from one another, they are then able to add to the important details in the cells.
