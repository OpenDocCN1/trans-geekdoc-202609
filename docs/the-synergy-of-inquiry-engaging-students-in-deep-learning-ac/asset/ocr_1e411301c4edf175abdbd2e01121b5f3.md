Students need some time to think on ideas and to struggle and figure out. However, at a certain point they can become too frustrated if they are heading completely in a nonproductive direction. Therefore, teachers should restrain themselves, but then when they need to facilitate, teachers should have some questions ready in their pockets.

# ## Step 9: Create Facilitating Questions

Maria realized how important it was to make up good questions ahead of time after her previous attempts at inquiry lessons. What she meant by “good” questions were questions that would have students think about the issue without leading them or giving away the answer. Here are some questions she had ready if students were really lost after struggling for a while.

+   - When a root grows in the ground, where do you think it is most likely that new cells will need to grow? Why?
+   - When a cell is about to divide into two new cells, what do you think it might look like?
+   - If a cell is going to pass all of its traits and information onto the new cell being formed, what part of the cell do you think needs to give this to the new cell?
+   - If the original cell needs to also have a copy of this information, what will the dividing cell look like?

# ## The Planning Process for First-Grade Social Studies

Any subject area, grade, or developmental level is appropriate for inquiry teaching. Andrea Egitto, a first-grade teacher in Northampton, Massachusetts, used inquiry teaching to combine the concept of community with the local history of the Florence section of Northampton where her school is located. During the period before the Civil War, a group of citizens in Florence were so outraged by the laws of the United States that allowed slavery, a lack of women’s rights, and other inequities that they formed a separate community, the Northampton Association of Education and Industry (NAEI). In addition to engaging in many other progressive activities, this group became an active and important part of the Underground Railroad.
