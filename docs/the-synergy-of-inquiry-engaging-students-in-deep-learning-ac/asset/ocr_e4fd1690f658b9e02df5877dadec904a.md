Many teachers do not attempt inquiry teaching or try to teach students to self-manage because they fear loss of control. Rather, if teachers plan well and give students the necessary support, students will be happy to share positive, group-supportive control with the teacher. It appears to be an enigma; by giving up certain types of control, you gain so much more. It is no longer them against you but rather we're all in it together. Yes, as the adult in the room, you have ultimate responsibility for students’ mental and physical safety and the behavior of individuals supports learning, but teaching is so much more effective when the whole group has created, and is supporting, the strategies to achieve learning.

# Group Building Games

It is not surprising when students have not created a set of behaviors for sharing objects, ideas, and space if no adult has facilitated some process for them to do so. Although students engage in aspects of group process during the content exploration activities, they do not necessarily acquire facility with those processes in that context. Therefore, teachers find it useful to engage students in a set of physical, kinesthetic activities outside of these explorations that foster a group-building ethic. However, engaging students in group-building activities will not lead them anywhere unless each activity is followed by a discussion facilitated by the teacher where students make sense of the activity. The teacher is responsible for directing the discussion of how features of the activity demonstrate particular parts of the process necessary for effective team building.

Although there are some activities that students can do sitting at their desks, a majority of them require students to get up and move around the room and physically interact with other students. These kinesthetic experiences are the most successful for group building (Griss 1998; Jensen 2000). We suggest that you start students with activities that require less contact and then move to more contact as the term progresses. For example, the first days of the class you might engage students in “Lineup” activities which are virtually contact free, then move to “Mirrors,” and follow this later in the term with the “Human Knot” which has a great deal of contact, although it does not require a lot of personal trust. Eventually, when the group has come together somewhat it is possible to try out other trust activities such as “Bridges” or “Finger Lifts.”

Group-building activities are designed so that they can only be accomplished if the group works together as a team; this includes students in the class who have physical or mental disabilities. Of course, some of the activities will need to be modified so that all students can participate—including students who have physical disabilities.
