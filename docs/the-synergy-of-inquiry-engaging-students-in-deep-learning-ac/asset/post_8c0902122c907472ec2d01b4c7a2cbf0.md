## Impact of the BONGO Program

That was six years into a three-period-a-day program that Terry and I co-taught and directed for 13 years and that in some form or another has been continued by teachers around the country. A ten-year quantitative and qualitative research study of the program uncovered some fairly amazing results. Despite the almost universal agreement among students in the program that the coursework was the most challenging they ever encountered in school, a ten-year study of the program demonstrated a remarkable success with a set of previously disengaged students. This structure accounted for some fairly compelling results with a population of urban African-American, Latino, and white students who almost all had a history of at-risk behavior. The results showed:

- Ninety-two percent did not cut classes during the time they were in the BONGO Program. Seventy-seven percent of those students had a history of cutting classes before they were in the program, which dropped to zero cuts while in BONGO.
- Attendance also increased, both for students who were mostly truant, and for students who attended school fairly regularly, but had significant absences. Sixty-five percent of students experienced increased attendance, 31 percent showing a massive increase. For any of these students to have above 95 percent attendance was unbelievable for their parents, their other teachers, and themselves. The number of students who showed either perfect or 95 percent attendance doubled (perfect attendance went from 3 to 5 percent; 95 percent attendance went from 21 to 45 percent). While students were in BONGO, 50 percent had 95 percent attendance or better.
- Eighty-nine percent of the BONGO students either increased or remained constant in their academic performance with 51 percent showing a major increase. Thirty-six percent failed science before BONGO, but passed during the program. Fifty-four percent had an increase in their science grades.
- The most startling finding was that 32 percent of students went from passing no courses, or one or two courses, to passing *all* of their subjects, including those classes outside of the three BONGO periods.

The Watson-Glaser critical thinking test administered to BONGO students demonstrated that 60 percent of those students significantly raised their critical thinking abilities, which was four times greater than their peers in control classes. The Process Skills and Science, Society and, Technology (PSST) test of process skills in science and abilities in understanding Science, Technology and Society (STS) relationships revealed that 41 percent of the BONGO group increased their science reasoning skills and STS awareness over the course of one term as compared to nine percent within the control group (Jablon 1989).

How was this possible? What were the components and activities that changed the behavior of a vast majority of these adolescents within a three-month period of time? We need to go back and revisit the section in Chapter 1 about what really matters to teenagers and the congruence of that with Maslow’s hierarchy of human needs. Most students who are disengaged from school have the same needs as typical teenagers, but some of those needs are poorly met. If the deficiencies are not met, students cannot focus while in school. However, it is possible to simultaneously meet these needs and engage students in quality inquiry teaching and learning. It is a matrix of components that make this possible. Many teachers have used one or more of these components in their teaching but it is the synergy of the matrix that allows these students a successful engagement with school. Before looking at the whole matrix, let us build it one component at a time. The components are (1) interdisciplinary units, (2) project-based learning, (3) meaningful themes, (4) issues of social conscience, and (5) final public projects.

## Interdisciplinary Units

> “Life decisions are interdisciplinary, but schools teach separate subjects.”
> —Dodd 1984

A review of the literature demonstrates a call for interdisciplinary education to support the Common Core even though most school practitioners have not embraced it (Heitin 2013; Mazzara 2014). Teachers as well as students suffer from the narrow organization of education. Creating only a narrow focus on specialized content actually diminishes comprehension of the content itself. There are aspects of a subject that are unnoticed or invisible until we view them from a distance or from a perspective of another subject. Significant knowledge is naturally interdisciplinary (Ortega y Gasset 1957).

As noted in the previous chapter, those schools employing effective middle school practices with early adolescents have teachers on teams who are meeting almost daily to sustain interdisciplinary units throughout the year. Likewise, effective elementary teachers rarely teach their subjects separately but use science or social studies activities as the topics for their nonfiction or fiction texts to teach language arts skills or use appropriate math skills during their science activities.
