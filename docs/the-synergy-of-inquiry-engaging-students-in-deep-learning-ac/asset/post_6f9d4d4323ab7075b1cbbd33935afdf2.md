Students will soon realize that they are being asked to figure things out, to provide evidence, and to work hard with their peers at their thinking. Often, they lack the skills to accomplish all of this. They may also lack the skills to apply the new concepts and skills that they are obtaining to unique real-world situations. It is a process of acclimation. They may moan and groan after a few weeks and say things like, “just tell us the answer.” However, those pioneer teachers who persevere will soon find the whole class slowly climbing out of the gulch.

Persevering through this period of adjustment may be one of the most difficult things the teacher does in his or her career. It is natural to want to go back to the old way of teaching that one has already mastered, but keep in mind that inquiry teaching helps students develop deep understanding.

## Starting the Planning Process

To help illustrate the inquiry learning process, a model lesson on mitosis is included here. I co-planned the lesson with Maria, a novice teacher, and we tested it in her classroom to ensure that it really does work with students.

Think about where you may have encountered the topic of mitosis. Most of us have been in classes where we were lectured at about this topic, and were told to go home and memorize the names of the stages that matched a set of diagrams in the text. If you were lucky, maybe you used a microscope for a few minutes to look at a cell that you could not see too clearly anyway. You probably did not do much figuring out, did not gain deep understanding, and what you did gain likely does not much remain in your long-term memory. Therefore, I do not expect many of you to be knowledgeable beforehand about the topic, but it will be something that you will now have a better understanding of as we progress through the planning, even though you do not get to physically manipulate some of the materials. To compensate for the lack of access to actual materials, I include a number of photomicrographs (pictures taken with a microscope) to help us visualize what students will be doing. Subsequently, we will go through the inquiry planning process for other subject areas and grade levels where you might feel more comfortable. However, in all situations, planning for inquiry teaching is a similar process.

Much of this planning process will not be new to you. It will be similar to how you plan now if you are using *Understanding by Design* (Wiggins and McTighe 2005), *Dimensions of Learning* (Marzano and Pickering 1997), *Dougherty* (2012) with her focus on assignments, *Authentic Instruction* (Newmann, Secada, and Wehlage 1995), or any other powerful planning approach. Nonetheless, you will see that in a number of places, the process of planning inquiry teaching differs from all of these.

## Planning Process for the Mitosis Unit

As Maria and I developed the mitosis unit plan and lessons, we spent a lot of time talking about what students would be doing, how she would keep them engaged, and how she would stay aware of their growth in understanding the concepts. The protocol we developed has nine steps:

- **Step 1:** Identify five to ten big ideas for the whole course.
- **Step 2:** Identify the sequence of topics, concepts, skills, and social issues for the year.
- **Step 3:** Identify the main concepts to be learned in the lesson.
- **Step 4:** Identify the initial idea for the unaided student exploration.
- **Step 5:** Identify the applicable thinking skills and habits of mind.
- **Step 6:** Create a rubric that defines specific levels of student performance—novice, competent, or expert.
- **Step 7:** Create a set of formative assessment questions to guide observations of students in the exploration and clarification phases.
- **Step 8:** Create a series of authentic applications to unique situations.
- **Step 9:** Create facilitating questions.

## Step 1: Identify Five to Ten Big Ideas for the Whole Course

### What We Were Thinking

In the few weeks prior to when Maria and I planned the lesson on mitosis, we had engaged in some discussions about the big ideas that would hold all of the yearlong biology concepts together. We knew that, just as short-term memory can only hold seven or less ideas at one time and effectively use them to make new understandings, students need ten or fewer big ideas around which to integrate all of the concepts they will learn during the year. These big ideas are not topics, but rather overarching concepts where everything else studied becomes just another example of one of these big ideas. The sequence of the course is not organized around these, but some of these are embedded in every unit in the course.

As the term goes on, these big ideas emerge from multiple explorations that students have engaged in, and during the concept clarification section of the lessons, the teacher explicitly points these out as central to the discipline. Eventually, all of these ideas are addressed and the teacher keeps referring back to them so that all the neuronal connections of new ideas created by students are strengthened. Because these are the key organizing structures of the concepts in the course, students will also begin to say, “Oh look, this is just another example of… (one of the big ideas).”

After much discussion among a number of science teacher colleagues, Maria had identified five big ideas that she thought were rich enough, important enough, and that would allow everything studied during the year to fall under one or more of them. After some discussion, we expanded some, condensed others, and clearly stated each of them in language that students in her class could, and would, use. I have done this with many scientists and teachers over the years, and each time, a consensus is reached that closely approximates what Maria and her colleagues had created with just a little nudging from me. There is no right answer to what the big ideas are for a course, they just need to match the criteria in italics in the first paragraph in this section.

Maria identified the following five big ideas for the unit:

- 3. **Ecosystems:** Populations of various organisms and non-living elements in particular geographic areas are interdependent upon one another, and the system maintains equilibrium when external factors change the balance (external balance).
