In fact, students that struggle most with heterogeneous groups and push to move at a faster pace are almost always those with the greatest social-emotional needs, and who have the most to learn about their own deficits and the importance of collaboration in a team. These students often benefit the most and soon learn that, while they are advanced in certain areas, they have a lot to gain from the other members of their group. In the Mystery Powders Unit, this often manifests in surprising ways. For instance, the most capable scientists in the group typically take charge early on, helping to develop potential tests and hypothesizing on the identify of the different powders. At the end of the unit, however, as we work to summarize our data into investigation reports, which we call scientific papers, students with writing and organizational skills often stand out. Every year, I find that students who struggle most with the vocabulary acquisition and complex concepts always bring some prior experience that is invaluable to the project. For example, students who have worked with a parent on fixing machines often know ways to use tools or develop methodological protocols that are well beyond the skill set of their more academic peers.

# Inquiry Planning Process

The natural modification of working in small groups dovetails nicely with the process of planning an inquiry-based curriculum. As new standards, particularly the Next Generation Science Standards (Achieve 2013) have made explicit, there are a myriad of components to incorporate into each lesson—science thinking skills, to habits of mind, to crosscutting concepts, and core ideas. Consequently, I often feel overwhelmed when beginning the planning process for a year or even a unit. Inquiry-based teaching has lightened the stress level dramatically. I have seen in countless ways how important skills and ideas naturally spiral throughout the various branches of science. This has meant, for me, that I can be okay when only half of my students internalize the concept of precision in measurement and the nature of significant digits, because these ideas will crop up again when we apply them in later units on technology or biology. Each inquiry lesson is so dense with relevant standards and potential learning moments that my job becomes less *what can I get them to learn,* and more *how can I focus their attention on the central ideas* while letting some students develop only peripheral understandings that we circle back to in a later unit.

This realization has led me to add new fields to my unit-planning document. I use the backwards-planning template from *Understanding by Design* (Wiggins and McTighe 2005), but have found that the initial box for lesson goals and standards is too general. Instead, I differentiate core standards and ideas from those that I am spiraling from previous units, as well as others that I am introducing for the first time.
