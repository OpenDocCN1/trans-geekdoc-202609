The Process Skills and Science, Society and, Technology (PSST) test of process skills in science and abilities in understanding Science, Technology and Society (STS) relationships revealed that 41 percent of the BONGO group increased their science reasoning skills and STS awareness over the course of one term as compared to nine percent within the control group (Jablon 1989).

How was this possible? What were the components and activities that changed the behavior of a vast majority of these adolescents within a three-month period of time? We need to go back and revisit the section in Chapter 1 about what really matters to teenagers and the congruence of that with Maslow’s hierarchy of human needs. Most students who are disengaged from school have the same needs as typical teenagers, but some of those needs are poorly met. If the deficiencies are not met, students cannot focus while in school. However, it is possible to simultaneously meet these needs and engage students in quality inquiry teaching and learning. It is a matrix of components that make this possible. Many teachers have used one or more of these components in their teaching but it is the synergy of the matrix that allows these students a successful engagement with school. Before looking at the whole matrix, let us build it one component at a time. The components are (1) interdisciplinary units, (2) project-based learning, (3) meaningful themes, (4) issues of social conscience, and (5) final public projects.

# Interdisciplinary Units

> > “Life decisions are interdisciplinary, but schools teach separate subjects.”
> > —Dodd 1984

A review of the literature demonstrates a call for interdisciplinary education to support the Common Core even though most school practitioners have not embraced it (Heitin 2013; Mazzara 2014). Teachers as well as students suffer from the narrow organization of education. Creating only a narrow focus on specialized content actually diminishes comprehension of the content itself. There are aspects of a subject that are unnoticed or invisible until we view them from a distance or from a perspective of another subject. Significant knowledge is naturally interdisciplinary (Ortega y Gasset 1957).

As noted in the previous chapter, those schools employing effective middle school practices with early adolescents have teachers on teams who are meeting almost daily to sustain interdisciplinary units throughout the year. Likewise, effective elementary teachers rarely teach their subjects separately but use science or social studies activities as the topics for their nonfiction or fiction texts to teach language arts skills or use appropriate math skills during their science activities.
