## Get the Community Involved as Much as Possible in the Classroom and Vice Versa

When students see a disconnect between their life outside and inside school, their engagement plummets. The more students can use their out of school lives as grist for their in school assignments, the more it all starts to come together for them. When we practice persuasive writing and argumentation, I ask my students to identify real people they want to try and persuade (their parents, their boyfriend or girlfriend, the school principal, the president), and then address their argument to those individuals. We write letters to the editor of the local newspaper, or policy advice to local elected representatives—and then we send them off! One of my students’ perennial favorite activities involves sending compliment and complaint letters to local or national businesses. Quite frequently, the companies write back with gift cards and free samples. This process makes the question of what makes for an effective, persuasive argument for a given audience come alive.

Involving the community as an audience, or even as *assessors*, of student work, has also proved highly successful as a student motivator. I always invite parents, other class’s teachers and students, administrators and community members to our project fairs. It allows my students to know that their work will have an authentic audience beyond just me, and it strengthens ties and lines of communications with other stakeholders in the community. That community contains experts in a variety of fields. Involving a professional as co-teacher and co-evaluator has become a hallmark of my projects ever since I saw an Edutopia video where a math teacher enlisted the volunteer services of a local architect in helping her students use geometry to design hypothetical new school buildings. ## Andrea Egitto’s Reflection

*Andrea shares how experience in an inner-city public school inspired her to use inquiry teaching throughout her 15-year career. This experience provides the context for her first-grade history lesson presented in Chapter 2.*

I began during my first year of teaching using an inquiry approach mostly in science with fourth-grade students in the Brownsville section of Brooklyn. It was a challenging class of students who came from an area of high crime, drug dealing, and a lot of instability in their homes.

The inquiry all started with two gerbils that began as class pets. We had one tank with one male and one female gerbil. I soon realized that I could engage these students if the lessons focused on these gerbils. Students quickly became enthralled in the life cycle of the gerbils when in the first month they had their first litter of seven offspring.

Although these students were in fourth grade, many were reading way below grade level. Having them work with district-provided reading materials was a painful experience for both them and me. However, when I purchased books from a pet store about gerbils, written probably at an eighth-grade level, many of them would work diligently and persist until they found their answers. Almost everyone was happy to work on their writing skills if they could write about the gerbils. We wrote observations, letters to pet stores and parents.

I soon realized that for them to all be regularly engaged in explorations that would lead to understandings and skills in science, math, and language arts, they needed to be working in small groups of four. The prolific gerbils obliged, and we soon had enough gerbils for seven tanks with two gerbils in each so that each group had their own pair. This was still a tough class to teach, but things that they would previously resist doing in language arts and math, they would now work on, if they first could explore with the gerbils.

The natural progression of my teaching led me to be trained in the workshop model for language arts. By having hundreds of fiction and nonfiction trade books in my classroom at students’ independent reading levels, my students could choose the books they wanted to read. They worked with reading partners to analyze and discuss what they were reading. In writer’s workshop, students chose their topics and learned to critique one another’s work, like great writers do. They shared their work with the class and their partners to get feedback, and then revised and edited their work before they published it for the world to see.

Many years later, I attended some problem solving, inquiry teaching math workshops for three summers. These caught my interest right away because we used the data from swinging pendulums and many other hands-on activities as a way to figure out a solution without the instructor telling us. We also did a lot with geometry and explorations with shapes and figures that led us, as adults, to many mathematical understandings we did not get as students during our own school years.

However, if the school I have been teaching in for the past 10 years had not had the NSF nationally validated math curriculum that was already inquiry based, I could not have devised all the explorations that students can do so they own the math concept or skill even if they forget the algorithm. They know how and when to use their understandings because, “you always know something about the problem you are trying to solve.”

This might be the solution.” In first grade, we want them to memorize number combinations to ten, but then they explore larger numbers on their own, first using materials, trying number combinations.
