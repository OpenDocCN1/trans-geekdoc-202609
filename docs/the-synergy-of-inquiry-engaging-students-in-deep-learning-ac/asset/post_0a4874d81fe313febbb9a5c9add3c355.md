
## Issues of Social Conscience

Cecelia Cullen was the principal of Middle College High School for a good part of the time that Terry and I ran the BONGO Program there. Because of her ability to allow teachers to create programs in her school that met the needs of students, we were able to continually tweak what we were doing and try new components in order to respond to student insights into what worked for them. In an interview with her about what she thought contributed to the success of BONGO that appeared in a documentary about the program (Jablon and Born 1989), she noted, “One of the strongest parts of the program is that it deals with social issues. These appeal to teenagers’ wishes and needs to create a perfect world and to contribute to making that world a reality.”

In the BONGO Program, a majority of the time students wrote and toured original plays that were dramatic manifestations of the *theme* that they wanted the audience to wrestle with. Other times they created and published a community newspaper or a combined photography-literary magazine through which they could express their understandings that evolved during the academic aspects of the interdisciplinary program. Adolescents, especially those who are disenfranchised from school, cannot resist becoming enthralled with issues relating to fairness, especially the distinction between equal and equitable.

> If we allow students to role play selected real life situations between these individuals or groups that are analogous to interrelationships in social studies, science, or literature situations, then students not only *experience* this larger complex relationship but simultaneously *understand* related emotional needs.
>
> (Jablon 2010, 3)

## Analogous Interrelationship Role Plays

For the past 40 years I have observed adolescents in a wide range of classes. For some of these classes I was the teacher, but since 1990 I have been fortunate enough to have the opportunity to be an outside observer of a cross section of classes in various disciplines, in both urban and suburban locations, and in small non-traditional schools and large fairly traditional schools. In each of these classrooms there were at least three or four students who were completely disengaged from any learning activities no matter how much the inquiry learning and interdisciplinary format was used. If we multiply these students by the number of classes in the school, this number becomes substantial. In many urban schools this number was much higher.

During the 21 years—1970–1990 and 2001–2002—that I taught adolescents, together with my colleagues and our students, we created an approach to truly engage these disengaged students in learning, and in becoming full participants in our community.

The concepts students are to acquire, whether in social studies, science, technology, or in the relationships of characters in novels, are universal relationships between individuals and groups, individuals and other individuals, or between groups and groups. They are in many ways based on the same psychological and sociological foundations as the relationships these students are having with their peers and their parents, the two primary areas of concern to adolescents.

Once these needs are met, students will not only engage in the study of the analogous societal relationships, but will delve into the deepest esoteric questions that are posed. In addition, they will acquire conflict resolution skills and coping mechanisms that they can utilize in their everyday interpersonal relationships. The personalization of the subject matter by having students investigate these analogous interrelationships between their own personal interrelationships and those that exist at a societal level is a central element of the most successful of these programs (Jablon 1989). My partner in team teaching for 13 years, Terry Born, taught me this technique of role plays and together we enriched it to become Analogous Interrelationship Role Plays as described here.

## Example of an Analogous Interrelationship Role Play

Three students assume the roles of the following characters:

- Fifteen-year-old high school student who must work long hours to add to her family's income because a parent has recently died
- Student’s teacher who is upset by the drop in quality of the student’s work in her class
- Student’s parent who is caught between wanting the best for the student and the rest of the family’s welfare

This role play has allowed classes of students studying about the cutting of the rainforests to understand the complex relationship between the poor native inhabitants cutting the rainforests (the student), the government of the third world country who is encumbered with a large international debt (the parent), and the environmentalists trying to help both the native and the future world ecological situation (the teacher). Students not only had a unique insight into world economics but into their own personal relationships and therefore identified positively with their academic classes.

If the psychological needs of students are met as they are engaged in activities with the other students in the societal class, then a positive alternative peer group is created that students can go to as an alternative to their negative neighborhood peer groups where they normally turn in their search for fulfillment.

This method of personalizing subject matter in a single-subject classroom has limitations even though the societal format facilitates the ease with which it can be integrated. Teachers become frustrated with the amount of single-subject academic concepts that cannot be addressed because certain of the issues are better developed in the arenas of other subject areas. If subject areas—science and language arts, science and social studies, math and social studies, or all four—are integrated and taught in a truly interdisciplinary manner, then the personalization mechanism supports completely the areas being studied.

## Final Public Projects

A final public project, the culminating experience, for students working on an interdisciplinary unit is intended to provide the opportunity to address an issue of their interest that draws from their academic experience. It provides students with the opportunity to apply, integrate, and synthesize knowledge and experience obtained during their academic course of study to a question or problem of public relevance.

Final public projects can take various forms depending on the issues students have investigated, for example, presentations to community governing boards, public service announcements, or brochures to assist community agencies or prompt civic participation in community initiatives such as curbside recycling. Students can assess school lunch programs and make proposals to school and community agencies to help improve them. They can create community gardens or help elementary teachers engage their students in reading and science projects. (Even elementary students themselves can create informational signage for trails through local conservation areas.) Students can monitor local ecosystems in conjunction with local environmental protection agencies. They can research town histories, make scale models of the town over decades or centuries, make videos of historical events and show them on public access television channels, and engage senior citizens in writing their own oral histories. They can build and finance local playgrounds, skate parks, vest-pocket parks. This is just a brief description of the myriad of projects represented in these teacher-designed units.

Do an online search for examples of teacher-designed interdisciplinary units. You will find an infinite number of public projects that allow adolescent students to demonstrate their *competence* to their peers and adults in their community in a manner that is of consequence to them. Meeting this Maslow *esteem* need, while studying academics, allows those previously disengaged from school to prosper, and those regularly engaged to fly.

Most teachers after first doing project-based learning where the projects deal with real issues soon move to public presentation of projects. They do so because meeting the needs of students to demonstrate their competence in a public forum exponentially increases the motivational power of the projects.
