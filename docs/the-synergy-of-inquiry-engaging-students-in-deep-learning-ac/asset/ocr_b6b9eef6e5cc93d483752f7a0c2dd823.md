Teachers are not alone in their concern about interpersonal interactions. Surveys show that business leaders have similar concerns about worker interaction (Weissberg and Cascarino 2013). On surveys, they report that graduates often lack writing and math skills, but their number one concern is a lack of ability to work effectively as a member of a team. It does not matter whether it is an engineering team, a design team, a team at a fast food restaurant, or a middle-school team of teachers. Actually many schools, though not intentionally designed this way, appear to be structured to teach students to compete and *not* assist one another in learning.

In our work with teachers on facilitating conflict resolution activities with their students, we find that invariably they mention how their own interpersonal relationships with their significant others improve if they practice and utilize the conflict resolution skills themselves. The most candid often point out that the faculty and administration in their school could use some work on these skills as well.

I have come to view these abilities as Social Interpersonal Habits of Mind (SIHM). In other words, these are skills and insights about group and interpersonal interaction that need to become part of the fabric of thinking of all individuals and can be utilized every day of their lives, in all settings.

# Defining Group Roles

In earlier chapters, I made the case that students create their understandings and negotiate their ideas when they can have a voice in a small group of four while they engage in an exploration. Such group learning will not be successful unless students, whether in first or twelfth grade, learn *how* to work with one another successfully. To do so, they need structured, explicit experiences with the elements that contribute to effective cooperative interaction. When you overtly teach them how to effectively cooperate, cooperative learning occurs. Students are doing two things simultaneously: (1) learning academic content concepts and thinking skills, and (2) learning the skills necessary to be effective group members.

I keep mentioning groups of four—what is so special about four? Why not create groups of five or three? With more than four students in a group, someone will not be heard and will be left out. Many teachers have found that larger groups create discipline problems, as students’ attention waivers. In addition, many times a pair of students is all that can focus and communicate on a given exploration, so you do not need to move chairs if they are already sitting in groups of four. It is so easy to pair students up without wasting time. The two pairs at a table will overhear each other’s discussion, which is a good thing, since it allows for a richer discussion. However, just having them seated appropriately will not teach them what effective group members do. Therefore, it is necessary to assign students a role in their group. This role changes daily, so they get to practice the thinking and interpersonal skills needed to accomplish each role.
