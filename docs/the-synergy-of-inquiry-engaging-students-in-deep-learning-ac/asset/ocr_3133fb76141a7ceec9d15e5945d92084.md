# # Protocol for Transforming Lessons to Inquiry Teaching

As noted in Chapter 1, many activities teachers have traditionally used serve as confirmation for students after they have been *told about* some conceptual understanding, historical event, writing or reading skill, or strategy for problem solving. We have come to understand that students need to *construct* knowledge if they are to have any deep understanding and retention of concepts, or insight into the nature of inquiry in an academic area. They need to first explore the phenomenon through some type of inquiry exploration that allows them to negotiate ideas with their teammates, construct naïve conceptions of the phenomenon, and especially, identify and relate the factors relevant to the phenomenon. Then they are ready to hear what the larger academic community knows about the phenomenon under study. While doing this, students also acquire thinking skills and problem-solving skills that empower them as learners for a lifetime.

However, many times teachers have used lessons that have some useful elements and wish they would not have to abandon these lessons completely. These educators would like to *transform* these confirmation experiences into inquiry explorations. Unfortunately, making the change is not that simple, and the exploration portion is only one part of the process.

Educators need to take the whole didactic package of lecture and confirmation experience and transform it into an inquiry teaching and learning experience; where explorations that are focused on concepts, thinking skills, and habits of mind are commingled with negotiation, construction, and introduction of models that describe the phenomenon. These explorations by small groups of students, in which they create understandings and negotiate ideas, must come first. In science and mathematics, these almost always involve manipulating physical materials or model making. In social studies, role-playing, comparing primary source documents, or interviewing real people are typical activities. Role-playing, comparing various types of literature, and writing in various modalities for specific purposes are familiar activities in language arts classes.

Although inquiry teaching is an iterative process where when you get to a later step you often go back and modify the first step, teachers have found the progression of steps in this protocol to be useful.
