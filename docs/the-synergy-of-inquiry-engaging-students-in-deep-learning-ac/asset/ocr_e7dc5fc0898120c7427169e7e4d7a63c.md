# The Utility of Rubrics for Teachers: They Keep You Honest

The most effective teachers I have observed and spoken with have all expressed their deeply held understanding that they teach students, not subject areas. They also understand that they need to have a deep mastery of whatever subject area they are teaching so that they know how to create appropriate explorations that allow students to own the understandings and skills in that subject. Teachers are so invested in each individual student that sometimes it becomes difficult on a daily basis to separate out the personalities of students from their current mastery of a concept or skill.

By creating rubrics for each lesson with very rich entries in each cell, teachers can remind themselves of the difference between a student who is competent and has truly mastered a concept or skill and a novice student who, despite being a wonderful kid, is still working toward mastery. Likewise, it allows teachers to look with some objectivity at the somewhat difficult child and realize that he or she has mastered the material despite possibly having the teacher at wits end.

Rubrics keep teachers focused on what level of mastery every student should be aiming for. Some students enter the class having mastered some or all of a concept or skill that is the focus of the lesson. These students need to be enriched with a higher or tangential investigation using the same exploration that the rest of the students are doing that day. The teacher has planned this extension ahead of time and created the expert category in the rubric. This assures parents that although their child is in a heterogeneous group, the teacher is differentiating appropriately for each level of mastery. In the mitosis lesson, Maria had questions ready for her expert students about spindle fibers and cell size that could be answered with the same materials but were not required understandings of all students.

Another issue that can arise is that once teachers hear the expert-level students speaking about their insights, it becomes too easy to unintentionally expect the rest of the students to get to this level. This is true despite the original appropriate objective the teacher wrote in her or his plan. By having the competent and expert categories next to one another in the rubric, the teacher does not frustrate students who are not yet ready for expert understandings. It also allows the teacher to create appropriate assessments for the competent objectives expectations, and not to think of themselves as ineffective because their students have not all mastered the expert level.

Just as with all the other parts of a plan, the cells in rubrics get richer and more useful as they are revised because teachers are paying close attention to student dialogue. Each time teachers better understand the intricacies that differentiate the categories of mastery from one another, they are then able to add to the important details in the cells.
