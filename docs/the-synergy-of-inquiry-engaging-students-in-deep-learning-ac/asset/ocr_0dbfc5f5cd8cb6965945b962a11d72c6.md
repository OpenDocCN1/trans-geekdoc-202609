# Student Observations of Stages of Mitosis

When students returned the next day, they discussed their thoughts in small research teams of four for about 10 minutes. When they reported their ideas back to the class and used their drawings to defend their thinking, it only took about another 10 minutes of negotiations for the larger scientific community—the class—to come to consensus. They agreed that the steps were as follows:

+   1. The center part (the nucleus) transformed from a solid mass into a swirl of strands.
+   2. These strands then lined up in pairs across the center and two sets became apparent.
+   3. Then the sides of the cell pushed in next to the strands and the strands were pulled to each side of the cell and the walls kept pushing in until there were two new cells.

Students figured that each new cell now had the same “nuclear stuff” as the original cell. Some kept insisting that it cloned itself (this actually was a rather conceptually accurate description).

Since the class concluded at this point, Maria assigned students two tasks for homework:

+   1. Read the section in their text on mitosis, and compare their findings and understandings with those of the larger worldwide scientific community. What was the same between their findings and what the scientific community now accepted? They also needed to note what was different and why the rest of the world knew something they did not, if that was the case.
+   2. Write a report of their findings using the format that all scientists in the world use to have their evidence accepted as valid. Although somewhat similar to a traditional lab report, this report was unique: students designed some of the procedure, they evolved all of the theories, and in the conclusions, they compared their findings to what the larger worldwide community knows.

In their published reports, students demonstrated some deep understandings across the class population. They now had the universal names for each of the stages (prophase, anaphase, and so on) of their drawings that they got from their readings and the Internet animated simulations. More importantly, most students also appeared to own the concept objectives.
