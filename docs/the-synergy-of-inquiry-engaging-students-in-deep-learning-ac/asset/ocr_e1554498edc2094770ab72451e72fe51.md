+   - Students first record their ideas for procedures to use in the exploration and then work with team members to create a group procedure.
+   - Students articulate these ideas verbally, modifying their understanding, syntactical structures, and sometimes the vocabulary as a result of verbal interaction with team members.
+   - The teacher cruises the room, checking levels of understanding in groups and facilitates the direction of each group through fairly open and not-too-leading questions.
+   - The teacher also asks individual students to verbalize their understandings and then asks them to write down these ideas in their notebooks, paying particular attention to see if students include important syntactical structures in their writing. For example, "if-then" phrases occur in everyday speech and writing but take on a special significance in academic thinking and communication.
+   - Team members create naïve conceptual understandings about the topic under investigation, enrich their thinking skills, and may be influenced by habits of mind of other team members.
+   - During this process, students are practicing clearly communicating their ideas, utilizing language structures that they hear from team members, and organizing their exploration by creating a sequenced oral, and then written, description. This non-threatening small group modeling of language, and the subsequent chance to rehearse this language, is especially important for second language learners (Krashen 1982).
+   - While moving from group to group, teachers assess student understandings and skills and their abilities to communicate those skills both verbally, and in rough form in their notebooks. This is also formative assessment for conceptual learning, and prompts appropriate and differentiated language and conceptual instruction for the next day.
+   - Teachers urge all students to write their understandings so far in a fairly shorthand, but syntactically accurate, manner.

# Step 3: Concept Clarification—The teacher facilitates a more logical and richer creation of meaning.

+   - *Reporters* from student research teams report back to the larger class community. This early process of *meaning making from our results so far* allows students on a team to begin organizing and synthesizing ideas, data, and inferences, and making preliminary conclusions based on evidence from explorations in progress with this activity students begin in their practice of learning and utilizing *strategies* for organizing ideas into a logical argument.
+   - Students from other teams are critical friends, seeking evidence for claims and procedural descriptions for explanations that contradict their own.
