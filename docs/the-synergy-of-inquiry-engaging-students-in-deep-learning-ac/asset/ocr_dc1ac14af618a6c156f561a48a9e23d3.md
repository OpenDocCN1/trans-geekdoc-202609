I adapted a *prezi* lesson using Pixar Short Films as a means of teaching short story elements. After explaining specific terms, we watched a Pixar Short Film (Pixar 2012) and applied those literary terms. Because there is often no dialogue in the Pixar Short Films, they are particularly useful for teaching about inferring character traits and other story elements. Furthermore, the work did not involve reading text, and so students who were less confident in their reading abilities were not intimidated. Their homework that night was to identify the elements in a Pixar Short Film that we had not watched in class. That is where choice came in. I posted nine links to various films and students could watch and reflect on any of them for their homework. The next day, students came in discussing the videos. I grouped them by the video they watched. They went over what they thought about characterization. Students were engaged and made insightful judgments when it came to direct and indirect characterization. They had successfully applied the terms to their own video. What is more, their motivation was apparent as I listened to them sell their video to students in the class, encouraging others to watch the video they had selected. Once we began reading a short story, they all had confidence in their ability to identify indirect and direct characterization, and furthermore to recognize when they were making inferences about characterization within the text. This lesson supported inclusion by utilizing choice, technology, and small group work which led to all ninth graders not only being engaged, but learning and using elements of short stories.

# Eleventh-Grade British Literature: *Frankenstein* Mock Trial

An example of how I use choice as a final assessment is drawn from my *Frankenstein* unit in eleventh grade. For this unit, I used multiple assessments: an exam, a writing assignment, and a project. The project portion was a mock trial. Students were randomly assigned roles (lawyer or witness) on the prosecution or defense team, but they had the option of switching roles with a willing peer. Once the teams were finalized, students began working as a unit with a shared goal. Each team had roughly four lawyers and four witnesses. The teams had to either defend or prosecute Frankenstein, who is charged with criminal negligent manslaughter for having created and then promptly abandoning a creature who went on to murder several innocent people. I provided students with computers, and they created a Google Doc for their own team. This allowed individual students to not only work specifically with their witness/lawyer, but also to work collaboratively as a unit. Students wrote their questions in the Google Doc, and everyone on the team could view and edit them. This was great because members of the team aided students who were not as confident or capable.
