Next, students are asked to see if they can come to consensus in their small groups about how they would like to see whole-class discussions occur. After a reasonable amount of time, the teacher calls the whole class together so that each group can put forth one idea at a time and see if the rest of the class can come to agreement about each idea. These ideas usually include features such as the following:

+   - listening when someone else is speaking
+   - not interrupting someone else when he or she is speaking
+   - expecting people to look at the person who is speaking
+   - not making derogatory remarks about what someone has said
+   - expecting everybody to participate, although not necessarily in every discussion

Usually there are long discussions and negotiations about whether students need to raise their hand in order to speak or whether this action takes away from the flow of discussion. This consideration usually occurs in middle and high school classrooms, although some elementary classrooms have dealt with this issue as well. If hand-raising does not occur, then many times strategies such as “the person who just finished speaking will call on the next speaker” are suggested.

Once the class has come to its best consensus on a list of strategies and structures that it can agree to, the teacher posts the list. In lower-grade classes, many times students all sign this post, so it is clear that they understand their responsibilities. In upper-grade classes, students verbally agree to abide by the list. No matter what the list, within a couple of weeks, students will encounter situations that arise where the ideas on the list do not make for the best student interaction. At this point, either the teacher or students in the class can ask for a class meeting to be called to negotiate modifications. Once there is buy-in by students, and academic activities are engaging, the teacher no longer needs to be the police officer, and students will begin to self-manage one another. This process is the basis for a democratic classroom. It sets up an experiential structure that leads students to become effective participatory citizens in their local, state, and national communities.

Of course all of these devised approaches need to be in compliance with whole-school regulations, unless exceptions are negotiated in advance with the principal. You may already have noticed that I have avoided the use of the word *rules*. It is a word that comes with a world of connotations and baggage, and some students who think something is a good strategy, may defy the idea just because it is a rule. There generally needs to be some bottom line, unacceptable behaviors that have an immediate consequence.
