# Meaningful Themes

In candid conversations with teachers about what prompted them to enter the profession, I frequently observe that they share a common concern: to have students acquire some central core understandings about life that will enable them to lead happy, fruitful, and productive lives. These understandings tend to be some big, universal ideas. If teachers are working with adolescents, these ideas tend to be at the core of the complex issues that we all deal with as adults. They also are the ideas that are at the core of teenagers’ dealings with one another, with their parents, and with other adults in authority. Thinking back to Maslow and what adolescents care about, these are ideas that will help them deal with the complexity of life from their perspective. Teachers will mention such ideas as, “many times you cannot control what happens to you, but you can control how you react to it.”

Yet these same teachers note that they rarely get to teach about these ever so important insights into dealing productively in our complex human societies. Occasionally language arts and social studies teachers can touch upon them, but they never really get to visit the issues in any depth. When students get to explore one of these understandings from multiple disciplinary perspectives and then use this understanding in creating something that has an impact on their community, the theme becomes a powerful component of school success.

This view of what constitutes a theme is different than what you find in educational books and in discussions with some teachers. In many cases, teachers are urged to integrate their lessons around a topic. Take for example ancient Egypt, the fishing industry in New England, recycling plastics, or small family-owned farms. It is surely possible to create interdisciplinary units around these, but these are topics—not effective themes—in the way that we are addressing them here. Teachers find that using topics as the integrating issue limits what they can do in each of the subject areas in addition to not allowing the opportunity to engage students in these large generalizable life lesson ideas. Many very effective units will have these and other topics as part of the unit, but they are not the theme. A theme is a complex universal idea with certain qualities that allows for a meaningful integration of the various disciplines.

Over three decades of working with teachers on creating effective interdisciplinary thematic units, my colleagues and I have added to an initial set of criteria first compiled by Martinello and Cook (2000) and created components for an effective theme. Figure 5.1 presents guiding questions to evaluate a theme. Using these criteria, countless teachers have created very effective themes for the units they have developed. Such units have engaged many previously disengaged students in their schools.
