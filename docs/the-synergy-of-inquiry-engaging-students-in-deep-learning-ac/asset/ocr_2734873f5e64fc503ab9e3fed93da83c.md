It slowly began to dawn on me, lecturing was hard work and nobody seemed to be enjoying it, neither me, nor my students. However, I did find myself enjoying the conversations and behaviors that they engaged in when they were working together. I enjoyed creating those experiences for them. As a result, lecturing began to occupy an increasingly small percentage of my total class time. It was also around this time that I began to experiment with essential questions in a meaningful way.

I began to blend technology and group work together in meaningful mini assignments; it worked. I gradually expanded the mini assignments in scope by adding an essential question as the driving force for the activity. My seventh and eighth graders started having difficulties. It was too difficult for any one student in general to deal with the combined tasks of comprehending the nature of the assignment (challenge or exploration), researching relevant information, processing information, taking a perspective, crafting a series of outcomes, and creating a final deliverable. Although I experimented with breaking these assignments into small chunks, the most success I had, and continue to have, is from ensuring that students have the ability to learn from each other and with each other, and only sometimes, alone.

As I reflect on the aforementioned and the way my classes operate today, several rules of thumb have emerged that I believe work, at least with my student populations:

+   1. Clarify what the task is and be very clear about your expectations. Help students discover what they may need to know. Allow them almost complete freedom in determining how to get it.
+   2. Use technology that works. The five-year-old computer with a fuzzy Internet connection is a bad idea.
+   3. Encourage flexible grouping structures where sometimes students will work with others, and other times will work alone. This does require a great deal of finesse to ensure that students stay on task while you are not being the teacher who constantly hovers around.
+   4. Do not step in too early; let students sort it out by themselves or with their peers first.
+   5. At the end of each activity (mine tend to last from one to two weeks) be prepared to orchestrate a discussion with the whole class about lessons learned. Highlight the practices they should or should not be focused on going forward.
