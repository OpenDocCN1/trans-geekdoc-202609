
This synergy is explained by a similarity in thinking skills across the subject areas. If students use similar skills as they are engaged in figuring out ideas in each of the subject areas, this reinforcement throughout the school day will cause the neural networks associated with them to become hardwired.

The processes for creating knowledge differ across subjects; for example, what makes something acceptable knowledge in science is different than what makes something acceptable knowledge in literature as demonstrated earlier in this chapter. Nonetheless, there are similarities in the knowledge creating processes. All subjects require evidence, though where the evidence comes from and how it is validated may be somewhat different. In each area, the difference between observation and inference is vital, but what makes for fruitful inference and when it is advisable to use it is different in each individual academic area. However, the thinking skills are analogous and need to be reinforced in each area, so they become a habit of mind. The differences also need to become part of the neuronal connections, as students need to know when to use the distinctions in their daily lives. This rationale also presents yet another reason for carefully planned and facilitated interdisciplinary studies as part of each student’s school week. In many inner-city schools and some rural areas, significant numbers of students have needs that are not fulfilled. In these situations, school practices need to be modified to fulfill student needs. Doing remedial and low level work is not the answer to this situation. If a school is not as successful as it would like to be, a change in the way students are engaged needs to occur. Human brains are designed to learn and do so efficiently. We need to utilize inquiry teaching, analogous interrelationship role-plays, project-based learning, internships, and other ways that will engage students in learning that stimulates their basal centers, the pleasure centers of their brains. These approaches are addressed in more detail in Chapter 5.

## Conclusion

Although the preceding examples of inquiry teaching in various disciplines may sound intriguing, perhaps even exciting, some of you may be getting a little nervous about the approach. Does it seem to reverse the order in which you do things? Does it modify the role you currently play in your classroom? Is there evidence from research studies that demonstrates that students learn more deeply in inquiry-teaching classrooms? Throughout this book you will hear from classroom teachers and principals about their experiences with inquiry teaching. Practical experience was combined with evidence from research because both are important.

Take a moment to briefly address this issue of effectiveness now. “Decades of research illustrate the benefits of inquiry-based and cooperative learning to help students develop the knowledge and skills necessary to be successful in a rapidly-changing world” (Barron and Darling-Hammond et al. 2008, 2). A good place to start looking at the literature about inquiry teaching is in another, more comprehensive book edited by Linda Darling-Hammond et al. (2008) reviewing the research on inquiry teaching. Furthermore, the bonus chapter on the Digital Resource CD will further enrich the understanding that you have begun in this chapter by comparing the inquiry teaching process with what we know from current neurobiology about the processes by which the human brain learns. As this understanding of brain structures and function related to cognition and emotion matches this inquiry-learning format of engaging learners, it makes for a compelling argument for educators to fully embrace inquiry teaching. It is not just a better way for students to excel on Common Core exams, but it is a likely way to eliminate the achievement gap, and to have students succeed in their careers.

We are all aware of how difficult it is to do controlled research studies in real school situations. It is virtually impossible to create comparable groups of classes of students, and then to have the same teacher using two distinct pedagogical approaches for each of these classes is also most difficult to accomplish. Although we have evidence from educational research to defend the effectiveness of inquiry teaching, these studies are muddied by the nature of social science research. When I speak with teachers around the country about inquiry teaching, I can now explain to them how the brain learns in neurological terms
and show how this correlates with inquiry teaching techniques. Now you can also do this, but equally importantly, you can understand what needs to happen in the brains of your students during each phase of the inquiry teaching learning cycle and adjust your practice accordingly. For additional information on brain research, please refer to the Bonus Chapter on the Digital Resource CD.

This chapter builds on information from Chapter 1 about the relationship between inquiry teaching and how the brain learns. It also serves to illustrate the implementation of inquiry teaching and the learning cycle. It contains protocols and templates of inquiry teaching unit plans and lessons that are consistent with what we know about how the brain learns and the nature of child development. You will meet four teachers who share their experiences developing inquiry teaching lessons and unit plans. These individuals represent a range of teaching experiences from novice, to mid-level, to expert.

- Andrea, a first-grade teacher, plans a unit on the history of a local community using primary documents. Andrea’s 22 students are also a diverse group—one student is a non-English speaker and 7 students are on IEPs for autism, language learning issues, and some developmental delays.
