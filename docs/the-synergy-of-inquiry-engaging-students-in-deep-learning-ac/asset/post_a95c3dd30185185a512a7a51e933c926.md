Carolina asks each student to write down which of the boxes that they made in their group holds the most stuff. They then discuss the responses in their groups, explaining why they think their answer is correct and seeing if they can convince the others as to why theirs is correct about how to decide which holds the most, such as filling it with beans or pennies and counting the number it takes to fill them. These are all reasonable responses, but she asks students about the space between the beans and how they will measure that.

Most of the time, one of the students will bring up how when they did surface area of the outside of similar rectangular prisms the outside surfaces had one centimeter grids drawn on them. By asking how this might help them measure what is inside, students will say what they need are little boxes to put inside, that way there will not be spaces between them if they put them in neatly one atop another.

Earlier Carolina used one clear plastic open-topped box so that students could see that when filled with beans, there are spaces between them, but when using the one centimeter cubes that she has, they can fill the space without gaps.

She gives each group a set of cubes to fill their boxes to see which box holds the most. However, she does not give them enough to fill every box, so they need to figure out a way to extrapolate beyond the level that they are at in the unfilled boxes. They soon realize that once they have filled the first layer, they can see how many layers there are in the box by stacking just one cube atop another and multiply the number of layers by how many cubes are in each layer. ## What Carolina Was Thinking

If you look closely at Carolina’s plan you will also see some nice authentic performance assessments where students needed to apply their newly created concept of volume to some unique, real-world situations. She went right to the heart of the application part of the inquiry teaching learning cycle.

Even better, was an example of an extension using the same materials for students who became experts by acquiring the concept long before the other students in their group. They were given a challenge to optimize the volume using the least amount of cardboard (surface area). It was done as a real-life situation where they worked for a company that wanted to save money (and the environment) by using the least amount of materials. This focus really extended their understanding of the relationship between two-dimensional surface area and three-dimensional volume using trial and error to make an optimal relationship between the two.

Parents never need to worry about their children being in a heterogeneous group in Carolina’s class, as students of varying abilities assist one another in figuring out and understanding. Students who are ahead of the curve are also challenged to go where their abilities lead. This differentiation is possible because they are figuring out using concrete materials in small groups.

## Carolina’s Reflection on Inquiry Teaching

Inquiry-based teaching was not my first approach. When I began my first teaching job in 2007, I was given a traditional math textbook. Although it was colorful and had a few interesting activities, most of it was written in the, “I do, you copy, then you do 35 times,” format. If I found it boring, how bored my students must have been!

Slowly but surely, I began incorporating more inquiry-type lessons into the curriculum. I wanted to make things more interesting, more relevant, and more fun. I now try to create explorations to pique student interest. These explorations are ever changing because I continually reflect on and adapt my teaching. I always ask myself, “Is this the best way to engage my students?”

I love project-based, interdisciplinary learning. My ultimate goal as an educator is to facilitate experiences that span multiple aspects of life and involve meaningful, long-term objectives. As teachers, we want our students to be complex thinkers who, instead of compartmentalizing their knowledge, can make connections across the curriculum, to their own lives, and to the world at large.

We also want our students to maturely express their opinions and debate their ideas with their peers. To this end, I facilitate cooperative group work. Developing a classroom of sharers and negotiators has been challenging yet invigorating. I continue to hone all of these facets of my teaching skill set in my seventh year as an educator.

It is not easy. There are days that do not go as planned, and I am left feeling disappointed and discouraged. There are days when carefully thought out lessons fail to ignite the expected interest of my students. There are days that leave me deflated and near tears. On these days, I say to myself what I will now say to you: Please, do not give up. Your students need you. They need an energetic, caring, and thoughtful role model. You can always make tomorrow better than today. They are lucky to have you and, although they may have a funny way of showing it, they appreciate you.

Fortunately, I have seen signs that I am having success. I have heard students groan when the bell rings and class is over because they have to wait another day before continuing our lesson. I have heard students say, “This is fun!” while solving proportions because I created an experience in which they want to find the answers. I have even had students who previously found little satisfaction in their math classes tell me that math is now their favorite class.

## The Planning Process for Eleventh-Grade Language Arts

Katie Houle, a high school English teacher in Chelmsford, Massachusetts, developed five lessons for a unit on gothic literature and Edgar Allan Poe’s poem The Raven. She identified two essential questions for the lessons:

- 1. Why do gothic literature and scary stories resonate with us as readers?
- How exactly do writers make their stories scary? For example, what key devices do writers of gothic literature use to convey an eerie feeling to their readers?
- 2. Can we trust the narrator of every story? Why or why not?

Brief descriptions of Katie’s lessons follow. For a full description of Katie’s unit, “Gothic Literature and ‘The Raven’: An Example of a Five Lesson Inquiry Teaching Unit,” refer to the Digital Resource CD. In each subsequent lesson, the concepts that students have created in previous lessons *spiral* throughout the unit. I use *spiral* here in the way first described by Jerome Bruner (1960) where students begin by creating developmentally appropriate concepts by manipulating materials and then, through further explorations, create and apply iconic and symbolic understandings. Implicit in this design is that those students who have not yet fully mastered particular concepts from previous lessons can revisit them in the new explorations.

Since the language used in gothic literature can seem stilted to students in the 21st century, Katie clearly delineates her genre objectives, but also looks at her larger literature and writing objectives.

She then approaches the understandings she wishes students to uncover by having them first analyze something that is in their current lives and is universally intriguing to most adolescents—scary movies.

Each student works alone, and then in a small group, to uncover what makes for a good scary movie. Through their discussion, students figure out the elements that writers, directors, and cinematographers use to create a scary movie. The reporters from each group share their insights with the whole class and Katie facilitates a lively discussion leading to a categorized set of qualities. She gives the class what the larger academic community considers the qualities inherent in gothic literature, which students then compare and contrast for homework.

The next four lessons have students exploring the life of Edgar Allan Poe and how it might have moved him to write this type of story. They then dissect his poem *The Raven*, utilizing the elements of fear and horror that they created during their first lesson and expanding upon each of the concepts. They delve further into other poetic devices such as onomatopoeia and alliteration that can also be used to create tone.

Finally, they are given an authentic assessment in which they are challenged to write a modern day poem using much of the tone and form of gothic literature, and *The Raven* in particular. They need to illustrate it and present a dramatic reading to the class.

## What Katie Was Thinking

This assignment was designed to accommodate a variety of learning styles while fostering the linguistic intelligence of students. The homework assignments leading up to the final product were designed to facilitate the learning of more concrete sequential learners who benefit from guidelines and requirements. From these assignments, students understand the format and expectations for the piece, which appeal to the straightforward and controlled learning styles of many students.
