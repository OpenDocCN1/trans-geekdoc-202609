Only when I feel we have reached the limitations of their pre-existing knowledge, do I then bring in new terminology or information with any sort of direct instruction, like a lecture.

For example, before reading Studs Terkel's *Working*, I ask my students to reflect in writing or discussion upon jobs that they have held—the challenges, what they were proud of, what they hated, and how it affected their identity. Before beginning our unit on news and journalism, my students role play reporting on a breaking news story, informed only by the scenario description and their own ideas of how various parties (reporters, editors, advertisers) influence a situation. Before examining the courtroom scenes in *To Kill a Mockingbird*, I always poll my kids for what they already know about courtroom procedure from their own lives and exposure to other media, and then, as we read further, have them then test their expectations against what actually happens when Atticus and Mr. Gilmer face off before Judge Taylor in Maycomb.

## Let Students Have as Much Choice and Ownership as Possible

Now that the national standards call for schools to teach students *skills* over particular texts, facts, or products, it is easier than ever to afford students the chance to engage in a way that suits their learning styles and preferences, as well as their background knowledge.

I frequently engage student groups in literature circles, where students select their own texts (either from our class library or of their own choosing entirely). Sometimes the basis for this choice is convergence with a theme we are studying, or sometimes we are practicing a certain skill (e.g., close reading and textual analysis, or learning and applying vocabulary) where nearly any choice of text will suffice. For some students, this is a chance to read about topics they have always been interested in but have never found the time to investigate. For others, it might be the first engagement with the whole idea of reading a book they have chosen, for pleasure. Nearly always, students have opportunities to engage in discussion with their peers. In this way, they reinforce the universality of what we study and enrich all of our knowledge by drawing in more sources than I ever could have found on my own.

This is a kind of differentiated instruction, which has long been best practice for working with heterogeneous groups (as all classes inevitably are). I also differentiate by assessment method, as students will often be able to demonstrate knowledge and mastery of skills better through some types of assessments than through others. On events from a novel, I usually have them split up these tasks and then *clue in* their classmates. Individual students within a group will often choose different short stories or articles on the same theme, and then jigsaw back together to teach one another about what they read. The next day, a quiz will hold all students in the group responsible for knowing ideas from all of the stories. Sometimes I collect only one quiz from an entire group, and students do not know whose quiz will be used to grade all of them; therefore, it is in their best interests to divide up work and help everyone else out. Any one of them could end up being *the important one* for their grade. After quizzes, if one of my students performs poorly, it is the team’s job to help them correct their errors. We do peer editing of writing, particularly on simple, well-defined issues like comma usage, integration of quotations, or transition sentences.

Whenever a student gets stuck on an issue of knowledge or comprehension, they know that their first job is to ask a group mate. If the whole group is stumped, they need to ask members of another group, and/or look online. This has several benefits:

- 1. It reinforces the idea that, in a community of learners, we are all responsible for one another’s education. And it does not take up precious teacher time and resources.
- 2. It engages multiple students in the responsibility for one’s own learning, either as seeker or provider of knowledge.
- 3. Finally, it helps students develop the vital tasks of self-advocacy and independence—when out in the real world, they will not always be dependent upon a singular authority figure for answers.

As they work, I help facilitate and advise them about the *process*, rather than filling in the answers that stump them, except as an ultimate last resort, or if students are barking so far up the wrong tree that I feel they need a nudge back.

The most exciting parts of group work, however, go beyond the knowledge and comprehension levels. Because so much of the work that we do in my classroom is project based, students are frequently working in groups on a long-term task, such as providing an annotated analysis guide to a novel, creating a print or video advertising campaign, or writing and producing a modern retelling and staging of a Shakespeare play. Here it is even more vital for students to have defined roles (e.g., notetaker, discussion leader, and video editor). Sometimes I assign them these roles, but usually I give them the choice based on their interests and abilities. Sometimes I even have students define for themselves what the different roles will be, checking in to ensure the roles require equal or equitable amounts of work, or to remind them of questions they have not yet asked. One of the most successful lessons in my toolbox is asking students to create a study guide for the complex texts we read, dividing up responsibility for plot, character analysis, finding and explicating metaphors, and more. Instead of just diving into the Internet cheat sites when faced with a challenging text, they now have to collectively create their own, and in doing so, not only help one another decode and discuss the book, but also figure out what makes for an effective and valuable study guide in the first place.

## Reward Questions and Privilege Them over Answers

Higher-order thinking questions—those that call upon application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation—do not have simple, fill in the correct bubble kind of answers. Much of the work I do with my students involves helping them to understand how to ask questions like these, and I give them a great deal of practice in creating them. Whenever we start a new unit, I always poll students for what sort of questions they have about the topic at hand, and keep up that polling process as we go. Most class discussions begin with student-generated prompts, and particularly provocative or discussion-generating questions get rewarded on my whiteboard’s Hall of Fame section. By the time we get ready for our summative assessments, it is students who should be asking the questions.

This is the core of inquiry. At minimum, I want students to draft, debate, develop, and employ their own approaches to problems, such as how to create an effective advertising campaign or how to make Shakespeare relevant to their own lives. At best, I want them to come up with these core questions on their own.

## Let Students Make and See the Value of Mistakes

Traditional classrooms privilege final products and punish error. Small wonder so many students either disengage due to low self-concept, or cheat and plagiarize. “We don’t get a grade for honesty,” an honor student I caught cheating once told me. “A college that sees me get a ‘C+’ is not going to care that I got it through hard work. They want to see the ‘A.’” After hearing this argument, in various forms, for years, I finally decided I had to change the way I handled and rewarded mistakes.

Mistakes are often the genesis of our best learning, and school should be the place where students still have a safety net and second chances. Although I tend to grade very precisely, I also do so with the expectation that students can and will come to me to discuss the strengths and weaknesses of their products and seek to rewrite and revise. I always take the better of the two grades, so they have nothing to lose in making the attempt.
