## Acknowledgments

So many of the ideas about effective learning and teaching in this book came from a lifetime of experience working with others. If you are familiar with any of the people listed below, you will be able to hear their voices ring out loudly at various places in the text. However, before I get to the litany of colleagues, I must credit above all others Terry Born with whom I co-planned and team-taught in the same classroom for over a decade. We co-directed and produced student-written plays, literary magazines, oral history books, and community newspapers. Countless numbers of effective practices that appear in these pages, I learned from carefully observing her interact with our students. She really is a virtual co-author. Then there is the rest of my amazing support network.

I first learned how to “figure out how to figure out” from my older brother Ken, while learning perseverance and resilience from my parents Rudy and Edna Jablon. My first role model for what a truly dedicated teacher is, who worked with the whole student, was one of my own teachers, Brother Walter Doyle. Professor Fred Geis, my first doctoral advisor at NYU, supplied my first clinical and theoretical understandings of inquiry teaching. Arthur Greenberg and CeCe Cullen, principals at the original Middle College H.S. in Queens, NY, supported Terry and me and the rest of the faculty in trying new and somewhat radical ideas that led to success with our students. Burt Rosenberg had the perspicacity to create the distributed counseling model at the high school that I describe in Chapter 3. In similar fashion, I was deeply supported by Dean Madeleine Grumet at Brooklyn College, CUNY and Dean Bill Dandridge at Lesley University where I was given the opportunity to create innovative courses and programs with inquiry at their core for K–12 teachers.

Without the brilliant insights and collaboration of Arthur Camins, in his role as district curriculum supervisor and co-director on many of our NSF funded grants, my education about implementing inquiry teaching in whole school districts would be much more limited. Likewise, Diane Varano, Andrea Egitto, and Karen Buonomo, my cofounders and collaborators of the Children’s Science Materials Workshop, along with Dolly Lau, Ingrid Thomas-Clark and the other facilitators, inspired me to pursue successful inquiry professional development.

Cecily Selby, chairperson of my dissertation committee, supported a rather radical 10-year study that few others understood. Janice Koch, Pamela Abder, and Meta Van Sickle, three science education professors and colleagues from across the country, pushed me to deeper understanding of inquiry teaching. Without the incredible energy, commitment, and feedback from all of my students of the last four decades, especially those in the BONGO Program, I would not have been able to make inquiry teaching the success it has been in our classrooms.

Sara Johnson and her team at Shell Education, especially my gifted editor, Joan Irwin, have made this a much more accessible publication. First drafts of each chapter were read by a volunteer group of teachers and principals who provided thought-provoking feedback. I am grateful to all of them, but especially Bill Cummiskey and Carolina Vila who were consistently vigilant with their insights and edits.

My three best friends, Stuart Siegelman, Rick Rippetoe, and Harold Greenspan have supplied the emotional and intellectual assistance when needed through these past 44 years of teaching. Finally, I need to thank Scott and Nicole, my two teenage children, for their love and support over the past three years as I worked too many of their days and nights on this manuscript. They make my life such a joy.

He did not distribute the guiding worksheet, (although he did use some of the questions verbally with a few students) and the students were able to figure it out. Now I know that this does not always happen the first time, but imagine how much we have not allowed our students to learn on their own. We have deemed the work too hard so we give them the answers. We do not allow them to become problem solvers and they do not have control over their own learning.

Inquiry and letting students explore their own thoughts allows students to believe that they have the answers and shifts the ownership of learning to them. Students are more apt to remember this information if they have created their own learning, yet classrooms still mostly depend on direct instruction and memorization to deliver content.

Constructivism, differentiation, and high rigor are typical topics for education courses. But what does that look like in a classroom filled with students with a variety of abilities, experiences, and motivations? It is hard to imagine how these teaching ideals transpire in reality in a classroom with so many variables. There is a gap in the education of teachers in how to effectively use these strategies to help students gain the knowledge through more effective means. The gap is not what needs to be done but rather how to do it and The Synergy of Inquiry attacks this gap. Paul Jablon has written this book to not only define inquiry, but to also give practical strategies on how to implement this in a classroom the next day. Furthermore, Jablon acknowledges the struggles that educators have with classroom management, the challenges of a classroom with a variety of students, including those that may be unmotivated in the subject, and the struggle of creating systemic change within a school. Jablon uses real teachers’ accounts to help one see that while this strategy may be difficult at first, it can produce great change and great gains in the educational growth of students.

—Michelle Nye, Ed.D.
