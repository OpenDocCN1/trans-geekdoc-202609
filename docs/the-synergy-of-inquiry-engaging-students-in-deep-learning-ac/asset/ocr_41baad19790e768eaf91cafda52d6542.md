Every teacher committed to this work. Additionally, the paraprofessionals who supervise students during recess came to me asking, “We want to learn from Mr. Ted, too.” Together we wrote a small grant that enabled them to have a series of after-school workshops to learn inquiry science. They produced a handy recess guide offering questions that adults could ask students at recess to encourage their conjectures and experiments such as figuring out what determines the course of the rivers that are created by melting snow. The adults encouraged the students to experiment by building tiny dams with found objects like sticks, stones, and leaves to divert the playground rivers. We also began our annual “Last Surviving Snowbank Contest.” This involved recess observations and conjectures from each class about which of the plowed snow banks around the playground would melt last (and why) with the prize being an extra recess. Thus inquiry and problem-solving were encouraged—even at recess, and our students were invited to ask questions and collaborate throughout the day. It was our hope that our students would continue these investigations at home in their neighborhoods. Their sharing at classroom morning meetings indicated that this happened.

# Writing Workshop Approach

Another academic area that our teachers felt needed improvement was writing. As they shared their concerns and questions with me, I realized that they had had limited training in the workshop approach to writing, which can be based on inquiry. We began to consider ways to put in place our own writing workshop approach. I wrote a small grant through which I was able to offer teachers training under Lynn Bowmaster’s leadership of writing workshops in third-, fourth-, and fifth-grade classrooms. We set up the grant to allow each class a series of sessions with Lynn and with the teacher participating along with students in order to experience being a writer, and to learn how to manage a writing workshop. In participating with their students, teachers were able to puzzle over their own questions:

+   - What does it mean to be a writer?
+   - Why is choice so important in writing?
+   - What inspires writers?
+   - How does the audience provide feedback to the writer?
+   - How can a writing workshop reach each student and inspire individual creativity?

We found our staff room and faculty meetings were becoming lively with conversations about math, science, and writing that revolved around our professional questions and led to improved classroom practice in all subject areas.
