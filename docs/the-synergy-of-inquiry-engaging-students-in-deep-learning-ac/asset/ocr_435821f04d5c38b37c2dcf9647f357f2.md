# Final Public Projects

A final public project, the culminating experience, for students working on an interdisciplinary unit is intended to provide the opportunity to address an issue of their interest that draws from their academic experience. It provides students with the opportunity to apply, integrate, and synthesize knowledge and experience obtained during their academic course of study to a question or problem of public relevance.

Final public projects can take various forms depending on the issues students have investigated, for example, presentations to community governing boards, public service announcements, or brochures to assist community agencies or prompt civic participation in community initiatives such as curbside recycling. Students can assess school lunch programs and make proposals to school and community agencies to help improve them. They can create community gardens or help elementary teachers engage their students in reading and science projects. (Even elementary students themselves can create informational signage for trails through local conservation areas.) Students can monitor local ecosystems in conjunction with local environmental protection agencies. They can research town histories, make scale models of the town over decades or centuries, make videos of historical events and show them on public access television channels, and engage senior citizens in writing their own oral histories. They can build and finance local playgrounds, skate parks, vest-pocket parks. This is just a brief description of the myriad of projects represented in these teacher-designed units.

Do an online search for examples of teacher-designed interdisciplinary units. You will find an infinite number of public projects that allow adolescent students to demonstrate their *competence* to their peers and adults in their community in a manner that is of consequence to them. Meeting this Maslow *esteem* need, while studying academics, allows those previously disengaged from school to prosper, and those regularly engaged to fly.

Most teachers after first doing project-based learning where the projects deal with real issues soon move to public presentation of projects. They do so because meeting the needs of students to demonstrate their competence in a public forum exponentially increases the motivational power of the projects.
