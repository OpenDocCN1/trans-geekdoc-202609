My personal challenge as a teacher is to permit room for wonder. In teaching a unit on the play *Inherit the Wind* (Lawrence and Lee 2003), a retelling of the Scopes monkey trial in 1920s Tennessee, my culminating activity has involved students formulating, and then exploring, a question about which they are still wondering after they complete the play. *Inherit the Wind* is rich in areas where student curiosity can be triggered, from questions of law and First Amendment rights, to religion in the American South, the theory of evolution, the role of media in the early 20th century, and the differences between the play and the actual trial. I teach at an Orthodox Jewish day school, where a number of students dig far deeper than I require because they want to get to the bottom of how Judaism reconciles Genesis and evolution. For those students genuinely interested in such questions, they are off and running. Here is what I find does not work: When I present students with a long and varied list of possible questions about which they might be wondering, then suddenly the assignment mutates from one truly driven by inquiry to one where they are simply selecting a choice from a teacher-approved list. I can see the difference in their motivation, or lack of, as they either enthusiastically or dutifully track down leads.

Clearly, I can benefit from the collaborative thinking of other teachers as I seek to fine tune assignments like mine for *Inherit the Wind*. That is why I am so pleased that my school has finally recognized the problem of “learned helplessness.”
