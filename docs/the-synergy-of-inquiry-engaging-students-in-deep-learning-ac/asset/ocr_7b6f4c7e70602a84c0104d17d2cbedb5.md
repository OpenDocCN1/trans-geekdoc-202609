# Curriculum

+   - Exploring interdisciplinary, integrated, and thematic approaches to curriculum design that include multicultural perspectives and an active, student-centered approach to learning
+   - Connecting school curriculum to community needs and activities
+   - Accommodating diversity—learning the skills necessary for working with students with a wide range of special needs and refining techniques for inclusionary practices
+   - Integrating technology across the curriculum
+   - Improving academic performance by fostering health and fitness
+   - Providing a rigorous, core academic program with success experiences for all learners—eliminating tracking, having skilled teachers teach heterogeneous groupings and promoting cooperative learning

# Teacher Support

+   - Empowering teachers and administrators to make decisions about the experiences of middle-grade students
+   - Organizing the school into interdisciplinary academic teams of teachers and multi-age students with shared decision making by a close-knit school community
+   - Staffing with teachers who are expert at teaching young adolescents
+   - Investigating unique roles for teachers at the middle-school level, such as team participating and teaching, building advisor/advisee programs, and facilitating community-based participation
+   - Providing teachers with common planning time in order to work almost daily together on making these powerful interdisciplinary thematic units

My colleague, Peter Cook, has been a staff developer for NELMS (the New England League of Middle Schools), a principal of a highly-functioning middle school for many years, and an international ambassador for middle school practices in countries as far as Australia and Macedonia. I think he sums up the spirit of it when he says, “You know you have really become a middle school teacher when you find yourself and a group of parents cooking pasta for 200-300 students and adults for some evening presentation that students on your team have created, are hosting, and carrying out throughout the evening.”

# You Really Need to Believe

When I began teaching, I taught at a school with many disciplinary issues. It was common to encounter disruptive behavior in the hallways and in most classrooms. I learned a very negative set of coping skills where I needed to get control. It was certainly them against me, and from this experience I became afraid to trust my students. Over the next five years, as I began inquiry, project-based teaching, and working with students in their outside communities, I transitioned from being the dictator and started to again trust teens. As we succeeded together, I began to have relationships with individuals whom I soon implicitly trusted. I think they began to trust me also. But it was not until I transferred to another school with the same type of kids but some very different teachers who, together with students, had created a very different culture that I started to change my viewpoint.
