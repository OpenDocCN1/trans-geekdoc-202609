# Authenticity in School Work

Finally, as shown directly above, inquiry-style teaching and learning lends an authenticity to school that is very often lacking. When I co-taught with our seventh-grade ELA teacher, we often discussed the need for student work to be meaningful and authentic. Rather than writing book reports, this teacher assigned students to draft book reviews that mirrored reviews from websites and newspapers. He argued that when these students graduate and want to write in the future, they will never have to, or want to, write a book report that summarizes a text. Instead, real people *review* books they read, offering analysis and argument alongside any necessary summary. In my experience, students recognize and appreciate this type of work. They find it valuable and empowering to participate in the types of work the professionals in our disciplines are doing. As I previously mentioned, rather than doing lab reports, we write scientific papers modeled off of the format most professional journals require. I bring in my wife’s lab notebook and reference her publications (she is a microbiologist) to show students that *real scientists and engineers* write these papers about their findings, and so, since we are engaging in the same sort of exploration, we share our findings in a similar fashion.

Beyond authentic work, inquiry fosters authenticity in student questions and interest. Students find their own ways to connect our work to their personal lives and even to relevant topics across the curriculum.
