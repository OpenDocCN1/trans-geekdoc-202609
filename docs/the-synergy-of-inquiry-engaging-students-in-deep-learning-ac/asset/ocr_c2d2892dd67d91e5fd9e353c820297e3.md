# Math Class

The first lesson was in a math class in which the teacher had taught the formulas for calculating the areas of a circle, rectangle, and triangle. The project assignment was to work in small groups to determine the most efficient shape. That struck me as an odd, but interestingly open-ended, question.

The group I sat with had brought in pennies from home and proceeded to see how many pennies they could fit in a circle, rectangle, and triangle of the same area. Sure enough, the triangle was able to hold more pennies. That served to remind me of something I once read about why the ancient Egyptians built pyramids. However, that is only where the learning for these students began. One of them noticed that in the circle, each successive concentric circle had six more pennies than the last. The group decided to find out why.

They took up a collection of quarters, carefully noting how much they needed to return to each contributor. Once again, each successive concentric circle had six more quarters than the previous one. Now students got really excited. They went down to the school cafeteria and came back with 6-inch paper plates. Again, each new concentric circle had six more paper plates than the previous one. Students now knew they were on to something.

They ran up to the teacher to explain their findings and ask why it occurred this way. The teacher, recertified in mathematics, confessed that he did not know. But he suggested that they go down the hall to check with the physics teacher, who was more of a mathematician.

The physics teacher listened to students’ findings and smiled. He said that each concentric circle was not exactly 6 units larger, but 6.28318530718 or 2pi, with pi being approximately 3.14. These students had inadvertently discovered pi, and once having done so, would likely never forget. I know I have not.

# Independent Science Project

The next instance of most memorable examples of inquiry learning was an independent science project. One student had determined to study the difference in vision between carnivores and herbivores. In order to do so, she had talked her way behind the exhibits in the small mammals’ wing of the American Museum of Natural History in New York City. There she was permitted to make plaster of Paris masks of the skulls of a deer and a leopard.
