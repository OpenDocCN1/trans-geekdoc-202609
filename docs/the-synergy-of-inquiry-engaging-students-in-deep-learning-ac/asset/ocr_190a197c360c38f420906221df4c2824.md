# Reward Questions and Privilege Them over Answers

Higher-order thinking questions—those that call upon application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation—do not have simple, fill in the correct bubble kind of answers. Much of the work I do with my students involves helping them to understand how to ask questions like these, and I give them a great deal of practice in creating them. Whenever we start a new unit, I always poll students for what sort of questions they have about the topic at hand, and keep up that polling process as we go. Most class discussions begin with student-generated prompts, and particularly provocative or discussion-generating questions get rewarded on my whiteboard’s Hall of Fame section. By the time we get ready for our summative assessments, it is students who should be asking the questions.

This is the core of inquiry. At minimum, I want students to draft, debate, develop, and employ their own approaches to problems, such as how to create an effective advertising campaign or how to make Shakespeare relevant to their own lives. At best, I want them to come up with these core questions on their own.

# Let Students Make and See the Value of Mistakes

Traditional classrooms privilege final products and punish error. Small wonder so many students either disengage due to low self-concept, or cheat and plagiarize. “We don’t get a grade for honesty,” an honor student I caught cheating once told me. “A college that sees me get a ‘C+’ is not going to care that I got it through hard work. They want to see the ‘A.’” After hearing this argument, in various forms, for years, I finally decided I had to change the way I handled and rewarded mistakes.

Mistakes are often the genesis of our best learning, and school should be the place where students still have a safety net and second chances. Although I tend to grade very precisely, I also do so with the expectation that students can and will come to me to discuss the strengths and weaknesses of their products and seek to rewrite and revise. I always take the better of the two grades, so they have nothing to lose in making the attempt. When student groups try to collectively create a product, they often stumble, and sometimes outright fail.
