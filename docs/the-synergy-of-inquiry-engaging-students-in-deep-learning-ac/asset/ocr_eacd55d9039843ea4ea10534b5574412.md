# Determining Group Membership

The questions of who to assign to groups and when to change groups require a more complex and multi-dimensional answer. I usually began the term by arbitrarily assigning students to teams, literally having them count off, so they would end up in random groups. Since I had no basis to make any judgments on, I thought this a good method of getting students who did not regularly interact to get to know one another. I found it best to let these groups run for about a month to six weeks before rearranging the groupings. This timing allowed for students to not only encounter difficulties in getting certain group members to accept particular duties, but it also gave them time to attempt to work out these difficulties utilizing the conflict resolution and group-building skills that they had acquired. Through journal writing and seeing other students’ responses as well as through regular social student interaction, various groups shared strategies that they employed.

The next regrouping depended on my objectives. Sometimes I wanted stronger students paired with weaker students in order to give assistance to those who needed it and to raise the esteem levels and comprehension levels of the stronger students that come with having to explain concepts. Other times, I wanted to place students who were not contributing their part in their original groups with students who I knew were socially attracted to as a motivation for them to be proficient. Later in the term I sometimes changed the groups so that all the most motivated people were in the same groups and those who were still trying to “coast” were together. It is amazing the effect that having four students who have been dependent on the work of others placed together in the same group for success can have on some constituents of that group. Other times, I divided groups such that there would be four “quiet” students together. Many times, leaders emerged from this mixture. Even later in the term, I let them select their own groups. It was most interesting to note, both from my own observations, and from their journal entries, that by this time in the semester they had learned not to always select their social friends. Rather, they began to use criteria they had developed during the beginning of the term regarding the attributes needed in members of a good team, to select their new teams. During a 15-week term, I would change groups at least three times, sometimes as many as five times.

Lastly, what is most important is the flexibility this structure gives you as the teacher. You are constantly doing formative evaluations of groups and individuals as you circulate the room. You hear team members trying to articulate conceptual frameworks and investigative strategies. You are there to ask the appropriate questions that will guide them to procedures that will lead to greater understanding. You are on the scene to hear of differing data, interpretations, or strategies between groups so that you know when to stop them mid-exploration and have them compare results. The discrepancy in results at this juncture will allow them to see the need to control a variable or evaluate an approach or procedure before continuing.
