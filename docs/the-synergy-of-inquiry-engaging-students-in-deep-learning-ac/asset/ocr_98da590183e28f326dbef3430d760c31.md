Concepts are abstracted from experience—creating them is a process. Since concepts are learned, it is misleading to speak about *teaching* concepts. As noted earlier, the teacher’s responsibility is to design explorations where students can construct, with guidance and other assistance, their own concepts. This is what students carry away from a facilitated exploration and clarification. Students from PreK to fourth grade are generally preoperational and understand concrete concepts. For example, fourth graders studying heat understand that when the thermometer registers an increase it is showing that an area is hotter. However, a formal abstract thinker could utilize the kinetic molecular model and understand that the molecules are moving faster. The concrete operational learners could also learn this model after creating some concrete examples through explorations, but they do not understand it is only a model.

# Thinking Skills in Practice

“Figuring out how to figure out,” is my description of acquiring higher-order thinking skills.

The Educational Policies Commission, established in 1961, defined “thinking ability” in terms of certain rational powers of the mind: “They are recalling, comparing, inferring, generalizing, deducing, classifying, analyzing, imagining, synthesizing and evaluating” (5). The commission saw these as natural innate abilities that must be developed through facilitated explorations.

Since then, we have created disciplined-based thinking skills objectives in both our national and state standards. They are distinct for each discipline. However, if we look a bit more closely, we see there is also an incredible commonality among these disciplinary thinking skills. We will investigate these commonalities within the differences in detail in Chapter 2 where we look at synergy across the school day. Until the advent of the CCSS, the assessment of these thinking skills had taken a back seat to the content. Now they have finally reached equal footing during standardized testing.

Some people question whether young students reason. They do reason, but they reason differently than young adolescents, who in turn reason differently than adults. For example, when we look at the thinking skills that exist within “doing science,” we see that preoperational students, (usually up until third grade) have the ability to operationalize only the *basic* “science process skills,” but these are absolutely foundational to the integrated-process skills that then become available to students as they physiologically evolve into concrete operational learners. This will be addressed later in the book.
