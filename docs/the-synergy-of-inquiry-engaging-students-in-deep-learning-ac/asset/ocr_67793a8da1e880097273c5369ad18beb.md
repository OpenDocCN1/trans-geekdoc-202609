# Jigsaw Reading

Each teammate has part of the handout to read. Student 1 must read and teach section A to Students 2 and 3. Student 2 must teach B, and Student 3 must teach section C. All students with section A, plan lessons. Sections B and C do the same; then they return to the original group of one, two, and three and begin to teach.

For more information on Sample Cooperative Learning Structures, refer to the Digital Resource CD.

If you are not familiar with many of these structures and other aspects of facilitating lessons in small groups, I would recommend that you research cooperative learning. I have been using The Cooperative Classroom by Lynda Baloche (1998) with teachers in staff development sessions and my classes. Because Baloche’s work has been so useful to me, it is likely that some of this chapter reflects some of her ideas about creating an effective community of learners in the classroom. She also has much more detailed descriptions of cooperative group structures and details about the kinds of lessons that they are best suited for. Every time you begin to question whether the changes she suggests for your classroom are worth the trouble, she will reference studies that demonstrate their efficacy.

# A Grade So Far

If we follow on the structure of the spiraled inquiry teaching learning cycle where concepts and skills spiral through subsequent lessons, we can see that this structure enables all students to master a competent level of concept understanding or skill acquisition with the help of their peers and the teacher. However, whether this total mastery will occur within a day, or even three days, is not always guaranteed. Therefore, when students hand in their reports, videos, or authentic assessments, if they are not given a grade so far, they will usually not continue to try and master what they have not fully accomplished.

Students should always receive comments and questions back on their assignments that allow them to redo the work and master the material. Of course there is a great deal of conversation among teachers about how to tweak the redo procedures so that it does not encourage students to be lazy on their first attempts. If inquiry teaching is to succeed, whatever an inquiry teacher finally decides as the procedure, teachers need to have a grade so far policy accompanied by some level of response to the original student work. Similar to the practice of asking students to explain their thinking rather than just answers, this procedure lowers the incredible tension that accompanies testing and assessment.
