Although the year-to-year development of inquiry-science projects has been interrupted for me, there is one large overarching project that I have been able to facilitate every year for the past seven years with my seventh graders. It is called “Little Jars of Crud,” or LJOC. I believe I have been able to improve this project a little bit every year, and after seven years, I am really impressed with the student learning, output, and understanding of the scientific process that results from completing this project. I hesitate to say “complete” because I hope students continue their learning with this project after it ends, and I believe it allows them to explore further on their own, as they desire.

LJOC is the project associated with the middle school unit on the classification of life. Students learn how and why life is classified as well as the characteristics of the kingdoms and phyla and what is necessary for an organism to survive. They become familiarized with the science skill of classification themselves and try to identify species. As students are reading the textbook and doing activities, the overarching project is introduced. Sometimes I even start it right on the first day of school. In pairs, students put grass and water in a baby food jar and a few days later look at a sample of the water under the microscope. It is *teeming* with life! This is the *hook* that grabs their attention. They immediately want to know about the creatures. I refuse to tell them, and actually do not tell them even months later. They must come to the understanding themselves as we begin going through the characteristics of the different kingdoms. Long story short, between all the groups in the class, they will inevitably see a species from all five kingdoms (if you count both bacteria types as one kingdom)! This is fantastic for me as a teacher and them as students. After the hook, we do a brief review of the scientific method, and students are asked what questions they have about the LJOC. This leads to the creation of investigable questions. Students then pick a topic they’d like to research, such as “What is the effect of different types of soap on the life in the jar?” They create a controlled experiment to answer the question, make up the necessary jars, observe them over the course of a month, then analyze their data and write a lab report. When the data is synthesized, we take a class period to do a round table discussion to share the results. In this way, all students can hear what was researched (everyone has a different topic) and decide the answer to the questions of “What is the effect of ________ on the life in the jar?” Obviously there is a lot more detail involved in how all of those steps are done, but the way I run it, it is all student-driven.
