In many inner-city schools and some rural areas, significant numbers of students have needs that are not fulfilled. In these situations, school practices need to be modified to fulfill student needs. Doing remedial and low level work is not the answer to this situation. If a school is not as successful as it would like to be, a change in the way students are engaged needs to occur. Human brains are designed to learn and do so efficiently. We need to utilize inquiry teaching, analogous interrelationship role-plays, project-based learning, internships, and other ways that will engage students in learning that stimulates their basal centers, the pleasure centers of their brains. These approaches are addressed in more detail in Chapter 5.

Although the preceding examples of inquiry teaching in various disciplines may sound intriguing, perhaps even exciting, some of you may be getting a little nervous about the approach. Does it seem to reverse the order in which you do things? Does it modify the role you currently play in your classroom? Is there evidence from research studies that demonstrates that students learn more deeply in inquiry-teaching classrooms? Throughout this book you will hear from classroom teachers and principals about their experiences with inquiry teaching. Practical experience was combined with evidence from research because both are important.

Take a moment to briefly address this issue of effectiveness now. “Decades of research illustrate the benefits of inquiry-based and cooperative learning to help students develop the knowledge and skills necessary to be successful in a rapidly-changing world” (Barron and Darling-Hammond et al. 2008, 2). A good place to start looking at the literature about inquiry teaching is in another, more comprehensive book edited by Linda Darling-Hammond et al. (2008) reviewing the research on inquiry teaching. Furthermore, the bonus chapter on the Digital Resource CD will further enrich the understanding that you have begun in this chapter by comparing the inquiry teaching process with what we know from current neurobiology about the processes by which the human brain learns. As this understanding of brain structures and function related to cognition and emotion matches this inquiry-learning format of engaging learners, it makes for a compelling argument for educators to fully embrace inquiry teaching. It is not just a better way for students to excel on Common Core exams, but it is a likely way to eliminate the achievement gap, and to have students succeed in their careers.

We are all aware of how difficult it is to do controlled research studies in real school situations. Although we have evidence from educational research to defend the effectiveness of inquiry teaching, these studies are muddied by the nature of social science research.
