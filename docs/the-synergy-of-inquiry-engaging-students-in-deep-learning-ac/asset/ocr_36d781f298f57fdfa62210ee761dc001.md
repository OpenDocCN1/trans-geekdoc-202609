A teacher who had previously been in a DMI (developing main ideas) seminar I had taught, reflected this change when she remarked to me one day, “It’s all because of YOU. We used to be able to sit around teachers’ rooms and talk about our grandchildren and recipes. NOW all we talk about is math!” Although she expressed this as an apparent complaint, she said it with a grin, acknowledging that she enjoyed the change in her professional environment.

Teachers at Ryan Road School have commented similarly, “We love talking about math at lunch!” The climate of inquiry was not only taking hold at the school, it was also proving to be professionally rewarding for teachers as it encouraged them to delve deeply into improving their practice.

# Inquiry Teaching in Science

In those years, we found our attention turning to other subject areas as well. One day, a first-grade teacher stopped me in the hall with a question about science. According to the district STC science curriculum, she told me, her students were supposed to study trees. The lesson in the written curriculum actually suggested, “Show the children pictures of leaves in a book to help them learn to identify trees.”

She explained, “I think the kids would learn more about trees if we went outside, but I don’t know how to manage that.”

“I completely agree,” I replied, “and I know who can help us.” I resolved to connect our teachers to the skillful educators from the Hitchcock Center for the Environment in Amherst, Massachusetts. Our PTO (Parent Teacher Organization) agreed to fund visits for each class from Hitchcock educators. I encouraged teachers to think of the visits as a way for them to learn how to do what the visiting naturalist was demonstrating, rather than as a one time visit from an expert. As a result, Ted Watt of Hitchcock began as our mentor to understanding the world of nature and implementing inquiry science—a relationship that continues today.

These visits were so successful and welcomed by teachers that they were eager to more deeply commit to inquiry nature studies. Luckily, a perfect opportunity presented itself in the form of a new endowment grant offered by our local Northampton Education Foundation. Led by Andrea Egitto, a committed and knowledgeable primary grade teacher, we wrote and were awarded the first large grant which provided three years of grade level specific training by Ted Watt. The grant enabled us to transform relevant portions of our science program to experiential inquiry field study science in natural areas within walking distance of the school. These included a nearby conservation area with varied habitats from forest to rock outcroppings to vernal pools.
