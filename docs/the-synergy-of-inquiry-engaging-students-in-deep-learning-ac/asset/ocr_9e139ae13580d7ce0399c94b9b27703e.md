I always tell my kids that I hated group work in high school, and it was only years later that I realized this was because no one ever taught me how to do it; teachers just plunked us in groups and told us to work. Would just plunking someone in the cockpit of a jet airplane and telling them to fly result in a successful experience?

Although these activities might at first seem to threaten precious time in content-area learning, I have found that activities like this eventually enable successful cooperative and inquiry-based work. Besides, it is seldom an *either/or*, as these activities can often be paired with relevant content. I have had my students practice “I” statements and address root causes of conflict, applying them both to scenarios from their own school and to the feud between the Montagues and the Capulets in *Romeo and Juliet*. When practicing empathy and perspective-taking to analyze our own personal conflicts, we are then explicit in talking about how these are the same skills that let us analyze whatever character or theme we are looking at in the novel or short story we are reading. The humanities are not the only disciplines with easy cross-applicability between skills for cooperative work and skills for learning content. The hypothesis you develop and defend in a science class, the pattern you attempt to construct through inferential logic in math class, all such draw upon that ability to look at data (behavior), search out root causes, and arrive at an explanation or solution that works for a given problem. A teacher who can help students find those links can, simultaneously, help students develop the skills to work together effectively in their immediate lives, cover required content, and, most importantly, help students develop links between what school asks of them and what will help them in their everyday experiences. Common Core has helped this process immensely by asking schools to no longer privilege students learning specific names, dates, and formulas as a goal in themselves, but to limit that kind of declarative knowledge to its applicability in helping students develop a core set of skills.

# Making the Classroom a Nicer Place

I spent most of my school career hiding behind the student sitting in the seat in front of me. Luckily I was small in stature, so it was not too difficult a task. I constantly felt like I was a contestant on a game show where I needed an answer at all times. At any moment, the teacher might call on me for the answer that I might or might not have.
