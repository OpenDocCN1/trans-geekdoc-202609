# Think and Reflect

+   1. Inquiry lessons require students to work in small groups, engaging in an exploration where they uncover the concept you want them to own. In what ways, if any, would this student-led model differ from the way you currently plan lessons? Who can you plan with or get help from as you plan effective explorations?

+   2. During each of the three parts of the inquiry teaching learning cycle, the teacher plays different roles. Thinking about your classroom, perhaps even a particular lesson, briefly describe the role you would be playing during each of the three stages if you used the inquiry teaching approach.

+   3. Just how important is it for you to ask for a student’s “thinking so far” rather than asking for the answer, and how might it change the whole tone and dynamic of the classroom?

+   4. How does the “figuring out how to figure out” aspect of inquiry teaching have students naturally prepared for the skills required as part of the Common Core State Standards?

+   5. If indeed a concept—a complex idea—cannot be taught, but rather can only be constructed by the learner, then our understanding of neuronal networks (and how they are built, modified, and expanded in the frontal cortex) must inform what you need to do as a teacher. What are two to three elements of inquiry teaching discussed earlier in this chapter that match your new understanding of how the human brain learns?

+   6. If students in a didactic classroom are being told answers and they are memorizing procedures, which areas of the brain are being used? Do these areas construct concepts that can be used in solving real world problems?
