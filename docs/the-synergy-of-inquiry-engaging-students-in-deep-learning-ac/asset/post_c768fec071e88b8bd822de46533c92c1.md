At first, the most unusual thing about International High School at LaGuardia Community College was that students had to fail a test of English language proficiency to be eligible for admission. True, it was on a college campus, but by this time, so were several other schools. We thought that if eight 40-minute periods each day was good, then nine 35-minute periods would be even better, and proceeded to shave five minutes off each class so that students could enroll in an additional ninth period class. Everything would change, however, when we implemented “student-for-a-day.”

## Student for a Day

About a decade after our school began, Theodore Sizer and Nancy Sizer (1999) wrote a book entitled, Students are Watching. The implication of that title and the book is that students may not be listening, but they are watching and emulating the behavior of the adults around them. Like so many others, I had first connected to Theodore Sizer (1984) after reading his book, Horace’s Compromise, which pointed to the failures of large traditional high schools; then followed his work with an organization he founded called the Coalition of Essential Schools, which was founded to promote whole school reform; and, eventually, had International join the coalition. One of Theodore and Nancy’s most important insights was that you never knew a school until you had seen it from the viewpoint of a student.

Capitalizing on that insight, I offered every teacher the opportunity to be freed up from their daily classes and responsibilities to spend the day with a student. Their classes would be covered. To stack the deck a bit, we identified the 10 percent of students who were not as successful as we wanted them to be, and asked participating teachers to select a student partner from among these disengaged students. At the very least, this would mean that our disaffected kids got to spend an entire day with a faculty member who got to know them. If that student had gym, then the teacher needed to bring his or her sneakers. Participants were required to meet the youngsters at point of school arrival, go to all of their classes with them, including lunch, and remain with students until they departed for home at day’s end.

Within a three-month period, every teacher on staff volunteered to participate. At the end of that period, we piled into a room to debrief. Teachers’ comments were, “the most interesting thing that happens in this school occurs in between periods,” and “you can’t do anything meaningful in a 35-minute period because no sooner do you take your books out, then it’s time to copy the homework assignment and pack up for the next class.”

From this experience, we put together a faculty committee to study International’s schedule. The result was that we went from 35-minute periods to 70-minute ones, students would take four classes each day, and teachers would teach only three. True to Sizer’s philosophy, we were able to reduce student load for each teacher by 40 percent without incurring any additional costs. As importantly, 70-minute classes lent themselves better to the activity-based, project-driven collaborative learning that the school had committed to in its mission and statement of educational philosophy.

## Form Defines Function

In the early days of the International High School, we had ordered trapezoidal tables and chairs to replace the one-arm torture chairs still prevalent in most schools and colleges. We reasoned that placing two trapezoidal tables together would seat up to six comfortably, with ample room for students’ books. We also hoped that it would facilitate the kind of small group collaborative learning that the school aspired to. However, even the best-laid plans can be effectively defeated by precedent and tradition, which along with incrementalism, are the true enemies of real school reform.

When the furniture arrived, I waited until everyone had gone home in the late afternoon and set up each of our five classrooms with four pairs of tables facing each other, and six chairs around each pair. I also moved teachers’ desks to a sixth room, which was to become a faculty workroom. By the close of first period the next day, my teachers had figured out how to align trapezoidal tables into rows facing front, and some even moved their desk back into their classrooms.

Once again, I waited for everyone to leave the next evening, and returned the classrooms to the organizational schema I had originally put them in. Sure enough, teachers placed the desks and chairs into rows once again the following day. This daily and nightly exchange went on for three weeks until I wore teachers down, and they stopped moving the furniture around. They were, to be sure, still teaching from the front of the room. However, now half the class had their backs toward them, which at the very least forced teachers to begin moving around the room. It would take years until we learned together how to do collaborative learning well.

## The Motion Program

The major catalyst in moving us toward a school model based on collaborative inquiry learning began without fanfare, or even notice. Felicita Santiago, a math teacher, came into my office late one August to discuss the work she had done with Uri Treisman that summer at the University of California at Berkeley. She was so excited about what she had just learned about facilitating math learning through group work, that the sounds and laughter emanating from my office attracted David Hirschy, the school’s physics teacher, who entered, sat down, and listened. At one point, he said that the work Felicita was describing is the mathematics of motion, the underlying concept in Newtonian physics. I suggested that if they wanted to do a joint class together, I would support it (Jablon 1989b).

In the days to come and in typical International High School fashion, David and Felicita recruited Kathie Rugger, an English teacher, to teach Poetry in Motion, as well as the physical education teacher, Alan Krull, who had just launched Project Adventure, an indoor version of Outward Bound. Together, this team of teachers created the Motion Program, the first full-day, integrated, interdisciplinary community of learners that would later come to define the International approach (Jablon 1989b).

The Motion Program ran the following trimester with just one class as a pilot project. Faculty members planned together and were often in each other’s classes. Felicita and David team taught a double 70-minute period combined physics and math class. Small teams of four or five students worked on projects together over several days of work as teachers circulated from table to table supporting students and extending their thinking. It was the first time I experienced the exhilaration of multiple voices engaged in meaningful work with one another. It was not noisy but purposeful.

At the end of the first trimester, 100 percent of students passed, not just one class, but all of their classes. We tried it with another class for the next trimester with the same results. In contrast at the time, 66 percent of all students enrolled in New York City high schools failed one or more subjects; 45 percent failed two or more; and a full 33 percent failed three or more subjects each semester. By any measure that 100 percent of students enrolled in the Motion Program pilot passed all of their courses was an extraordinary outcome.
