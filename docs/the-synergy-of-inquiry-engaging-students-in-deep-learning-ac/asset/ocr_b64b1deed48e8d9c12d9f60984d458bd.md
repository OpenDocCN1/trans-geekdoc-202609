I love project-based, interdisciplinary learning. My ultimate goal as an educator is to facilitate experiences that span multiple aspects of life and involve meaningful, long-term objectives. As teachers, we want our students to be complex thinkers who, instead of compartmentalizing their knowledge, can make connections across the curriculum, to their own lives, and to the world at large.

We also want our students to maturely express their opinions and debate their ideas with their peers. To this end, I facilitate cooperative group work. Developing a classroom of sharers and negotiators has been challenging yet invigorating. I continue to hone all of these facets of my teaching skill set in my seventh year as an educator.

It is not easy. There are days that do not go as planned, and I am left feeling disappointed and discouraged. There are days when carefully thought out lessons fail to ignite the expected interest of my students. There are days that leave me deflated and near tears. On these days, I say to myself what I will now say to you: Please, do not give up. Your students need you. They need an energetic, caring, and thoughtful role model. You can always make tomorrow better than today. They are lucky to have you and, although they may have a funny way of showing it, they appreciate you.

Fortunately, I have seen signs that I am having success. I have heard students groan when the bell rings and class is over because they have to wait another day before continuing our lesson. I have heard students say, “This is fun!” while solving proportions because I created an experience in which they want to find the answers. I have even had students who previously found little satisfaction in their math classes tell me that math is now their favorite class.

# The Planning Process for Eleventh-Grade Language Arts

Katie Houle, a high school English teacher in Chelmsford, Massachusetts, developed five lessons for a unit on gothic literature and Edgar Allan Poe’s poem The Raven. She identified two essential questions for the lessons:

+   1. Why do gothic literature and scary stories resonate with us as readers?
+   
+   How exactly do writers make their stories scary? For example, what key devices do writers of gothic literature use to convey an eerie feeling to their readers?
+   
+   2. Can we trust the narrator of every story? Why or why not?
