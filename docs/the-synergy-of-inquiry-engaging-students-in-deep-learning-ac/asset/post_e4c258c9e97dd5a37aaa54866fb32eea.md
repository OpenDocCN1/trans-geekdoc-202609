
When they have made inferences. Technology is one tool I use all the time, and for this lesson, I drew on one of the seemingly infinite ideas available online from other teachers (Kratter 2014). I adapted a *prezi* lesson using Pixar Short Films as a means of teaching short story elements. After explaining specific terms, we watched a Pixar Short Film (Pixar 2012) and applied those literary terms. Because there is often no dialogue in the Pixar Short Films, they are particularly useful for teaching about inferring character traits and other story elements. Furthermore, the work did not involve reading text, and so students who were less confident in their reading abilities were not intimidated. Their homework that night was to identify the elements in a Pixar Short Film that we had not watched in class. That is where choice came in. I posted nine links to various films and students could watch and reflect on any of them for their homework. The next day, students came in discussing the videos. I grouped them by the video they watched. They went over what they thought about characterization. Students were engaged and made insightful judgments when it came to direct and indirect characterization. They had successfully applied the terms to their own video. What is more, their motivation was apparent as I listened to them sell their video to students in the class, encouraging others to watch the video they had selected. Once we began reading a short story, they all had confidence in their ability to identify indirect and direct characterization, and furthermore to recognize when they were making inferences about characterization within the text. This lesson supported inclusion by utilizing choice, technology, and small group work which led to all ninth graders not only being engaged, but learning and using elements of short stories.

## Eleventh-Grade British Literature: *Frankenstein* Mock Trial

An example of how I use choice as a final assessment is drawn from my *Frankenstein* unit in eleventh grade. For this unit, I used multiple assessments: an exam, a writing assignment, and a project. The project portion was a mock trial. Students were randomly assigned roles (lawyer or witness) on the prosecution or defense team, but they had the option of switching roles with a willing peer. Once the teams were finalized, students began working as a unit with a shared goal. Each team had roughly four lawyers and four witnesses. The teams had to either defend or prosecute Frankenstein, who is charged with criminal negligent manslaughter for having created and then promptly abandoning a creature who went on to murder several innocent people. I provided students with computers, and they created a Google Doc for their own team. This allowed individual students to not only work specifically with their witness/lawyer, but also to work collaboratively as a unit. Students wrote their questions in the Google Doc, and everyone on the team could view and edit them. This was great because members of the team aided students who were not as confident or capable. Students became engaged, bouncing ideas off one another until all were satisfied not only with their own questions and answers, but with that of their entire team. While I monitored the groups, the case was entirely in their hands, their line of questions completely up to them. Additionally, I removed myself from the jury and acted solely as judge. I did this for two reasons. One, their grade was not based on who “won” the trial but on how they presented their case. By acting as the judge, it allowed me to focus more on their questions, use of the text, and so on. I had a rubric that I completed as each student participated in his or her portion of the trial. The jury (about nine people) was made up of the principal, the head of the English department, two aides (for moral support for some specific students within the class), and students who had taken my class the previous year and thus were familiar with the proceedings. The jury composition created an authentic audience for students. Having an audience other than me increases engagement for students and helps them to take it more seriously since it seems more real. The mock trial was a hit and students of all abilities were motivated, competitive, and showed what they knew about their text and the law system, in an interesting and engaging manner.

## Final Thoughts

Choice is not always easy to work into a lesson or unit. In my classroom, choice sometimes takes the form of options, both for students and me. I never have a single final assessment because that rarely gives a clear picture of what students know and what they can do with such knowledge. A heterogeneous class is comprised of students with different skills and interests. I have found that students engage more when they have some control over what they are doing. In the short story unit, they chose their movie, utilized technology, and interacted in small groups. I heard students talk who never participate during large group discussion. As with the mock trial, they too had an authentic audience comprised of their peers. They were not discussing the video to impress me; they were showing each other what they had learned and why the others should see that film. During the mock trial, students who really struggled with the text demonstrated their understanding of the novel when they worked with their team and then took the stand.

## Heidi O'Donnell's Reflections

*Up until this point, teachers’ descriptions have focused on short-term guided inquiry experiences. In the section that follows, Heidi O’Donnell describes a long-term “open” inquiry experience she has perfected over the past seven years.*

Inquiry science has been a focal point of teaching for me since I started seven years ago. It is ever evolving in my classroom as I have taught a number of grades and subjects. This year, I am splitting two sections of seventh-grade science and two sections of eleventh-grade college prep chemistry, whereas last year I had three sections of seventh-grade science and one section of eighth-grade science. The process has been stop and start in many ways.

Although the year-to-year development of inquiry-science projects has been interrupted for me, there is one large overarching project that I have been able to facilitate every year for the past seven years with my seventh graders. It is called “Little Jars of Crud,” or LJOC. I believe I have been able to improve this project a little bit every year, and after seven years, I am really impressed with the student learning, output, and understanding of the scientific process that results from completing this project. I hesitate to say “complete” because I hope students continue their learning with this project after it ends, and I believe it allows them to explore further on their own, as they desire.

LJOC is the project associated with the middle school unit on the classification of life. Students learn how and why life is classified as well as the characteristics of the kingdoms and phyla and what is necessary for an organism to survive. They become familiarized with the science skill of classification themselves and try to identify species. As students are reading the textbook and doing activities, the overarching project is introduced. Sometimes I even start it right on the first day of school. In pairs, students put grass and water in a baby food jar and a few days later look at a sample of the water under the microscope. It is *teeming* with life! This is the *hook* that grabs their attention. They immediately want to know about the creatures. I refuse to tell them, and actually do not tell them even months later. They must come to the understanding themselves as we begin going through the characteristics of the different kingdoms. Long story short, between all the groups in the class, they will inevitably see a species from all five kingdoms (if you count both bacteria types as one kingdom)! This is fantastic for me as a teacher and them as students. After the hook, we do a brief review of the scientific method, and students are asked what questions they have about the LJOC. This leads to the creation of investigable questions. Students then pick a topic they’d like to research, such as “What is the effect of different types of soap on the life in the jar?” They create a controlled experiment to answer the question, make up the necessary jars, observe them over the course of a month, then analyze their data and write a lab report. When the data is synthesized, we take a class period to do a round table discussion to share the results. In this way, all students can hear what was researched (everyone has a different topic) and decide the answer to the questions of “What is the effect of ________ on the life in the jar?” Obviously there is a lot more detail involved in how all of those steps are done, but the way I run it, it is all student-driven.
