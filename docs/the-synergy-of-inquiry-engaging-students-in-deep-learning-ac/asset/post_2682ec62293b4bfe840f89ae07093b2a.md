Concepts are abstracted from experience—creating them is a process. Since concepts are learned, it is misleading to speak about *teaching* concepts. ## Thinking Skills in Practice

“Figuring out how to figure out,” is my description of acquiring higher-order thinking skills.

The Educational Policies Commission, established in 1961, defined “thinking ability” in terms of certain rational powers of the mind: “They are recalling, comparing, inferring, generalizing, deducing, classifying, analyzing, imagining, synthesizing and evaluating” (5). The commission saw these as natural innate abilities that must be developed through facilitated explorations.

Since then, we have created discipline-based thinking skills objectives in both our national and state standards. They are distinct for each discipline. However, if we look a bit more closely, we see there is also an incredible commonality among these disciplinary thinking skills. We will investigate these commonalities within the differences in detail in Chapter 2 where we look at synergy across the school day. Until the advent of the CCSS, the assessment of these thinking skills had taken a back seat to the content. Now they have finally reached equal footing during standardized testing.

Some people question whether young students reason. They do reason, but they reason differently than young adolescents, who in turn reason differently than adults. For example, when we look at the thinking skills that exist within “doing science,” we see that preoperational students (usually up until third grade) have the ability to operationalize only the *basic* “science process skills,” but these are absolutely foundational to the integrated-process skills that then become available to students as they physiologically evolve into concrete operational learners. This will be addressed later in the book.

## Science Process Skills

The following are thinking skills needed to do and understand science.

### The basic processes (pre-operational thinkers and beyond)

- Observing
- Using space/time relationships
- Classifying
- Using numbers
- Measuring
- Communicating
- Predicting
- Inferring
- Estimating

### The integrated processes (concrete operational and formal thinkers)

- Formulating hypotheses
- Defining operationally
- Identifying and controlling variables
- Interpreting data
- Graphing
- Modeling

## Habits of Mind in Practice

When I was first introduced to the concept of *habits of mind*, I had a hard time differentiating them from thinking skills. They seemed to overlap so much. One day I was working with a wonderful colleague of mine, Arthur Camins, who at the time was a science coordinator in the Bedford-Stuyvesant area of Brooklyn, NY. We were struggling with how we were going to have our elementary teachers grasp this important idea if we could not clearly differentiate the two ourselves. That night he telephoned me and said, “You can have a thinking and reasoning skill and can apply it appropriately when asked to do so in school. However, if you do not regularly use it in your daily decision making, then you have not acquired the habit of mind.” That seems to have not only made it clear to me but to all teachers we were working with. If you use the most important decision-making and thinking skills, even when you are not required to, then they become part of the fabric of your thinking.

Some principals and teachers have decided that developing habits of mind is so important for students’ academic success that they have made them a central part of the mission for their schools. Debbie Meier’s Central Park East Secondary School in New York City is probably the one most cited, but in the past decade I have encountered many other successful schools in New York City, Minneapolis, Atlanta, Buffalo, Oakland and in smaller towns in Mississippi, New Jersey, Maine, and Massachusetts. All these schools specify habits of mind at their core.

Meier and the K–8 teachers at Mission Hill School in Boston focus on five habits of mind they think are seminal to their students’ success:

1. **Evidence:** How do we know what is true or false? What evidence counts? How sure can we be? What makes it credible to us?
2. **Viewpoint:** How else might this look if we stepped into other shoes? What would it look like if we were looking at it from a different direction? What would it look like if we had a different history or expectations?
3. **Connections/Cause and Effect:** Is there a pattern? Have we seen something like this before? What are the possible consequences?
4. **Conjecture:** Could it have been otherwise? Supposing that...? What if...?
5. **Relevance:** Does it matter? Who cares?

There are many other versions of habits of mind, some of which are subject-area specific. For example, some in science follow:

### Experimental evidence might have more than one interpretation.

1. Be open-minded, willing to change your mind in the face of reliable evidence, and respect another’s point of view.
2. Look at a matter from many sides before drawing a conclusion. Do not jump to conclusions or decide on the basis of one observation; deliberate and examine until you are as sure as you can be.
3. Know that hypotheses are valuable, even if they turn out not to be true, if they lead to fruitful investigations.
4. Offer reasons for findings and consider reasons suggested by others.
5. Know that different explanations can often be given for the same evidence, and it is not always possible to tell which one is correct.
6. Know why curiosity, openness, and skepticism are so highly regarded in science and how these are incorporated into the way science is carried out; exhibit these traits in our lives and value them in others.

### Gather accurate and testable evidence.

1. Go to reliable sources for evidence. Challenge sources to make sure they are reliable.
2. Avoid being superstitious; realize that nothing happens without cause.
3. Be curious. Be careful and accurate in observations.
4. Keep records of investigations and observations; do not change the records later.
5. Know why it is important in science to keep honest, clear, and accurate records.

You might be saying to yourself that many of these fit other academic disciplines as well, or that they are just traits of logical common-sense people. That is the point. These attributes need to become part of the fabric of thinking of all of our students—just part of who they are as they travel through life.

If you are thinking that this is too multifaceted and you are getting worried that you cannot fit all of this into your lessons, remember that all of the concepts, thinking skills, and habits of mind exist naturally and organically in a well-planned inquiry lesson. Once you approach planning and facilitating lessons in this fashion, you will soon become a master at having your students succeed in each of these arenas. Not only will they be successful, but they will also enjoy themselves immensely while doing it.

## An Example of Three Objectives Accomplished Simultaneously

In planning the second day of the John Brown lesson, Mike planned to have a concept, thinking skill, and habit of mind objective incorporated when students were engaged in the inquiry lesson. These objectives were accomplished simultaneously while students explored and clarified their understanding of John Brown's raid. They are listed here as they were all listed in the objectives part of Mike's plan. In addition, he wrote facilitating and assessment questions throughout the plan for each objective.

- **Concept**: Although John Brown's raid of 1859 was an illegal action according to both South Carolina and U.S. law, some saw it as an act of civil disobedience while others viewed it as almost treasonous.
- **Thinking Skill**: Students interpret data to recognize how evidence of historical events can be manipulated to present a point of view.
- **Habit of Mind**: Students develop perspective by considering how else the events might look if we stepped into other shoes. They learn about and understand viewpoint.

## Apply Knowledge to New Situations

Before we can say that students have acquired deep understanding or mastery of skills, it is necessary for them to demonstrate their *ability to apply* any learning related to objectives for explorations and clarifications to unique new situations. Once again, in inquiry teaching and learning, this assessment is built right into what the teacher and students are already doing. Everything that you have ever learned about effective formative assessment that occurs on a daily basis and informs lessons for the next day is organic to inquiry teaching. Likewise, if you have been working on authentic assessment where students are required to apply their newly acquired understandings or skills to real life situations, you are going to be very comfortable with inquiry teaching. Authentic assessment is one of the key components of inquiry teaching.
