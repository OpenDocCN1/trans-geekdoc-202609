For me, inquiry-based methods have slain the proverbial two birds with one stone: how to engage reluctant learners and how to help all students develop and hone their higher-order thinking faculties. I have worked with students for years in co-creative classroom environments that demand and reward student independence and interdependence. Students in my classroom work cooperatively, as a class and in small groups; it is rare that all of us are pursuing the same task in the same fashion all at once. Within their groups, students have tasks for which they are individually accountable—differentiated by interest and ability level. At every turn, I attempt to give my students as much opportunity as I can to direct the course of what they learn, and to actively learn from and teach one another, with the teacher serving as guide or facilitator.

From the beginning, I emphasize with students that we are a community—this is not a classroom of 27 students and 1 teacher, but of 28 students and 28 teachers. While I hold a role of authority in the classroom, I am not the sole repository of knowledge. Rather, I try to reinforce how it is our job to teach one another, and we cannot do that unless we have a climate of mutual respect and openness. I am very explicit with these values. During the first few weeks of class, we practice routines such as “how to have a class discussion.” We mutually negotiate rules—I have a few non-negotiables, like *no put-downs*, but I have almost never had to be the one to articulate them, as the kids come up with them on their own. We also mutually design how to transition between activities, how to pass out new materials, and so on. We do the same process for setting up norms for group work, norms for when a substitute teacher is present, and then revisit these norms on occasion as they need clarification or revision.

Activities like our daily check-in (when we go around the room and students give a 10-word or less summary of how they are feeling) or “Don’t Drop the Ball” (where students have to collectively create a sentence in round-robin style) help the kids get to know one another, and practice how to work independently. Although they seem non-content related, they in fact demand of the kids that they develop and use skills in listening to one another, working together towards a common goal, and so on. “Don’t Drop the Ball,” for example, helps students practice listening, paying attention, anticipating, and cooperative goals (the group cannot succeed unless all members pull their weight). As the year goes on and the groups bond more, I vary the type of the games to be less about *get to know you* and more about *how to self-evaluate meaningfully*, and eventually, *how to say goodbye*.
