## 12.2 Political Polarization

People focused on their networks to collect political information, create their political identity, and decide how to vote. Social networking networks, e.g., Twitter or Facebook, have the ability to enhance the value of peer effects in political actions by significantly lowering the costs of interpersonal contact. Social networking, in particular, makes two big improvements to the way people interact: Firstly, the amount of knowledge to which persons have been exposed rises; secondly, the scale and diversity of their personal communication networks are impacted. The former would certainly not alter the political behavior of the population in isolation. Higher interaction with families and close friends, traditionally considered to have a more significant social effect, does not necessarily suggest that people are more open to political messaging, as that knowledge also flows through offline networks.

There seems to be an ongoing debate on whether the internet promotes public dialogue and thereby expands the public sphere or exacerbates a tendency to group like-minded people, thus creating echo chambers for optimistic and pessimistic arguments. The research design sources for the “public sphere or echo chamber” issue are social media, where ordinary people produce, share, and distribute their private content with political opinions and ideas.

The signature phenomenon of our time is political polarization. Even so, in recent years, it has established considerable attention in research and journalism. A rising number of works state that theory as a relevant tool for communication contributes to this trend [14].

What is being discussed in detail might get confusing as the issue of political polarization spreads. A white paper on political polarization by Stanford economist Matthew Gentzkow (2016) is the most helpful and extensive reference for sorting through that jumble. We mainly depend on Gentzkow’s description of the many metrics that have been used to quantify and research political polarization in this passage, and we also add a few other noteworthy measures.

According to Gentzkow (2016), it makes sense to divide polarization research into the following categories: polarization as measured by party identification, political ideology, voting behavior, opinions on policy issues, alignment between policy positions and political identification (also known as “party sorting”), or attitudes towards those who support opposing viewpoints. We will now go into further details about them.

### 12.2.1 Identification of the Parties

Party identification is perhaps the easiest and most direct approach to evaluating political polarization, as Gentzkow (2016) notes. Researchers examined changes in how strongly individuals identify with the two major U.S. political parties—the Republican Party and the Democratic Party—in studies that concentrated on this metric (e.g., Miller, 1991). People are asked to categorize themselves into one of five categories, such as Republican, Lean Republican, Independent, Lean Democrat, and Democrat. This one-dimensional continuum is used to gauge party affiliation. A higher percentage of people moving to the extremes (or “poles”) of the scale would indicate a rise in polarization.

### 12.2.8 Definition of Voting Behavior

Another method of gauging polarization that depends less on subjective factors is voting patterns, as mentioned by Gentzkow (2016). There are at least two basic techniques for analyzing voting behavior to measure polarization. One looks at smaller units of geography, such as counties, and analyzes how voting patterns have shifted there. This is also sometimes called “geographic sorting” (see, for example, Fiorina & Abrams, 2008). These studies look for signs of polarization by calculating the share of very unbalanced regions. Some studies, for instance, track the growth or decline of “landslide counties” (counties in which the victorious candidate wins an overwhelming majority of the vote) through time (Bishop, 2009). There would be signs of polarization if there were more regions with disproportionate vote totals (so-called “landslide counties,” for example). Another method of measuring polarization through voting patterns looks at how people vote in different election types and levels (such as congressional vs. presidential elections).

### 12.2.9 Policy Positions (Definition)

Measuring attitudes on important policy issues like health care, immigration, and gun control may also reveal polarization. Under this method, as Gentzkow (2016) rightly points out, there are at least two ways to assess whether polarization has taken place. First, as Fiorina and Abrams (2008) contend, polarization may be evinced by a change in the shape of the overall, aggregated distribution of attitudes on policy issues from one that is unimodal to one that is more bimodal. This looks at the views of all partisans together. However, another way that one may assess polarization about policy issues is to first separate people into political camps (i.e., Republicans and Democrats) and assess whether the positions of members of these parties diverge over time.
