## Making Invisible Bluewater Visible Using Machine and Deep Learning Techniques–A Review

Dineshkumar Singh¹* and Vishnu Sharma²

¹Department of Computer Science and Engineering FCE, Poornima University, Jaipur, India

²Department of Computer Science and Application FCE, Poornima University, Jaipur, India

*Corresponding author: 2022phdodd.dinesh12942@poornima.edu.in

### Abstract

Two billion people across the globe are still far from a viable source of potable water and rely on rain and groundwater for daily living and farming. The supply of groundwater is limited. In India itself, groundwater abstraction beyond the safe limit is causing rapid groundwater table depletion at a rate of 1–2 m/year in many districts. Uncontained and unplanned usage may affect food production by 20%. Due to the significant impact of this imperceptible resource on various aspects of life—the economy, the environment, and society—there is a pressing need to enhance the scientific comprehension, estimation, and administration of groundwater management. A scientific framework for the demarcation of its potential storage and recharge zonal maps, i.e., groundwater potential storage zone (GWPSZ) and groundwater potential recharge zone (GWRZ), can be instrumental in this regard to help urban and rural water committees objectively manage the resources at the regional level.

This article examines the endeavors of multiple researchers who have employed statistical modeling and GIS-remote sensing alongside the analytical hierarchy process, or multi-influencing factors techniques (MIF), to understand, prognosticate groundwater, and create zonal maps. Additionally, we scrutinized the GW research using different forms of machine learning (ML)-based algorithms like decision tree (DT), support vector machine (SVM), random forest, and the composition of hybrid multi-wavelet artificial neural network-based models for the prediction of the GW table or its variations over a given time duration. This review suggests the decisive parameters to be considered for analyzing the groundwater potential and the appropriate ML algorithms and techniques to be used for the decision support system and the creation of hydrological thematic maps. This entire pipeline can help in the automatic generation of the zonal maps. This review also summarizes various groundwater domain research questions in data and method identification, application, and future direction.

**Keywords:** Sustainable groundwater levels, GWP zones, storage and recharge, remote sensing, AIML, IoT, groundwater research questions

### 9.1 Introduction

In its Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) no. 6, the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) has set a target “to achieve universal and equitable access to safe and affordable drinking water for all by 2030” [1]. While half of the world’s population struggles with water availability for at least a month every year, for 0.5 billion, it is an almost daily struggle for water throughout the year [2]. This is highly observed in areas with high population density or irrigated agriculture. Fresh surface water and groundwater are the major sources of this water demand. As per the UN World Water Development Report (2022), in 2017, an estimated 959 km³ of groundwater were extracted globally, out of which, 75% were withdrawn in Asia [3]. Water resources present in the Earth’s subsurface, in the rocks or rock fractures and soil pores, are known as groundwater. They could be in an unsaturated or saturated zone [4]. They may be available in a large volume in underground rocks and sediments, geological formations, and a natural reservoir known as an aquifer [5]. Groundwater is not visible, but its impact on human life is quite evident. In India, 89% of the water abstracted from groundwater is used for irrigation. Subsidized or free electricity usage in agriculture prompts growers to increase crop areas under groundwater irrigation, especially in the semi-arid region. Groundwater data analysis from 370 Indian districts from 1995 to 2004 shows an 18% increase in groundwater extraction. The study also found that by reducing the electricity subsidy by 10%, groundwater utilization could come down by 6.6% [6]. Groundwater availability is a delicate balance between its use (in residential areas for daily activities, cultivation, and the goods and services sector) and the quantity supplied (through precipitation, lakes, ponds, or streams). When the annual water discharge (or extraction) is greater than the recharge, it leads to groundwater level depletion. India has a surplus of 20 million tube wells, open wells, and borewells, which is an indication of the massive amount of groundwater being pumped every day. If left unmanaged, this pace will adversely affect future groundwater availability in the coming years [7].
