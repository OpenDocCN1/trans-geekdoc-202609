
Bhargava et al. [22] emphasized that machine learning was not sufficient for the classification of various objects; only by using machine learning, the results were not up to the benchmark to solve this problem, and they put forward the use of deep learning-based techniques for this purpose. They proposed effective methods of satellite image classification based on deep learning using CNN for feature extraction and tested them on UC Merced, SIRI-WHU, and Corel-1K datasets with transfer learning and fine-tuning on ResNet50. This technique was able to achieve 94% accuracy. In this research, a single model, which is ResNet50, was used and was fine-tuned for performance. Various models can also be used for the same problem statement.

Luus et al. [7] proposed a multi-view deep learning-based system for land-use classification that was able to achieve 93% accuracy on UC Merced data, compared to the scale-invariant feature transform (SIFT)-based method which was able to achieve 85.37% and 90.26% in unsupervised feature learning. Deep learning performed better when compared to the traditional approach.

Szegedy et al. [24] provided thorough assessments of current advances in satellite image scene classification, including benchmark datasets and cutting-edge methodologies. The authors then suggested a large-scale, publicly available dataset to enable the community to design, and by analyzing the constraints of existing datasets, propose and evaluate novel data-driven algorithms. Finally, using the suggested dataset, they assessed a variety of representative state-of-the-art approaches, including deep learning-based methods, and provided the results as a helpful performance benchmark for future studies.

Andrew et al. [23] proposed that the network based on pre-trained ImageNet weights performs an important role in remote sensing scene classification. The model trained from scratch and the fine-tuned model trained on ImageNet have been compared, and there was a small gap between them. The models that were trained from scratch outperformed the models that were trained on ImageNet as feature extractors, but in most of the experiments, the results were in favor of the pre-trained model for high-resolution remote sensing images.

Kumar et al. [25] noted that deep learning is a huge improvement in computer vision and also provides the best performance in noisy and low-quality data. To improve the performance and effectiveness of CNN, they proposed the use of feedback and recurrent features to improve the existing feed-forward CNN on satellite images for solar power plant classification.

## 4.3 Methodology

In the proposed network architecture, as shown in Figure 4.1, the base model is an architecture of any deep learning model with ImageNet weights. We explored the VGG, ResNet, Inception, Xception, EfficientNet, MobileNet, and NasNet models. All models were extended with three fully connected layers of 2048, 1024, and 512 neurons for uniformity and performance enhancement. After that, the model converged into 21 classes, which is the output layer of every model. The UC Merced dataset was pre-processed before being fed to the model, as images were converted to grayscale along with resizing operations.

### 4.3.1 Pre-Trained Models

A pre-trained model is one that has already been taught to solve a problem. For pre-trained models, we used Keras Applications, a collection of deep learning models that can be used with already trained ImageNet weights. This collection consists of 31 different models belonging to eight families and two versions. The major models belong to the families of VGG, Xception, Inception, ResNet, DenseNet, NasNet, EfficientNet, and MobileNet. Among these models, EfficientNet, ResNet, and MobileNet have two versions each.

### 4.3.2 Dataset

The dataset used in the study was UC Merced Land-Use Dataset, which was made publicly available on October 28, 2010. It consists of 21 classes with 100 images per class, for a total of 2,100 images with a 256 x 256-pixel resolution.

### 4.3.3 Training Processes

The UC Merced dataset is divided into three parts: training, testing, and validation, with 80%, 10%, and 10%, respectively. Since each class has 100 images, the first 100 were given the label '0' and the next 100 were given the label '1' for all the classes. An array contains the names of all classes. Combining the array and label together forms the ground truth for the classification. Every model in the experiment has a deep learning model as its foundation, with three layers for performance enhancement. The base or foundation model is frozen first, followed by 18 epochs of training with a batch size of 24. Categorical cross-entropy was used as the loss function throughout the experiment, with RMSprop as the optimizer and max pooling. Figure 4.3 shows the expanding various deep architectures.

### 4.4.2 ResNet Family

The ResNet model is a type of ‘exotic architecture’ that uses micro-architecture modules [16], and is available in 50, 101, and 152 layers. The ResNet50 model was able to achieve 90% accuracy on training data, as shown in Figure 4.5(a). However, the testing accuracy exhibited fluctuations, ranging from 90% to 60%, until the 10th epoch. Subsequently, it dropped significantly at the 12th epoch. After the 8th epoch, the training loss became stable, but the validation loss is still fluctuating. A dramatic rise in validation loss was observed at the 12th epoch, but it returned to normal afterward, as shown in Figure 4.5(b).

In comparison to ResNet50, the ResNet50v2 model is more stable, but the accuracy score is only 20%, as shown in Figure 4.5(c), and the loss is also extremely high, as shown in Figure 4.5(d). Since the input range is 0-1 while the version 2 models accept values in the range of -1 to 1, there is no improvement in performance.
