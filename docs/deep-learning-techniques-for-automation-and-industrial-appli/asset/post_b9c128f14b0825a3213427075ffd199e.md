## 12.3.5 Multitask Learning (MTL)

A typical approach in the ML area is multi-task learning (MTL), which is used to carry out many tasks simultaneously. These techniques provide advantages including improved data efficiency, less overfitting by employing common representations, and quick learning using auxiliary data. However, learning many tasks simultaneously poses significant design and optimization issues, and selecting which tasks to learn simultaneously is a difficult decision.

### 12.3.5.1 Learning-Related Problem

Challenges that do not arise in one task result from learning several tasks. In particular, conflicting demands may arise in many tasks. It is known as negative transfer or destructive interference when improving a model’s performance in one task causes damage to another task with different criteria [20].

### 12.3.5.2 Multi-Task Learning (MTL)

In the MTL learning paradigm, ML models are concurrently trained using data from several tasks to uncover commonalities across a number of related tasks. These common representations enhance data quality, and may hasten learning for subsequent or related tasks, helping to overcome two well-known drawbacks of deep learning (DL): the need for vast amounts of data and high processing demands. However, it has not been simple to arrive at these conclusions, and the area of research is now active. MTL more closely depicts the learning process for humans than a single task, since knowledge integration across domains is a crucial component of human intelligence, as shown in Table 12.1.

### 12.3.5.3 Architectures for Multiple Tasks

The proportion of model parameters that are shared throughout tasks and the feasibility of building and integrating both task-specific and shared modules are among the common factors that must be taken into account while designing a shared architecture. When evaluating designs for a given issue area, other factors come into play, such as how convolutional filters for certain vision tasks are divided into general and task-specific classes. Many of the MTL designs that have been developed strike a compromise between how much information is shared across tasks: a negative transfer caused by too much sharing may make joint multi-task models perform worse for each task than solo models, but a lack of sharing prevents the model from effectively transferring information across tasks. The designs that share effectively are the most effective in MTL. The MTL architecture is divided into four categories by us [21]:

- Task-domain architectural designs,
- Architectural constructions with several modes,
- Known architectural designs, and
- Conditional architecture.

We take into account domains for computer vision, NLP, and reinforcement learning for single-domain architectures. Multimodal architectures handle input tasks in more than one modality, such as responding to visual requests using linguistic and visual features. It is vital to remember that we only accept multimodal designs that support numerous tasks. Architectures are organized in such a way that the same computation may be performed with the same task input across architectural learning phases. In conditional architectures, the architecture chosen for a certain piece of data depends on the data itself.
