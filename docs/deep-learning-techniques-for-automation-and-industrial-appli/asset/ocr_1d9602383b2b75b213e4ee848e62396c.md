# 12.2.8  **Definition of Voting Behavior**

Another method of gauging polarization that depends less on subjective factors is voting patterns, as mentioned by Gentzkow (2016). There are at least two basic techniques for analyzing voting behavior to measure polarization. One looks at smaller units of geography, such as counties, and analyzes how voting patterns have shifted there. This is also sometimes called “geographic sorting” (see, for example, Fiorina & Abrams, 2008). These studies look for signs of polarization by calculating the share of very unbalanced regions. Some studies, for instance, track the growth or decline of “landslide counties” (counties in which the victorious candidate wins an overwhelming majority of the vote) through time (Bishop, 2009). There would be signs of polarization if there were more regions with disproportionate vote totals (so-called “landslide countries,” for example). Another method of measuring polarization through voting patterns looks at how people vote in different election types and levels (such as congressional vs. presidential elections).

# 12.2.9  **Policy Positions (Definition)**

Measuring attitudes on important policy issues like health care, immigration, and gun control may also reveal polarization. Under this method, as Gentzkow (2016) rightly points out, there are at least two ways to assess whether polarization has taken place. First, as Fiorina and Abrams (2008) contend, polarization may be evinced by a change in the shape of the overall, aggregated distribution of attitudes on policy issues from one that is unimodal to one that is more bimodal. This looks at the views of all partisans together. However, another way that one may assess polarization about policy issues is to first separate people into political camps (i.e., Republicans and Democrats) and assess whether the positions of members of these parties diverge over time.
