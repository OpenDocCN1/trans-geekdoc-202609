## 5.3 Research Gap

Before the rise of social media, most of the qualitative studies on the construct of sarcasm focused on verbal form of it [31–34]. However, little is known about how the ways of expressing sarcasm get changed for the limited yet versatile ways of expressing oneself on social media. For example, the non-verbal cues as described in [35] are difficult to express on social media. Existing literature lacks insights about how these non-verbal cues get transformed according to the expressive capability of social networking platforms like Twitter.

Again, we feel that the use of topics in text data as in [5, 9, 24] might limit the application of those approaches to a particular domain of text data generated over a certain period of time. Therefore, it is more important to know the “ways” in which sarcasm gets expressed in text data, i.e., how sentiment overall fluctuates in a sarcastic piece of text data. Thus, knowing the general nature of variation of sentiments in text will be more applicable to text data from more diverse domains, time periods, or languages.

On top of that, the need for multimodality in sarcasm detection has been raised during recent times [29, 33]; we want to investigate this approach in more detail. We think that the collection of multi-modal datasets (i.e., datasets that will have more than text-based data), studying the role of modes of multi-modal data individually, and the gradual performance change of the sarcasm detection process with the inclusion of more modes can be gaps in this regard.

Thus, in the following chapters, we presented a qualitative study focused on users’ ways of expressing sarcasm on social media platforms, sentiment/emotion-based analysis of textual sarcasm data, a multimodal approach to the sarcasm detection process, and evaluated the process and its performance.

## 5.4 Objective

Recognizing sarcasm in Hindi text is an important task for natural language processing to avoid misinterpretation of sarcastic statements as literal statements. Accuracy and robustness of NLP models are often affected by untruthful sentiments that are often of sarcastic nature. Thus, it is important to filter out noisy data from the training data inputs for various NLP-related tasks. For example, a sentence like “So thrilled to be on call for work the entire weekend!” could be naively classified as a sentence with a high positive sentiment. However, it is actually the negative sentiment that is cleverly implied through sarcasm.

Our primary goal is to analyze whether a Hindi language tweet is sarcastic or not.

Computational detection of sarcasm has seen attention from the sentiment analysis community in the past few years. Sarcasm is an interesting problem for sentiment analysis because surface sentiment of words in a sarcastic text may be different from the implied sentiment. For example, ‘रिश्तों में दूरियां तो पहले ही आ चुकी थी... कोरोना ने आकर इल्जाम अपने सर ले लिया........!!” this sentence is sarcastic because it initially expresses a positive intent regarding the existing distance between relations. However, during the COVID-19 pandemic, we are now following social distancing and avoiding physical meetings, which introduces a negative sentiment into the latter part of the sentence.

We have proposed an algorithm to understand whether a Hindi text, sentence, or tweet is sarcastic or not, from the dataset we have acquired. We are trying to train our model so that we can test live streaming Hindi tweets from Twitter API to detect whether they are sarcastic or not. This will result in obtaining accurate sentiment analysis of the sarcastic Hindi sentence, and this result will be very helpful for opinion mining, predicting the customer or user perspective over different social networking platforms like Twitter.
