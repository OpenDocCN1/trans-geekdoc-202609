# ## 9.1 Introduction

In its Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) no. 6, the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) has set a target “to achieve universal and equitable access to safe and affordable drinking water for all by 2030” [1]. While half of the world’s population struggles with water availability for at least a month every year, for 0.5 billion, it is an almost daily struggle for water throughout the year [2]. This is highly observed in areas with high population density or irrigated agriculture. Fresh surface water and groundwater are the major sources of this water demand. As per the UN World Water Development Report (2022), in 2017, an estimated 959 km³ of groundwater were extracted globally, out of which, 75% were withdrawn in Asia [3]. Water resources present in the Earth’s subsurface, in the rocks or rock fractures and soil pores, are known as groundwater. They could be in an unmatured or saturated zone [4]. They may be available in a large volume in underground rocks and sediments, geological formations, and a natural reservoir known as an aquifer [5]. Groundwater is not visible, but its impact on human life is quite evident. In India, 89% of the water abstracted from groundwater is used for irrigation. Subsidized or free electricity usage in agriculture prompts growers to increase crop areas under groundwater irrigation, especially in the semi-arid region. Groundwater data analysis from 370 Indian districts from 1995 to 2004 shows an 18% increase in groundwater extraction. The study also found that by reducing the electricity subsidy by 10%, groundwater utilization could come down by 6.6% [6]. Groundwater availability is a delicate balance between its use (in residential areas for daily activities, cultivation, and the goods and services sector) and the quantity supplied (through precipitation, lakes, ponds, or streams). When the annual water discharge (or extraction) is greater than the recharge, it leads to groundwater level depletion. India has a surplus of 20 million tubes, open wells, and borewells, which is an indication of the massive amount of groundwater being pumped every day. If left unmanaged, this pace will adversely affect future groundwater availability in the coming years [7].
