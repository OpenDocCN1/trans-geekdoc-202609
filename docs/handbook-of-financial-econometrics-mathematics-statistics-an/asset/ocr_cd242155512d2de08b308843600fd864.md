A high quality firm might prefer to have a higher level of collateral — given that it has a lower likelihood of defaulting on its obligations — and in return receive a lower interest rate. Low quality firms may be concerned with losing their collateral, and may find it too expensive to mimic. Consequently, a separating equilibrium ensues. So, firms using foreign bank debt collateral may be signaling to the market that they are of high quality. For this reason, one might expect that stockholders may view collateral posted against foreign bank loans in a positive way, while viewing collateral posted against domestic bank loans as either conveying very little information. To examine this, we include dummy variables for local bank and foreign bank collateral to examine whether foreign bank collateral has a differential impact on performance than does collateral pledged to local banks.¹¹ COLLATDB and COLLATFB are dummy variables that equal one if domestic and foreign corporate borrowings are secured by collateral, respectively.

We begin our analysis with an Ordinary Least Squares regression analysis. Tobin’s Q is our measure of firm performance. Tobin’s Q has been a widely employed proxy for both a firm’s growth opportunities and value or performance. Lang et al. (1989), Howe et al. (1992), Doukas (1995), and Szewczyk et al. (1996) are among a host of papers that use Q for this purpose. Similar to these prior papers, we calculate Q by summing the market value of equity and the book value of debt and dividing this sum by the total of the book value of equity and the book value of debt.

Our regression model is specified in equation (25.1):
