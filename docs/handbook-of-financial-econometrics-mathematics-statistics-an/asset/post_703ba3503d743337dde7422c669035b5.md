### Abstract

This study relies on a structural approach model to investigate the determinants of Credit Default Swap (CDS) spread changes for Euro-zone’s financial institutions over the period January 2005 to October 2015. Going beyond the structural model, this study incorporates features such as the role of systemic risk factors, bank-specific characteristics and credit ratings. We adopt the dynamic framework provided by panel Vector Autoregressive Models which allows for endogeneity issues and this is a novelty of our approach. The main findings are that structural models seem to be more relevant during highly volatile periods and that the relation between the CDS and its theoretical determinants is not constant over time. Overall, the empirical results suggest that structural models perform well in explaining bank credit risk, but determinants of CDS also rely on the underlying economic situation which should be taken into consideration when interpreting CDS spread changes.

Credit Default Swaps • Bank credit risk • Panel vector auto-regressions.

### 60.1 Introduction

In the last two decades we have witnessed the extensive use of financial market information, by market participants and policy makers, in order to obtain answers to questions referring to an array of issues ranging from the business cycle stance to the level of stability in the financial system. Among the many market instruments that are being monitored a prominent role has been assigned to Credit Default Swap (CDS) contracts, the pricing of which hopefully offers an accurate estimate of the expected probability of default as perceived by market participants.[^1] The collapse of some of the largest banks during the Global Financial Crisis (GFC) has given impetus to research that segment of the CDS market that is related to financial institutions. The main issue of concern here has been the relevance of the banks’ CDS spreads to the intrinsic credit risks these institutions are facing.

[^1]: The CDS market experienced a spectacular growth in the early 2000s, when it reached its peak in the first half of 2007 with a nominal market size of US $57 billion, although its net market value was a small fraction of the nominal size, reaching US $5 billion at the same time. Since then the market has shrunk to its pre-crisis levels, this being partly the outcome of the significant regulatory overhaul this market has gone through in the aftermath of the Global Financial Crisis (GFC).

Some critics of the CDS market claim that CDS exacerbated the GFC since they hastened the collapse of institutions like Lehman Brothers and therefore they contributed to the development of systemic risks. Effectively, those critics claim that the CDS market functions in an inefficient way due to the lack of transparency, as a result of the absence of standardization, the inadequate central clearing of contracts as well as the speculative nature of the “naked” contracts which create huge incentives for defaults. On the other hand, there are those who claim that the CDS market functioned effectively during the GFC in spreading risk, as it was intended to do, and furthermore it did not wreak havoc to the markets after the collapse of Lehman Brothers, as many feared, since the net payouts were at the region of $7 billion far below the estimated $400 billion outstanding contracts.

Even if CDS spreads price efficiently the risk of default for the reference loans, an issue of paramount importance for the financial markets stability supervisory authorities is the identification of the driving factors behind the CDS spread changes. It is clear that micro-prudential regulation is needed if the main sources for defaults are to be found within the institutions issuing the reference bonds, while a macro-prudential regulation is more useful if systemic risk factors are behind the increase of the default probabilities (Acharya et al., 2014).

This study empirically investigates the determinants of CDS spread changes for Euro-zone’s FI over the period January 2005 to October 2015, covering both tranquil times, the GFC and the Euro-area sovereign debt crisis. We use as a benchmark, the structural model developed by Merton (1974) to investigate the influence of theoretical determinants on the quarterly changes in bank CDS spreads during an eleven-year period between January 2005 and October 2015. The literature suggests that the impact of variables from structural models seem to be strongly time varying (see Alexander and Kaeck, 2008; González-Hermosillo, 2008; Chiaramonte and Casu, 2012) and that they lose their explanatory power when applied to FI (see Raunig and Scheicher, 2009; Grammatikos and Vermeulen, 2012).

The empirical investigation is conducted by means of Panel Vector Autoregressive (PVAR) models that allow for the efficient estimation of parameters in systems with endogenous variables and a limited number of observations while at the same time they make possible the use of dynamic models. The choice of the PVAR models has been necessitated by the need to address the issue of endogeneity of equity returns and their volatility, which traditionally are treated as exogenous determinants of CDS spreads. Moreover, in the presence of thin trading for some CDS contracts and information asymmetries generated by the absence of central clearing and standardization of the CDS contracts, it is reasonable to expect frequent corrections of the quoted CDS prices, which rationalize the choice of dynamic representations.
