Davis (1998) applied option pricing in incomplete markets given certain utility function. He derives initial principles from BS ones. In modeling, he proposes a certain utility function at time given a certain marginal rate of substitution. In order for the function to be defined, he proposes a lemma made up of a real function. At maximum point, the lemma is similar to Radon–Nikodym derivative. Then, Davis (1998) uses a standard discrete model which is independent of Weiner process. Thus, the model incorporates a Markov process. And the standard model takes an exponential shape. For advanced modeling, he extends a stochastic process to include Weiner process where the discrete process is underpinned by bond, stock and wealth. That, resulted is the model by jumpy due to the drift of the stock. When he incorporated transaction costs, stocks jumped further. A similar process will be illustrated in the formulation section of the chapter. Finally, arbitrage opportunities emerged to the incompleteness of the environment.

In relation to pricing options when consumer economy is heterogeneous, Benninga and Mayshar (2000) stated that a less risk-averse consumer is not so much concerned with downside risk protection. And that corresponds to dominated consumption and consumption priced in high states. For pricing options with heterogeneous tastes, they state that the economy will generate prices similar to the homogenous economy. In the context of the option pricing theory (OPT), findings of Benninga and Mayshar (2000) are prevalent when one is invested in out-of-the-money options, especially in puts. They went further and calibrate their findings. Calibrated results illustrate that far out-of-the-money options are more expensive in a heterogeneous economy. Another reason why out-of-the-money options are expensive is that they are hardly traded. The volatility smile (i.e., implied volatility) of calibrated options is log-normally distributed; however, overall distribution curves are normally shaped. Further, the results illustrated that distribution curves have fat tails.

Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002), and Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) exemplify optimal equilibrium models when markets are incomplete.
