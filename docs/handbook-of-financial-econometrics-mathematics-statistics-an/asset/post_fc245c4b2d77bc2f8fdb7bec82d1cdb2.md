
## 24.3.3 Trading in financial markets with frictions

In an incomplete market, using no-arbitrage argument to price options will suffer from the problem of frictions such as the transaction costs of dynamic replicating strategies (Leland, 1985; Bensaid et al., 1992; Edirisinghe et al., 1993) and the derivation of option bounds as well (Henin and Pistre, 1998; Constantinides and Zariphopoulou, 1999, 2001; Masson and Perrakis, 2000; Constantinides and Perrakis, 2002; Constantinides et al., 2009). In this section, we review some studies that have investigated the tighter bounds derived by adding the consideration of market imperfections such as transaction costs or bid-ask spreads.

Henin and Pistre (1998) use this stochastic dominance method to derive bounds of European concave option prices incorporated with transaction costs and dividend payments on underlying asset. A concave option is one whose exercise payoff is a concave function of the difference between the underlying asset and the strike price. The bounding conditions for European concave options only depend on final distribution of the stock price which has adjusted to dividends. The transaction costs only affect the initial costs of replication strategy because of its European style. Instead of dealing with transaction costs directly, Masson and Perrakis (2000) pursue the tight option bounds by the consideration of bid-ask spread of option prices. Their upper and lower bounds follow two different risk-neutral distributions related to the implied volatilities of observed ask and bid option prices, respectively.

Many studies concentrate the analysis on option bounds in an incomplete market with proportional transaction costs. Constantinides and Zariphopoulou (1999, 2001) derive the bounds for option prices with proportional transaction costs and investors’ preferences. Constantinides and Perrakis (2002) extend Constantinides and Zariphopoulou’s (1999, 2001) approach under stochastic dominance framework. Their tight upper bound of the reservation write price of a call option is related to the transaction costs of selling and purchasing a stock. On the other hand, their tight lower bound of the reservation purchasing price of a put option is also based on the factors of transaction costs and the return rate on the bond. The advantages of the bound formulas are as follows:
- these bounds of European options can be viewed as a generalization of single-period model to a multi-period economy since they are independent of the trading frequency;
- The upper (lower) bound on the write price of a put (call) option can be derived from the upper (lower) bound on the write price of a call (put) option and the put-call parity.
Furthermore, the bounds of European option price also can compute the implied volatility bounds. Constantinides and Perrakis (2002) show implied volatility skew of the bounds on European options, that is, the implied volatility of both upper and lower bounds is decreasing in the strike-to-price ratio. Constantinides et al. (2009) also consider market frictions involved in the trading frequency, bid-ask spread and the transaction costs into the derivations of European option bounds under the assumption that traders’ assets consist of a riskless bond, a stock with cash dividends over time and European options. They conclude that S&P 500 index option market is not rational because the empirical results violate stochastic dominance. The detailed results are discussed in the section of empirical applications.

## 24.4 The Option Bounds for American Options and Asian Options

In this section, the previous studies that investigate the option bounds for American options, Asian options, currency cross-rate options, and real options are reviewed in detail.

### 24.4.1 American option bounds

American option valuation requires considering not only the option pricing problem itself but also simultaneous determination of the early exercise boundary. Most previous papers use lattice model to estimate American options because there doesn’t exist closed-form function for American style options. A lattice model is a tree model with nodes to represent the time and state spaces of underlying assets in order to approximate the American option price. Practical implementation involves numerical methods that may become computationally burdensome, in particular, when there are multiple state variables. Carr, Jarrow and Myneni (1992) derive the upper bound for American put options as a European put option plus a fixed premium. Chaudhury and Wei (1994) studied the bounds on American future options. Broadie and Detemple (1996) derive the lower bound of the American call option by using lattice model where the optimal exercise boundary of the American call option is set as one of nodes on binomial tree. Perrakis (1986) applies the bounds by Perrakis and Ryan (1984) to obtain the bounds for American put option that can be represented in the binomial model.

In contrast with heavy computational burden in lattice method, Chen and Yeh (2002) derived closed-form upper bounds under stochastic interest rate, stochastic volatility and jumps model. Chung and Chang (2007) further generalize Chen and Yeh’s (2002) upper bounds to solve the case of inadequate upper bounds for American options when the interest rate is smaller than the dividend yield. They also extend their upper bounds to American options with several underlying assets. Handley (2005) utilizes the arbitrage strategy to derive the upper bound American call options with multiple known dividends. The arbitrage strategy for American call options is like a trade-off decision between the underlying asset’s dividend received and the value of an American call option exercised on ex-dividend date before maturity. Based on this insight, Handley (2005) derives the upper bounds for American call options which depends on the value of dividends received compared to the strike price of the contract.

In addition, some studies obtain the tighter bounds for American option in incomplete market. Perrakis and Lefoll (1997, 2000, 2004) investigate the American option bounds with proportional transaction costs in lattice method framework. Constantinides and Perrakis (2007) extend the bounds by Constantinides and Zariphopoulou (1999, 2001) to American option markets with transaction costs in stochastic dominance framework.

### 24.4.2 Asian option bounds

An Asian option is a kind of exotic option and its price is determined by the average underlying prices over whole period, not only dependent on the price of the asset at the maturity date. Even though there is no closed-form solution for an Asian option, the bounds of an Asian option can be derived by replication of a portfolio incorporated with other related financial derivatives. Nielsen and Sandmann (2003) express bounds of an Asian call option as the portfolio of delayed payment European call options which depend on the exercise price of the Asian option. By using put-call parity, the bounds of an Asian put option can be derived as well. Vanmaele et al. (2006) investigate bounds for Asian options under the assumption that the underlying asset follows lognormal distribution. They employ the bounds approach proposed by Kaas et al. (2000) for sums of random variables and then obtain computable bounds for Asian options. Asian option bounds not only can appropriately evaluate the prices of Asian options, but also can be applied to hedging strategies. Nielsen and Sandmann (2003) and Vanmaele et al. (2006) also discuss hedging positions based on their Asian option bounds.

## 24.5 Empirical Applications

In this section, we survey the papers that apply option bounds in empirical work. The data and major results in each study are briefly described and categorized by markets of underlying assets.

### 24.5.1 Equities and equity indices

The options with underlying assets in equity market are the most investigated and priced in both academic and industry. The option bounds can hence be used to price the options which the underlying assets are traded in equity market. Chung et al. (1997) use option data with either stock price or index as underlying asset from 1987 to 1989 to test the law of one price proposed by Chen and Welch (1991) that options are redundant if they can be replicated by its underlying stock. According to Chung et al. (1997)’s major results, they found that the distributions of trading volumes for options on both stock and index are bound in a narrow range when underlying assets remain constant. The narrow width of option trade price bands not only confirms Chen and Welch (1991)’s single price law, but also support the sense of using option bounds to price option price without complex option models.

The option pricing bounds also can be transferred to the bounds of Black–Scholes implied volatilities to compare the implied volatilities of stock options and test market efficiency. Cochrane and Saa-Requejo (2000) use S&P 500 index options which expire in the end of June, September, and December, 1996 to compare its implied volatilities with the implied volatilities of their option bounds. The results show that Cochrane and Saa-Requejo’s bounds are only valid for at-the-money options. Masson and Perrakis (2000) use the implied volatilities of observed ask and bid call options on S&P 500 during 1993–1995 and obtain tight option bounds which only less than 0.2% of observations fall outside these bounds. Constantinides, Jackwerth and Perrakis (2009) utilize implied volatility criteria based on Constantinides and Perrakis (2002) options bound model to test whether S&P 500 index options market is rational or not. It is the first large-scale S&P 500 empirical investigation of whether the observed cross sections of 1-month S&P 500 index option prices over 1986-2006 are consistent with various economic models that explicitly allow for a dynamically incomplete market and an imperfect market that recognizes trading costs and bid-ask spreads. They used both the unconditional distribution and the conditional distribution of the stock returns to avoid that the problem of mismatching S&P 500 index options in Black–Scholes–Merton (BSM) model is contributed to the assumption of lognormal distribution of the index returns. The unconditional distribution of the stock returns is based on the historical data; on the other hand, the conditional distribution of the stock returns can be calculated by three different ways: GARCH(1, 1) process, Black-Scholes implied volatility method, or Chicago Board Options Exchange Volatility Index(VIX) data.
