## Abstract

In this chapter, we show that, as the world becomes increasingly integrated, the benefits of global diversification still remain positive and economically significant over time. Both regression analysis and explanatory power tests show that international integration, measured by adjusted $R^2$ from a multifactor model, has more profound impact on the diversification benefits than correlation. Our results support Roll (2013)’s argument that $R^2$, but not correlation, is an appropriate measure of market integration. We examine the impact of market integration determinants such as default risk, inflation, TED spread, past local equity market return, liquidity, and the relative performance of domestic portfolio on the potential diversification benefits.

Diversification benefits • Investment constraints • International portfolio.

## 13.1 Introduction

The effectiveness of international diversification has been well documented.¹ As the global market has increasingly integrated over the past decades, several critical issues related to asset management emerge: Are the benefits of international diversification to local investors still significant? How do economic dynamics and market integration affect the time-varying benefits of diversification across countries? Noticeably, these questions are appealing to academia as well as to Wall Street, particularly during the financial crises. Since portfolio feasibility and temporal dynamics in return are not taken into account in the conventional model, it is essential to consider these factors in optimal diversification for designing practical strategies. In this chapter, we (i) analyze the impact of global market integration on the time-variation of diversification benefits while considering feasibility; (ii) explore how dynamics in economies affect the diversification benefits to local investors across countries; and (iii) study the economic significance and explanatory power of these dynamics.

### What Drives Variation in the International Diversification Benefits?

Two contradictory forces in markets found by the recent studies leave the relation between international integration and diversification benefits with an open question. The lack of perfect business cycle synchronization across markets serves as a foundation of diversification. As the markets become more integrated, the proportion of global systematic risk of local assets increases, but the percentage of idiosyncratic volatility that can be offset by global diversification diminishes (e.g., Christoffersen et al., 2012; Parsley and Schlag, 2007; McDowell, 2018a). The fact that the non-diversifiable risk in each country has stronger impact on the aggregate volatility in the global optimal portfolio leads to a decrease in diversification benefits. This negative relation between market integration and diversification benefits is assumed by academics and practitioners. However, the expected returns in domestic markets have declined as the markets become opened (e.g., Ang et al., 2009; Bekaert and Harvey, 2003; Bekaert et al., 2005; Bekaert et al., 2009), which should have a positive effect on the benefits of global diversification. We study how the determinants of market integration such as price level, growth, interest rates, risk premiums, and the dynamics of financial markets influence international diversification benefits. Driessen and Laeven (2007) conclude political risk estimated from the International Country Risk Guide (ICRG) rating is the only key determinant of diversification benefits. Other studies including Bekaert et al. (2011) and Christoffersen et al. (2012) examine the determinants of market integration. Since market integration and diversification benefits might be endogenously determined, we apply instrumental variables and GMM.

This paper differs from the previous studies in that we study the relation between market integration and diversification benefits while controlling other variables that impact international diversification. The literature has shown that the international market has increasingly integrated in the past half century (e.g., Errunza et al., 1992; Bekaert and Harvey, 1995; Bae et al., 2003, 2003; Stulz and Williamson, 2003; Bekaert et al., 2005; De Jong and De Roon, 2003, 2005; Goetzmann et al., 2005; Bekaert et al., 2007; Pukthuanthong and Roll, 2009). But none of the studies have shown the impact of market integration on diversification benefits while controlling the effect of the related economic variables.

Market integration can hardly be described by one factor. Our study applies a straightforward measure developed by Pukthuanthong and Roll (2009), R² from a regression on a number of factors obtained by applying the principal component (PC) analysis, to explain the spanned portion. As suggested by Bekaert et al. (2011), Bekaert and Wang (2009), and Carrieri et al. (2013), we find the patterns of integration differ from country to country.

We further analyze the impact of the economic variables that are used to characterize the business cycle on the diversification benefits in 21 developed countries and 13 emerging markets.

We find market integration is more statistically and economically significant than correlation in explaining diversification benefits when controlling various economic variables. Diversification is believed to be effective when assets have low or negative correlation in a portfolio. However, correlation and other correlation-based metrics have been subject to a severe criticism. Bekaert et al. (2009) argue that correlations are an important concept in the analysis of international diversification benefits and international financial market integration, but are not a perfect measure of either concept. Pukthuanthong and Roll (2009) also mention the simple correlation between broad financial market index returns from two countries can be a poor measure of their economic integration. Carrieri et al. (2007) and Christoffersen et al. (2012) suggest that correlations do not provide a complete and accurate measure of overall market integration. When using the correlation with a single factor, one just simplifies but fails to fully account for the structure of risk by incorporating the full covariance matrix. Carrieri et al. (2007) and Pukthuanthong and Roll (2009) agree that there are multiple factors that should be used to measure integration. We then include both market integration and correlation as determinants of diversification and compare their impact.

To examine the relation between diversification benefit and market integration, we develop direct measures of the global diversification gains that model both returns and correlations, examine their dynamics, and further analyze how global and local economic factors affect diversification benefits in a wide range of countries. De Roon et al. (2001), Eun et al. (2010), Ehling and Ramos (2006), Eiling et al. (2012), Harvey (1995), Li et al. (2003), Mullen and Berrill (2017), and Pástor and Stambaugh (2000) have shown that domestic investors can enhance their investment performance by including assets from other markets when short-sales are not allowed. Similar to Bekaert and Urias (1996), Chiou (2009), and Driessen and Laeven (2007), we use an increase in Sharpe ratio and a reduction in volatility to estimate the mean-variance benefits. Our measure considers the infeasibility of strategies associated with low asset diversity in diversification.
