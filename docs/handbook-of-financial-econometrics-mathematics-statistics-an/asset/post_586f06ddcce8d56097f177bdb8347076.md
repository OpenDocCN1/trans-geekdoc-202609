The disposition effect exists in financial markets by using individual and/or professional investor-level data (Odean, 1998; Barber and Odean, 1999; Garvey and Murphy, 2004; Dhar and Ning, 2006; Barber et al., 2007; Chong, 2009; Ammann et al., 2012; Frino et al., 2015; Wulfmeyer, 2016; Muhl and Talpsepp, 2018; Leal et al., 2018).¹ In addition, a line of lab literature finds that subjects reveal the disposition effect bias (Weber and Camerer, 1998; Wong et al., 2006; Da Costa et al., 2008, 2013; Fischbacher et al., 2014; Rau, 2014; Ploner, 2017; Kohsaka et al., 2017). Some studies further indicate that the disposition effect is negatively associated with investment performance (Odean, 1998; Weber and Camerer, 1998; Garvey and Murphy, 2004; Wong et al., 2006; Chong, 2009; Ammann et al., 2012; Leal et al., 2018).

An authentic test of the disposition effect is usually difficult by using real market data because investors’ decisions cannot be controlled or observed in real markets (Weber and Camerer, 1998; Da Costa et al., 2013). Besides, the aggregate data offer no direct insight into the decision-making processes of individual investors (Richards et al., 2018). Therefore, when some studies that use investor-level or market-level data employ cognitive/emotional biases to account for the disposition effect, the link is still ambiguous. Only very few studies attempt to examine the relationship between cognitive/emotional biases and the disposition effect while these studies generally highlight one single bias, such as regret avoidance (e.g., Fogel and Berry, 2006; Lin et al., 2006) or self-control (Fischbacher et al., 2014; Richards et al., 2017). Unlike existing literature, this study adopts multiple cognitive/emotional biases and proposes a conceptual model to examine the relationship among cognitive/emotional biases, the disposition effect, and investment performance simultaneously.

Based on previous research, this study adopts mental accounting, regret avoidance, and self-control (bias) to investigate whether these biases influence the disposition effect, resulting consequently in underperformance. In addition, prior literature has indicated that demographic variables might influence investors’ behavioral decision-makings (Barber and Odean, 2001; Da Costa et al., 2008, 2013; Rau, 2014; Lu et al., 2016). Therefore, this study also examines whether gender and marital status moderate the relationship between cognitive/emotional biases and the disposition effect. Furthermore, from the empirical results, this study can develop strategic implications that can mitigate investors’ biases and reduce the disposition effect.

This study adopts a questionnaire survey to collect quantitative data and further employs an SEM approach to examine the proposed conceptual model. An SEM approach is broadly utilized in marketing research, which is usually related to consumers’ cognitive dissonances (e.g., perceived value, perceived service quality, etc.). These dissonances are latent variables measured by observed variables (i.e., question items). Many marketing studies adopt the SEM approach to examine the influences of cognitive dissonances on acceptance of specific products and services (Kleijnen et al., 2004; Olorunniwo et al., 2006; Soureli et al., 2008; Theron et al., 2008; Tu et al., 2011; Fernando and Alben, 2015). Like cognitive dissonances, cognitive/emotional biases are latent variables, which means that the SEM approach is appropriate in examining whether these biases influence investor’s behavior (MacCallum and Austin, 2000; McDonald and Ho, 2002). To the best of my knowledge, only a very few studies to date have utilized the SEM approach when examining the disposition effect.

The results of this study find that mental accounting and regret avoidance are significant biases that influence the disposition effect, resulting consequently in underperformance. Mental accounting is the most significant factor influencing the disposition effect. As such, prospect theory (an S-shaped value function) could be an alternative to expected utility theory (expected utility function) to account for investors’ behavioral decisions. However, there is no evidence to show that investors’ self-imposed disciplines work efficiently, which implies that investors have self-control bias. The findings of moderating analysis indicate that female investors and married ones are more likely to be influenced by mental accounting, whereas male investors and single ones are more easily affected by regret avoidance. Moreover, female investors seem to have greater disposition effect bias.

This study offers several substantial contributions. Firstly, unlike prior literature, this study examines multiple cognitive/emotional biases to uncover the relationship between these biases and the disposition effect. Secondly, this study employs an appropriate approach to conduct the estimation procedure. The SEM approach can provide more precision for model specification, and increase confidence in the proposed measures for decision-making and substantive hypothesis testing. Finally, based on the empirical results, this study highlights several strategic implications that might assist investors, financial institutions, and regulatory authorities in developing their investment strategies, promoting their financial products and services, and improving financial discipline, respectively.

The study is organized as follows: Section 16.2 outlines the hypotheses, Section 16.3 illustrates the proposed conceptual model and the questionnaire, Section 16.4 discusses the empirical results, and Section 16.5 offers concluding remarks and strategy implications.

## 16.2 Hypothesis

Behavioral finance theory indicates that cognitive/emotional biases result in investors’ behavioral biases, such as the disposition effect, escalation of commitment, and herding effect. Following Shefrin and Statman (1985), this study adopts three cognitive/emotional biases, mainly consisting of mental accounting, regret avoidance, and self-control to investigate whether these biases result in the disposition effect.²

### 16.2.1 Mental accounting

Mental accounting, a cognitive bias, refers to the implicit methods that investors use to frame and evaluate their outcomes (gains and losses). Loss aversion means that investors are more sensitive to losses than gains. Kahneman and Tversky (1979) employ certainty effect, reflection effect, and isolation effect to demonstrate an S-shaped value function. According to the S-shaped value function, investors frame their outcomes into different accounts (gains and losses) relative to a reference point and reveal loss aversion. Therefore, mental accounting is a core element in prospect theory.

Thaler (1985, 1999) extends the S-shaped value function to incorporate compound outcomes and uses it to summarize four principles, namely segregate gains, integrate losses, cancel losses against larger gains, and segregate “silver linings”, while consumers use mental accounting to frame their outcomes in whatever way makes them the happiest. Barberis and Huang (2001) indicate that investors are in loss aversion over the fluctuation of individual stocks rather than over the fluctuation of the portfolio of stocks, which implies that investors use a method of narrow framing.
