In equation (61.3), y_it is the time-t observation from the ith maturity or cross-section of the yield curve, β_i is a N×1 vector of factor loadings and it describes how the yields relate to the ith principal component factor at time-t f_it. Finally, ε_it is the idiosyncratic noise component at time-t corresponding to the ith bond yield. In most applications ε_it i.i.d. → N(0, σ²). As a small demonstration of the suitability of PCA to parameter estimation in the factor model setting, we use zero coupon yield curve data from the US term structure spanning the period January 2000 through December 2017 to conduct principal component regressions (PCR). To implement PCR, we compute the eigenvectors using PCA, then, we regress the actual yield on the eigenvectors and finally, we compute the fitted yield as the product of the principal component factor and the factor loading as prescribed above. The root mean squared error representing the difference between the actual yield and the aforementioned fitted yield was of about order of magnitude 10⁻¹² for each yield. Moreover, the computation was completed in a matter of only a few seconds. We can also see from this discussion that, in principle, the application of PCA to a problem works well when large variances have important interpretations (e.g., see Schlens, 2005). Litterman and Schienkman (1991) note that the first three principal components explain about 98% of the variation in the yield curve, and they interpret these factors as level, slope, and curvature based on how their changes impact bond yields. Hence, based on its suitability to linear factor structures, the ease of computational burden on the computer, and remarkable fit of the yield curve, early applications of PCA to the modeling of DTSM involve estimation with ordinary least squares regressions.
