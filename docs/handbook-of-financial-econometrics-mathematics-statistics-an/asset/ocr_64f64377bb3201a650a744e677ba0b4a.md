In contrast to linear correlation, a copula captures the complete dependence structure inherent in a random vector. Different copulas exhibit different tail dependencies, ranging from tail independence to tail dependence, and different types of asymmetries (Chen and Tu, 2013), which makes it possible to integrate risks involved in a wide variety of dependence structures. Among all types of copulas, frequently used copulas include Gaussian copula and t copula from the elliptical copula family and Gumbel copula, Clayton copula and Frank copula from the Archimedean copula family (Cherubini et al., 2004; Koliai, 2016). The Gaussian copula is very popular in practice, at first due to the common assumption of normality in many financial modeling applications and its ease of understanding. However, it does not allow for tail dependence, marked as “×” in the rows of tail behavior, which is a major drawback that makes it a less favorable candidate (Kole et al., 2007; Tang and Valdez, 2006; Hua, 2017). A growing mass of literature on the usefulness of the student-t copula as an alternative to the Gaussian copula for modeling financial risk is associated with its ability to incorporate tail dependence (Tang and Valdez, 2006). It is often chosen in order to incorporate an explicit degree of tail dependence into the integrated risk distribution. Generally, elliptical copulas do not have symmetric structure unless all off-diagonal elements of the correlation matrix are the same (BCBS, 2010). However, a drawback is the strong symmetry in tails (Weiß and Scheffer, 2015), which brings about the only “×” in the row of tail asymmetry for the t copula.

Some Archimedean copulas are actively used because they allow for asymmetric tail dependence. For example, the Gumbel copula shows strong association in the right tail, while the Clayton copula shows strong association in the left tail. However, a major flaw of these two copulas is that they cannot capture negative dependence (Jaworski et al., 2009; Viebig and Poddig, 2010), so the rows of negative nonlinear dependence of the two copulas are marked as symbol “×”. The Frank copula can capture both positive and negative nonlinear dependencies; however, no clear association in the tails can be observed. Another notable disadvantage of the copulas in the Archimedean class is that they are highly symmetric in structure (Tang and Valdez, 2006; Koliai, 2016). This symmetry limits the use of these copulas to aggregating risks that are uniform and interact in the same manner, such as credit portfolios of homogeneous risks.
