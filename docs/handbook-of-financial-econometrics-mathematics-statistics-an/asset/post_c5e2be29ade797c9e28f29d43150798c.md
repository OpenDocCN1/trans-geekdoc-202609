### 47.3.2 Gibbons, Ross and Shanken and Hotelling’s T² multivariate tests

The GRS multivariate test of whether the alphas are jointly equal to zero is

$$J = \frac{T - n - 1}{n} \left(1 + \frac{(\mu_m^s - r_f)^2}{(\sigma_m)^2}\right)^{-1} \alpha'\Omega^{-1}\alpha, \qquad (47.5)$$

where $T$ is the number of time-series observations, $n$ is the number of assets, $\alpha$ is an $n$-vector of the alphas from equation (47.4), and $\Omega^{-1}$ is the $n \times n$ inverse of the covariance matrix of the residual returns from equation (47.4). The Sharpe ratio of portfolio $j$ is $Sh_j = (\mu_j - r_f)/\sigma_j$. Therefore, the term in the brackets is one plus the squared Sharpe ratio of the market portfolio. GRS show that an equivalent test can be formulated in mean–standard deviation space as the difference in two-squared Sharpe ratios

$$J = \frac{T - n - 1}{n}(Sh_q^2 - Sh_m^2)/(1 + Sh_m^2), \qquad (47.6)$$

where $m$ is the market portfolio and $q$ is the ex post tangency portfolio constructed from the $n$-assets plus the market portfolio.

This chapter employs a multivariate test based on raw (rather than excess) returns. The multivariate test of the null hypothesis

H0 : αt = (μt^s − μt^e) = 0 against the two-sided alternative H1 : αt = (μt^s − μt^e) ≠ 0, employs the test statistic

Hotelling’s T² = T αt′ Σt⁻¹ αt = T (μt^s − μt^e)′ Σt⁻¹ (μt^s − μt^e), (47.7)

where Σt⁻¹ is the inverse of the covariance matrix of raw returns. When the null hypothesis is true, the quantity F = (T−n)/(n(T−1)) T² has the F distribution with degrees of freedom n and T − n, where n is the number of portfolios and T is the number of time-series observations. The decision rule is

Accept H0 : μt^s = μt^e if T² ≤ n(T − 1)/(T − n) Fα;n,T−n and reject H0 otherwise.

See Morrison (1967, p. 120).

Interestingly, the Hotelling’s T² test is more closely related to MV portfolio theory than the GRS test. It is stated in terms of the alphas and the covariance matrix of raw returns, α′ Σ⁻¹ α. Then, using the efficient set mathematics and some algebra it can be shown that the sample means tangency portfolio is x_Tan^s = (Σ⁻¹ α + θ2 x_m)/(ι′ Σ⁻¹ α + θ2). And, the difference in the squared Sharpe ratios of the sample means and equilibrium means frontiers is Sh_s² − Sh_e² = α′ Σ⁻¹ α + 2θ2 α′ x_m. In the GRS test and the (Σ, x_m)-compatible means specification, where θ_Int = r̄_ft and θ_Slt = μ^s_mt − r̄_ft, the alpha of the market portfolio is equal to zero, which means that the market portfolio’s sample mean is equal to its equilibrium mean. That is, α_mt = α_t′ x_mt = 0, and μ^s_mt = μ^e_mt.⁷

⁷ The market portfolio’s sample mean is equal to its equilibrium mean in two other cases: when θ_Int = r_ft and θ_Slt = μ^s_m − r_ft, and when the slope and intercept of the (Σ, x_m)-compatible means are generated from an unconstrained GLS regression of sample means on betas that employs the covariance matrix of raw returns.

In this (Σ, x_m)-compatible means case, the difference between the squared Sharpe ratios of the sample-means and equilibrium-means minimum-variance frontiers, both generated from the same n-assets, is

## 47.4 Data and Estimation Procedures

The monthly value-weighted rates of return and market portfolio weights data are from Ken French’s data library http://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html. The risk-free rates of return are from the Fama/French factors dataset. The rates of return for three datasets are examined. Twenty-five portfolios sorted by size and book-to-market equity are from the 25 portfolios formed on size and book-to-market equity (5x5) dataset. Twelve industry portfolios are from the *12 industry portfolios* dataset. Ten size portfolios are from the *portfolios formed on size* dataset. The data are from July 1926 to July 2012. In the size and book-to-market dataset, there are no missing data from July 1931 to July 2012. Therefore, we use this period as the full sample period.

The datasets contain the number of firms in each portfolio and the average firm size in each portfolio at each point in time. The market capitalization of a portfolio is equal to the number of firms in the portfolio times the average firm size of the firms in the portfolio. The sum of these market caps is the total market cap of the portfolios. The jth element of the market portfolio x_m is

x_{mj} = (market cap of portfolio_j) / (∑ market cap of portfolio_k).

The market portfolio weights differ in magnitude and vary over time — sometimes in opposite directions. For example, the values of the mean, maximum and minimum, the large-growth portfolio's weight (in percent) are 35.1, 67.9 and 17.3, respectively. The time-series correlation between the portfolio's weight and the risk-free rate is negative 0.47. The corresponding values of the small value portfolio's weight are: 0.50, 1.45 and 0.11. And, the time-series correlation between this portfolio's weight and the risk-free rate is positive 0.67.
