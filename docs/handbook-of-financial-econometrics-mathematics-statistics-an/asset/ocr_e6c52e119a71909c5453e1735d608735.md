Similarly, Banerjee et al. (2016) provide an updated perspective on modeling IPO dynamics using waves and illustrate that firms with better growth opportunities issue their shares early, accepting a steeper discount, but, in aggregate, those leaders continue to invest in their firms and their sales and profitability grow faster when compared to IPOs issued later in the cycle. Further, Baxamusa and Jalal (2018) contend that customer–supplier relationships propagate IPO waves and they create centrality measures for industries to illustrate wave propagation via industry clustering. Santos (2017) provides an update on Lowry (2003) by developing a model of IPO timing that predicts that the quality of the issuers’ investments and their long-run performance are both inversely related to investors’ demand for the issue. More recently, researchers have leverage explanations about the general market environment to illustrate why IPOs may experience hot or cold cycles. This vein of inquiry seems to data back to the Ritter (1984) update of Ibboston et al. (1975) paper that first identified market cycles and in Ritter (1984) the researcher concluded that if you take away all of the natural resource stocks from the hot period that he was examining, the market would not have been considered hot. This trend continues with work done by Colak et al. (2017) in which the researchers provide localized examples of cyclical patterns in IPO performance and illustrate that uncertainty surrounding the results of US gubernatorial elections leads firms to delay their IPOs and that firms obtain lower IPO prices when dealing with uncertainty associated with these contests.
