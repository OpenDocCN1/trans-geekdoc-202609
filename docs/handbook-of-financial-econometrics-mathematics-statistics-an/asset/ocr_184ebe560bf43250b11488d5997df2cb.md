The diagnostics use two types of related information. One is bounds on the f_i, the fraction of the variation in each of the regressors that is attributable to measurement error. The other is a bound on R*2, the R² of the direct regression if all the measurement error in the regressors were removed. The strategy of the diagnostics is to report measures that indicate how severe the bounds on the f_i, and R*2 need to be to bound the set of feasible values of the regression coefficients. In other words, one may place limit on the relative magnitude of measurement error in a variable as a fraction of the overall error in the model and incorporate this information as a way of removing sign differences and bounding the coefficient estimates (Cready et al., 2001). This will allow the researcher to prioritize more serious measurement errors of one or more regressors and fix those before considering less serious measurement errors in other regressors. See Klepper (1988a) and Klepper et al. (1993) for details and illustrative examples.

# 75.4 Standard Error of Reverse Regression Estimates

We now derive the standard error of reverse regression estimates of k-variable generalization of Klepper and Leamer (1984). The standard errors of direct regression estimates are readily available and can be used to test the statistical significance of these estimates. However, in order to test the statistical significance of reverse regression estimates, we need to derive their standard errors. Note that the derived reverse regression estimates are nonlinear functions of direct regression estimates of equations (75.2) and (75.3) and this causes some problem when estimating the standard error of reverse regression estimates.

Although there is no simple way of estimating the standard error of the reverse regression estimates from the standard error of direct regression estimates of equations (75.2) and (75.3), these standard errors can be obtained through approximation for a large sample size. Kmenta (1986) has suggested using an approximation formula in this case. The formula refers to the general case in which an estimator, say w, is a function of k other estimators, x₁, x₂, …, x_k; that is, w = f(x₁, x₂, …, x_k). The approximate variance of w can be obtained by using a Taylor series expansion for f(x₁, x₂, …, x_k) around x₁, x₂, …, x_k, dropping terms of the order of two and higher, and then obtaining the variance by the standard formula. Following Klein (1953), the large sample variance of w can be approximated as

var(w) ≈ Σ_{i=1}^K (δf/δx_i)² var(x_i) + Σ_{i≠j} (δf/δx_i)(δf/δx_j) cov(x_i, x_j), (75.6)

where var is the variance and cov is the covariance.
