The main results of the paper indicate that the calibrated variance process lacks internal consistency. That is, it cannot be adequately described by the postulated stochastic differential equation of the calibrated stochastic volatility model. Moreover, there is strong evidence that the characteristics of the calibrated and statistically estimated measures of variance are substantially different in ways that cannot be attributed to probability measure changes. These results lead to the conclusion that variance in stochastic volatility models is an artificial device used to confer desired properties to the theoretical implied volatility surface generated by the model. This conclusion has implications for the gradient of innovations within the framework of stochastic volatility models.

# 33.2 Variance in the Stochastic Volatility Model

This paper uses the Heston (1993) stochastic volatility model, which is the de facto industry standard. Formulated under the risk neutral probability measure, Q, driven by calibration to market prices, the model comprises two stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

dS_t = r_{RN} S_t dt + sqrt(V_t) S_t dW_{s,t}^Q,

dV_t = λ(V_t - \bar{v}) dt + η sqrt(V_t) dW_{v,t}^Q,  (33.1)

dW_{s,t}^Q dW_{v,t}^Q = ρ dt.

The Heston model has two variables — the underlying asset value, S_t, and its variance, V_t. Each variable evolves in accordance with a stochastic differential equation, which is described by a series of parameters: r_{RN} for the evolution of S_t; λ, \bar{v}, η for the evolution of V_t; with ρ further linking the Brownian motions, W_{i,t}^Q (i = sv), in the two SDEs. The popularity of the Heston model is in large part due to the computational ease of obtaining European call and put options as inverse transforms.
