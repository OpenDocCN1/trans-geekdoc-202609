
Since \(S(t)=\exp[-\Lambda(t)]\), the estimator for the cumulative hazard function is

\[
\hat{\Lambda}(t)=-\log \hat{S}(t)=-\log \prod_{i\le t}\left(\frac{n_i-d_i}{n_i}\right)=-\sum_{i\le t}\log\left(\frac{n_i-d_i}{n_i}\right).
\]

Because \(\log(1+x)\approx x\) for small \(x\), the previous equation can be expressed as

\[
\hat{\Lambda}(t)=\sum_{i\le t}\frac{d_i}{n_i}.
\]

The variance of the Nelson–Aalen estimator is estimated by

\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}[\hat{\Lambda}(t)]=\sum_{i\le t}\frac{d_i}{n_i^2}.
\]

The plot of the Nelson–Aalen estimator illustrates the hazard rate. A comparison between multiple Nelson–Aalen estimators provides a visual examination of the difference between different hazard types. We will discuss it in a later section.

The logrank test, also called the Mantel–Cox test, is the best known and most widely used test for differences in the survivor functions. Assume there are two groups, i.e., the treatment group versus the placebo group, with different risk exposure. The question of interest is whether one group has a better survival rate than the other. The underlying null hypothesis would be written as

\[
H_0: S_1(t)=S_2(t)\quad \text{for all }t.
\]

We first plot the Kaplan–Meier estimators of the survivor function for each group. If the survivor function for one group is always higher than the survivor function for another group, then we can infer intuitively that the first group might have a higher probability of survival than the second group.

Assume there are \(k\) events observed in both groups. These events occur at either distinct times or the same time, \(t_1\le t_2\le\cdots\le t_k\). Let \(n_{1i}\) and \(n_{2i}\) be the number of subjects at risk at time \(t_i\) (\(1\le i\le k\)) in each group, and \(n_i=n_{1i}+n_{2i}\). Assume \(k\le n\), and let \(d_{1i}\) and \(d_{2i}\) be the number of observed events at time \(t_i\) (\(1\le i\le k\)) in each group, where \(d_i=d_{1i}+d_{2i}\).

Under the null hypothesis, for given parameters \(n_i\), \(n_{1i}\) and \(d_i\), the number of observed events in the first group, \(D_{1i}\), is a random variable following a hypergeometric distribution. It can be written as

\[
\Pr(D_{1i}=d_{1i}\mid n_i,n_{1i},d_i)=\frac{\binom{d_i}{d_{1i}}\binom{n_i-d_i}{n_{1i}-d_{1i}}}{\binom{n_i}{n_{1i}}}.
\]

Denote the expected value by \(e_{1i}=E(D_{1i}\mid n_i,n_{1i},d_i)\) and the variance by \(v_{1i}=\operatorname{Var}(D_{1i}\mid n_i,n_{1i},d_i)\). We get

\[
e_{1i}=\frac{d_i n_{1i}}{n_i}
\]

and

\[
v_{1i}=\frac{d_i(n_i-d_i)n_{1i}(n_i-n_{1i})}{n_i^2(n_i-1)}.
\]

For group 1, Mantel and Haenszel (1959) proposed to sum up the differences between \(d_{1i}\) and \(e_{1i}\) for all observed survival times,

\[
\sum_{i=1}^k(d_{1i}-e_{1i}).
\]

Its standardized form is the following

\[
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^k(d_{1i}-e_{1i})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k v_{1i}}}.
\]

The logrank statistic developed by Peto and Peto (1972) is the following:

\[
\chi^2_{\text{logrank}}=
\frac{\left[\sum_{i=1}^k\left(d_{1i}-\frac{d_i n_{1i}}{n_i}\right)\right]^2}
{\sum_{i=1}^k\left[\frac{d_i(n_i-d_i)n_{1i}(n_i-n_{1i})}{n_i^2(n_i-1)}\right]}
\sim \chi^2_1,
\]

which follows a chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom asymptotically.

To illustrate the application of the logrank test, we use a sample of 232 Nasdaq firms whose stock price fell below \$1 for 30 consecutive trading days in 2010. We track these firms to June 29, 2018, to check how many of them are delisted or merged by the end of the sample period.

The variables are defined as follows:

- *start*: the last date of the 30 consecutive days when a Nasdaq firm's stock prices remain below \$1 (\(t=0\))
- *end*: the last date of observation, either the date of delisting, merger, or June 29, 2018, for firms still trading on Nasdaq
- *dur*: *end* − *start* + 1
- *status*: 1 if the firm is delisted from Nasdaq or merged, and 0 otherwise
- *young*: 1 if a firm's age when its stock price drops below \$1 for 30 consecutive days (\(t=0\)) is less than 10 years, and 0 otherwise.

Similar to the career data in the previous section, a constant was added to the calculated date difference between *start* and *end* to avoid zero being the time to event. We use the number 1 here, but any reasonable positive number works. It is widely believed that younger firms are more fragile than established firms (Nicolò, 2015). Many do not last due to the lack of history to establish their ability to meet the expectations of stakeholders, such as debtholders, customers, and investors. We thus use the categorical variable *young* to partition the sample and examine whether there is any difference in the survival functions between young and established firms. Because the variable *young* is derived at \(t=0\), it is a time-invariant variable.

### 120.3.2.3 Likelihood-ratio test

The likelihood-ratio test is less popular than the (weighted) logrank test, because it requires the unnecessary assumption that the hazard function is constant in each group. But the likelihood-ratio test is powerful if the survival time follows the exponential distribution.

All of the nonparametric estimators and nonparametric tests can be conducted by SAS via the PROC LIFETEST procedure.

## 120.4 Parametric Methods

Researchers are often interested in how the survival time depends on explanatory variables. Assume a vector \(x_i=(x_{i1},x_{i2},\ldots,x_{ip})^\top\) for the explanatory variables of an individual \(i\), which can include both continuous variables and indicator variables for qualitative classifications. The vector \(\beta=(\beta_1,\beta_2,\ldots,\beta_p)^\top\) represents the coefficients to be estimated. We assume that a linear predictor \(\beta^\top x_i=\sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j x_{ij}\) affects the survival rate through some function \(\psi(\beta^\top x)\). The most common models are the accelerated failure time (AFT) model and the Cox proportional hazards (PH) model. Other models include the proportional odds model, additive hazards model, etc.

All parametric regression models discussed here are AFT models. That is, they assume \(S_i(t)=S_0(\psi t)\) and \(\lambda_i(t)=\psi\lambda_0(\psi t)\) for all \(t\), where \(S_0\) and \(\lambda_0\) denote the baseline survivor function and the baseline hazard function. The survival time is defined as follows:

\[
\log T_i=\sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j x_{ij}+\sigma\epsilon_i=\beta^\top x_i+\sigma\epsilon_i,
\]

where \(\beta\) represents unknown regression parameters and \(\sigma\) is an unknown scale parameter. The baseline distribution of the error term \(\epsilon_i\) can be specified as one of several possible distributions, including extreme value, log-gamma, etc. However, AFT models are named after the distribution of survival time \(T\) rather than for the distribution of \(\epsilon\) or \(\log T\).

### 120.4.1 Parametric regression models

In this section, we introduce the five most commonly used parametric regression models, all of which can be estimated by SAS via the PROC LIFEREG procedure.

#### 120.4.1.1 Exponential model

If \(T\) is assumed to follow an exponential distribution, then three basic functions are defined as follows:

\[
\lambda(t)=\lambda>0,\quad f(t)=\lambda\exp(-\lambda t),\quad S(t)=\exp(-\lambda t),
\]

where \(\lambda=\exp(-\beta^\top x)\).

The exponential model has the simplest form in parametric regression models, with an assumption of a constant hazard function. Besides, the exponential model assumes the scale parameter \(\sigma=1\). Under the exponential model, for an individual \(i\), the hazard function \(\lambda_i(t)=\exp(-\beta^\top x_i)\) is independent of survival time \(t\), and the survivor function is \(S_i(t)=\exp[-t\exp(-\beta^\top x_i)]\). In most cases, these assumptions are not realistic, so the exponential model is often not the most suitable choice, but it serves as a good starting point for model selection.

#### 120.4.1.2 Weibull model

If \(T\) follows a Weibull distribution, then three basic functions are as follows:

\[
\lambda(t)=\lambda\gamma t^{\gamma-1},\quad \lambda>0,\ \gamma>0,
\]

\[
f(t)=\lambda\gamma t^{\gamma-1}\exp[-\lambda t^\gamma],
\]

\[
S(t)=\exp[-\lambda t^\gamma],
\]

where \(\gamma=1/\sigma\) and \(\lambda=\exp(-\beta^\top x/\sigma)\). \(\sigma\) is called the Weibull scale parameter.

The Weibull model is an extension of the exponential model when \(\sigma\ne 1\). In other words, the exponential model can be treated as a special case of the Weibull model with \(\sigma=1\). In this case, the exponential model is considered to be nested within the Weibull model. Under the Weibull model, the hazard rate and survivor function for an individual \(i\) are \(\lambda_i(t)=\exp(-\beta^\top x_i)\gamma t^{\gamma-1}\) and \(S_i(t)=\exp\{-\exp(-\beta^\top x_i)t^\gamma\}\).

One advantage of the Weibull model is that it belongs to both the AFT model and the Cox PH model, but the Cox PH model is often more powerful when the survival time follows the Weibull distribution.
