Shefrin and Statman (1985) suggest that investors frame winning stocks (risk-aversion) and losing stocks (risk-seeking) as different accounts and employ mental accounting to explain the disposition effect. Thaler (1999) indicates that a prediction of mental accounting is that investors will persistently hold losing stocks because closing an account at a loss is painful. In addition, although many studies adopt prospect theory to illustrate the disposition effect, they do not employ explicitly cognitive/emotional biases to account for this behavioral bias (e.g., Weber and Camerer, 1998; Dhar and Zhu, 2006; Da Costa et al., 2008; Dacey and Zielonka, 2009; Kohsaka et al., 2017). Since mental accounting is closely related with prospect theory, it is more likely to be a cognitive bias influencing investor’s decision-making. Following Shefrin and Statman (1985), this study assumes that mental accounting has a positive influence on the disposition effect. Hypothesis 16.1 is shown as follows:

**Hypothesis 16.1:** Mental accounting positively influences the disposition effect.

### 16.2.2 Regret avoidance

Regret, an emotional bias, consists of omission and commission. Omission means not performing an act that is usually expected to be performed, and commission means performing an act that results in some losses. According to traditional theory, the feeling of regret from omission and that from commission do not differ when investment decision led to a bad outcome. However, behavioral finance argues that omission is associated with decreased gain, whereas commission is related with increased loss. Based on the S-shaped value function, commission has a higher feeling of regret than omission (Fogel and Berry, 2006; Wong and Kwong, 2007; Huang and Zeelenberg, 2012).

Investors regret holding winning stock now if the stock price will decline tomorrow, and selling losing stock now if the stock price will increase tomorrow (Shefrin and Statman, 1985; Lin et al., 2006; Barber et al., 2007; Chong, 2009). Therefore, the feeling of regret might help account for why investors defer the selling of losing stocks and accelerate the selling of winning stocks (Shiller, 1999; Summers and Duxbury, 2012). Lin et al. (2006) indicate that the feeling of regret is affected by losses (gains) relative to a reference point, rather than the size of losses (gains). Fogel and Berry (2006) argue that satisfaction with investment decision is not simply a function of outcome and conclude that anticipated regret might lead investors to reveal the disposition effect bias. As such, these studies imply that the regret avoidance can explain the disposition effect.

Garvey and Murphy (2004) indicate that professional traders often trade together, i.e., a proprietary trading team, which implies that the rivalry among traders could enhance the tendency to seek pride and avoid regret at the expense of the team’s overall performance. Chong (2009) finds that investors are more willing to flip winning than losing IPOs because of the fear of regret. Lai et al. (2010) find both the trading volume and the turnover rate are higher in a buy-signal domain than in a sell-signal domain and conclude that market traders’ fear of regret results in the disposition effect. Shefrin and Statman (1985), Barber and Odean (1999), Wong et al. (2006), and Barber et al. (2007) indicate that the disposition effect may be highly related to the reduction of anticipated regret and further employ regret avoidance to account for the disposition effect. Therefore, this study assumes that regret avoidance has a positive impact on the disposition effect. Hypothesis 16.2 is shown as follows:

**Hypothesis 16.2:** Regret avoidance positively influences the disposition effect.

### 16.2.3 Self-control

Self-control bias causes investors to fail to pursue their long-term and overarching goals because of insufficiency of self-discipline (Thaler and Shefrin, 1988; Thaler and Benartzi, 2004; Pompian, 2006). Thaler and Shefrin (1981), Shefrin and Statman (1985), and Statman (1995) argue that investors could be rational planners and emotional doers at the same time and reveal intrapersonal conflicts when making decisions. Shefrin and Statman (1985) point out the disposition effect to be a self-control problem or a conflict between the part of rational planner and the part of emotional doer. They argue that the doer is related to emotions of regret and pride, which leads investors to hold losing stocks to defer the emotion of regret and realize winning stocks to hasten the feeling of pride.

To prevent the doer from adopting a myopic behavior, the planner needs some psychological techniques to influence the doer’s behavior. These techniques alter the doer’s incentives by modifying their preferences and monitoring their behaviors explicitly. However, when the monitoring costs are high, investors are advised to adopt self-imposed rules of thumb that limit the range of doer discretion (Thaler and Shefrin, 1981). Shefrin and Statman (1985), Garvey and Murphy (2004), Locke and Mann (2005), Wong et al. (2006), and Richards et al. (2017) indicate that doers’ resistance to realizing losses can be minimized by adopting automatic rules and devices to force the realization of a loss (gain) once the predetermined loss (gain) level has reached, such as the stop-loss (gain) order. Weber and Camerer (1998) and Da Costa et al. (2008) show that deliberate selling has a greater disposition effect than automatic selling. Fischbacher et al. (2014) find that both stop-loss and take-gain order can reduce the disposition effect. Thus, this study assumes self-control (discipline) has a negative influence on the disposition effect. Hypothesis 16.3 is shown as follows:

**Hypothesis 16.3:** Self-control (discipline) negatively influences the disposition effect.

### 16.2.4 The disposition effect and investment performance

Shefrin and Statman (1985), Odean (1998), Barber and Odean (1999), and Leal et al. (2018) indicate that individual investors who have greater disposition effect reveal poorer performance. Garvey and Murphy (2004), Locke and Mann (2005), and Choe and Eom (2009) find that professional investors with greater disposition effect have underperformance. Chong (2009) find that investors have the tendency to hold their losing IPOs, whereas the ex post performance of losing IPOs is not significantly better than their initial performance. Although some studies using fund managers’ data do not find the negative link between the disposition effect and investment performance (Cici, 2012; Ammann et al., 2012), this study highlights individual investors’ behavior and, thus, assumes that the influence of the disposition effect on investment performance is negative. Hypothesis 16.4 is shown as follows:

**Hypothesis 16.4:** The disposition effect negatively influences investment performance.

### 16.2.5 Moderating effect

Demographic variables might influence investors’ behavioral decision-making. Dhar and Ning (2006) argue that the mean value could not account for the whole story and find that investor characteristics influence the disposition effect. Barber and Odean (2001) use investor-level data to examine whether gender influences investors’ decision-making and conclude that single women exhibit the most cautious investment attitude. Barber et al. (2007) and Frino et al. (2015) find that female investors exhibit greater preference for realizing gain than loss. Wong et al. (2006), Da Costa et al. (2008), Rau (2014), and Kohsaka et al. (2017) investigate whether gender interferes with the disposition effect through a lab experiment and find that female subjects have greater disposition effect.

Wong et al. (2006) examine whether marital status influences the disposition effect and find that the result is insignificant. However, the economic pressure of supporting a family between married investors and single ones may be different. Lu et al. (2016) find that hedge fund managers are susceptible to the disposition effect during the marriage event. Therefore, the current study further examines whether gender and marital status moderate the relationship between cognitive/emotional biases and the disposition effect, respectively. Hypothesis 16.5 is shown as follows:

**Hypothesis 16.5:** Gender (marital status) moderates the relationship between cognitive/emotional biases and the disposition effect.
