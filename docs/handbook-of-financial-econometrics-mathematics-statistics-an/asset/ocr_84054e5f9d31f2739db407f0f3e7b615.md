# 16.2 Hypothesis

Behavioral finance theory indicates that cognitive/emotional biases result in investors’ behavioral biases, such as the disposition effect, escalation of commitment, and herding effect. Following Shefrin and Statman (1985), this study adopts three cognitive/emotional biases, mainly consisting of mental accounting, regret avoidance, and self-control to investigate whether these biases result in the disposition effect.²

# 16.2.1 Mental accounting

Mental accounting, a cognitive bias, refers to the implicit methods that investors use to frame and evaluate their outcomes (gains and losses). Loss aversion means that investors are more sensitive to losses than gains. Kahneman and Tversky (1979) employs certainty effect, reflection effect, and isolation effect to demonstrate an S-shaped value function. According to the S-shaped value function, investors frame their outcomes into different accounts (gains and losses) relative to a reference point and reveal loss aversion. Therefore, mental accounting is a core element in prospect theory.

Thaler (1985, 1999) extends the S-shaped value function to incorporate compound outcomes and uses it to summarize four principals, namely segregate gains, integrate losses, cancel losses against larger gains, and segregate “silver linings”, while consumers use mental accounting to frame their outcomes in whatever way makes them the happiest. Barberis and Huang (2001) indicate that investors are in loss aversion over the fluctuation of individual stocks rather than over the fluctuation of the portfolio of stocks, which implies that investors use a method of narrow framing.
