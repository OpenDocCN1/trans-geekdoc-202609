The ICA model attempts to maximize the nongaussianity through alternative measures. In our estimation of the independent components, we use a fixed-point algorithm that measures nongaussianity using the negentropy in the data and maximize it by choosing the mixing matrix. Negentropy measures the randomness given in an observed variable. The more unpredictable and unstructured the data are (i.e., no immediately discernable patterns in its structure), the larger is its entropy. In information theory and among all random variables, random variables that follow a Gaussian distribution have the largest entropy among all that have equal variance. We use this idea to measure nongaussianity through the employment of differential entropy (the difference between the entropy of a Gaussian random variable of the same correlation and covariance as the actual data and the data itself) also known as negentropy. Although the entropy of a random variable has a proper definition, it is widely regarded as a theoretical quantity that is computationally very challenging to estimate in practice as it involves the probability density function of the observable random variable. Fortunately, approximations do exist in practice that allow us to estimate it. We approximate negentropy using the classic method involving polynomial density expansions of the bond yields with a cubic power. Hyvärinen, Karhunen, and Oja (2001) note that these polynomial density expansion-based approximations are possibly coarse in practice.¹⁴ Hence, each time we implement the ICA model, we generate a slightly different set of bond yields and so we generate N = 10,000 sets of independent components. Then, we use the first three of them as the factors in constructing bond yields following the JSZ scheme. Federal Reserve Bank keeping the target for the Fed Funds Rate close to zero for nearly a decade have prompted researchers to study the implications of negative interest rates on the estimation of dynamic term structure models (e.g., see Kim and Singleton, 2012). Recent works find that affine term structure models perform poorly near the ZLB. In this chapter, we study the empirical performance of affine term structure models around the ZLB when PCA is used to extract factors for model estimation.

We begin by providing a detailed and comprehensive overview of the major affine term structure models that use PCA to describe the evolution of factors and this includes Ang and Piazzesi (2003), Collin-Dufresne et al. (2008), and JSZ (and by extension, through the inclusion of macro variables, JLS). We also include plots of model-fitted yields for the model of Ang and Piazzesi (2003), JSZ, and JLS spanning our sample period January 2000 through December 2017. As a baseline, we include a plot of the actual bond yields. From these plots, we see very clearly that our economy went through four major business cycles over this period and so we studied the empirical performance of the model of JSZ and JLS during these various economic environments. We only studied these models because of their unique advantages from the perspective of speed of estimation. Consistent with much of the cited literature, our results demonstrate that the class of affine term structure models when PCA is employed to extract factors shows poor empirical performance. These models frequently cross the ZLB and are associated with poor out-of-sample forecasts. However, in-sample model fits produced by each model do fit the cross-section of the data well. Indeed of all sampled models, the model of Ang and Piazzesi (2003), in spite of its complex nonlinear joint latent factor/macro factor dynamics, never violated the ZLB in our experiments.

We believe that our study leaves some ideas for future research. In our study, we estimated and provided cross-sectional model-fits for the dynamic latent factor model of Diebold et al. (2006) which uses Nelson–Siegel factor loadings. This model produced model-fits that did not violate the ZLB and its estimation was quite fast conditional on the usage of the parameters the authors supplied in their paper as initial conditions for Kalman filtering algorithm. Nevertheless, absent those parameters, which served as excellent initial conditions, this model would be very computationally burdensome to estimate. A study that produces a model that combines the benefits of the reduced computational burden of the model of JSZ with the model-fitting behavior of the dynamic latent factor model of Diebold, Rudebusch, and Aruoba (2006) around the ZLB would be desirable for researchers to estimate and study. We leave these ideas for future research.
