## 43.3.3 Objective functions of intelligent portfolio optimization

The Asset Portfolio: Suppose, after asset selection, a set of n assets are selected, forming an asset list A = (A1, A2, ..., An). The given total capital C is about to be allocated over this asset list with a corresponding list of weights w = (w1, w2, ..., wn), in which wi, i = 1, 2, ..., n, is the weight of capital allocation onto the asset Ai. Note that the allocation weight is defined at a predefined initial time t0, i.e., wi = Ci0 / C0, where C0 is the total capital at t0, Ci0 is the capital allocation at t0 on asset Ai. The weights satisfy two constraints: ∑_{i=1}^n wi = 1 and 0 ≤ wi ≤ 1, i = 1, 2, ..., n. Thus the asset portfolio is a long-only portfolio. For a portfolio with given weights, there is a holding period during which the weights are fixed. When the weights are changed, the portfolio is considered a new one. For a portfolio with weights w, its return of investment Rt at time t is defined and calculated as

$$R_t = \frac{C_t}{C_0} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \frac{C_{it}}{C_{i0}} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i R_{it}, \tag{43.1}$$

where

$$R_{it} = \frac{C_{it}}{C_{i0}}$$

is the return of investment of asset Ai at time t relative to the initial time t0, Cit is the current capital (wealth) value of the investment on asset Ai at time t.

The Strategy Portfolios: Now suppose, after the asset portfolio construction and optimization, for each asset Ai with the allocated capital Ci0 = wiC0 during the holding period t0 ≤ t ≤ T, we load a set of m trading strategies Si = (Si1, Si2, ..., Sim) onto asset Ai, each is given a partial capital Cij0 = wijCi0 = wijwiC0, so a portfolio of trading strategies is constructed on asset Ai with weights wi = (wi1, wi2, ..., wim), where wij is the capital allocation weight on the trading strategy Sij. We call such a portfolio of trading strategies the strategy portfolio on asset Ai. The weights of trading strategies satisfy two constraints: ∑_{j=1}^m wij = 1 and 0 ≤ wij ≤ 1, j = 1, 2, ..., m. Obviously, the strategy portfolios so constructed are also long-only portfolios. However, it must be pointed out at this stage that each trading strategy in a strategy portfolio is long-short, i.e., the trading strategy is free to trade long or short. For the time being, we only focus our attention on the return of investment R_ijt of trading strategy S_ij at time t relative to the initial time t0 on asset A_i. The return of investment on asset A_i is calculated as R_it = sum_{j=1}^m w_ij R_ijt. (43.2)

So far, it looks like an equal number m of trading strategies are required to trade every selected asset. Actually it is not necessary because m here only means the maximum number of trading strategies applied on any of the selected assets, because when the actual number of trading strategies for a given asset is less than m, the weight for the non-existent trading strategies can be simply set to zero. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can assume the use of an equal number of trading strategies for each selected asset.

### The Intelligent Portfolio:

Substituting formula (43.2) into (43.1), the return of investment R_t of the multi-asset multi-strategy portfolio as a whole can be expressed as R_t = sum_{i=1}^n w_i R_it = sum_{i=1}^n ( w_i sum_{j=1}^m w_ij R_ijt ). (43.3)

We shall call a portfolio as expressed by formula (43.1) an asset portfolio, and a portfolio as expressed by formula (43.2) a (trading) strategy portfolio. Note that both the asset portfolios and the strategy portfolios so constructed are long-only portfolios; but each trading strategy S_ij, i = 1,2,...,n; j = 1,2,...,m, in any strategy portfolio is long-short. Logically this structure of two-level long-only portfolios — the asset portfolio and the strategy portfolios — with long-short trading strategies can realize all the functions of any long-short asset portfolio. We shall call such a structure of an asset portfolio and its strategy portfolios with long-short trading strategies as defined by formulas (43.1)-(43.3) an intelligent portfolio. Note the concept and mathematical structure of the intelligent portfolio are of generality, and the traditional multi-asset portfolio is a subclass. Let m = 1, i.e., only one trading strategy is loaded onto each asset, and the simplest instance of this strategy is the Buy-and-Hold (B&H), then the portfolio return in formula (43.3) is reduced to R_t = sum_{i=1}^n w_i R_it = sum_{i=1}^n w_i P_it / P_i0. (43.4)

For the portfolio return $R_t$, let $\hat{R}_t$ denote the maximum value of $R_t$ throughout the time period since initial time $t_0$ to time $t$

$$\hat{R}_t = \max_{t_0 \le s \le t} \{R_s\}. \tag{43.8}$$

Then the MDD of the portfolio capital as risk measure is defined by

$$\Psi(R(\mathbf{w})) = MDD(R_t(\mathbf{w})) = \max \frac{\hat{R}_t(\mathbf{w}) - R_t(\mathbf{w})}{\hat{R}_t(\mathbf{w})}. \tag{43.9}$$

The three objective functions as defined by formulas (43.5)–(43.7) and the MDD as risk measure are applicable not only for the asset portfolio of (43.1) but also for the strategy portfolios of (43.2).

## 43.3.4 Multi-factor models for asset and strategy portfolios

As expressed in formula (43.3), an intelligent portfolio is structured as a tree whose first-level nodes are assets, and second-level nodes are (trading) strategies. Therefore, we have two types of portfolios — assets and strategies — to optimize. Naturally, we need to generalize multi-factor models over assets to strategies.

### Multi-Factor Models for Asset Portfolios

According to the Arbitrage Pricing Theory (Ross, 1976), irrespective of which of the three objective functions (43.5)–(43.7) is chosen, the capital allocation weight on each asset $w_i$, $i = 1,2,...,n$, can be expressed by a (linear) multi-factor model:

$$w_{it} = a_{i0} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_{ik}F_{kt}, \tag{43.10}$$

where the unknown coefficients $a_{ik}$, $k = 0,1,2,...,K$, are to be solved out by an optimization process with the objective function chosen from (43.5)–(43.7). For a strategic asset portfolio, the multiple factors include mainly macro-economic indices reflecting consumption growth, corporate bond spread, industrial production, inflation, commodity prices, real GDP growth, slope of yield curve, unemployment rate, etc. For stock selection, main multiple factors cover fundamental valuation, solvency, operating efficiency, operating profitability, financial risk, and liquidity risk, as well as price-based and volume-based technical factors and overall market movement and sentiment. Additional ones include alternative analyst, corporate finance policy, and social responsibility factors. Chincarini and Kim (2006, pp. 119–130) provides a classification of multiple factors important to equity portfolios. For an equity portfolio of a given country (economy), which economic indicators, fiscal policies, fundamental, technical and sentimental factors to select involves a complex process of modeling and testing as well as data mining using historical financial data and associated big data. It is important to point out that forecast of the economic, fundamental, technical and sentimental factors produced by nonlinear complex models using historical financial data and big data should become even more important additional factors to the standard linear multi-factor models of (43.10). This is actually one of the most important lead-edge research fronts in intelligent finance.

It is worth noting that Frazzini et al. (2012) affiliated with AQR Capital Management released an important paper entitled “Buffett’s Alpha”, documenting a rigorous empirical analysis, seeks to deconstruct Warren Buffett’s remarkable investment returns with a history of more than 30 years. Their approach is a multi-factor model in which six factors are identified and tested as most relevant for explanation of Buffett’s stock selection and holding: (1) market, (2) size, (3) value, (4) momentum, (5) quality, (6) capital (leverage). This 6-factor model is the state of the art of the multi-factor models started by Fama and French (1993) with three factors. Griffin (2002) shows the Fama and French factors are country specific (Canada, Japan, the UK, and the US) and concludes that the local factors provide a better explanation of time series variation in stock returns than the global factors. It is a natural reasoning that Frazzini et al.’s six factors should be country specific too.

### Multi-Factor Models for Strategy Portfolios

For a given asset of the asset portfolio, the capital allocation on that asset is further diversified into a strategy portfolio as expressed in (43.2). In the same way, we assume that each strategy weight in the strategy portfolio $w_{ij}, j = 1,2,...,m$, can be expressed by a multi-factor model:

$$w_{ijt} = b_{ij0} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} b_{ijl}F_{ilt}, \qquad (43.11)$$

where the multiple factors mainly reflect the characteristic aspects of market regime, price-based and volume-based technical indicators, price and volume volatilities, as well as market ecology and market sentiment. Actual selection of factors for strategy portfolios depends on the actual assets or markets of trade, and this selection must be a complex process of modeling and testing as well as data mining. In particular, this provides a field for big data to add value. This type of multi-factor models is still largely an area of research to be exploited.
