
As discussed, there is no frequently used individual copula that can achieve this goal. Therefore, in this section, we aim to present a solution to this issue by reasonably mixing some individual copulas.

As Table 38.2 shows, the candidate copulas include four popular copulas, i.e., the Gaussian copula, t copula, Gumbel copula and Clayton copula. These copulas have their own strengths and weaknesses, denoted by symbols “√” and “×”, respectively. The Frank copula is excluded as it does not have any advantage over the four other copulas. The mixture copula nests the dependence structures of its component copulas. In this case, the mixture of copulas is the combination of their feature capturing capabilities. For example, if a copula has a “×” in some feature, another copula with “√” in this feature will make their mixture also have “√” in this feature. Therefore, by carefully choosing component copulas, the mixture copula with all “√” can be constructed.

### (1) t-Gumbel Copula

Among these candidate copulas, we start with the t copula because it can capture most types of features. The only “×” in Table 38.2 shows its only weakness of tail asymmetry. Therefore, a copula that can capture tail asymmetry will make a good partner. As Table 38.2 shows, the Clayton copula and Gumbel copula, with high sensitivities to lower and upper tail dependence, respectively, are marked as “√” in the row of tail asymmetry. Significantly stronger dependencies are often observed when bank risks are at their large extremes, so the Gumbel copula is finally chosen. The mixture of the t copula and Gumbel copula is expected to be able to capture all the typical features of bank risk dependence. It is named t-Gumbel copula and defined as equation (38.2).

$$C_{\mathrm{t\text{-}Gumbel}}(u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n \mid w_t, w_{Gu}, \nu, R, \alpha) = w_t C_t(u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n \mid \nu, R) + w_{Gu} C_{\mathrm{Gumbel}}(u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n \mid \alpha), \tag{38.2}$$

where \(w_t, w_{Gu} \in [0,1]\) and \(w_t + w_{Gu} = 1\). Parameters \((\nu, R, \alpha)\) are the association parameters reflecting the degree of dependence and \((w_t, w_{Gu})\) are the weight parameters reflecting the structure of dependence.

According to Nelsen (1999), the upper and lower tail dependence coefficients \(\lambda^U\) and \(\lambda^L\) in terms of copula \(C\) are:

\[
\lambda^U = \lim_{u\to 1} \frac{1 - 2u + C(u,u,\ldots,u)}{1-u}, \tag{38.3}
\]

\[
\lambda^L = \lim_{u\to 0} \frac{C(u,u,\ldots,u)}{u}. \tag{38.4}
\]

**Table 38.2: Constructed mixture copulas and their capabilities of capturing the features of risk dependence.**

| Major features of risk dependence | Candidate individual copulas: Gaussian | Candidate individual copulas: t | Candidate individual copulas: Gumbel | Candidate individual copulas: Clayton | Mixture copulas: t-Gumbel | Mixture copulas: Gaussian-Gumbel | Mixture copulas: Gaussian-Gumbel-Clayton |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nonlinear dependence Positive | √ | √ | √ | √ | √ | √ | √ |
| Nonlinear dependence Negative | √ | √ | × | × | √ | √ | √ |
| Tail behavior Tail dependence Upper | × | √ | √ | × | √ | √ | √ |
| Tail behavior Tail dependence Lower | × | √ | × | √ | √ | × | √ |
| Tail behavior Tail asymmetry | × | × | √ | √ | √ | √ | √ |
| Structure asymmetry | √ | √ | × | × | √ | √ | √ |

Note: Symbol “√” denotes this approach can capture the corresponding feature, and symbol “×” denotes this approach cannot capture the corresponding feature.

Therefore, the upper and lower tail dependence coefficients of t-Gumbel copula \(\lambda_{t-\text{Gumbel}}^U\) and \(\lambda_{t-\text{Gumbel}}^L\) can be derived as follows.

\[
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{t-\text{Gumbel}}^U
&= \lim_{u\to1} \frac{1 - 2u + C_{t-\text{Gumbel}}(u,u,\ldots,u)}{1 - u}, \tag{38.5}\\
&= \lim_{u\to1} \frac{1 - 2u + w_t C_t(u,u,\ldots,u) + w_{Gu} C_{\text{Gumbel}}(u,u,\ldots,u)}{1 - u}, \tag{38.6}\\
&= w_t \lim_{u\to1} \frac{1 - 2u + C_t(u,u,\ldots,u)}{1 - u} + w_{Gu} \lim_{u\to1} \frac{1 - 2u + C_{\text{Gumbel}}(u,u,\ldots,u)}{1 - u}, \tag{38.7}\\
&= w_t \lambda_t^U + w_{Gu} \lambda_{Gu}^U, \tag{38.8}\\
&= w_t \lambda_t^U + w_{Gu} \cdot \left(2 - 2^{1/\alpha}\right), \tag{38.9}
\end{aligned}
\]

\[
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{t-\text{Gumbel}}^L
&= \lim_{u\to0} \frac{C_{t-\text{Gumbel}}(u,u,\ldots,u)}{u}, \tag{38.10}\\
&= \lim_{u\to0} \frac{w_t C_t(u,u,\ldots,u) + w_{Gu} C_{\text{Gumbel}}(u,u,\ldots,u)}{u}, \tag{38.11}\\
&= w_t \lim_{u\to0} \frac{C_t(u,u,\ldots,u)}{u} + w_{Gu} \lim_{u\to0} \frac{C_{\text{Gumbel}}(u,u,\ldots,u)}{u}, \tag{38.12}\\
&= w_t \lambda_t^L + w_{Gu} \lambda_{Gu}^L, \tag{38.13}\\
&= w_t \cdot \lambda_t^L, \tag{38.14}
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(\lambda_t^U\) and \(\lambda_t^L\) are the upper and lower tail dependence coefficients of the t copula, and \(\lambda_{Gu}^U\) and \(\lambda_{Gu}^L\) are the upper and lower tail dependence coefficients of the Gumbel copula, respectively. The complex expression of \(\lambda_t^U\) and \(\lambda_t^L\) can be found in Nikoloulopoulos et al. (2009). As shown by equations (38.9) and (38.14), the t-Gumbel copula can capture both upper and lower tail dependencies, and the upper tail dependence is stronger than the lower tail dependence.

### (2) Gaussian–Gumbel copula

The Gaussian copula is very well understood and has been tested in a diverse range of applications until its inability to describe tail behavior has been fully realized. To take into account the possible upper tail dependence, the Gumbel copula is selected to improve the single Gaussian dependence structure. However, for this mixture, there is still a symbol “×” in the row of lower tail dependence because both component copulas fail to model this feature. The mixture of the Gaussian copula and Gumbel copula is named the Gaussian–Gumbel copula and defined as equation (38.15).

$$C_{\mathrm{Gaussian\text{-}Gumbel}}(u_1, \ldots, u_n \mid w_{Ga}, w_{Gu}, R, \alpha) = w_{Ga} C_{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(u_1, \ldots, u_n \mid R) + w_{Gu} C_{\mathrm{Gumbel}}(u_1, \ldots, u_n \mid \alpha), \tag{38.15}$$

where \(w_{Ga}, w_{Gu} \in [0,1]\) and \(w_{Ga} + w_{Gu} = 1\). The parameters \((R, \alpha)\) are the association parameters, and \((w_{Ga}, w_{Gu})\) are the weight parameters.

By analogy, the upper and lower tail dependence coefficients of Gaussian–Gumbel copula are shown in equations (38.16) and (38.17), respectively. It can only capture upper tail dependence.

\[
\lambda^U_{\mathrm{Gaussian\text{-}Gumbel}} = w_{Ga} \lambda^U_{Ga} + w_{Gu} \lambda^U_{Gu} = w_{Gu} \cdot \left(2 - 2^{1/\alpha}\right), \tag{38.16}
\]

\[
\lambda^L_{\mathrm{Gaussian\text{-}Gumbel}} = w_{Ga} \lambda^L_{Ga} + w_{Gu} \lambda^L_{Gu} = 0. \tag{38.17}
\]

### (3) Gaussian–Gumbel–Clayton copula

Three or more than three copulas can be mixed for embodying more flexibility. Generally, the more the component copulas are, the more flexible their mixture is. For example, to further cover possible lower tail dependence, the Clayton copula can be added into the mixture of the Gaussian and Gumbel copula. In comparison with the Gaussian–Gumbel copula, this three-copula mixture actually nests a greater capability of capturing sensitive lower tail dependence and has all “√” in Table 38.2. The mixture of the Gaussian copula, Gumbel copula and Clayton copula is named the Gaussian–Gumbel–Clayton copula and defined as equation (38.18).

$$C_{\mathrm{Gaussian\text{-}Gumbel\text{-}Clayton}}(u_1, \ldots, u_n \mid w_{Ga}, w_{Gu}, w_C, R, \alpha, \beta) = w_{Ga} C_{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(u_1, \ldots, u_n \mid R) + w_{Gu} C_{\mathrm{Gumbel}}(u_1, \ldots, u_n \mid \alpha) + w_C C_{\mathrm{Clayton}}(u_1, \ldots, u_n \mid \beta), \tag{38.18}$$

where \(w_{Ga}, w_{Gu}, w_C \in [0,1]\) and \(w_{Ga} + w_{Gu} + w_C = 1\). The parameters \((R, \alpha, \beta)\) are the association parameters, and \((w_{Ga}, w_{Gu}, w_C)\) are the weight parameters.

Additionally, by analogy, the upper and lower tail dependence coefficients of the Gaussian–Gumbel–Clayton copula are shown in equations (38.19) and (38.20). It can capture both the upper and lower tail dependencies but these two tail dependencies are asymmetric.

\[
\lambda^U_{\mathrm{Gaussian\text{-}Gumbel\text{-}Clayton}} = w_{Ga} \lambda^U_{Ga} + w_{Gu} \lambda^U_{Gu} + w_C \lambda^U_C = w_{Gu} \cdot \left(2 - 2^{1/\alpha}\right), \tag{38.19}
\]

\[
\lambda^L_{\mathrm{Gaussian\text{-}Gumbel\text{-}Clayton}} = w_{Ga} \lambda^L_{Ga} + w_{Gu} \lambda^L_{Gu} + w_C \lambda^L_C = w_C \cdot 2^{-1/\beta}. \tag{38.20}
\]

A total of three mixture copulas are constructed in this section, among which the t-Gumbel copula can comprehensively capture the typical features of risk dependence with the least copula components and is considered the most suitable for modeling bank risk dependence. For comparison, the less favorable Gaussian–Gumbel copula and Gaussian–Gumbel–Clayton copula are also constructed. Compared with individual copulas, the mixture copula is more flexible and has stronger feature capturing capability; however, it is noteworthy that the addition of more component copulas also increases the complexity of the model and brings in extra parameters, leading to higher computational costs and efforts in model estimation and application.
