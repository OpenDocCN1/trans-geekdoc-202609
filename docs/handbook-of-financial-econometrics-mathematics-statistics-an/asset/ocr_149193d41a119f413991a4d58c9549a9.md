In Lee *et al.* (2009), firms with higher agency cost of expropriation minority shareholders’ wealth are more likely to issue debt-like CBs. In this respect, the CBs should have debt-like features. In European, CB issuers tend to be larger firms having the potentials to issue debt-like securities, and the CB market is perceived as an extension to the bond market by the CB investors (see, e.g., Dutordoir and Van de Gucht, 2009). In contrast, other survey studies found that the majority of managers issue CBs in the hope of eventually converting them into equity (see, e.g., Graham and Harvey, 2001; Brounen *et al.*, 2006). This result supports Stein (1992) that CBs serve as back-door equity issues to help in reducing the adverse selection costs associated with information asymmetry (see, e.g., Kim, 1990; Stein, 1992; Nyborg, 1995; Davidson *et al.*, 1995; Brown *et al.*, 2012; Lyandres and Zhdanov, 2014). In this respect, the CBs should have equity-like features. This is highlighted in an investment-based theoretical framework by Lyandres and Zhdanov (2014) that the issuance of CBs alleviates the underinvestment problem of Myers (1977).

Be it debt-like or equity-like, in response to the adverse selection cost associated with information asymmetry, both Constantinides and Grundy (1989) and Stein (1992) emphasize on the signaling role of CBs to the market so that efficient investments can be achieved. However, there is a significant difference between the models by Constantinides and Grundy (1989) and Stein (1992). In Constantinides and Grundy (1989), the issue of CBs must be accompanied with stock repurchase and investment financing. Spatt and Sterbenz (1988) further argued that if the firm uses the proceeds from the CB issues to repurchase common stocks and/or to increase the firm’s capital/investment expenditures, a gain from hoarding can arise and, as a result, the rate of conversions is slowed down. Further evidence can be found in an empirical study that the issue of CBs represents a positive signal if it enables the firm to make profitable investments and to optimize its financial structure (see, e.g., Gillet and de La Bruslerie, 2010).

In contrast to Constantinides and Grundy (1989), Stein (1992) argued that CBs are issued by firms as back-door equity with the expectation to have more equity to eventually attain a less-levered capital structure. It is less likely the proceeds of the CB issues are to be used for stock repurchase (see, e.g., Loncarski *et al.*, 2006), and a shorter time to conversion facilitates the firm to acquire the equity financing it needs.
