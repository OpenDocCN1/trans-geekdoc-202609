With these empirical sophistications in mind, this chapter employs downside risk to examine the risk–return relation. Our justification is consistent with the recent literature on risk management using downside risk as a risk measure to explain asset returns (see Caporin, 2008; Bali et al., 2009; Kinateder and Wagner, 2014; Chen et al., 2018).³ Looking at the recent experience in financial markets, we find that shocks triggered by extreme market conditions tend to generate a long-lasting fear, affecting investors’ attitudes toward risk. As a result, an empirical study based on the model specification of Bali and Cakici (2004) and Bali et al. (2009), who treat downside risk as a short memory, may not concisely describe the underlying time series property of downside risk.

Empirically, the literature suggests that during recessions, the risk–return relation is prone to be negative (Ang et al., 2006); it is also evident that during highly volatile periods, the risk–return relation displays a negative sign (Chen and Chiang, 2016). Thus, the presence of a long memory property over the autocorrelation functions on downside risk could potentially cause a negative relation between downside risk and returns. In terms of the time series property, the long memory property inherent in risk measure is incompatible with the return series, which exhibits a short memory. As a result, how to resolve the co-existence of a short memory in returns and a long memory in downside risk becomes a key issue in the empirical investigation of the risk-return relation. In this study, we demonstrate that taking a fractional difference on the downside risk series by filtering out the underlying long memory element helps to achieve a positive and statistically significant coefficient in the estimation of the risk-return relation.

This study presents a bivariate VAR model composed of the following effects:
- a risk-return tradeoff referring to the risk premium demanded by investors to compensate for the higher risk in the domestic market;
- a cross-market risk-return tradeoff capturing the risk premium demanded by investors to compensate for the higher risk across markets;
- a financial leverage effect to reflect the bad news on market volatility and, in turn, the downside risk; and
- a feedback effect to account for the impact on excess returns resulting from downside risk.

This chapter contributes to the literature by presenting new evidence on several emerging Asian markets. First, a long memory is present in downside risk, leading to a long-lasting impact of downside risk on excess returns for Asian markets. The impact becomes modest and short-lived after removing the long memory component. Second, the negative sign on the relation between downside risk and return is closely tied to the existence of a long memory in downside risk. After filtering out the long memory component, the risk-return relation turns positive and significant. Third, the cross-market risk-return relation is positive and statistically significant, supporting the finding that a risk premium is required to compensate for contagion from global stock markets (Chen et al., 2018). Fourth, even when we incorporate the leverage effect and risk feedback, there is still a positive risk-return tradeoff. Our findings do not exclusively depend on the GARCH-type model specification. Fifth, since the memory property of downside risk carries over to the stock return process through the downside risk-return relation, applying the fractionally integrated exponential generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (FIEGARCH) process to model the conditional return variance (see Bollerslev and Mikkelsen, 1996) helps to capture the asymmetric effect and other GARCH properties such as the volatility clustering phenomenon.

The rest of the chapter is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the data and measures of value-at-risk (VaR). Section 3 presents a bivariate VAR model pertinent to the empirical analysis of dynamic risk-return relations. The leverage effect, risk feedback effect, and cross-market risk effect are incorporated. Section 4 introduces a model of long memory analysis, presents evidence of a long memory in the downside risk series, and reports the empirical evidence on the dynamics of downside risk and returns after adjusting for the long memory property of downside risk. Section 5 conducts further empirical analyses by checking the impulse responses to exogenous shocks and investigating the performance of our model by introducing the FIEGARCH model to return series. Section 6 contains concluding remarks.

## 58.2 Data Description

The data in this study cover nine markets. The US and eight major Asian markets: China (CN), Hong Kong (HK), Indonesia (ID), Malaysia (MY), Singapore (SG), South Korea (KR), Thailand (TL), Taiwan (TW). We included the US to serve as a global factor, so that the parametric impact of US risk on Asian markets can be examined. The sample period is 1997 to 2011. The choice of starting date is constrained by the availability of data for the Chinese market, since before 1997, the Chinese stock data were lacking consistency in their earlier trading stage. Missing information for holidays has been carefully inspected, and we have applied the same rules to all variables for these days by eliminating holidays from the data. However, if there are only one or two missing data points, we use an interpolation method by averaging two adjoining data points if they are not influencing outliers. If necessary, we use a forecast value to fill in the missing data. The risk-free rate is proxied by the one-month US Eurocurrency deposit rate, since this rate is widely used in the international markets and less influenced by an individual country’s monetary policy. All of the data are taken from Datastream International.

Both excess return and VaR are calculated on a weekly basis. Stock returns are calculated by taking the natural log-difference between the daily prices on Wednesday, and excess stock returns are constructed by subtracting the risk-free rate at time t from the stock index return from time t (Wednesday) to t + 1. VaR is defined as the minimum daily return observed during this period from Wednesday of week t−1 to Wednesday of week t.⁴ As a result, we are able to construct a non-overlapping series for the weekly VaR series. Such a treatment not only alleviates the statistical problem with overlapping data (Lettau and Ludvigson, 2010, p. 638), but also helps to avoid the criticism that the overlapping design creates a long memory in downside risk.
