(2013) develop a binormal GARCH (BiNGARCH) model to analyze the S&P 500 stock series and support the finding that the relation between conditional mode and relative downside risk is positive and significant. Ghysels et al. (2005) examine the risk–return relation using mixed data sampling (MIDAS) to model conditional variance and allow for regime switches. Their evidence together with Chen and Chiang (2016) shows that a positive risk–return tradeoff holds in a regime of high ex post returns and low volatility. The risk–return relation is reversed in a “flight-to-quality” regime or highly volatile regime.² These studies clearly indicate that empirical estimations of the risk–return relation depend crucially on the risk variable selected and the procedure used to model the conditional variance.

With these empirical sophistications in mind, this chapter employs downside risk to examine the risk–return relation. Our justification is consistent with the recent literature on risk management using downside risk as a risk measure to explain asset returns (see Caporin, 2008; Bali et al., 2009; Kinateder and Wagner, 2014; Chen et al., 2018).³ Looking at the recent experience in financial markets, we find that shocks triggered by extreme market conditions tend to generate a long-lasting fear, affecting investors’ attitudes toward risk. As a result, an empirical study based on the model specification of Bali and Cakici (2004) and Bali et al. (2009), who treat downside risk as a short memory, may not concisely describe the underlying time series property of downside risk.

Empirically, the literature suggests that during recessions, the risk–return relation is prone to be negative (Ang et al., 2006); it is also evident that during highly volatile periods, the risk–return relation displays a negative sign (Chen and Chiang, 2016). Thus, the presence of a long memory property over the autocorrelation functions on downside risk could potentially cause a negative relation between downside risk and returns. In terms of the time series property, the long memory property inherent in risk measure is incompatible with the return series, which exhibits a short memory.
