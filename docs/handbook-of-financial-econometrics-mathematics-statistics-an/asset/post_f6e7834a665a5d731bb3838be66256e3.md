$\varphi_X(\lambda_j)=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\max \operatorname{diag} P_j^{-1}}}$ (36.25)

where $P_j$ is the $(n \times n)$ correlation matrix of $W_{jt}$, and the max diag(.) operator selects the largest element in the diagonal of the argument.

The wavelet WMC coefficient can be expressed in terms of wavelet variance and covariance⁶:

$\varphi_X(\lambda_j)=\operatorname{Corr}(w_{ijt},\hat w_{ijt})=\frac{\operatorname{Corr}(w_{ijt},\hat w_{ijt})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(w_{ijt})\operatorname{Var}(\hat w_{ijt})}}$ (36.26)

where $w_{ij}$ are chosen to maximize $\varphi_X(\lambda_j)$ and $\hat w_{ij}$ are the fitted values in the regression of $w_{ij}$ on the rest of the wavelet coefficients at scale $\lambda_j$.

Similarly, allowing a lag between observed and fitted values at each scale $\lambda_j$, the WMCC is defined as follows:

$\varphi_{X,\kappa}(\lambda_j)=\operatorname{Corr}(w_{ijt},\hat w_{ijt+\kappa})=\frac{\operatorname{Corr}(w_{ijt},\hat w_{ijt+\kappa})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(w_{ijt})\operatorname{Var}(\hat w_{ijt+\kappa})}}$ (36.27)

where $\kappa$ is a lag between the observed and fitted values of the variable selected as the criterion variable at each scale $\lambda_j$.

A consistent estimator of the WMC is:

$\tilde\varphi_X(\lambda_j)=\operatorname{Corr}(\tilde w_{ijt},\hat{\tilde w}_{ijt})=\frac{\operatorname{Corr}(\tilde w_{ijt},\hat{\tilde w}_{ijt})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde w_{ijt})\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\tilde w}_{ijt})}}$ (36.28)

where $\tilde w_{ij}$ is such that the regression of $\tilde w_{ij}$ on the set of the regressors $\{\tilde w_{kj},k\neq i\}$ maximizes the coefficient of determination and $\hat w_{ij}$ are the fitted values.

Similarly, a consistent estimator of the WMCC is given by

$\tilde\varphi_{X,\kappa}(\lambda_j)=\operatorname{Corr}(\tilde w_{ijt},\hat{\tilde w}_{ijt+\kappa})=\frac{\operatorname{Corr}(\tilde w_{ijt},\hat{\tilde w}_{ijt+\kappa})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde w_{ijt})\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\tilde w}_{ijt+\kappa})}}$ (36.29)

⁶For a detailed explanation, see the original reference (Fernández-Macho, 2012a).

## 36.3 Results and Discussion

In this section, we apply the wavelet approach described in the Section 36.2.2 to investigate the linkage between global stock market returns in the time-frequency domains. The computation has been performed using the wavemulcor R package developed by Fernández-Macho (2012b) and the W2CWM2C R package developed by Polanco-Martínez (2015).

Figure 36.2 shows the wavelet correlation at each time scale between six EU stock market returns: France (C1), Germany (C2), Spain (C3), Netherlands (C4), Belgium (C5) and Italy (C6). The scale used in the figure indicates the strength of the wavelet correlation coefficients, from weak correlations to strong ones. At the same time, the wavelet correlation coefficients that are significantly different from zero (within a 95% confidence interval) are reported in a table superimposed on the plot.

As shown in Figure 36.2, all six EU stock markets are highly correlated with each other at all frequencies and the correlation coefficients range from 0.65 to 0.95. This can be interpreted as a result of growing integration in EU countries and, consequently, the EU stock markets tend to move together in short- and long-term investment horizons. Potential benefits from international diversification within the six EU stock markets are expected to be inoperative either in the short-run or in the long-run, highlighting the argument of regional stock market integration. The France, Germany and Belgium index returns are highly correlated with that of the Netherlands at all wavelet scales; the correlation coefficients ranging from 0.81 to 0.95 are significant at a 5% level.

Figure 36.3 shows that the European stock markets are positively correlated at all wavelet scales and at all lags. This figure suggests that France is the potential leader or follower of the other European stock markets at low and high frequencies. This figure shows that up to level 1, that is, four days, there exists no lead–lag effect. However, as the scale increases to level 2 and more, the lead–lag effect becomes visible between the European stock market returns, and the influence degree of the potential country on the other markets is based on the level scales. Indeed, for example, for an investment horizon of 4–8 days, a change in the stock market return in France influences the returns of other European stock markets after two days and, for an investment horizon of 16–32 days, a change of the stock market return in France influences the returns of other European markets after eight days. This analysis is crucial for portfolio risk management given that the change of the France stock market return provides information to the international investors regarding the effect on the other European market returns and, consequently, the international investors have enough time to manage and rebalance their investment portfolios. Besides, we also notice that the correlation gets weaker with successive lags and tends to zero for short-term investment horizons while it remains high for long-term investment horizons.

The wavelet multiple cross-correlation of six EU stock markets (France, Germany, Spain, Netherlands, Belgium and Italy) is reported in Figure 36.4.

Figure 36.4 shows that if the six EU countries’ stock market is a harmonious symphony, the orchestra conductor is France.

In this chapter, to investigate the coherence between stock market returns, the time scale axis is divided into high, medium and low frequencies (Bogdanova, 2014). Indeed, the high frequencies are considered to span from two days to three months and represent the random component in a given time series. This random component is in its turn driven by the news. Consequently, if two markets are affected by the same news, strong co-movements at the high frequencies would be observed in both crisis and non-crisis periods. The medium frequencies which span from three months to one year indicate the presence of a contagion phenomenon. The low frequencies, which are driven by the fundamentalists and which indicate the interdependence of the markets, are considered for investment horizons above one year.
