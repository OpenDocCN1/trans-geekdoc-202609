
The estimation of $(\alpha, \delta), \hat{\beta}$, is

$$\hat{\beta} = (x_h' x_h)^{-1} x_h' y_h, \quad \text{var}(\hat{\beta}) = (x_h' x_h)^{-1} \sigma_v^2 (1 - \rho^2)$$

The posterior of $(\alpha, \delta)$ is

$$\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \delta \end{pmatrix} \mid \hat{\beta}, d, \rho, \sigma_v \sim N(\tilde{\beta}, \tilde{V})$$

where $\tilde{V} = \text{var}(\hat{\beta})^{-1} + (\sigma_{\beta_0}^2)^{-1}$ and $\tilde{\beta} = \tilde{V}\big(\text{var}(\hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta} + (\sigma_{\beta_0}^2)^{-1} \beta_0\big)$.

The posterior distribution of $d$ can be derived in a similar fashion. From the relation between $u_t$ and $\varepsilon_t$, we have

$$\varepsilon_t = \frac{\rho}{\sigma_v} u_t + b_{1t},$$

where $u_t$ and $b_{1t}$ are independent and $b_{1t} \sim N(0, 1 - \rho^2)$.

Since we have $\tilde{r}_t = d + \sqrt{h_{t-1}} \varepsilon_t$, we plug in the relation of $u_t$ and $\varepsilon_t$. The equation can be written as

$$\left( \frac{\tilde{r}_t}{\sqrt{h_{t-1}}} - \frac{\rho}{\sigma_v} u_t \right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{t-1}}} d + b_{1t}.$$

Let

$$y_r = \frac{\tilde{r}_t}{\sqrt{h_{t-1}}} - \frac{\rho}{\sigma_v} u_t \quad \text{and} \quad x_r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{t-1}}}$$

then $\hat{d} = (x_r' x_r)^{-1} x_r' y_r$, $\text{var}(\hat{d}) = (x_r' x_r)^{-1} (1 - \rho^2)$.

Assuming a normal prior for $d$ with mean $d_0$ and variance $\sigma_{d_0}^2$, then we have a posterior distribution $d \sim N(\tilde{d}, \tilde{V}_d)$, where $\tilde{V}_d = \text{var}(\hat{d})^{-1} + (\sigma_{d_0}^2)^{-1}$ and $\tilde{d} = \tilde{V}_d\big(\text{var}(\hat{d})^{-1} \hat{d} + (\sigma_{d_0}^2)^{-1} d_0\big)$.

We use a single move sampler to update $h_t$. That is, given other state variables and parameters at time $t$, the posterior of volatility $h_t$ is derived. As time shifts forward, we continue to update the remaining volatility state variables. The random walk Metropolis algorithm is used to sample the posterior of $h_t$. Note that there is a jump component in the state equation, and we are updating $h_t$ while taking the jump size as given. We rewrite equation (52.4) as follows:

$$\tilde{r}_t \equiv r_t - \xi_t^r J_t^r = d + \sqrt{h_{t-1}} \varepsilon_t$$

$$\tilde{h}_t \equiv h_t - \xi_t^v J_t^v = \alpha + \delta h_{t-1} + \sqrt{h_{t-1}} u_t,$$

where $\tilde{h}_t \equiv h_t - \xi_t^v J_t^v$ is the non-jump-related part of $h_t$.

At time $t + 1$, we have

$$\tilde r_{t+1} \equiv r_{t+1} - \xi_{t+1}^r J_{t+1}^r = d + \sqrt{\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v}\, \varepsilon_t$$

$$\tilde h_{t+1} \equiv h_{t+1} - \xi_{t+1}^v J_{t+1}^v = \alpha + \delta(\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v) + \sqrt{\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v}\, u_t,$$

since

$$p(\tilde h_t|h_{t-1},\tilde h_{t+1},\xi_t^v J_t^v,\xi_{t+1}^v J_{t+1}^v)=\frac{p(\tilde h_t,h_{t-1},\tilde h_{t+1},\xi_t^v J_t^v)}{p(h_{t-1},\tilde h_{t+1},\xi_t^v J_t^v,\xi_{t+1}^v J_{t+1}^v)}$$

$$=\frac{p(\tilde h_{t+1}|\tilde h_t,\bullet)p(\tilde h_t|h_{t-1},\bullet)p(h_{t-1})}{p(\tilde h_{t+1}|h_{t-1},\bullet)p(h_{t-1})}$$

$$\propto p(\tilde h_{t+1}|\tilde h_t,\bullet)p(\tilde h_t|h_{t-1},\bullet)$$

Thus, we have the posterior of $\tilde h_t$

$$p(\tilde h_t|h_{t-1},\tilde h_{t+1},\xi_t^v J_t^v,\xi_{t+1}^v J_{t+1}^v)$$

$$\propto (\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v)^{-1} \exp\left\{ -\frac12 \left[ \operatorname{tr}\left( \sum^{*-1} \tilde e_t \tilde e_t' \right) + \operatorname{tr}\left( \sum^{*-1} \tilde e_{t+1} \tilde e_{t+1}' \right) \right] \right\}$$

$$\propto (\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v)^{-1} \exp\left\{ -\frac{[\tilde h_t - (\alpha + \delta h_{t-1} + \psi(\tilde r_t - d))]^2}{2\Omega h_{t-1}} - \frac{(\tilde r_{t+1} - d)^2}{2(\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v)} \right.$$

$$\left. - \frac{\delta^2}{2\Omega(\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v)} \left[ \tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v - \frac{1}{\delta}[\tilde h_{t+1} - \alpha] \right]^2 + \frac{\psi}{\Omega} \frac{(\tilde h_{t+1} - \alpha)\tilde r_{t+1}}{\tilde h_t + \xi_t^v J_t^v} \right\}.$$

(52.6)

The posterior of $\xi_t^v$ can be derived from the following relation:

$$p(\xi_t^v|\xi_t^r, r_t, h_t, J_t = 1)$$

$$\propto p(r_t, h_t|\xi_t^v, \xi_t^r, J_t = 1) p(\xi_t^v|u_v, J_t = 1).$$

We know that $p(r_t, h_t|\theta, h_{t-1}, \xi_t^v, \xi_t^r) \propto h_{t-1}^{-1} |\Sigma|^{-1/2} e^{-\frac12 tr(\Sigma^{-1} e_t e_t')}, e_t = (\varepsilon_t, u_t)'$. As a result,

$$p(\xi_t^v|\xi_t^r, r_t, h_t, J_t = 1)$$

$$\propto p(r_t, h_t|\xi_t^v, \xi_t^r, J_t = 1) p(\xi_t^v|u_v, J_t = 1)$$

$$\propto \left\{ e^{-\frac12 tr(\Sigma^{-1} e_t e_t')} \right\} \left\{ e^{-\frac{\xi_t^v}{u_v}} \right\}$$

where $u_t = (h_t - \alpha - \delta h_{t-1} - \xi_t^v J_t^v)/\sqrt{h_{t-1}}$. $J_t^r$ follows a Bernoulli distribution with the probability of $p_t^r$ equaling one. It is easy to see that $\frac{p_t^r}{1-p_t^r}=O_t^r$, so $p_t^r = \frac{O_t^r}{1+O_t^r}$.

We choose Beta as the prior of $\lambda_r$; that is, $\text{prior}(\lambda_r)=\text{Beta}(a,b)\propto \lambda_r^{a-1}(1-\lambda_r)^{b-1}$. The posterior is $p(\lambda_r|J_r)\propto p(J_r|\lambda_r)\text{prior}(\lambda_r)\propto[\lambda_r^{\sum_{t=1}^T J_t}(1-\lambda_r)^{T-\sum_{t=1}^T J_t}]\lambda_r^{a-1}(1-\lambda_r)^{b-1}\propto\lambda_r^{a^*}(1-\lambda_r)^{b^*}$, where $a^*=a+\sum_{t=1}^T J_t-1$, $b^*=b+T-\sum_{t=1}^T J_t-1$.

The posteriors of $u_r$ and $\sigma_r^2$ can be derived by following the standard regression theory, and therefore is omitted. The posterior of a jump in the state equation can be derived with a similar method.

The prior of $u_v$ is assumed to follow $IG(\varphi_v,\lambda_v)$. The posterior can thus be written as

$p(u_v|\xi_{t|t=1}^v)\propto p(\xi_{t|t=1}^v|u_v)\text{prior}(u_v)$

$\propto u_v^{I}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{u_v}(\xi_{i1}^v+\xi_{i2}^v+\cdots+\xi_{iI}^v)\right)u_v^{-(\varphi_v+1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda_v}{u_v}\right)$

$\propto u_v^{-(\varphi_v+I+1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda_v+\sum_{t=1}^I\xi_{it}^v}{u_v}\right)$.

Thus, the posterior of $u_v$ follows the inverse gamma distribution, $IG(\varphi_v+I,\lambda_v+\sum_{t=1}^I\xi_{it}^v)$.

## 52.3 Empirical Results

Our dataset is the daily change rates of the Thai baht from January 10, 1996 to October 12, 2003, totaling 1,877 observations. Our sample periods cover the Asian financial crisis, which allows us to exploit various jump models to study episodes of market crashes. Figure 52.1 plots the daily change rate of the Thai baht; the volatility of different periods is dramatically different. For example, periods before May 1997 seldom have daily change rates of more than 1%, but this becomes very common in later periods. Extreme values also play an important role in our time series; some data points are significantly larger than others.

We begin our empirical investigation of the Thai baht jump behavior with Merton’s (1976a, 1976b) independent jump model.

$r_{t+1}=u+\sigma\varepsilon_{t+1}+\zeta_{t+1}J_{t+1}$,

where $\varepsilon_{t+1}$ follows standard normal distribution and $\zeta_{t+1}$ is a normal random variable with mean $\zeta_0$ and variance $\sigma_\zeta^2$. Here, $\zeta_{t+1}$ represents the observations

that are rare but large in quantity, and σ_ζ² > σ². In other words, the model assumes that the change rate of a foreign exchange is a mixture of two normally distributed random variables. If the Thai baht has any jump behavior, the estimation result of this model should give us a basic understanding of it. Table 52.2 summarizes the MCMC estimation result of Merton’s independent jump model. The estimation converges quickly, so the statistics are calculated from the last 2,000 sweeps; the first 1,000 sweeps are discarded. The model divides the Thai baht daily change rate into two groups: the low-jump group, with a mean of u = −0.0019 and a standard deviation of σ = 0.316, and the high-jump group, with a mean of ζ₀ + u = 0.145 and a standard deviation of √(σ_ζ² + σ²) = 2.2. The probability of a high jump is λ = 0.138. Note that the sample standard deviation is 0.926 in Table 52.3, so the majority of the exchange rate changes (the low-jump group) are much smaller than the sample standard deviation. Thus, the estimation results suggest that a small sample of observations with a greater-than-usual daily change rate contributes much of the sample standard deviation.

Though Merton’s independent jump model indicates that certain parts of the daily change rate of the Thai baht are substantially larger than others, the estimation results are somewhat unreasonable. The probability of having a high-jump observation, λ, is 0.138, which means that more than 30 trading days in a year have high jump values. Common sense tells us that this frequency is too high. One reason for this result is that the model does not consider the well-known heteroscedasticity effect in exchange rate time series. To account for the conditional heteroscedasticity effect, we first estimate a square root SV model, as reported in Table 52.1.

As with the estimation of UK pound sterling/US dollar, German deutsche mark/US dollar, Japanese yen/US dollar, and Swiss franc/US dollar (Kim, Shephard, and Chib, 1998), the Thai baht/US dollar time series has a high persistence variance (δ = 0.993). The leverage effect is small (ρ = −0.012), which is similar to the behavior of the UK pound sterling and the deutsche mark (Jacquier et al., 2003). However, the estimation is biased, which is clear from the following three points. First, the expected estimate value volatility is 0.637, which is much smaller than the sample variance of 0.857. Second, before the depression on July 2, 1997, the estimated volatility continued to rise for 53 days after April 17. There were no signs of abnormal behaviors in the exchange market that can be connected to the volatility increase in April. Third, the estimated variance on July 2 is 5.261, which means that the movement of 17% is a realization of more than seven standard deviations. The probability of such an event is so rare that it strongly suggests estimation bias.
