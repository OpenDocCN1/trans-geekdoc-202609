
A recent study by Lorenzo-Valdes et al. (2016) introduced the copula-TARCH model to examine the tail dependence between Mexican reference crude oil prices (MECOB) and the Mexican stock market index (IPC). Similar to the copula-GARCH model mentioned earlier, the errors from the TARCH model are assumed to follow a standardized Student’s t-distribution, so that the Clayton and Gumbel copula functions can be applied to capture the lower and upper tail dependence structure between these financial assets. Their analysis with a sample of weekly data from 2010 to 2014 found a relatively weak lower tail but strong upper tail dependence between the stock returns and oil returns during the volatile periods.

Portfolio risks are often investigated via dependence modeling using copula methods. Hsu et al. (2012) incorporated EVT into Clayton copula and found that the model performs well for capturing downside portfolio risks for different return distributions. Although copula methods are better for dependence modeling, the underlying dynamic process of its structure cannot be ignored. Other Archimedean and Gumbel copulas were used by Guégan and Zhang (2010) in their proposed multivariate models for financial data that allows for change in the dependency structure. Their change analysis incorporates both changing parameters in a particular copula family, and sequence of copulas that cater for the dynamic process. Further, Boubaker and Sghaier (2013) argued that dependence is not only present in financial returns, but its structure is affected by the long memory in the mean and variance of the returns. This in turn has an impact on portfolio optimization. Archimedean copulas were used in their analysis on two daily exchange rates and a pair of stock market indices to prove their argument. Based on model selection criteria, the Gumbel copulas capture the extreme co-movements of the returns rather well. Their findings show that the dependence structure between financial returns can be underestimated if the long-range dependence is not taken into consideration.

## 73.3 Definition of a Copula and Transformation

Let X_i be a continuous variable with domain D(−∞,∞), while its distribution function F is also a continuous monotonically increasing function with domain D(−∞,∞), such that F(x_i) = P(X_i ≤ x_i). The Sklar’s (1959) theorem states that every multivariate cumulative distribution function F(x_1,…,x_n) = P[X_1 ≤ x_1,…,X_n ≤ x_n] of a random vector (X_1,X_2,…,X_n), can be expressed in terms of its marginals F_i(x) = P[X_i ≤ x_i]. Its unique copula function C is given by F(x_1,…,x_n) = C(F_1(x_1),…,F_n(x_n)).

Subsequently, the multivariate distribution density function is defined as the derivative of the distribution function

$$f(x_1,\dots,x_n)=F'(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\partial^n C(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_n(x_n))$$
$$=c(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_n(x_n))\cdot f_1(x_1)\cdots f_n(x_n),\quad (73.1)$$

where $f_i(x)=F_i'(x)$ are marginal densities, $c$ is the density of the copula, and $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are independent. By taking $n$ differentiation, we can obtain the associated copula density as

$$c(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_n(x_n))$$
$$=\partial^n C[F_1(x_1),\dots,F_n(x_n)]/[\partial F_1(x_1)\cdots\partial F_n(x_n)].\quad (73.2)$$

By definition, a $n$-dimensional copula $C$ is a joint cumulative distribution function of the $n$ marginals which are uniformly distributed. Thus, when the marginal distribution is uniform, $u_i=F_i(x_i)$ we can write the copula density function as $c(u_1,\dots,u_n)$. In order to make sure that the marginal distribution is uniform, the distributions of the continuous variables must be transformed. The probability integral transform process can be used for this purpose.

Under the probability integral transform process, the marginal distribution $F$ with domain $D$ $(-\infty,\infty)$ will be changed to $[0,1]$ accordingly. By applying the concept of “quantile”, the $\alpha$ quantile of $X$, denoted as $x_\alpha$, is defined as $P(X < x_\alpha)=\alpha$ or the inverse function $x_\alpha=F^{-1}(\alpha)$, with $\alpha\in[0,1]$.

Since $\alpha$ is ordinary monotonic increasing, it follows a standard uniform distribution, $\alpha\sim U[0,1]$, where $P(U<\alpha)=\alpha$. Hence, $P(F(X)<\alpha)$ is $P(X<F^{-1}(\alpha))=F(F^{-1}(\alpha))=\alpha$. As a result, the distribution is also uniform, i.e., $F(X)\sim U(0,1)$. In other words, $P(U<F(x))=F$, or $P(F^{-1}(U)<x)=F(x)$.

The continuous variable can be transformed to be uniformly distributed via the standardized Student’s $t$-distribution. A description of the standardized Student’s $t$-distribution is provided in Appendix 73A. The cumulative distribution function for the standardized Student’s $t$-distribution is within the range of 0–1, which approximates a uniform distribution. The random variable is fitted into a standardized Student’s $t$-distribution so that the domain of its distribution will be transformed to $[0,1]$ accordingly.

## 73.4 Definition of a Copula: A Two-Dimensional Case

Consider two random variables $X_1$ and $X_2$ with continuous marginal distribution functions $F_1(x_1)$ and $F_2(x_2)$, respectively. Let $u_i=F_i(x_i)$, $i=1,2$.

The definition of a bivariate copula is with the following conditions:

- 1. \([0,1]\times[0,1]=[0,1]^2\to[0,1]\),
- 2. \(C(u_1,0)=C(0,u_2)=0\),
- 3. \(C(u_1,1)=u_1\) and \(C(1,u_2)=u_2\),
- 4. \(C(v_1,v_2)-C(u_1,v_2)\ge C(v_1,u_2)-C(u_1,u_2)\),

for every \(u_1,u_2,v_1,v_2\in[0,1]\) with \(u_1\le v_1\) and \(u_2\le v_2\). Note that condition (i) shows that the copulas are unique given that marginal distributions are uniform, while conditions (ii)–(iv) specify a copula as a joint distribution function for random variables \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) that are uniformly distributed.

Given that any joint distribution function \(F(x_1,x_2)\), there is a unique copula function

\[F(x_1,x_2)=C\bigl(F_1(x_1),F_2(x_2)\bigr).\tag{73.3}\]

When \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are independent, their joint distribution is given by the product of the marginals. Thus, the copula can be written as

\[C\bigl(F_1(x_1),F_2(x_2)\bigr)=F_1(x_1)\cdot F_2(x_2).\tag{73.4}\]

Differentiating the function, we get the joint density function

\[f(x_1,x_2)=c\bigl(F_1(x_1),F_2(x_2)\bigr)\cdot f_1(x_1)\cdot f_2(x_2),\tag{73.5}\]

where \(c(F_1(x_1),F_2(x_2))\) or \(c(u_1,u_2)=\partial^2 C[F_1(x_1),F_2(x_2)]/[\partial F_1(x_1)\cdot\partial F_2(x_2)]\) is the copula density function, while \(f_1(x_1)\) and \(f_2(x_2)\) are the marginal density functions.

### 73.4.1 Elliptical copulas (two-tail)

Elliptical copulas are symmetric in tails. Generally, these copulas are derived from a multivariate distribution, and they are built using an inversion method. Thus, elliptical copulas are considered as implicit copulas. Note that the most commonly used elliptical copulas are Gaussian (normal) copulas and Student’s \(t\) copulas.

An elliptical Gaussian copula with multivariate \(\Phi\) and univariate \(\phi\) standard normal distribution functions is defined as

\[C(u_1,\ldots,u_n;\Sigma)=\Phi\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(u_1),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(u_n)\bigr),\tag{73.6}\]

where \(\Sigma\) is the correlation matrix of the variables. Differentiating the copula function, we can obtain the copula density as follows:

\[c(u_1,\ldots,u_n;\Sigma)=|\Sigma|^{-\frac12}\exp\left(-\frac12\xi'\left(\Sigma^{-1}-I\right)\xi\right),\tag{73.7}\]

where |Σ| is the determinant of Σ and ξ = (ξ₁, …, ξₙ)′. Note that ξᵢ is the uᵢ quantile of the standard normal random variable Xᵢ:

$$u_i = P(X_i < \xi_i), \quad X_i \sim N(0,1), \quad i = 1,\dots,n. \tag{73.8}$$

An elliptical copula is a function of (u₁, …, uₙ) and not a function of (ξ₁, …, ξₙ). Due to this, the elliptical copulas are considered as implicit copulas. Since ξ = (Φ⁻¹(u₁), …, Φ⁻¹(uₙ)), elliptical copulas are built by using inversion method. Using this method, the bivariate Gaussian copula function is given by

$$c(u_1, u_2;\varrho) = (1 - \varrho^2)^{-(1/2)} \exp\left(-\frac{\varrho^2 \xi_1^2 - 2\varrho \xi_1 \xi_2 + \varrho^2 \xi_2^2}{2(1 - \varrho^2)}\right), \tag{73.9}$$

where ρ is the correlation coefficient between the two variables (see Appendix 73B for details).

The Student’s t copula is derived from the multivariate (tᵥ) and univariate (tᵥ) Student’s t-distribution functions with v degrees of freedom:

$$C_v(u_1, \dots, u_n; \Sigma) = t_v\big(t_v^{-1}(u_1), \dots, t_v^{-1}(u_n)\big). \tag{73.10}$$

The bivariate Student’s t copula density function is

$$c(u_1, u_2;\varrho) = K(1 - \varrho^2)^{-(1/2)} \big[1 + v^{-1}(1 - \varrho^2)^{-1}(\xi_1^2 - 2\varrho \xi_1 \xi_2 + \xi_2^2)\big]^{-(v+2)/2} \cdot \big[(1 + v^{-1}\xi_1^2)(1 + v^{-1}\xi_2^2)\big]^{(v+1)/2}, \tag{73.11}$$

where ξ₁ = tᵥ⁻¹(u₁) and ξ₂ = tᵥ⁻¹(u₂) are realizations of the Student’s t variables, and K is a constant, K = Γ(v/2)·Γ((v+1)/2)⁻²·Γ((v+2)/2) (see Appendix 73B for details).
