In 2003, the Taiwanese Ministry of Finance placed a moratorium on the de novo creation of bank branches. It is no surprise that the second Taiwanese banking reform in 2004 was focused on mergers and acquisitions. The financial reform had a fourfold purpose. The first was to consolidate the Taiwanese banking sector so that there were three large Taiwanese banks with an individual market share of over 10%. The second was to reduce by 50% the number of state-owned banks. The third was a major reduction in the number of financial holding companies. The fourth is the fostering of an environment that facilitated an increase in the amount of cross-border investment by foreign banks into Taiwanese banks.

Although there were four goals, there are nine pillars upon which the Taiwanese Financial Reform was built. The first pillar was the creation of the Financial Supervisory Committee: an independent surveillance agency that is responsible for the oversight of Taiwanese financial institutions. The second pillar was the creation of the Financial Reform Task Force, also known as the Executive Yuan, whose responsibility it was to propose reforms in banking, capital markets, insurance companies, and defining and qualifying what constituted financial crimes. The purpose of these reforms was to enhance competition within Taiwan’s financial sector. The third pillar was, as discussed above, an initiative to improve the overall asset quality of Taiwan’s financial institutions. Between 2002 and 2003, the Taiwanese government managed to cut the ratio of non-performing loans by nearly 50%. The fourth pillar started as the Merger Law of Financial Institutions (2000) and then culminated in the Financial Holding Company Act that allowed an unprecedented wave of industry consolidation through the formation of Taiwanese financial holding companies. In total, over 68 acquisitions occurred. As a result, Taiwanese banks were getting bigger and concentration was increasing. The fifth pillar was a relaxation of capital controls.⁸ The Taiwanese government removed many of the restrictions placed on foreign investors interested in investing in Taiwan’s securities markets. They did away with the outdated Qualified Foreign Institutional Investor system and began the deregulation of securities markets. The goal was to encourage foreign investment in Taiwan’s securities markets.
