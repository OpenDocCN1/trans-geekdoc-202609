# 50.2.2.4 Default probability estimation

Leland (2004) examines the default probabilities predicted by the Longstaff and Schwartz (1995) model with the exogenous default boundary, and the Leland and Toft (1996) model with endogenous default boundary. Leland uses Moody’s corporate bond default data from 1970 to 2000 in his study and follows similar calibration approach similar to Huang and Huang (2003). Rather than matching the observed default frequencies, Leland instead chooses common inputs across models to observe how well they match observed default statistics. The empirical results show that when costs and recovery rates are matched, the exogenous and endogenous default boundary models fit observed default frequencies equally well. The models predict longer-term default frequencies quite accurately, while shorter-term default frequencies tend to be underestimated. Thus, he suggests that a jump component should be included in asset value dynamics.

Tarashev (2008) analyzes the intertemporal evolution of PDs (probabilities of default) of five structural models, including models by Leland and Toft (1996), Anderson et al. (1996), Longstaff and Schwartz (1995), Collin-Dufresne and Goldstein (2001), and Huang and Huang (2003). Tarashev uses firm-level data from Moody’s for the period 1990:Q1 to 2003:Q2, and focuses on firms with BBB, BB, or B rating. The iterated procedure by KMV is used to estimate asset volatility. The sample data are of quarterly frequency and the average size are 78 BBB-, 80 BB-, and 67-rated firms. Tarashev finds that the PDs implied by the model tend to match the level of actual default rates, and the models explain a substantial portion of the variability of default rates over time. However, the models fail to fully reflect its dependence on macroeconomic cycle. The model-based forecast of default rates can be substantially improved by the introduction of macroeconomic variables.
