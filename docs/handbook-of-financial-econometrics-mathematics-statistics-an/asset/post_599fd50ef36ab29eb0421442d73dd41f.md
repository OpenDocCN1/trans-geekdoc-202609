
## 48.2.2.2 The model in Barberis et al. (2001)

Although Barberis et al. (2001) is the first to introduce prospect theory into the consumption-based asset pricing model, they do not actually apply prospect utility as the representative agent’s utility over his consumption streams. In their model, they separate the utility function of the representative consumer into two parts. One part is a risk averse consumption utility function, and the other is a utility function over financial investment. Without consideration of the prior income effect, their model is briefly shown as follows:

```
$$\max_{c_t,S_t} E\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left(\delta^t \frac{c_t^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta} + b_t \delta^{t+1} v(X_{t+1})\right)\right]$$
```

s.t. $W_{t+1} = (W_t - c_t)R_f + X_{t+1}$

where

```
$$X_{t+1} = S_t R_{t+1}^s - S_t R_f = S_t(R_{t+1}^s - R_f)$$
```

and

```
$$v(X_{t+1}) = \begin{cases} X_{t+1} & \text{if } X_{t+1} \ge 0 \\ \lambda(X_{t+1}) & \text{if } X_{t+1} < 0.\end{cases}$$
```

Inside the equations, $W_t$ is the wealth level at time $t$. The parameter $c_t$ represents the consumption of the representative agent and $S_t$ is the representative agent’s amount of stock in possession at time $t$. In addition, $X_{t+1}$ is the payoff from the excess return of the stock, $\delta$ is the time discount factor, and $b_t$ is an exogenous scaling factor. The utility over consumption $c_t$ is a standard power utility function, and the utility over financial investment is a simplified linear prospect utility function found by setting the risk aversion coefficient in the prospect theory to be zero.

However, there is a vital problem in this model. Whenever an investor has gains or losses from financial investment, $X_{t+1}$, these will bring him utility via $v(X_{t+1})$. Minus his consumption at time $t$, the residual gains or losses will accumulate into the next-period wealth, $W_{t+1}$. The $W_{t+1}$ will affect the next-period consumption, from which he can derive utility over consumption again. As a result, their model actually counts the effect of the financial return twice. One is via the simplified linear loss aversion utility function, and the other is via the power utility function over the next-period consumption.

## 48.2.3 Returns of risky and riskless assets

In this section, we derive the asset returns via the Euler equation based on our consumption-based asset pricing model. The spirit of the Euler equation states that consumers will sacrifice today's consumption level in exchange for increasing their holdings of some assets. Holding the asset will bring them returns that can be transformed into consumption goods in the next period. The Euler equation is stated as follows:

$$E_t\left[ -\frac{\partial U_t}{\partial c_t} + \delta R_{t+1}^i \left( \frac{\partial U_{t+1}}{\partial c_{t+1}} \right) \right] = 0,$$

where $R_{t+1}^i$ is the rate of the return of the asset $i$ between today and the next period. Separating the above equation, we obtain:

$$E_t\left[ \frac{\partial U_t}{\partial c_t} \right] = E_t\left[ \delta R_{t+1}^i \left( \frac{\partial U_{t+1}}{\partial c_{t+1}} \right) \right]. \tag{48.4}$$

Following equations (48.1) and (48.3), $\partial U_t / \partial c_t$ is represented as follows:

$$\frac{\partial U_t}{\partial c_t} = u_{c_t}(c_t, v_t) + \delta u_{v_{t+1}}(c_{t+1}, v_{t+1}) \frac{\partial v_{t+1}}{\partial c_t}. \tag{48.5}$$

Similarly,

$$\frac{\partial U_{t+1}}{\partial c_{t+1}} = u_{c_{t+1}}(c_{t+1}, v_{t+1}) + \delta u_{v_{t+2}}(c_{t+2}, v_{t+2}) \frac{\partial v_{t+2}}{\partial c_{t+1}}. \tag{48.6}$$

Notice that $\partial U_t / \partial c_t$ depends not only on the derivative of $u(c_t, v_t)$ with respect to $c_t$, but also on the derivative of $u(c_{t+1}, v_{t+1})$ with respect to $c_t$. By the same reasoning, we can see that in order to obtain $\partial U_{t+1} / \partial c_{t+1}$, we should take the derivatives of both $u(c_{t+1}, v_{t+1})$ and $u(c_{t+2}, v_{t+2})$ with respect to $c_{t+1}$ into account.

When calculating $\partial U_t / \partial c_t$, because $u(c_{t+1}, v_{t+1})$ is needed to be taken into consideration, $\partial U_t / \partial c_t$ does not belong to the information set of time $t$. So it is still necessary to maintain the expectation at $t$ of $\partial U_t / \partial c_t$. This is different from those in previous articles. We divide both sides of equation (48.4) by $E_t[\partial U_t / \partial c_t]$, then

$$E_t\left[ \delta R_{t+1}^i \left( \frac{\frac{\partial U_{t+1}}{\partial c_{t+1}}}{E_t\left[ \frac{\partial U_t}{\partial c_t} \right]} \right) \right] = 1. \tag{48.7}$$

According to the general definition, $M_t^{t+1} = \frac{\partial U_{t+1}/\partial c_{t+1}}{E_t[\partial U_t/\partial c_t]}$ is the intertemporal marginal rate of substitution, and equation (48.7) can be rewritten as follows:

$E_t[\delta R_{t+1}^i M_t^{t+1}] = 1.$ (48.8)

If the asset is a risk-free security, then equation (48.8) is rewritten as:

$R_{t+1}^B E_t[\delta M_t^{t+1}] = 1.$ (48.9)

When the asset is a risky security, the situation is different. In this chapter, the processes of consumption growth and dividend growth are separately assumed. According to the definition of $R_{t+1}^s = (P_{t+1}^s + D_{t+1})/P_t^s$, where $P_t^s$ means the price of the risky security at time $t$ and $D_t$ means the dividend at time $t$, equation (48.8) can be rewritten as:

$E_t\left[\delta \frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}\left(1 + \frac{P_{t+1}^s}{D_{t+1}}\right) M_t^{t+1}\right] = \frac{P_t^s}{D_t}.$ (48.10)

In order to derive $R_{t+1}^B$ and $R_{t+1}^s$, we must figure out $M_t^{t+1}$ first. From equations (48.5) and (48.6), we can see that in order to obtain $M_t^{t+1}$, we need to take $u(c_t, v_t)$, $u(c_{t+1}, v_{t+1})$, and $u(c_{t+2}, v_{t+2})$ into consideration. Since prospect theory is applied, the utility function of each period may not be the same. If $c_t - v_t \ge 0$, the utility function is $1 - e^{-\beta(c_t - v_t)}$. If $c_t - v_t < 0$, because of the characteristic of loss aversion, the utility of the representative agent becomes $-\lambda[1 - e^{\frac{2}{\lambda}(c_t - v_t)}]$. In order to calculate the intertemporal marginal rate of substitution $M_t^{t+1}$, in addition to comparing the values of $c_t$ and $v_t$, the comparisons of the values of $c_{t+1}$ and $v_{t+1}$ and the values of $c_{t+2}$ and $v_{t+2}$ are also necessary. Because the agent is in either a good or bad state at each date, it is needed to decompose the marginal rate of substitution, $M_t^{t+1}$, into $2^3 = 8$ different scenarios.

In order to simplify the representation, we define the following indicator variables:

$l = I_{\{c_t - v_t \ge 0\}}, \quad \phi_t = 1 \cdot l + \left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right) \cdot (1 - l)$
$m = I_{\{c_{t+1} - v_{t+1} \ge 0\}}, \quad \phi_{t+1} = 1 \cdot m + \left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right) \cdot (1 - m)$
$n = I_{\{c_{t+2} - v_{t+2} \ge 0\}}, \quad \phi_{t+2} = 1 \cdot n + \left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right) \cdot (1 - n)$

8 If a risky security is treated as a stream of consumption claims, the Euler equation is as follows.
$E_t\left[\delta \frac{c_{t+1}}{c_t}\left(1 + \frac{P_{t+1}^s}{c_{t+1}}\right) M_t^{t+1}\right] = \frac{P_t^s}{c_t}$,
where the return $R_{t+1}^s = (P_{t+1}^s + c_{t+1})/P_t^s$.

Following the definition of $M_t^{t+1}$ and some derivative calculations, the marginal rate of substitution $M_t^{t+1}$ is

$$M_t^{t+1}(l,m,n)=\frac{e^{-\beta\phi_{t+1}(c_{t+1}-v_{t+1})}-\delta wE_t[e^{-\beta\phi_{t+2}(c_{t+2}-v_{t+2})}]}{e^{-\beta\phi_t(c_t-v_t)}-\delta wE_t[e^{-\beta\phi_{t+1}(c_{t+1}-v_{t+1})}]}\tag{48.11}$$

Following equation (48.11) and equations (48.9) and (48.10), the pricing equations of the returns of assets can be rewritten as follows⁹:

$$R_{t+1}^{B}E_t[\delta M_t^{t+1}(l,m,n)]=1,\tag{48.12}$$

$$E_t\left[\delta\frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}\left(1+\frac{P_{t+1}^{s}}{D_{t+1}}\right)M_t^{t+1}(l,m,n)\right]=\frac{P_t^{s}}{D_t}.\tag{48.13}$$

Like the original consumption-based asset pricing model, we assume the growth rate of both individual consumption and aggregate consumption per capita follow the same distribution:

$$\ln\frac{c_{t+1}}{c_t}=\ln\frac{\overline{c}_{t+1}}{\overline{c}_t}=g_t^{t+1}=\mu_{g_t}^{t+1}+z_g,\quad z_g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{g_t}^{t+1}).\tag{48.14}$$

In Mehra and Prescott (1985), they use not only $\mu_{g_t}^{t+1}$ and $z_g$ to describe the consumption process, but also the correlation between $g_t^{t+1}$ and $g_{t-1}^{t}$. Following their approach, we also take $\operatorname{corr}(g_t^{t+1},g_{t-1}^{t})=\rho_{gg}$ into consideration.

In our model, dividend growth is not assumed to follow the same distribution as consumption growth, so another lognormal distribution for dividend growth process is assumed. In addition, it is assumed that there is an imperfect correlation between consumption and dividend growth processes. We assume

$$\ln\frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t}=y_t^{t+1}=\mu_{t}^{t+1}+z_y,\quad z_y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y_t}^{t+1}),\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{corr}(z_y,z_g)=\rho_{yg}.\tag{48.15}$$

In the equation (48.15), the means of the consumption growth and dividend growth are the same, but the standard deviations of these two processes are different. This is because the consumption and dividend streams will ultimately share the same trend in the long run, but the volatilities of these two streams are not the same.

⁹ If treating the risky security as a pure consumption stream, the corresponding equation is

$$E_t\left[\delta\frac{c_{t+1}}{c_t}\left(1+\frac{P_{t+1}^{s}}{c_{t+1}}\right)M_t^{t+1}(l,m,n)\right]=\frac{P_t^{s}}{c_t}.$$
