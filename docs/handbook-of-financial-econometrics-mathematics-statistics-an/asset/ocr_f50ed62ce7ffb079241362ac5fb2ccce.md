Table 58.6 reports the maximum likelihood estimations of the parameters for equations (58.15) and (58.14) and the related statistics in the conditional variance equation, respectively. Two striking points are worth noting. First, the estimated values of γ_{ii} in Panel A are significantly negative when using a conventional VaR. The estimated values of γ_{ii}** turn positive after the original V_{i,t-1} is fractionally differenced by using ΔV_{i,t-1} in the test equation (see Panel B of Table 58.6). The evidence of significantly positive values on γ_{ii}** for all of the markets suggests that long memory plays a significant role in determining the sign of the risk–return relation. Second, the estimated fractional parameters, d, are seen to decline in equation (58.14)' compared with (58.15)' in all cases. The estimated d values for Korea and Hong Kong are insignificantly different from zero, while they are significantly positive in equation (58.15)'.

In sum, the inherent long memory in downside risk is likely to blur its relationship to future excess returns. After taking the fractional difference on downside risk, we are able to estimate the behavioral relation between excess returns and downside risk more precisely, without fear of the contamination of long memory. Moreover, applying the fractional difference to the conditional variance of return residuals is also useful, since it helps to filter out the impact of long memory while estimating the other time series properties of the conditional variance of returns. An advantage of the approach in equations (58.14) and (58.14)' is that the model features a double fractionally integrated process and it shares some common qualitative properties with the bivariate VAR setting in Section 4 and the results reported in Table 58.5, Panel B, concerning the positive risk–return relation. However, this model ignores the cross-market setting and fails to trace out the impact of foreign risk on the domestic market.
