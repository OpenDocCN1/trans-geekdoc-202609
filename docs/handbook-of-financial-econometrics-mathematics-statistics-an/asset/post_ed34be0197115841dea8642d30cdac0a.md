## 42.5 Conclusions

The empirical evidence concerning differences in high moments of risk-neutral distribution between Taiwanese call and put options suggests deviations from put–call parity in the Taiwan options market. The previous behavioral finance literature (Chen et al., 2002; Miller, 1977; Ofek and Richardson, 2003) suggests that investors have diverse beliefs under short sale constraints. Those studies demonstrate that the stock and options markets are segmented. Irrationally optimistic investors choose not to invest in the options market and tend to bid high prices in the stock market, while rational investors are unable to simultaneously bid the price down because of short sale constraints. Therefore, stock prices are more likely to deviate from fundamental values. This chapter also studies the behavioral bias of option traders and provides another possible explanation for deviations from put–call parity. Our study is more closely related to that of Constantinides et al. (2011), in which the index options market is segmented by the diverse beliefs of call and put option traders. Because of different expectations about underlying asset prices between call and put option traders, call option prices diverge away from put option prices.

To summarize our empirical results, investor sentiment and consumer sentiment have different impacts on call and put option traders' expectations about the magnitude of underlying asset price jumps and uncertainties of market volatility. The differential influence of irrational and rational sentiment components is observed in call and put option traders' expectations as well. Furthermore, our analysis suggests a significant sentiment effect on the magnitude of the difference in beliefs between call and put option traders.
