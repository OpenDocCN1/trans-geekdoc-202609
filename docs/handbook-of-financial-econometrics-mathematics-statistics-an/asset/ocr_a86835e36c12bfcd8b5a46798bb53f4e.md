# 121.2.2.3 Money market liquidity premium

Rounds of the Fed’s Quantitative Easing after the 2007–2009 Great Recession helped money flow in the economy and improved stock market liquidity. The President of the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston Eric Rosengren (2010) argued that the seriousness of the recent financial crisis was underestimated by economic forecasters because, financial channels, like the provision of liquidity, to the real economy was “only crudely incorporated into most macroeconomic modeling” (p.221). Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) find that asset market liquidity and funding liquidity (of financial intermediaries) are codependent. Particularly, during the periods of financial distress when margins are destabilizing, deterioration of market liquidity is intensified interactively with that of funding liquidity, leading to “liquidity spirals”. As a result, institutional investors shift their holdings of assets toward the ones with lower margins. They also show that the impact of money policy on market liquidity is asymmetric, i.e., expansionary (contractionary) monetary policy causes a smaller (larger) increase (decrease) in liquidity. Similarly, Chordia, Sarkar, and Subrahmanyam (2005) find that during crisis periods, increases in both stock and bond market liquidity are associated with monetary expansions. Their finding that innovations to liquidity in both stock and bond market are significantly correlated and “macro” liquidity (fund flows between sectors in the economy) is linked to “micro” (or trading activities) liquidity.

Examining bank deposits market and monetary policy, Drechsler et al. (2017) find that as federal funds rate increases, so do deposit spreads. As a result, deposits flow out of the banking system, which can lead to a reduction of financial market liquidity and an increase of liquidity premium. Their study implies that the liquidity premium of near-money assets (Federal funds loans and T-Bills) is likely to affect asset prices and firms’ cost of capital. Following Drechsler et al. (2017), we measure the money market liquidity as the difference between Federal funds rate and three-month T-Bill rate.
