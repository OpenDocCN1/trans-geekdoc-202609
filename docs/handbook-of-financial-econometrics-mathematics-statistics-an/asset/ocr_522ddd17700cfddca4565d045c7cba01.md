# 71.2 Literature Review

The term “mobile banking” could be interpreted in different ways. It is often used to describe different kinds of interactions between banks and customers via mobile phones. For example, mobile phones could be used to make phones to call-centers or personal consultants so that they could be a part of telephone banking. One of the oldest types of m-banking which certainly requires mobile phones is based on the use of short message services (SMS). The development of technology has allowed banks to develop separate applications for smartphones and tablets (Shaikh and Karjaluoto, 2015). Actually, it would be suitable to study smartphone and tablet banking separately in countries with mature mobile commerce. However, in Russia, only a few of the largest banks offer special apps for tablets while most of tablet owners just use the same apps as smartphone owners do (NBJ, 2015).

As the main conceptual framework, the lion’s share of m-banking studies considers IT adoption theories. Actually, the three most widespread IT adoption theories are Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT), Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Unified Theory of the Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). In practice, these models are empirically tested with the main dependent variable which reflects the intention to use mobile banking rather than real usage. It is one of the biggest challenges of such models because even high intention to use does not guarantee the real usage of the technology. However, such a simplification is widely used because of the difficulties in measuring real usage. Moreover, as shown in Baptista and Oliveira (2016), the intention to use is the best predictor of the real usage, so such simplification appears not to cause a significant bias.

Chronologically, IDT could be considered as the first adoption theory. Rogers initially developed this theory in 1962 and has since been amended by him. According to IDT, the intention to use mobile banking arises mainly from five factors or innovation attributes: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, observability and trialability (Rogers, 2003).

Another model, TAM, includes two key factors, “perceived usefulness” and “perceived ease of use”, which influence the intention to use mobile banking; another intermediary variable is “attitude” (Davis, 1989, Davis et al., 1989). However, in empirical explorations, this intermediary variable is often dropped from TAM for simplification. Actually, this model was criticized for simplicity, and TAM was extended in 2000 by adding some determinants of “perceived usefulness” like “subjective norms” or “job relevance”, and this new model was called TAM2 (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000).
