
The generator function can be any strictly convex and monotonic decreasing function with Ψ(1)=0 and Ψ(u)→∞ as u→0. Thus, by using only one parameter to specify the generator function, we can generate a variety of Archimedean copulas. Within this family, the Clayton copula and Gumbel copula are most popular for financial applications.

The Clayton copulas capture lower tail dependence. They are generated by using α as the parameter, where the generator function is

Ψ(u)=α^{-1}(u^{-α}-1), α≠0  (73.13a)

and its inverse generator function is

Ψ^{-1}(x)=(αx+1)^{-(1/α)}.  (73.13b)

The Clayton copula can be expressed in the form of

C(u1,…,un;α)=(u1^{-α}+…+un^{-α}-n+1)^{-(1/α)}.  (73.14)

Differentiating the function, we obtain the following Clayton copula density function:

c(u1,…,un)=(1-n+∑_{i=1}^n u_i^{-α})^{-n-(1/α)}·∏_{j=1}^n(u_j^{-α-1}·((j-1)α+1)).  (73.15)

When n=2, the bivariate Clayton copula density function is

c(u1,u2)=(α+1)(u1^{-α}+u2^{-α}-1)^{-2-(1/α)}·u1^{-α-1}·u2^{-α-1}.  (73.16)

The Gumbel copulas are useful for examining upper tail relationships. Consider δ to be the parameter of the density, where the generator function for the Gumbel copula is

Ψ(u)=-(ln u)^{δ}, δ≥1.  (73.17a)

and its inverse generator function is

Ψ^{-1}(x)=exp((-x)^{1/δ}).  (73.17b)

## 73.5 Tail Dependence

In finance, the co-movements of two asset return series are often of interest. The application of copula allows tail dependence to be estimated. Generally, higher values (densities) in the copula tails indicate stronger dependence between the returns. Tail dependence is useful for examining the association between two random variables in the extremity of a joint distribution. In other words, it provides a measure of the likelihood that the movement of one variable will follow the movement of another variable, i.e., whether they tend to increase (decrease) when both variables assume extreme values in their upper (lower) tail.

For a bivariate copula, the $i$, $j$th lower tail dependence coefficient is defined as

$$\lambda_{ij}^l = \lim_{q\to 0} P(X_i < F_i^{-1}(\xi) | X_j < F_j^{-1}(\xi)); \quad \lambda_{ij}^l \in [0,1], \tag{73.20}$$

where $\xi$ represents the quantile of the distribution. When $\lambda_{ij}^l > 0$, the lower tail dependence between $X_i$ and $X_j$ exists. In addition, a higher $\lambda_{ij}^l$ indicates a stronger lower tail dependence. Conversely, the $i$, $j$th upper tail dependence coefficient is defined as

$$\lambda_{ij}^u = \lim_{q\uparrow 1} P(X_i > F_i^{-1}(\xi) | X_j > F_j^{-1}(\xi)); \quad \lambda_{ij}^u \in [0,1]. \tag{73.21}$$

When $\lambda_{ij}^u > 0$, the upper tail dependence between $X_i$ and $X_j$ exists. Similarly, a higher $\lambda_{ij}^u$ suggests a stronger upper tail dependence. If the value of a variable falls in a lower (upper) tail of the distribution, the tail dependence coefficient gives the conditional probability that the value of the other variable also lies in the lower (upper) tail of its distribution.

As mentioned earlier, elliptical copulas have symmetric tail dependence. Therefore, $\lambda_{ij}^l = \lambda_{ij}^u$ or $\lambda_L = \lambda_U$ for these copulas. Since $C(u_1,u_2) = C(u_2,u_1)$, we can conclude that

$$\lambda_{ij}^l = \lambda_{ji}^l \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_{ij}^u = \lambda_{ji}^u \text{ for all } i,j. \tag{73.22}$$

Gaussian copulas have zero or very weak tail dependence unless the correlation coefficient ($\rho$) is 1. The tail dependence coefficient is given by

$$\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 0 \quad \text{if } \rho < 1 \tag{73.23a}$$

and

$$\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 1 \quad \text{if } \rho = 1. \tag{73.23b}$$

Hence, Gaussian copulas are not appropriate for modeling dependencies between financial assets, which regularly have asymmetric tail dependence.

Student’s t copulas have relatively strong tail dependence compared with Gaussian copula. The dependence coefficient is given by

$$\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 2t_{v+1}\left(-\sqrt{v+1}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}}\right), \qquad (73.24)$$

where $t_{v+1}$ is the univariate Student’s t cumulative distribution function with $v+1$ degrees of freedom. Nevertheless, not all of the Student’s t copulas are symmetric. Demarta and McNeil (2005) have developed a number of Student’s t copulas with asymmetric tail dependence.

Contrariwise, Archimedean copulas have asymmetric tail dependence, $\lambda_{ij}^l \neq \lambda_{ij}^u$ or $\lambda_L \neq \lambda_U$. Thus, they are of higher relevance for examining the tail dependence between financial assets, such as stock returns. Clayton copulas have zero upper tail but a positive lower tail dependence coefficient. The tail dependence coefficient is given by

$$\lambda_L = \begin{cases} 2^{-(\frac{1}{\alpha})}, & \text{if } \alpha > 0 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad (73.25a)$$

and

$$\lambda_U = 0. \qquad (73.25b)$$

If $\alpha = 1$, the lower tail dependence coefficient $\lambda_L$ between two series is 0.5. If $\alpha > 1$, the coefficient $\lambda_L$ will be larger than 0.5. If a series has a value that is in the lower tail of the distribution, a coefficient of more than 0.5 suggests that the conditional probability that the other series will have a value that is in the lower tail of its distribution is more than 50%. This means that there is a fairly strong lower tail dependence between the two series if $\alpha > 1$. Gumbel copulas have zero lower tail but a positive upper tail dependence coefficient. The tail dependence coefficient is given by

$$\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{(\frac{1}{\delta})} \qquad (73.26a)$$

and

$$\lambda_L = 0. \qquad (73.26b)$$

The parameters in the copula densities can be easily estimated using MLE. Appendix 73C provides the details of the procedure using Clayton copula as an example. The same process is applicable to other copulas.
