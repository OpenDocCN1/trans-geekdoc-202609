The literature has documented ways to rank analysts by comparing the stock price reaction to various characteristics of their earnings forecasts. Prior studies report that stock price reacts more to a forecast revision made by analysts with more accurate earnings forecasts (Park and Stice, 2000; Hong and Kubik, 2003; Jackson, 2005), with more timely forecasts (Cooper et al., 2001; Jegadeesh and Kim, 2010; Loh and Stulz, 2011), and with more consistent forecasts (Hilary and Hsu, 2013). However, ranking analysts by either accuracy, timeliness, or consistency based on forecast data has its own limitation. Ranking based on earnings forecast accuracy can be plagued by herding, as follower analysts can free ride on the information released by lead (high-ranked) analysts. It has also been pointed out that analysts can at times prefer to release timely or more consistent forecasts at the expense of forecast accuracy (Schipper, 1991; Clement and Tse, 2003). Alternatively, Cooper et al. (2001) propose to rank analysts by forecast timeliness. However, ranking forecast timeliness is highly model dependent with subjective parametric specifications. Finally, rankings based on forecast consistency can require data of long history of earnings forecasts, inherently biased against young and new analysts.

Our paper utilizes a new methodology of ranking analysts based on the network structure of the analyst coverage data. Our study focuses on the fact that analysts do not work in isolation, but rather with peer analysts producing information and competing for clients. These peer analysts can form a valuable network of knowledge and information that may improve an analyst’s performance and her ranking in the profession. We hypothesize how an analyst can produce higher quality equity research if she is in a network position with a better access to the peer analysts’ wealth of knowledge and information.

To test our hypothesis, we construct a network based on analyst coverage data by linking together analysts (nodes) who cover the same firm(s). When two analysts cover the same firm(s), they learn from each other’s evaluation and judgment on the company disclosures and the conversation with firm managers. Meanwhile, they also learn from each other’s access to the market, industry and economy wide information. From the perspective of each individual analyst, she will have access to and can benefit from aggregating information gathered from peer analysts covering the same firm(s) incremental to her own. Furthermore, there is a network effect. From the perspective of each individual analyst i, she not only can learn directly from the peer analyst j covering the same firm(s), but indirectly from the information of analyst j gathering from his peers.
