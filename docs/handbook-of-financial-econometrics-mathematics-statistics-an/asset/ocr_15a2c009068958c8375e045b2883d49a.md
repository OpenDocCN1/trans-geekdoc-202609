Moreover, individual as well as institutional investors, except for specialized mutual or exchange traded funds, seldom form portfolios based on market cap, B/M, price-earnings (P/E) ratio, or any combination thereof. Investors form portfolios by bundling securities across markets, sectors, industries, etc., to achieve maximum diversification. From a practical standpoint, it is almost impossible to replicate investors’ (individual as well as institutional) portfolios. Tests of asset pricing models should preferably be applied to directly observed individual security returns. This will help investors select securities based on the exposure of securities to the factor(s) in a model.

Primary reason for grouping securities into portfolios is to employ a conventional multivariate *F*-test proposed by Gibbons *et al.* (1989), popularly known as the GRS test. The GRS test requires that the number of assets (*N*) does not exceed the number of time-series observations (*T*). This is because when *N* > *T*, the sample variance-covariance matrix of asset returns becomes singular and is not invertible (Knight and Satchell, 2005). To get around this restriction, portfolios are formed from securities, thereby ensuring that *N* < *T*. Grouping securities into portfolios to satisfy the conditions of the GRS test does not always serve this purpose well. The GRS tests easily rejected all models tested in Fama and French (2015, 2016).

Another motivation for grouping stocks into portfolios is to reduce the errors-in-variables (EIV) problem inherent in estimating betas. Since measurement errors in betas across securities are not perfectly correlated, grouping would likely decrease the asymptotic bias in OLS estimators (Litzenberger and Ramaswamy, 1979). Errors-in-variable bias would be reduced and should approach zero as the number of securities (*N*) grows without bound, since portfolios would have less idiosyncratic risk (Jegadeesh *et al.*, 2015). However, forming portfolios by grouping stocks results in a reduction of efficiency caused by the loss of information regarding the cross-sectional behavior of individual stocks unrelated to portfolio groupings.
