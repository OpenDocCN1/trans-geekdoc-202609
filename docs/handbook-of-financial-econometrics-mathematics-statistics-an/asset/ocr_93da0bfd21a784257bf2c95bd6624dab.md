In this study, we demonstrate that taking a fractional difference on the downside risk series by filtering out the underlying long memory element helps to achieve a positive and statistically significant coefficient in the estimation of the risk-return relation.

This study presents a bivariate VAR model composed of the following effects: (i) a risk-return tradeoff referring to the risk premium demanded by investors to compensate for the higher risk in the domestic market; (ii) a cross-market risk-return tradeoff capturing the risk premium demanded by investors to compensate for the higher risk across markets; (iii) a financial leverage effect to reflect the bad news on market volatility and, in turn, the downside risk; and (iv) a feedback effect to account for the impact on excess returns resulting from downside risk. This chapter contributes to the literature by presenting new evidence on several emerging Asian markets. First, a long memory is present in downside risk, leading to a long-lasting impact of downside risk on excess returns for Asian markets. The impact becomes modest and short-lived after removing the long memory component. Second, the negative sign on the relation between downside risk and return is closely tied to the existence of a long memory in downside risk. After filtering out the long memory component, the risk-return relation turns positive and significant. Third, the cross-market risk-return relation is positive and statistically significant, supporting the finding that a risk premium is required to compensate for contagion from global stock markets (Chen et al., 2018). Fourth, even when we incorporate the leverage effect and risk feedback, there is still a positive risk-return tradeoff. Our findings do not exclusively depend on the GARCH-type model specification. Fifth, since the memory property of downside risk carries over to the stock return process through the downside risk-return relation, applying the fractionally integrated exponential generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (FIEGARCH) process to model the conditional return variance (see Bollerslev and Mikkelsen, 1996) helps to capture the asymmetric effect and other GARCH properties such as the volatility clustering phenomenon.
