Just like power options, the Margrabe formula allows proper indexing, flexibility and proper parameterization (see Blenman and Clark, 2005). In this chapter, the Margrabe formula allows for inflexible parameterization and this has not been explored before. Those listed characteristics among other things make it interesting for one to incorporate incompleteness in the Margrabe formula because the original formula does not incorporate incompleteness.

Although continuous pricing is widely highly regarded, it has its limitations when pricing assets when markets are incomplete. One typical situation where continuous pricing will have limitations is when one prices options especially in incomplete markets. One of the reasons why it is interesting for one to correctly price assets in incomplete markets is that hedges are imperfect in incomplete markets. In a complete world, one can compute the option surface in full. The latter statement would imply that is a challenge to price options accurately in incomplete markets. This chapter extends the Margrabe formula such that it is suitable for pricing options under incompleteness (i.e., Lévy process). Lévy process is a Brownian Motion (BM) process with a drift.

The issue of incompleteness when pricing options is not definitive; however, on general terms it implies either not all information relating to the price of asset is incorporated in the price or all possible products that can be written on that asset are currently not written. The advantage of using a Poisson process is that, should there be more than one jump in stock price at any given time, a Poisson process becomes a Cox process¹ — same as Hawkes process. On the other hand, Eraker (2004) states that the advantage of jumpy models is that they capture black swans (i.e., rare events) such as the crash in 1987 is that they are correctly parametrized. Jumps in prices of assets and positive skewness are common features of options in incomplete markets (see Lambrecht and Perraudin, 2003). Applebaum (2004: 1339) stated that “Gaussian and Poisson measures give rise to extreme points of the convex cone of all characteristic exponents”. Other advantages of pricing options in incomplete markets include finding equilibriums, expected return and time to exercise of options. Jumps are assumed to be normally distributed. The latter phenomenon can be traced back to Scott (1997).
