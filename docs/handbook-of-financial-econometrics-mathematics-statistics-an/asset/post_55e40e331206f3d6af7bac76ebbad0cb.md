In our case, the cash conversion systems under study include an inventory, treasury and receivables, and as such, this chapter integrates two key sub-areas of supply chain management (SCM): supply chain operations management (SCOM) and supply chain financial management (SCFM). SCOM includes operations planning and scheduling, inventory management (mainly replenishment), etc. SCFM (also known as supply chain finance (SCF)¹) comprises the activities of planning, management and optimization of financial flows across the supply chain. Supply chains, like all economic activities, strive to achieve economic efficiency, and with few exceptions (charities, non-profits), their primary driving force is financial gain. Thus, the financial underpinnings of supply chains are of paramount importance. Supply chain assets, liabilities, etc. often figure prominently in the firm’s financial statements, and can affect its market value. Supply chain assets (including inventories) and operations give rise to metrics that are used by analysts to assess a firm’s profitability and efficiency, as well as its liquidity and even solvency. In the wake of the recent credit crisis, cash management has evoked increased interest by firms and researchers, forcing firms to more carefully examine their sources and uses of cash, including net working capital.

There are many ways to measure the efficacy of working capital management on a firm’s financial performance. Key performance metrics are related to net profit and return on capital investment, among others. One metric, introduced by Richards and Laughlin (1980), which seems to be employed universally, is the cash conversion cycle (CCC), also known as the cash cycle or cash-to-cash cycle (C2C). The CCC is a duration metric, defined as the length of time (typically, the number of days) between the clearance of payment of a purchased item to the collection of payment for the sale of the same item. Like all financial managers, smart supply chain managers aim to speed up collection of receivables and delay disbursement of payables in order to take full advantage of financial float in their supply chain.

This chapter examines the issue of the optimal capital structure (choice of debt and equity) and inventory sizing via a Markovian cash conversion model in stochastic equilibrium (equilibrium, for short) for cash-constrained firms, in the sense that the firm is capital-rationed and credit-limited. Capital rationing means that for a period of time the firm has no access to additional external capital, while credit limiting means that the firm has access to a limited line of credit. In such cases, the firm often precludes the reinvestment of any internally generated profits (that is, it either does not have retained earnings or cannot reinvest them), and consequently, it is particularly sensitive to improvements in cash management. Instances of fully capital-rationed firms include start-up companies, firms in conservatorship, and wholly-owned subsidiaries of larger corporations. A subsidiary receives an allocation of funding from the parent company at the beginning of the period (quarter, month, etc.). Over the course of the period, the parent company injects no new capital into the subsidiary. Moreover, any profits generated by the subsidiary are paid out to the parent company, which then decides on the best distribution of said profits. Furthermore, the parent company covers any shortfalls of the subsidiary. In this chapter, we shall focus on a cash conversion system that resides in a subsidiary of a parent company, and assume that the parent never goes bankrupt, and therefore, neither does the subsidiary. The treatment in this chapter is based on Melamed and Rogers (2015) and Chen et al. (2017).

Why might a parent company refrain from investing excessive cash in a subsidiary and/or force the subsidiary to pay out all of its earnings, rather than retain some of them? One potential answer lies in agency theory. Managers have a choice: they can either efficiently invest funds in activities that create value for the parent company, or waste the funds on activities that benefit them personally. An example of such a wasteful choice is excessive perquisite consumption. Jensen (1986) shows that when monitoring is imperfect, then the accumulation of cash and other very liquid assets permits managers to pursue their own interests rather than working in the interests of the parent company or shareholders. Yafeh and Yosha (2003) finds that relatively high levels of cash suggest that a manager is unable to locate profitable projects in which to invest. Han and Qiu (2007) documents the motivations for financially constrained firms to hold precautionary cash. Yu et al. (2015) verifies that managerial incentives and board monitoring are substitutes for each other, and the substitution effect is pronounced when firms have poorly incentivized managers. In light of the agency-theoretic reason why firms might be capital rationed, and that capital-rationed firms are the most sensitive to having an inadequate amount of cash to fund working capital, it is of interest to study their optimal cash policy.

The approach we take in this chapter in computing financial performance metrics of the system under study is Equilibrium Rate Analysis (ERA); see Melamed and Rogers (2015). In the context of supply chain management, ERA adopts two key elements that facilitate the evaluation and optimization of attendant metrics: first, it assumes a mathematical setting where the underlying system is in equilibrium; and second, it computes rate-oriented metrics instead of cumulative (time-additive) ones. The first element takes advantage of major computational simplifications afforded by equilibrium settings (that is, replacing time-dependent metrics with time-invariant counterparts). The second element brings about conceptual simplifications from which cumulative time-dependent metrics are readily obtained for both undiscounted and discounted cases. In fact, the time invariance of equilibrium rates leads trivially to the same optimized solutions for both the undiscounted case as well as its discounted counterpart. For more details, refer to ibid.

Buzacott and Zhang (2004) incorporates asset-based financing into production decisions. The cash available in each period is modeled as a function of assets and liabilities that are periodically updated based on production planning. The importance of joint production and financing decisions is demonstrated in a start-up setting. Hu and Sobel (2007) studies a multi-echelon inventory model with the objective of optimizing the expected present value of dividends, and shows no optimal echelon base stock policy exists in the presence of financial constraints. Chao et al. (2008) studies a dynamic inventory control problem of a self-financing retailer, whose replenishment decisions are constrained by cash on hand. This paper finds the optimal inventory control policy, which maximizes the expected terminal wealth at the end of the planning horizon. Protopappa-Sieke and Seifert (2010) develops a model to determine the optimal purchasing order quantity under working capital restrictions and payment delays, and exhibits the interaction between payment delays, working capitals and the firm’s operational and financial costs. Chen and Wan (2011) investigates the impact of financing on the performance of a supply chain with one supplier and one budget-constrained retailer. The results show that credit financing can create value for the supply chain under study, and greater competition in financial markets leads to better financial performance. Gupta and Dutta (2011) formulates the problem of scheduling the payment day for each invoice, issued by upstream supply chain partners, as an integer programming model. Money received from downstream partners, but not used in payments, can be invested to earn interest. Heuristics are further developed to minimize the net present value of cash outflows. Luo and Shang (2015) studies supply chains owned by a single firm with two divisions, and develops an optimal joint inventory replenishment and cash retention policy. It further shows that in the presence of transaction costs, the firm may stock more inventory, even if holding costs increase.

When measuring the financial performance of supply chains, the CCC is a key metric that captures the efficacy of capital management (Farris and Hutchison, 2002), which is a strong indicator of companies’ success and profitability (Johnson and Soenen, 2003). In general, a firm with a shorter CCC is more efficient and profitable, everything else being the same. This result seems to hold worldwide and across small and large firms, even in emerging markets. To wit, such negative correlation between CCC and profitability is reported by empirical studies for a large cross-section of US firms (Jose et al., 1996), Belgian firms (Deloof, 2003), small and medium-sized Spanish firms (García-Teruel and Martínez-Solano, 2007), and Turkish merchandising and manufacturing companies (Uyar, 2009).
