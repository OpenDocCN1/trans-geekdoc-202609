## Appendix 53A

We assume that the systematic risk ($\beta_t$) in equation (53.15) follows a stationary process to ensure that it moves around a fixed mean ($B$). Harvey (1981) suggests that this model can be represented by the state space model consisting of a measurement equation and a transition equation. The measurement equation can be written as

$$Y_t = X_t\alpha_t + W_t \quad \text{for } t = 1,2,\ldots,T,$$  (53A.1)

where $Y_t$ is a $(T \times 1)$ vector of individual security returns. $X_t$ is a $(T \times 2)$ matrix of 1 and market return. $\alpha_t$ is a $(2 \times 1)$ vector of intercept term and systematic risk; $W_t$ is a $m$-dimensional white noise with variance-covariance matrix $G_1$.

On the other hand, the transition equation can be written as:

$$\beta_t - B = \phi(\beta_{t-1} - B) + \mathcal{E}_t.$$  (53A.2)

Or equivalently

$$\beta_t = B + \phi(\beta_{t-1} - B) + \mathcal{E}_t,$$  (53A.3)

where $\phi$ is a $(2 \times 2)$ matrix of coefficients; and $\mathcal{E}_t$ is a $(T \times 1)$ vector of disturbances following a multivariate normal distribution with zero expectation and covariance matrix $Q$.

It should be noted that $\beta_t$ is a vector of stochastic parameters with mean $B$ being a nonstochastic $(2 \times 1)$ vector. $\mathcal{E}_t$ is normally distributed with mean zero and variance $Q$. $\phi$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix of parameters with characteristic roots less than one in absolute value to ensure the return to normality.

Substituting (53A.3) into (53A.1) leads to the following regression model:

$$Y_t = X_t' B + v_t,\tag{53A.4}$$

where

$$v_t = X_t'\phi(\beta_{t-1} - B) + X_t'\mathcal{E}_t.\tag{53A.5}$$

However, $X_t'\mathcal{E}_t = 0$ by definition. Therefore equation (53A.5) can be written as

$$v_t = X_t'\delta_t,\tag{53A.6}$$

where

$$\delta_t = \phi(\beta_{t-1} - B).\tag{53A.7}$$

Substituting (53A.6) into (53A.4) leads to the following measurement equation:

$$Y_t = X_t' B_t' + X_t'\delta_t,\tag{53A.8}$$

or equivalently

$$Y_t = (X_t' X_t')\begin{bmatrix}\beta_t\\\delta_t\end{bmatrix}.\tag{53A.9}$$

Let's define

$$Z_t' = (X_t' X_t'),\tag{53A.10}$$

and

$$\alpha_t = \begin{bmatrix}\beta_t\\\delta_t\end{bmatrix}.\tag{53A.11}$$

The measurement equation in (53A.8) can be rewritten as

$$Y_t = Z_t'\alpha_t,\tag{53A.12}$$

and the transition equation can be written as

$$\alpha_t = \phi\alpha_{t-1} + \mathcal{E}_t,\tag{53A.13}$$

or equivalently

$$\alpha_t = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}_k & \mathbf{0}\\\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{\Phi}_k\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{t-1}\\\delta_{t-1}\end{bmatrix} + \mathcal{E}_t.\tag{53A.14}$$

The Kalman filter algorithm can then be applied to monitor the revision of systematic risk following the announcement of earnings. For the purposes of this study, the stock returns and market indices from the 100th day to the 1st before earnings announcement were used to estimate the systematic risk. The OLS estimates and the variance–covariance matrix of the estimated parameters were then used as the initial values, $\alpha_0$ and $\Omega_0$, respectively.

The Kalman filter consists of the prediction equations and updating equations. The prediction equations can be written as

$\alpha_{t|t-1} = \phi \alpha_{t-1}, \qquad (53A.15)$

and

$\Omega_{t|t-1} = \phi \Omega_{t-1} \phi + Q, \qquad (53A.16)$

where $\hat{\alpha}_{t-1}$ is the optimal estimator of $\alpha_{t-1}$ and $\Omega_{t-1}$ is the covariance matrix of the estimated error terms, which is just a quadratic form of (53A.4). The prediction equations state that $\alpha_{t|t-1}$ is the optimal estimator of $\alpha_t$ can be estimated at time $t-1$ if $\phi$ and $\hat{\alpha}_{t-1}$ have been known or estimated.

The updating equations can be written as

$\alpha_t = \alpha_{t|t-1} + \Omega_{t|t-1} Z'_t F_t^{-1} (y_t - Z_t \alpha_{t|t-1}), \qquad (53A.17)$

where

$F_t = Z_t \Omega_{t|t-1} Z'_t + G_t. \qquad (53A.18)$

The updating equations state that $\alpha_t$ cannot be observed at time $t$, but can be predicted at time $t-1$ via the prediction equations. However, the variable $y_t$ is observable at time $t$ and can be used to update the prediction of $\alpha_t$ by incorporating the new information via the updating equations. This process continues until the current state of interest is reached.

## Abstract

In today's dynamic but ambiguous business environment the fuzzy set applications are growing continuously as one of a manager's most useful decision-making tools. Recent fuzzy set business applications show promising results (Alcantud et al., 2017; Frini, 2017; Toklu, 2017; Wang et al., 2017). International transfer pricing recently has received more attention as the US wages trade wars with China and other countries as some firms try to choose minimizing taxes as a transfer pricing strategy. This chapter demonstrates how to apply the fuzzy set in international transfer pricing problems. Applications of Fuzzy Set to Other Business Decision are also discussed in some detail.

## 54.1 Introduction

China accused the US of waging “the largest war in economic history” as reported by the Wall Street Journal on July 6, 2018. A recent study of transfer pricing by Klassen et al. (2017) shows that some firms choose their transfer pricing strategy to minimize tax payments. They also find that tax savings are higher for firms with higher foreign income, tax haven use, and when research and development activities are combined with a tax minimization strategy. Besides, transfer pricing issues have become more important because most firms increased the transfer of goods or services dramatically among their divisions as a result of increased outsourcing of products or services due to restructuring or downsizing of their organizations. In addition, transfer pricing guidelines are an important element of the international tax system because 60% of world trade is done by multinational corporations reported by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development even more than 20 years ago. However, Martin and Vandekerckhove (2013) find that the transfer pricing rule has an impact on market performance if upstream firms can supply downstream firms on a normal rate of return on investment and all firms remain active in the downstream market. In addition, Autrey and Bova (2012) propose that mandatory arm’s length transfer pricing rules that were exercised by current US IRS rules, deter lower social welfare as long as gray market exists. Lin *et al.* (1993) also suggest that the harmonization of international accounting and taxation is necessary to deter the manipulation of international transfer pricing in Asian Pacific countries. Therefore, designing a good systematic transfer pricing strategy should be a major concern for both top management and divisional managers of multinational corporations as well as government regulators.

Transfer pricing problems have been extensively studied by a number of scholars. Many of them have recognized that a successful transfer pricing strategy should consider multiple criteria or objectives, such as overall profit after tax, total market share, divisional autonomy, performance evaluation, and utilized production capacity (Abdel-khalik and Lusk, 1974; Bailey and Boe, 1976; Merville and Petty, 1978; Yunker, 1983; Lecraw, 1985; Lin *et al.*, 1993; Azemar, 2010; Klassen and Laplante, 2012). Klassen *et al.* (2017) empirically support that maximization of global after-tax income through the choice of transfer prices is the most important goal of international transfer pricing. Other important variables include the competitive position of foreign subsidiaries, divisional performance evaluation, foreign restrictions on repatriation of profits, and the maintenance of adequate cash flows in foreign subsidiaries (Tang, 1992). However, multiple criteria approaches implicitly assume that only one decision maker sets up the transfer price. This assumption is not realistic in most real-world transfer pricing problems. For example, Toyota assembles and sells automobiles in the US using mostly parts made in Japan, multiple DMs, such as all types of managers including central management for the transferred goods or services must be involved in the determination of the transfer price. In order to remove this unrealistic assumption of one decision maker, Kwak *et al.* (1996) proposed a model with multiple criteria and multiple decision makers (MCMDM). This model originally adopted the framework of multiple criteria and multiple constraint level (MC²) linear programming to consider both multiple criteria and multiple DMs’ preferences in formulating capital budgeting problems. The well-known analytic hierarchy process (AHP) approach was employed in the solution procedure to reduce the complexity in computing the trade-offs among multiple criteria and multiple DMs’ preferences, respectively.
