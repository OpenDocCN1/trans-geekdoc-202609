## 31.2 Literature Review

Our paper provides a new approach to rank analysts based on the measures of structural holes in the analyst coverage network. This section presents a brief review on the prior literature on analyst ranking and the social network research on structural hole.

### 31.2.1 Literature on analyst ranking

Prior studies have proposed several quantitative approaches to rank analysts. These approaches differ from that used in the investment community. For example, Institutional Investor magazine ranks analysts by conducting surveys among directors of research and chief investment officers at major money management institutions. While these investment professionals are in the best position to know the best performing analysts, survey results could be biased for rewarding analysts who have been working in the profession for a long time or who aggressively solicit votes from their customers.

Presumably, high-ranked or lead analysts are those analysts whose published opinions are more informative than others and have greater ability to move price. Prior papers have made use of earnings forecast accuracy to evaluate the ability of analysts. For example, Park and Stice (2000) find that price reaction to a forecast revision made by an analyst with more accurate forecasting history is greater than to those by other analysts. However, the price effects of this firm-specific forecasting ability do not spill over to other firms followed by the same analysts. Hong and Kubik (2003) and Jackson (2005) demonstrate that analysts providing more accurate forecasts are more likely to have higher reputation and move up to better brokerage houses. Leone and Wu (2007) measure analyst performance with their earnings forecast accuracy and find a positive relation between All Star ranking of analysts and their earnings forecast accuracy. Other papers provide similar findings (e.g., Stickel, 1992; Sinha et al., 1997; Gu and Wu, 2003; Gleason and Lee, 2003; Clement and Tse, 2005).

As follower (lower ranked) analysts can update their forecasts using information released earlier by higher ranked analysts, use of the forecast accuracy to identify superior analysts can be problematic due to the “herding” among analysts. Other studies point to forecast timeliness, instead of forecast accuracy, as a better measure for analyst ranking. Among analysts providing research coverage on a chosen firm, an analyst with (unobservable) superior ability in collecting and processing information than other analysts can come up with an initial accurate earnings forecast, while other analysts have an incentive to delay their forecasts to incorporate information produced by that (leader) analyst. Cooper et al. (2001) compare the use of forecast timeliness and forecast accuracy to identify lead analysts, and find that only forecast timeliness is an effective way for analyst ranking. Clement and Tse (2003) also suggest that forecast timeliness is important to investors, which affects stock price response to forecast revisions of analysts. Jegadeesh and Kim (2010) and Loh and Stulz (2011) use the same method as Cooper et al. (2001) to calculate forecast timeliness and report stronger market reaction to those analysts who release their forecasts more timely.

Hilary and Hsu (2013) argue that the usefulness of analysts’ forecasts should be based on the extent to which an analyst delivers consistent forecasts, rather than on the forecasts’ accuracy as measured by the absolute distance from realized earnings. As consistent earnings forecast errors allow a predictable transformation of realized earnings and investors can more easily unravel a systematic bias, earnings forecasts made by analysts who deliver consistent forecast errors should have a greater effect on prices than those made by other analysts. Hilary and Hsu (2013) report consistent evidence that consistent analysts have greater ability to affect stock prices, are less likely to be demoted, and are more likely to be nominated as All Star analysts.

### 31.2.2 Social network research on structural hole

The three approaches in the literatures (forecast accuracy, forecast timeliness and forecast consistency) can be somewhat problematic. First, forecast accuracy may not be an effective way to identify high-ranked analysts because follower analysts can free ride on the information released by high-ranked analysts. However, ranking forecast timeliness is highly model dependent with the parameter specification in forecast timeliness calculation subjective and arbitrary. Measure of forecast consistency requires calculation of standard deviation, which entails a long history of earnings forecasts. Such a data requirement inherently biases against identification of young and new analysts as top performers.

Our paper utilizes a new approach for analyst ranking. We use the measure of structural hole, a concept developed in the social network research, to rank analysts. We review prior studies on structural hole below.

The idea of “groups”, formed by people with kinship, similar behavior or opinion, often focusing on activities within rather than outside, has been well-documented in social science studies (Granovetter, 1973; Blau, 1977; Burt, 2003). Granovetter (1973) puts forward the concept of “weak ties” to represent the between-group relations: agents with strong ties are part of a close-knit group who largely know one another, while agents with weak ties refer to acquaintances far less likely to know one another. Granovetter (1973) proposes that only weak ties can build bridges² between different groups and transmit the information among groups, while strong ties strengthen connections within groups. Blau (1977) examines the importance of weak ties and indicates that the whole society may fall apart without weak ties.

Burt (1992) delves further into the effect of weak ties and develops the concept of structural hole. The paper indicates that as people prefer to focus on activities inside their own groups, there is a “structural hole” as a gap in the information flow between groups. Meanwhile, agents standing at the position of structural holes and building bridges to connect two independent groups, can facilitate the information sharing among groups of agents over time. In the terms of Granovetter (1973), agents in the position of structural holes construct “weak ties” with different groups and can stand to control the information flow between these groups. As a result, agents sitting in the position of structural holes and having multiple contacts with different independent groups will be more likely to have early access to diverse and comprehensive information, giving them a competitive advantage in seeing good ideas (Burt, 1992).

Burt (2003) uses a survey in American electronic companies, and reports that people occupying more structural holes can give more valuable ideas. Ahuja (2000), Goyal and Vega-Redondo (2007), Leonardi and Bailey (2011), and Burt et al. (2013) provide similar evidence that people occupying structural holes provide non-redundant ties. The diverse and unique information makes them more likely to come up with new ideas.

Recent research has started to exploit the idea of structural hole in finance. Ahuja (2000) builds a network with detailed archival research on the chemicals and materials sector, with three types of data sources to identify linkage activity. The paper reports that access to diverse information types is beneficial for firms. Zaheer and Bell (2005) examine the network of Canadian mutual fund firms based on both board and management ties. The results indicate that firms occupying more structural holes are more capable of using their internal capabilities to enhance their performance.

Our paper utilizes a network constructed with analysts providing overlapping research coverage on firms. For many analysts, their assignments are industry-based, focusing on similar firms of interest and particular sets of the market and economy information. In other words, analysts can exhibit patterns of “group” behavior documented earlier in social science studies. If this is the case, analysts occupying more structural holes in the network can build bridges to connect groups. Those analysts are able to access information and knowledge of their peer analysts from different groups and obtain more diverse information about market, industries and firms. We therefore hypothesize those analysts (occupying more structural holes) are more likely to release more valuable forecasts.
