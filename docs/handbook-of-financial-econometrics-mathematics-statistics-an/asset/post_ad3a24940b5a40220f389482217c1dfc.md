## 46.8 Conclusion

The purpose of this research is to formulate and study a simple model for cash conversion systems in order to carry out performance analysis of equilibrium-rate financial metrics and obtain optimal operational and financial designs in terms of inventory sizing, capital investment and credit line. The cash conversion systems studied are closed in the sense of being cash-constrained. The model under study is composed of a single-product FMTS inventory, a receivables pool, and an available funds pool consisting of the firm’s cash on hand as well as borrowed funds. The key contributions reported in this chapter are threefold. First, the cash conversion system is modeled as a continuous-time Markov chain with a finite state space in equilibrium. The equilibrium state pmf is exhibited analytically for a special case (when the base stock level of inventory is equal to the available funds maximum), and solved numerically, otherwise. Second, the equilibrium statistics of the cash conversion cycle of the system are derived, including density, mean and variance. Third, financial metrics of equilibrium net profit rate and equilibrium rate of return are defined and optimized as functions of the system’s decision variables (inventory base stock level, amount of capital investment, and credit limit). Finally, sample optimal designs are illustrated by examples and discussed.

The findings reported in this chapter are relevant to managers and investors of capital-rationed and credit-limited firms, who focus on the firm’s long-term supply chain operational and financial performance and their optimization. However, the managerial implications generated by the results may have broader applicability. For example, supply chain managers are incentivized to optimize the net profit rate metric as their compensation is typically tied to it, whereas investors are inclined to prefer the rate of return metric as a measure of their investment performance. This is the classic agency problem of conflicts between managers and owners (see also references in Section 46.1), and this chapter makes a contribution to the understanding of its implications on shareholder value in a supply chain context. To this end, two key optimization problems are studied in the model, typified by a cash-constrained firm’s proposal of a budget plan to its parent company. In the first problem, the base stock level and the available funds maximum are fixed, and the problem is to search for the optimal allocation of available funds between the firm’s own capital investment and credit limit. Such an optimal allocation depends on the financial metric considered, as given by Theorems 46.4 and 46.5, respectively. The second problem is a search for the optimal design of the cash conversion system under study, i.e., the simultaneously optimal inventory base stock level, firm’s capital investment, and credit limit. For example, Figure 46.6(a) is a case in point where the equilibrium net profit rate is highly sensitive to the system’s design parameters, which limit the design error margin of the firm. In a similar vein, Figures 46.7 and 46.8 exemplify how to fine tune operational parameters (e.g., receivables payment rates and inventory replenishment rates) to improve the aforementioned performance metrics.

It should be noted that our model is assumed to be in equilibrium, and assumes that operational shortfalls are covered, say, by the parent company, thereby precluding bankruptcy. That is, from a Markov chain perspective, the cash conversion process can theoretically visit transient states, which would represent nominal firm bankruptcy on the way to stochastic equilibrium. Thus, a future research direction is to model the transient evolution of the system and its discounted cash flow up until bankruptcy. Another future research direction is to utilize the model to quantify the magnitude of the agency problem in a supply chain context.
