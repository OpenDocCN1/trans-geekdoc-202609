Manzon and Plesko (2002) argue that book-tax differences are positively associated with a firm's profitability, its growth rate, the proportion of tangible assets in its assets base and the age of its property, plant and equipment. We briefly explain the rationale underlying the explanatory variables in equation (32.2). Profitable firms can make more efficient use of tax deductions and tax credits. Accruals from sales are likely to differ between tax and financial reporting due to revenue recognition differences between both systems. The tax depreciable lives of property, plant and equipment are set by tax rules whereas the depreciable lives of property, plant and equipment for financial reporting purposes are determined by managerial judgment. If depreciable lives for tax purposes are lower than those used for financial reporting purposes, taxable income will be lower than accounting income in the early years of an asset's life and higher in the later years of an asset's life. Thus, we expect BTD to be positively associated with the utilization of depreciable assets measured using the ratio of gross property, plant and equipment to assets. Moreover, firms with younger assets (proxied by the ratio of net property, plant and equipment to gross property, plant and equipment) are more likely to have larger book-tax differences.⁵

## 32.4.2 Additional tests

We perform several robustness checks. First, we winsorize all variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles and we find that our results are not sensitive to outliers. Second, we consider the effect of mergers on CEO compensation. Bliss and Rosen (2001) find that bank mergers have a net positive effect on CEO compensation, even if mergers cause the acquiring bank’s stock price to decline. They find that the majority of mergers increase the overall wealth of the CEO, often at the expense of shareholders. To address the effect of mergers on CEO compensation, we identify all completed mergers from the Securities Data Corporation database and delete all mergers from our sample. Our results (not tabulated) are qualitatively similar — CEO compensation is negatively associated with temporary book-tax differences. Third, we have multiple observations for our firms, which suggests that the t-statistics may be overstated. To mitigate this issue, we averaged all the observations available for a given firm and then use the average of the observations in the regressions. Our inferences are not affected. Fourth, it is possible that CEO changes may affect accounting earnings and hence, compensation. Pourciau (1993) provides evidence of earnings management associated with top executive changes. She finds that incoming executives manage accruals in a way that decreases earnings in the year of executive change and increases earnings in the following year whereas departing executives record accruals that decrease earnings during their last year of tenure. To address the concern that executive change may affect our results, we exclude from our sample all CEOs who joined or left the firm during the sampling period. CEO compensation still exhibits a statistically significant negative association with book-tax differences.

## 32.5 Conclusions

We examine two competing hypothesis on the association between CEO compensation and book-tax differences: the efficiency hypothesis and the rent-seeking hypothesis. Under the efficiency hypothesis, the board of directors uses the information in book-tax differences to undo such attempts that artificially inflate accounting income. Thus, CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences. Under the rent-seeking hypothesis, CEOs gain effective control of the pay-setting process so that they set their own pay with little oversight from shareholders and directors. The directors do not use the information in book-tax differences to undo the CEOs’ attempted earnings manipulation. Consequently, CEOs derive private benefits as their compensation is positively associated with book-tax differences.

We find that CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences. This provides evidence in support of the efficiency hypothesis: directors appear to use information in book-tax differences to set CEO compensation. This result is consistent across different compensation components such as salary, bonus and stock-based pay. We also investigate the implications of large book-tax differences on CEO compensation. Results indicate that CEO compensation is negatively associated with large positive book-tax differences and positively associated with large negative book-tax differences. This provides additional evidence supporting the efficiency hypothesis as the directors do not seem to reward CEO when firms have large positive book-tax differences (accounting income greater than taxable income). Furthermore, the directors appear to shield CEO compensation from the income-decreasing effect of large negative book-tax differences.

We also provide evidence that firms with strong corporate governance structure limit the CEO’s ability to capture the pay process. Specifically, we document that firms with high insider ownership and high proportion of outside directors on the board have lower CEO compensation when book-tax differences are large.

Our study contains several contributions. First, we contribute to the stream of research on using book-tax differences to infer earnings quality. While prior studies examine whether investors use the information in book-tax differences to assess earnings persistence, we provide evidence that the firm in general, and the board of directors in particular, use the information in book-tax differences to determine CEO compensation. Second, we complement prior studies that find directors shield CEO compensation from the negative consequences of income-decreasing activities (Dechow et al., 1994; Gaver and Gaver, 1998; Adut et al., 2003). Specifically, our results suggest that directors shield CEO compensation from the income-decreasing effect of large negative book-tax differences.
