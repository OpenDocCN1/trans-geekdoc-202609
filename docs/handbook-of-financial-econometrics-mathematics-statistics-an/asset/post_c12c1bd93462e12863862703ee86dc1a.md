## Appendix 57A: Proof of Lemma 57.2.3

Recall the matrix Ξ is the variance–covariance for the whole population, which can be decomposed into the within-group variance–covariance matrix Σ and the variability between the K groups as Ξ = Σ + ∑_{i=1}^k w_i (μ_i - μ̄)(μ_i - μ̄)'.

The objective function g₂(ξ) in (57.5) can be decomposed accordingly and rewritten as g₂(ξ_i) = (1 / ξ_i' Σ^{-1} ξ_i) {ξ_i' (I_{p×p} + A) ξ_i}, (57A.1) where the positive-definite matrix A = Σ^{-1/2} (∑_{j=1}^K w_j (μ_j - μ̄)(μ_j - μ̄)')^{-1/2} Σ .

To maximize the two objective functions g₁(ξ) and g₂(ξ) subject to the constraints that ξ'ξ = 1 and ξ' ξ_j = 0 for all j < i, consider the matrices W₁ = (I_{p×p} - P_i)A(I_{p×p} - P_i) and W₂ = (I_{p×p} - P_i)Σ^{-1}(I_{p×p} - P_i), where I_{p×p} is the identity matrix and P_i is the projection matrix that maps vectors in R^N onto the subspace spanned by ξ₁,…,ξ_{i-1}. By Lemma 57.2.2, the objective g₁(ξ) can be rewritten as g₁(ξ) = ξ'W₁ξ. By Lemma 57.2.2 and (57A.1), ξ' ξ_j = 0 for all j < i, the objective g₂(ξ) can be rewritten as g₂(ξ) = ξ'{I_{p×p} + W₁}ξ / ξ'W₂ξ .

Let λ₁* ≥ λ₂* ≥ ⋯ ≥ λ_p* ≥ 0 and λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ λ_p ≥ 0 be the eigenvalues of W₁ and W₂, respectively. If W₁ and W₂ share the same normalized eigenvector v₁ corresponding to the largest eigenvalues λ₁* and λ₁, respectively, then the two objective functions g₁(ξ) and g₂(ξ) are maximized simultaneously at ξ* = v₁ subject to the constraints that ξ*'ξ* = 1 and ξ*'ξ_j = 0 for all j < i. In addition, g₁(ξ*) = λ₁* and g₂(ξ*) = λ₁(λ₁ + 1).

## Abstract

This chapter resolves an inconclusive issue in the empirical literature about the relationship between downside risk and stock returns for Asian markets. This study demonstrates that the mixed signs on the risk coefficient stem from the fact that the excess stock return series is assumed to be stationary with a short memory, which is inconsistent with the downside risk series featuring a long memory process. After we appropriately model the long memory property of downside risk and apply a fractional difference to downside risk, the evidence consistently supports a significant and positive risk–return relation. This holds true for downside risk not only in the domestic market but also across markets. The evidence suggests that the risk premium is higher if the risk originates in a dominant market, such as the US. These findings are robust even when we consider the leverage effect, value-at-risk feedback, and the long memory effect in the conditional variance.

## 58.1 Introduction

As inspired by Merton’s (1973) seminal paper on intertemporal capital asset pricing theory, voluminous studies have been devoted to the empirical investigation of the relation between the conditional variance/covariance and expected excess market returns. French et al. (1987), Baillie and DeGennaro (1990), Campbell and Hentschel (1992), Scruggs (1998), Ghysels et al. (2005), Bali and Peng (2006), Ludvigson and Ng (2007), and Bali and Cakici (2010) test the hypothesis that excess stock returns are positively related to risk, as measured by the conditional variance of stock returns. They report evidence to support a positive relation. However, testing the same hypothesis, Breen et al. (1989), Nelson (1991), Glosten et al. (1993), and Ang, et al. (2006) find a negative relation. The signs of the risk–return relation appear to be inconclusive.¹
