
In order to accommodate their variability, moreover, to harmonize every model parameter such that jumpy risk premia is well accounted for. Treating risk-free interest rate and dividends as stochastic variables has been done before by Pan (2002). Note that \(C\) can also be interpreted as put \(P\) because the Margrabe formula simultaneously prices a call option that the acquirer exercises during mergers and a put option that the target firm gives away. \(S_1\) (\(S_2\)) is the log-normally distributed price of the acquiring (target) firm. Tau (i.e., \(\tau\)) is the time remaining until the merger is completed, and \(\sigma_1\) (\(\sigma_2\)) is the volatility of the acquiring (target) firm. The combined volatility of merging firms is \(\hat{\sigma}_p\), and \(\rho_{1,2}\) is the correlation coefficient between merging firms. \(N(d_1)\) (\(N(d_2)\)) is the univariate cumulative normal distribution function integrated to the upper limit \(d_1\) (\(d_2\)). \(K\) is an exchange ratio in a merger, i.e., the number of target firm shares acquired for every share of the acquiring firm. Although the Margrabe formula is continuous, one of the things that prior studies never explicitly explained is the parameters of the Margrabe formula. Prices are assumed to follow a log-normal process (i.e., follow geometric BM) such that it is impossible to get negative prices. All exponential parameters are continuous. Given that inputs of \(d_1\) (\(d_2\)) and \(N(d_1)\) (\(N(d_2)\)) are continuous, it follows that \(d_1\) (\(d_2\)) and \(N(d_1)\) (\(N(d_2)\)) are continuous as well. Thus, the two-asset option formula will be modified such that it is suitable for pricing options in incomplete markets.

Given that \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) are continuous in the complete world, then in the incomplete one should adjust them such that they incorporate jumps. A stock price that follows a Lévy process can be represented as

$$S(t + \Delta t) = S(t) e^x \tag{34.5}$$

where \(S(t)\) is price at time \(t\), \(S(t + \Delta t)\) is price at time \(t + \Delta t\), and \(x\) is the size of the movement in log price of a small time interval \(\Delta t\), in addition, \(-\infty < \langle x \rangle < +\infty\). The \(x\) in equation (34.5) follows an exponential movement. The elegance of exponents is that they are applicable to logarithm movements which is consistent with the movement of options. The advantage of using a Lévy process is that on one hand the Margrabe formula maintains its continuous form and on the other hand it incorporates stock jumps. In the first case, the chosen martingale measure \(Q\) is a Poisson process. A Poisson process \(N_\lambda = (N_\lambda(t), t \ge 0)\) with intensity \(\lambda > 0\) is a Lévy process with characteristics \((0, 0, \lambda\delta_1)\), where \(\delta_1\) is a Dirac mass concentrated at 1. Just like Merton (1976), this chapter defines Poisson process as the arrival of “new” information. In case equation (34.5) follows a Cox process, \(\delta_1 > 1\) and the rest of parameters in equation (34.5) take the same sizes. \(N_\lambda\) takes positive integer values. Thus, Poisson process is

$$P\{N_\lambda(t)=n\} = \frac{e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^n}{n!}. \tag{34.6}$$

The paths of \(N_\lambda\) are piecewise constant on each finite interval, with predictable jumps of size 1 at the random times \(\tau_n = \inf\{t \ge 0, N_\lambda(t)=n\}\). Applebaum (2004) adopted similar principles for a Poisson process illustration. During a merger, specifically when there is a single bid, stock price movements tend to be stable. Rossi and Volpin (2004) illustrated this phenomenon before. It follows that the call option of the Margrabe formula when it is a Poisson process is

$$C(S_1,S_2,\tau,K,\sigma,r,\gamma,y) = \mathbb{E}_Q\left[\underbrace{\left(\frac{e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^n}{n!}\right)}_{PP}\left(S(t)-K\right)^+ | \mathcal{F}_s\right], \tag{34.7}$$

where \(\mathbb{E}\) denotes an expectation of a call option, \(Q\) is a martingale measure for the process \(S\), \(PP\) stands for a Poisson Process, and \(\mathcal{F}_s\) denotes the filtration of information at time \(s\). Note that \(\mathcal{F}_s\) is not only a filtration process in relation to information at time \(s\), but prior information up to time \(s\) is included as well. This would imply information at time \(t\) where \(t < s\) is included. The lemma in equation (34.7) is similar to the one used by Blenman and Clark (2005) for their first proof. Given the fact that jumps are experienced in \(S_1/S_2\), \(y\), because it includes \(r\) and \(\gamma\), and \(\sigma\), as it changes as per changes in prices, it follows that when equation (34.1) incorporates a Poisson process, it changes into

$$C_p(S_1,S_2,\tau) = \left(\frac{e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^n}{n!}\right)\underbrace{K S_1 e^{y_1 \tau} N(d_1) - S_2 e^{y_2 \tau} N(d_2)}_{\text{MCO}}, \tag{34.8}$$

where \(C_p\) is a call option underpinned by a Poisson process, MCO is the Margrabe call option, and the rest of the parameters are the same as before. Intuitively, equation (34.8) can be interpreted as jumps (as illustrated by Poisson process) multiplied by exchange option (as illustrated by Margrabe formula). Although equation (34.8) seems simple, it is quite dynamic. Just like in Schmutz (2011), this chapter allows \(\tau\) to be random. The latter phenomenon allows equation (34.8) to “capture” option value all the way up to positive infinity. To one’s knowledge this is the first Margrabe-type model that captures option value all the way to positive infinity. If one assumes that a Poisson process changes with respect to maturity, time \(t\) becomes \(\tau\). On the other hand, changes in time create consistency. A common phenomenon in the real estate investment trust (REIT) industry is that there is only one jump in stock price. Hence, with both period and intensity equal to one, equation (34.8) changes to

$$= (e^{-\tau}(\tau))(K S_1 e^{y_1 \tau} N(d_1) - S_2 e^{y_2 \tau} N(d_2)) \tag{34.9}$$

Multiplying throughout equation (34.9) yields:

$$= K S_1 e^{y_1 \tau - \tau}(\tau) N(d_1) - S_2 e^{y_2 \tau - \tau}(\tau) N(d_2) \tag{34.10}$$

Factorizing equation (34.10) in an ideal world (i.e., \(K = 1:1\)) yields:

$$= S_1 e^{\tau(y_1 - 1)}(\tau) N(d_1) - S_2 e^{\tau(y_2 - 1)}(\tau) N(d_2). \tag{34.11}$$

Note that costs of carry are predominantly positive; however, they can be negative in declining economies. Therefore, net cost of carry is either positive or negative, and equation (34.11) can be re-written as

$$= S_1 e^{\pm y_1 \tau}(\tau) N(d_1) - S_2 e^{\pm y_2 \tau}(\tau) N(d_2). \tag{34.12}$$

The derivation in equation (34.12) is similar to the derivation in Finnerty (2015, Appendix A). Factorizing equation (34.12) yields:

$$C_{pp} = \tau e^{\tau(\pm y_1 \pm y_2)}(S_1 N(d_1) - S_2 N(d_2)), \tag{34.13}$$

where \(e^{\tau(\pm y_1 \pm y_2)}\) is the discounting factor, which is similar to a bond discounting factor. Equation (34.13) is when the Margrabe formula follows a Poisson process and it is suitable for pricing exchange options when there are stock jumps only and only if stock jumps follow a Poisson process. Equation (34.13) is similar to the formula for a constant-volatility GBM for pricing currency options (see Bates, 1996). Tau in the equation illustrates that \(C_p\) accounts for stock jumps at different periods of \(C_p\), a phenomenon which is inconsistent with equation (34.1). Second, the overall cost of carry in equation (34.13) is positive. It follows from here that \(C_p\) in equation (34.13) approaches zero slower than \(C\) in equation (34.1) ceteris paribus because overall positive cost of carry has a maximization effect. When one compares equations (34.13) and (34.1), the “key” difference between those two equations is the positive premium, \(e^{\tau(\pm y_1 \pm y_2)}\). That is, this chapter illustrates how the change in discounting factor affects the value of equation (34.13). Equation (34.13) is indifferent to utility preferences. When one adopts a special case of two-asset options, i.e., \(S_1\) and \(S_0\), which has been used previously by Cox et al. (1985) and Scott (1997), equation (34.13), when not factorized, is:

$$= S_1 e^{\pm y_1 \tau}(\tau) N(d_1). \tag{34.14}$$

The resulting equation (34.14) is the pricing of an option if and only if the underlying assets are \(S_1\) and \(S_0\), and it illustrates the probability of being in the money when risk-free interest rate is a numéraire for a given time of option contract. A possible situation where equation (34.14) might be applicable is when a merger has been agreed upon in advance and some money has been put down for the transaction. Although equation (34.14) is a non-log formula, it is the moneyness of the option, similar to Yan (2011). Secondly, equation (34.14) is similar to the first term of the BS solution on the right-hand side except that it has “extra” time and risk premium parameters. Thirdly, it is also similar to equation (17) in Merton (1976). These two inferences make sense, as equation (17) in Merton (1976) and equation (34.14) in this chapter are drawn from the brilliance of the BS formula.
