Second, we investigate how the DCC-GARCH model works in practice through two empirical applications to the integration process of bond and stock markets in the emerging East Asian countries, comprising the ASEAN5 (Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand), China, Hong Kong and Korea. Following Kim and Lee (2012, p. 333), we take a fully integrated financial market as representing the situation in which traders can transact financial assets freely within the area.² Based on this definition, our analysis focuses on the commonality of bond yield and stock price movements between different markets in the region over time. The degrees of commonality are measured numerically by the DCCs and dynamic variance decompositions. By way of these measures, we assess the levels of bond and stock market integration in the region. In addition, we also assess the trends in integration during the past 15 years in the region. East Asia has grown rapidly during the past 25 years and is currently recognized as the growth center of the world economy, despite the experiences of the Asian financial crisis in 1997–98 and the global financial crisis in 2007–08. The emerging East Asia local currency bonds and stocks listed in the markets in this region have thus become indispensable asset classes for global investors. Hence, the investigation of financial market integration in the region is of great interest for practitioners as well as academics.³

The DCC model is now widely used for analyzing asset markets. See, for instance, Skintzi and Refenes (2006), Bauwens et al. (2013), Grier and Smallwood (2013), Connor and Suurlaht (2013), and Syllignakis and Kouretas (2013). Park and Lee (2011) find that the stock markets in the emerging East Asia are increasingly integrated both regionally and globally, whereas the bond markets remain largely segmented from others in the region as well as from global markets. Guimarães-Filho and Hee-Hong (2016) find that the increase in the net equity return and volatility connectedness indexes in most Asian emerging economies is consistent with the trend of growing intra-regional financial integration. Glick and Hutchison (2013) show that Asian stock markets correlate increasingly more with the Chinese stock market after the global financial crisis than is the case with bond markets. Our overall findings largely concur with those in Park and Lee (2011). However, our study also provides new insights into the financial integration process in the emerging East Asian countries, with the application confirming the practical usefulness of the DCC-GARCH model.

## 123.2 Univariate GARCH Models

This section briefly reviews the developments in the various types of univariate GARCH models as basic background knowledge for understanding the multivariate GARCH model in the next section. Although there are many models proposed in the literature, here we only explain some of the more important ones.

### 123.2.1 Univariate GARCH(p, q) model

Francq and Zakoian (2010) list seven stylized facts universally observed in financial markets:

- (i) nonstationarity of price series;
- (ii) absence of autocorrelation in price returns;
- (iii) presence of autocorrelation in squared price returns;
- (iv) volatility clustering;
- (v) fat-tailed distributions;
- (vi) leverage effects;
- (vii) seasonality.

The GARCH model specification is well able to capture most of the stylized facts characterizing financial time series. We begin with a univariate autoregressive (AR) model of order \(k\) (or AR(\(k\))),

$$
y_t = \mu + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \gamma_i y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t, \quad t = 1, \dots, T, \qquad (123.1)
$$

where \(\varepsilon_t = h_t^{1/2} z_t\), \(h_t > 0\), and \(z_t\) are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with \(E(z_t) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(z_t) = 1\). We assume that all solutions of the characteristic equation

$$
1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k} \gamma_i \lambda^{i} = 0, \qquad (123.2)
$$

are greater than unity in absolute value. Under this condition, the process \(\{y_t\}\) is stationary if \(\{\varepsilon_t\}\) is stationary. We do not assume any specific distribution of the standardized random variable \(z_t\). The process \(\{\varepsilon_t\}\) in equation (123.1) is then a GARCH(p, q) model if its first two conditional moments exist and satisfy⁴

- (i) \(E(\varepsilon_t \mid I_{t-1}) = 0, \qquad (123.3)\)
- (ii) \(h_t = \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t \mid I_{t-1}) = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^{q} \alpha_i \varepsilon_{t-i}^{2} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j h_{t-j}, \qquad (123.4)\)

where \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j \ge 0\) (\(i = 1, \dots, q\); \(j = 1, \dots, p\)), and \(I_{t-1}\) in (123.3) and (123.4) denotes the information available up to time \(t - 1\). If \(\beta_j = 0\) for \(j = 1, \dots, p\), the GARCH(0, q) model is known as an autoregressive conditional heteroscedastic (ARCH(q)) model. Engle (1982) first introduced the ARCH(q) model, with Bollerslev (1986) extending it to GARCH(p, q) model. The conditional expectation of \(y_t\) given the information \(I_{t-1}\) is

$$
E(y_t \mid I_{t-1}) = \mu + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \gamma_i y_{t-i}. \qquad (123.5)
$$

The conditional variance \(h_t\), given in (123.4), possibly changes over time, but is completely determined by past information. If all solutions of the

$$
1 - \sum_{i=1}^{q} \alpha_i \lambda^i - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j \lambda^j = 0, \qquad (123.6)
$$

are greater than unity in absolute value, the process \(\{h_t\}\) is stationary, and the unconditional variance of \(\varepsilon_t\) is given by

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t) = \frac{\omega}{1 - \sum_{i=1}^{q} \alpha_i - \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j}. \qquad (123.7)
$$

The GARCH(p, q) model is then able to trace some important stylized facts of financial time series such as the absence of the autocorrelation of returns, the autocorrelations of squared returns, volatility clustering and fat-tailed distributions. This model gave tremendous influence on the areas of financial data analysis and financial risk management. It is well known that a simple GARCH(1,1) model will often fit real financial data in practice.

### 123.2.2 Asymmetric volatility models

#### 123.2.2.1 GJR(p,q) model

Glosten, Jagannathan and Runkle (1993) (GJR) extended the GARCH(p,q) model to allow for the asymmetry impact of past positive and negative values on current volatility as follows:

$$
h_t = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^{q} (\alpha_{i1} + \alpha_{i2} J_{t-i}^-) \varepsilon_{t-i}^2 + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j h_{t-j}. \qquad (123.8)
$$

where \(J_{t-i}^- = 1\) if \(\varepsilon_{t-i} < 0\), \(J_{t-i}^- = 0\) otherwise, and \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha_{i1}\), \(\alpha_{i2}\) and \(\beta_j \ge 0\) (\(i = 1, \dots, q\); \(j = 1, \dots, p\)). Negative returns tend to increase volatility more than positive returns if \(\alpha_{i2} > 0\). Consider, for example, a simple model of \(p = q = 1\). Then, we have

$$
h_t = \omega + (\alpha_{11} + \alpha_{12}) \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta_1 h_{t-1}, \quad \omega > 0, \ \alpha_{11} \ge 0, \ \alpha_{12} > 0, \ \beta_1 \ge 0 \text{ if } \varepsilon_{t-1} < 0, \qquad (123.9)
$$

and

$$
h_t = \omega + \alpha_{11} \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta_1 h_{t-1}, \quad \omega > 0, \ \alpha_{11} \ge 0, \ \beta_1 \ge 0 \text{ if } \varepsilon_{t-1} \ge 0. \qquad (123.10)
$$
