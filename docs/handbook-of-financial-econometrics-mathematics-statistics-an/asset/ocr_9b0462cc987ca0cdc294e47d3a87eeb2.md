Patton et al. (2015) illustrate that over 50% of hedge funds in their sample restated performance and during the restatements, the performance that was initially reported typically presented an overly optimistic view of the hedge fund’s performance and indicated that after managers revise, they can predict with greater certainty which hedge funds will underperform based upon their previous revisions. Rudin (2018) argues that one of the determinantes of hedge fund performance that is truly persistent is hedge fund managers’ fees in his study of equity long–short and macro/managed futures hedge funds.

This chapter moves from an exploration of performance smoothing and the relative opacity of hedge funds to examining how researchers and practitioners should model hedge fund performance. Lo (2010) provides one of the most comprehensive reviews on hedge fund performance, but Chen and Tindall (2014), Grummitt and Satchell (2011), O’Doherty et al. (2017), and Hasanhodzic and Lo (2007) focus specifically on attempts to replicate hedge fund returns using various cloning techniques. Stafylas et al. (2018) review the literature on hedge fund performance and the estimation of hedge fund performance and illustrate that Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Common Factor Analysis (CFA) are common techniques that researchers use to model hedge fund performance and that different factors should be considered when examining different return series; this understanding has led some researchers to pursue using nonlinear estimation techniques to help account for the optionality involved with hedge fund performance results. Agarwal et al. (2018) make an important point in their segmentation between traditional factor exposures and more exotic factor exposures and indicate that there is evidence that investors have a preference for exotic alpha when allocating to hedge funds but find no evidence that hedge fund managers can persistently generate exotic alpha; so, investors should not base their evaluations of hedge fund managers based on their ability to generate alpha from exotic risk exposure. Panopoulou and Vrontos (2015) attempt to model hedge fund performance using models to integrate discounting factor errors, cluster combining, and principal components combing and the kitchen sink, bootstrap aggregating, lasso, ridge, and elastic net specifications and find that a simple combination of forecasting techniques works better than more sophisticated models.
