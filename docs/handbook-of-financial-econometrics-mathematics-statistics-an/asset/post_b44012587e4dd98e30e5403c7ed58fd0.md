## 65.1 Introduction

Brown and Gibbons (1985) developed a theory of relative risk aversion estimation in terms of average market rates of return and the variance of market rates of return. However, the exact sampling distributions of the relative risk aversion estimators have not been derived. The main purpose of this chapter is to derive the exact sampling distribution of an appropriate relative risk aversion (RRA) estimator. Statistical inference based on the sampling distribution has significant implications for the study of many important issues in finance. For instance, the appropriateness of a log utility function which is crucial for some important results in finance can now be tested based on the null hypothesis of a unitary relative risk aversion (see, e.g., Cox et al., 1985; Hakanson, 1970; Kraus and Litzenberger, 1975; Merton, 1971; Rubinstein, 1977; Samuelson, 1969); the effects of changes in the degree of risk aversion on the demand for risky assets and the saving decisions (see, e.g., Rothschild and Stiglitz, 1971) can now be empirically evaluated; the contribution of changes in aggregate risk aversion to the controversial issue of excessive volatility of stock prices (see, e.g., Grossman and Shiller, 1981) can be correctly estimated.

Section 65.2 will derive the exact sampling distribution of the uniformly minimum variance unbiased RRA estimator, as well as the maximum likelihood estimator, assuming the lognormal model. Applications of the sampling distribution of the RRA estimator will be explored in Section 65.3. Section 65.4 summarizes the results.

## 65.2 Derivation of the Probability Density Function of the RRA Estimator

We shall begin by establishing some notation and the underlying lognormal distributional setting. Denote the market rate of return as the random variable R_m and the risk-free rate of return as the random variable R_f. We also let X = (1 − R_m)/(1 − R_f) and Y = log X. Suppose a random sample of size T is available with sample values of R_m, denoted R_mt and the sample values of R_f, denoted R_ft, for t = 1,2,…,T. Then X_t = (1 − R_mt)/(1 − R_ft) is the tth sample observation on X and Y_t is the tth sample observation on Y. We identify

Y_t = log X_t = log(1 − R_mt)/(1 − R_ft) = log(1 − R_mt) − log(1 − R_ft), (65.1)

as the excess market rate of return for the tth sample observation. Finally, we denote the sample mean excess market rate of return by

Ȳ = log X̄ = overline{log(1 − R_m)} − overline{log(1 − R_f)} = Σ_{t=1}^T Y_t / T

and the sample variance of excess market rate of return by

S² = Σ_{t=1}^T (Y_t − Ȳ)² / (T − 1).

In investigating utility-based asset pricing models, Brown and Gibbons (1985) assumed that Y is normally distributed, or that X is lognormally distributed, with parameters μ and σ², the mean and variance of Y, respectively, and estimate the relative risk aversion parameter, β = (μ/σ²) + 1/2, with the estimator

β̂_ML = Ȳ / S² + 1/2. (65.2)

Note that β̂_ML would be the maximum likelihood estimator if, instead of using S² in (2), ((T − 1)/T) S² was used.

It is important to note that β̂_ML is a function of Ȳ / S².¹ It shall be shown that the uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator of β is also a function of Ȳ / S², and, therefore, we derive first the probability density function (pdf) of Ȳ / S², under the normality assumption of Y.

Thus, let Y₁, Y₂, …, Y_T be a random sample of size T from a normal distribution with mean μ and variance σ², where for t = 1,2,…,T, Y_t is given by (65.1). Then the following exact results are well known (Hogg and Craig, 1978) from statistical sampling theory;

- 1. Ȳ is normally distributed with mean μ and variance σ²/T,
- 2. (T − 1) S² / σ² is chi square distributed with T − 1 degrees of freedom,
