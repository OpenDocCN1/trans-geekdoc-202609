# Econometric measures of variance.

The second component of the dataset consists of high-frequency econometric estimates of the S&P 500 index variance. In particular, we consider realized variation, $RV$, realized kernel, RK, and bipower variation, BPV. These measures are obtained from the Oxford-Man Institute realized library (version 0.3), Heber et al. (2009). Days when the econometric measures of variation are not available are, by necessity, excluded from consideration.

With the advent of high-frequency trading and the consequent availability of ample intraday data on returns, it became possible to estimate the variation of a financial return series without resorting to a latent variable model (like the original ARCH of Engle (1982) or its stochastic volatility counterparts, see Kim et al., 1998). In particular, based on the path properties of stochastic processes, Andersen et al. (2001) introduced the realized variation as a model-free measure of variance over a given period of time (e.g., a trading day):

```
$RV_t = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \left[\log \left( \frac{S_{t+(i+1)\delta}}{S_{t+i\delta}} \right) \right]^2,$  (33.7)
```

where $t$ is the beginning of the trading day $t$ and $\delta > 0$ reflects the intra-day sampling frequency with $t+N\delta$ corresponding to the end of the trading day $t$. In a frictionless market without bid-ask spreads, one would prefer to sample the data as often as possible. However, the presence of market microstructure noise makes it optimal to select a lower sampling frequency. Following common practice, we use the realized variation measure constructed based on 5-minute returns. As a robustness check, we also consider the realized kernel of Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2008) (with Parzen weight function). Finally, to isolate the effect of jumps, we use the bipower variation measure of Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2004) to quantify the continuous component of variation. Table 33.1 contains the summary statistics for the calibrated stochastic volatility model parameters (columns $\eta$ and $\rho$) and calibrated variance time series (column $V_t^{SV}$), as well as econometric estimates of variance (columns $V_t^{RV}$ and $V_t^{RK}$) and its continuous component (column, $V_t^{BPV}$).

The calibrated Heston model parameters exhibit anticipated signs and magnitudes. In particular, the mean and median instantaneous correlation are both close to $-0.77$, which generates a negative implied volatility skew. Moreover, the calibrated instantaneous correlation is never positive. The calibrated volatility of variance is always positive and not close to zero, which naturally supports the stochastic volatility specification with the attendant implied volatility skew.
