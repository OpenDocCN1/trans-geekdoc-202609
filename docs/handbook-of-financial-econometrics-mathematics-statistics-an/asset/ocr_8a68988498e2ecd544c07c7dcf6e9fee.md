Equation (62.18) can be used to explain FB's principle finding that b < 0 since (62.18) can be rewritten as

Plim \hat{b} = \{-\sigma_s^2 k /( \sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_s^2 )\} - B + \tau, (62.19)

where the first term of equation (62.19) represents the bias caused by the sample variation of systematic risk in the first-round regression, −B represents the bias caused by the difference between the borrowing and lending rate, and τ represents the bias caused by the error in the measurement of R_m. Hence, if −B + τ is significantly negative (positive) ĥ asymptotically will also be negative (positive) when the bias caused by the sampling variation in β_j in the first-round regression is very small. Since −B = R_f − R_b < 0, FB's specification that the reason ĥ < 0 is due to the divergence of borrowing and lending rate appears as a reasonable proposition.

Finally, when the sampling period is divided into two subperiods, FB found that ĥ for 1960–1964 is negative while ĥ for 1965–1968 is positive. FB attributed the latter phenomenon to “unanticipated appreciation” in the market return, though FB did not define the term rigorously. If, following many economists, FB defined unanticipated appreciation as the difference between the realized return to the market and its expected value for the period, the latter cannot cause any bias in b since the one-factor model takes into account the stochasticity in R'_m. However, from our equation (62.19), b can be positive (negative) if −B + τ is positive (negative). Further, if the return to the segment of capital market not representing stocks listed in the New York Stock Exchange has a β greater than unity, τ for a subperiod can be positive (negative) when the realized R_m is positive (negative). Hence, even if −B < 0, b for the prosperity period can be positive while b for the recession period will remain negative.
