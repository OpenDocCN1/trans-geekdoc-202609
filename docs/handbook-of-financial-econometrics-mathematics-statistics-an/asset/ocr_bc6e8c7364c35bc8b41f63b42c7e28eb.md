Jagadeesh *et al.* (2015) point out that when using portfolios, rather than individual assets, there is an immediate issue of test power since the dimensionality is reduced, i.e., there are unavoidably fewer explanatory variables with portfolios than with individual assets. Berk (2000) shows that the standard practice of grouping stocks into portfolios to test asset pricing inferences introduces a bias in rejecting the model under consideration.

A more effective and efficient method to deal with these problems is to use individual stocks instead of characteristics or attribute-sorted portfolios in testing the asset pricing models (Hwang and Lu, 2007) and apply the reverse regression technique. By applying the direct and reverse regression technique to test asset pricing models and other capital market research, one not only corrects for the EIV and measurement problem with observed asset returns, but also avoids grouping securities into portfolios which results in loss of information and data snooping biases.

# 75.3 Direct and Reverse Regression Technique

In a two-independent variable regression model of $Y$ on $X_1$ and $X_2$, three regression equations consisting of one direct and two reverse regressions (i.e., regressing each of the independent variables on the dependent variable and the other independent variable) are

$$Y = a_0 + a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + \varepsilon_t, \tag{75.1}$$

$$X_1 = b_0 + b_1Y + b_2X_2 + \mu_t, \tag{75.2}$$

$$X_2 = c_0 + c_1X_1 + c_2Y + \varsigma_t. \tag{75.3}$$

Reverse regression estimates are derived by solving equations (75.2) and (75.3) for the dependent variable $Y$ so that

$$Y = -b_0/b_1 + (1/b_1)X_1 - (b_2/b_1)X_2 - (1/b_1)\mu_t, \tag{75.4}$$

$$Y = -c_0/c_2 - (c_1/c_2)X_1 + (1/c_2)X_2 - (1/c_2)\varsigma_t. \tag{75.5}$$

The reverse regression estimates from equation (75.2) are $-b_0/b_1$ (intercept), $1/b_1$ for $X_1$, and $-b_2/b_1$ for $X_2$ and from equation (75.3) are $-c_0/c_2$ (intercept), $-c_1/c_2$ for $X_1$, and $1/c_2$ for $X_2$.
