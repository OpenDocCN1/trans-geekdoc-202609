For computing the empirical powers of the proposed test statistic, we employ the true values of (μ1,β1,σ1) the same as in Section 72.4.1. For (μ2,β2,σ2), we consider nine different cases which are labeled as C3,C4,..., and C11. Each of the nine experiments follows one of these three designs: (i) μ1 < μ2, and σ1² < σ2², (ii) μ1 = μ2, and σ1² < σ2², (iii) μ1 < μ2, and σ1² = σ2². It is not difficult to see that the alternative hypothesis Cα1 ≠ Cα2 holds for all the three designs. Therefore, the rejection rate produced by each experiment is the empirical power of the test.

Table 72.3 lists the (μ2,β2,σ2)’s for the nine different experiment cases C3–C11, and summarizes their power performance in three categories as relatively smaller, middle, and relatively larger. The detailed powers for the nine experiment cases under 18 different (N,T)’s are reported in Table 72.4. In general, the power increases as the value of N or T goes up except for a few cases. We also notice that the level of power is relatively larger for case C11, and middle for cases C5 and C10. For the other cases, the powers are relatively smaller. All these results indicate that the test statistic has reasonably good power in detecting the difference between two CTEs generated from the integrated returns of two independent time series.
