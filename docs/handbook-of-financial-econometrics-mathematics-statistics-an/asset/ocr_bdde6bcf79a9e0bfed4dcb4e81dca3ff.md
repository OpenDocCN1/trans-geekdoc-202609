Grummitt and Satchell (2011), in their efforts to replicate hedge fund performance, explain that their estimation of hedge fund returns does a good job of estimating hedge fund returns using an OLS regression. The particular method that Grummitt et al. (2011) was based on Hasanhodzic and Lo (2007) in which the hedge fund return series was regressed on various systemic factors over the preceding 24 months to allow their model to adjust to changes in the weights to the holdings in the aggregate hedge fund managers’ portfolio. Grummitt et al. (2011) contend that at the extremes (i.e., the peaks and troughs), values seemed to be smoothed away in the hedge fund’s return series and reference Bollen and Pool (2009) and Agarwal et al. (2011) to explain the disconnect between the estimate and the actual return series. Di Cesare et al. (2014) illustrate that standard unadjusted risk measures may underestimate the true extent of individual and systemic risk factors that hedge fund managers take on.

Smith and Mumtaz (2017) use standard OLS and an in-sample estimation procedure to replicate hedge fund returns as illustrated in Figure 91.2. The returns, standard deviation, and return per unit of risk of the actual return series (i.e., average return: 0.72%; standard deviation: 2.13%; return per unit of risk: 33.69%) and the replication of that series (i.e., average return: 0.72%; standard deviation: 1.99%; return per unit of risk: 36.08%) were almost identical. For our purpose, which is to illustrate what systemic factors might be driving hedge fund performance, the Smith and Mumtaz (2017) replication do a relatively good job of estimating the hedge fund return series.
