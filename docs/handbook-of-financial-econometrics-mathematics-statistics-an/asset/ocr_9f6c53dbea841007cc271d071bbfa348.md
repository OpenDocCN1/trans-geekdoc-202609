# Abstract

This chapter investigates the characteristics of implied risk-neutral distributions derived separately from call and put options prices. Differences in risk-neutral moments between call and put options indicate deviations from put–call parity. We find that sentiment effect is significantly related to differences between call and put option prices. Our results suggest there is differential impact of investor sentiment and consumer sentiment on call and put option traders’ expectations. Rational and irrational sentiment components have different influence on call and put option traders’ beliefs as well.

# 42.1 Introduction

Put–call parity in options is a no-arbitrage relation in which a stock payoff is equivalent to the payoff for a portfolio that is long a call and short a put option. Kamara and Miller (1995) and Nisbet (1992) suggest that transaction costs, dividend payments, and the early exercise value of American options can cause call and put option prices to deviate from put–call parity. Another potential reason for deviations from put–call parity is that informed investors with private information trade in the options market before they trade in the underlying stock market. Therefore, Atilgan (2010) and Cremers and Weinbaum (2010) show that deviations from put–call parity measured by the volatility spread between call and put options contains information concerning future abnormal stock returns. Arbitrage restrictions also prevent the convergence of call and put option prices. When put option prices are sufficiently higher than the prices of underlying stock and corresponding call options, deviations from put–call parity arise in the presence of short sale constraints on the underlying stocks (Lamont and Thaler, 2003; Ofek and Richardson, 2003; Ofek et al., 2004). Szu et al. (2011) find different patterns for the implied volatility curves between Taiwan Stock Exchange Capitalization Weighted Stock Index (TAIEX) call and put option prices provided by the Taiwan Futures Exchange (TAIFEX).
