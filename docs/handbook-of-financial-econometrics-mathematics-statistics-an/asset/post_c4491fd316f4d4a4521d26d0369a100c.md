## Abstract
This chapter examines the empirical performance of dynamic Gaussian affine term structure models (DGATSMs) at the zero lower bound (ZLB) when principal components analysis (PCA) is used to extract factors. We begin by providing a comprehensive review of DGATSM when PCA is used to extract factors highlighting its numerous auspicious qualities; it specifies bond yields to be a simple linear function of underlying Gaussian factors. This is especially favorable since, in principle, PCA works best when the model is linear and the first two moments are sufficient to describe the data, among other characteristics. DGATSM have a strong theoretical foundation grounded in the absence of arbitrage. DGATSM produce reasonable cross-sectional fits of the yield curve. Both of these qualities are inherited into the model when PCA is used to extract the state vector. Additionally, the implementation of PCA is simple in that it takes a matter of seconds to estimate factors and is convenient to include in estimation as most software packages have ready-to-use algorithms to compute the factors immediately. The results from our empirical investigation lead us to conclude that DGATSM, when PCA is employed to extract factors, perform very poorly at the ZLB. It frequently crosses the ZLB en route to producing negative out-of-sample forecasts for bond yields. The main implication in this study is that despite its numerous positive characteristics, DGATSM when PCA is used to extract factors produce poor empirical forecasts around the ZLB.

### 61.1 Introduction
Interest rate models are very useful to both academicians and practitioners in completing many tasks. Lately, at the forefront of these tasks has been the performance of interest rate models around crises (e.g., Global Financial Crisis of 2007–2010). Between December 2008 and December 2015, the US Federal Reserve Bank kept its Federal Funds Target rate essentially at zero to stimulate growth in its economy with the hopes of pulling it out of the recession. Since it has been rare for researchers to imagine that interest rates could fall below zero, thoughts of negative interest rates becoming a reality, though admissible, traditionally have been taken lightly. In this chapter, we examine the empirical performance of dynamic term structure models (DTSMs) characterized by principal components as factors in a near zero environment.

Among researchers, one of the most popular and widely studied types of DTSM is affine in nature and appropriately called the class of affine term structure models. Duffie and Kan (1996) developed it and shortly thereafter Dai and Singleton (2000) gave it a taxonomy to aid researchers in their examinations of it. As it specifies bond yields to be simple linear functions of underlying factors, this class of models is relatively easy to estimate. It has a strong theoretical foundation consistent with the absence of arbitrage. It does produce reasonable in-sample fits for the yield curve. Although researchers to be affine functions of these factors and this allows them to employ the “inversion” method (e.g., Pearson and Sun, 1994) to infer unobservable state variables from the yields. Finally, the authors propose and implement maximum likelihood estimation techniques to obtain parameters for the joint system of unobservable (latent) state variables and observable (macroeconomic) state variables. The authors note the need in being able to find good initial conditions in order to achieve convergence of their algorithm as it is a highly nonlinear system. Indeed, they propose a very specific algorithm that is arguably path-dependent. Although model estimation did yield cross-sectional model fits that were remarkably close to the actual data, a researcher attempting to estimate the model with no initial hunch regarding starting conditions for the maximum likelihood estimation algorithm would find estimating this model to be frustrating, time consuming, and computationally burdensome.

Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein, and Jones (2008) develop a representation of affine models that allows state variables to be estimated using PCA in a manner that is similar to the “inversion” method described above. Instead of assuming three bonds are measured perfectly, the authors take the state variables to be the first three principal components of the yield curve and assume these are measured without error. In order to employ maximum likelihood estimation of the model in the ℚ-measure, the researcher would take additional N yields to be measured with error. Since it is specified in the ℚ-measure, one can only produce in-sample cross-sectional model fits with this method as time-series information and the 𝕡-measure are needed to be able to provide out-of-sample predictions of yield curve behavior.

Arguably, the major advance in this literature has been the development of the Joslin, Singleton, and Zhu (2011a) normalization (hereafter, JSZ) in which factors are taken to be portfolios of yields. JSZ apply invariant transformations (e.g., see Dai and Singleton, 2000) to the process involving the latent factors written as a vector autoregressive specification of the first order to one which is expressed in terms of a portfolio of bond yields (such as the first three principal components). Joslin, Le, and Singleton (2013a) (hereafter, JLS) provide an extension to the JSZ framework that accounts for macroeconomic dynamics in addition to being able to describe the evolution of the factors as bond portfolios. One of the main estimation advantages of the JSZ (and by extension JLS) framework is that the majority of the parameters can be estimated via linear regressions and this greatly simplifies the computational burden of the estimation and time for completion for locating the global optimum of the maximum likelihood function describing the dynamics of the model.

As we noted above, from December 2008 through December 2015, the Fed had kept its target for the Fed Funds rate at essentially zero and so a very popular question among people working on research in this area surrounds the performance of dynamic term structure models at the ZLB. After providing an exhaustive review of the above models, we show that, despite its convenience as a choice of methods, consistent with much of the literature, affine term structure models perform poorly near the ZLB when principal components are used as the factors. We come to these conclusions by examining the JSZ normalization and the JLS model. Our results are based on the demonstration that cross-sectional fits and out-of-sample forecasts of the yield curve produced by these models frequently violate the ZLB over the period of our examination spanning January 2000 through December 2017. We also demonstrate that cross-sectional fits produced by JSZ around the ZLB are characterized by especially large volatility compared to other sub-periods. In our study, sub-periods are verified through the usage of a Chow (1960) (hereafter, Chow)-based F-test in determining structural breaks in the relation between the actual bond yield and lagged realizations of each model-fitted yield. Our findings contribute to the growing literature that documents the inability of affine term structure models for producing reasonable model forecasts for the yield curve around the ZLB.

### 61.2 Overview of the Usage of PCA in DTSM
The method of PCA surrounds the basic concept of explaining the most variation in a dataset. In this section, we provide an overview of the method of PCA including how it works in practice and what attracts it to academics and finance practitioners. We begin below in Section 61.2.1 with a discussion on this and start with some variable definitions.

### 61.2.1 Why is PCA useful for the modeling of DTSM?
As noted above, arguably, the most popular class of DTSM studied in the relevant literature postulates that bond yields are a linear combination of a small number of factors. These models presuppose a low-dimensional affine relationship between yields and the state vector (Piazzesi, 2010). PCA is a mathematical technique that enables one to reduce the dimensionality of a dataset (i.e., a large number of interrelated variables such as factors) while explaining the maximum amount of variation present in that dataset.
