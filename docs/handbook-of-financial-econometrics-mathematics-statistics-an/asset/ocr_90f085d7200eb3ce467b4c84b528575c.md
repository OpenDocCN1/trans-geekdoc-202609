# 24.3.3 Trading in financial markets with frictions

In an incomplete market, using no-arbitrage argument to price options will suffer from the problem of frictions such as the transaction costs of dynamic replicating strategies (Leland, 1985; Bensaid et al., 1992; Edirisinghe et al., 1993) and the derivation of option bounds as well (Henin and Pistre, 1998; Constantinides and Zariphopoulou, 1999, 2001; Masson and Perrakis, 2000; Constantinides and Perrakis, 2002; Constantinides et al., 2009). In this section, we review some studies that have investigated the tighten bounds derived by adding the consideration of market imperfections such as transaction costs or bid-ask spreads.

Henin and Pistre (1998) use this stochastic dominance method to derive bounds of European concave option prices incorporated with transaction costs and dividend payments on underlying asset. A concave option is whose exercise payoff is a concave functions of the difference between the underlying asset and the strike price. The bounding conditions for European concave options only depend on final distribution of the stock price which has adjusted to dividends. The transaction costs only affects the initial costs of replication strategy because of its European style. Instead of dealing with transaction costs directly, Masson and Perrakis (2000) pursue the tight option bounds by the consideration of bid-ask spread of option prices. Their upper and lower bounds follow two different risk-neutral distributions related to the implied volatilities of observed ask and bid option prices, respectively.

Many studies concentrate the analysis on option bounds in an incomplete market with proportional transaction costs. Constantinides and Zariphopoulou (1999, 2001) derive the bounds for option prices with proportional transaction costs and investors’ preferences. Constantinides and Perrakis (2002) extend Constantinides and Zariphopoulou’s (1999, 2001) approach under stochastic dominance framework. Their tight upper bound of the reservation write price of a call option is related to the transaction costs of selling and purchasing a stock. On the other hand, their tight lower bound of the reservation purchasing price of a put option is also based on the factors of transaction costs and the return rate on the bond. The advantages of the bound formulas are as follows: (1) these bounds of European options can be viewed as a generalization of single-period model to a multi-period economy since they are independent of the trading frequency; (2) The upper (lower) bound on the write price of a put (call) option can be derived from the upper (lower) bound on the write price of a call (put) option and the put-call parity.
