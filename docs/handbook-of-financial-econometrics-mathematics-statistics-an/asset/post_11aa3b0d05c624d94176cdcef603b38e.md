## Appendix 3A

In this appendix, we replicate Column (2) of Table 3.4 by replacing industry fixed effects with firm fixed effects, where the result is presented in Column (1) of Table 3.10. We choose Column (2) because it includes *BTD* and this mitigates the concern that *BTC* captures the effects of firm-specific book–tax differences. The results of *CROSS* and *DISCL* are ignored because they are time-invariant. The result shows that the coefficient on *BTC* remains significantly negative, which is still consistent with our argument. However,

the significance level is substantially reduced, where the t-statistic changes from −69.92 to −9.23. The magnitude of the coefficient on BTC is also substantially reduced from −1.0942 to −0.1657. Other variables such as BTD also have similar results in that both the significance and the magnitude of the coefficient are reduced. These results suggest that a large portion of audit fee decisions is firm-specific and not explained by observable characteristics, and firm fixed effects explain a large portion of the variations in audit fees. This can be seen from the significant increase in the explanatory power of the model, where the adjusted R-square increases from 0.7566 to 0.9113.

We then explore how clustering standard errors of regression coefficients affect the result, where Column (2) of Table 3.10 extends Column (1) by clustering standard errors at the firm level. Clustering by firm does not change our conclusion, where the coefficient on BTC is still significantly negative, although the significance is reduced (t-statistic is changed from −9.23 to −6.45). This suggests that the potential correlations between sample firms does not overstate t-statistic of BTC very much.

Column (3) of Table 3.10 extends Column (2) by two-way clustering at both firm and year. The formula for two-way clustering can refer to Thompson (2011), who shows that in the simplest case where there are firm and time effects but no persistent common shocks, the variance estimate for an OLS estimator β is (Petersen, 2009; Chen and Lee, 2018):

Var(β) = V_firm + V_time,0 − V_white,0, (3A.1)

where V_firm and V_time,0 are the estimated variances that cluster by firm and time, respectively, and V_white,0 is the usual heteroskedasticity-robust OLS variance matrix (White, 1980).

Column (3) of Table 3.10 shows that the coefficient on BTC is marginally significant at the one-tailed 10% level (t-statistic = 1.53). This result suggests that two-way clustering substantially reduces the efficiency of our estimates. In fact, clustering by time presumes that there are no correlations across years, but this presumption is unlikely to hold for BTC, because the level of book-tax conformity is a country policy and country policy is usually constant and less changed by time. For example, the US consistently has the lowest rank of BTC among all our sample years. Given this nature, it is not efficient by clustering by time in our research context.
