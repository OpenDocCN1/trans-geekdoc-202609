## 121.4.3 Alternative asset pricing models and liquidity measures

In Table 121.4, we explore the alternative asset-pricing models (i.e., FF5 and FF5-PS) and alternative liquidity measures (i.e., PS<sup>Traded</sup>, DSS, and BPST). More specifically, the table summarizes time-series regressions over the entire sample period from 1963 to 2017 with 10 momentum portfolios as the test assets. In these unconditional tests, we do not allow the factor loadings to vary over time. Panels A to E show the detailed regression results. To save space, we drop the coefficient estimates on the Fama–French factors. Panel F presents the summary regression statistics, including the average absolute value of the intercepts (|ᾱ|), the average of the standard errors of the intercepts (s̄), the average adjusted R<sup>2</sup> (R̄<sup>2</sup>), and the GRS F-test statistic (GRS).

Panel A shows that the FF5 still fails to explain the momentum effects, as the intercepts on eight out of ten momentum portfolios are significantly different from zero. In particular, Portfolios Low and High have statistically and economically significant (negative and positive) intercepts unexplained by the Fama–French three factors. Because Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) find that their liquidity measure helps to explain momentum effects, in Panel B, we add the PS to the FF5. As we can see, again, only one out of 10 momentum portfolios (Portfolio 6) has statistically significant loading on PS. Furthermore, the intercepts on the momentum portfolios only change slightly. For instance, the intercept for Low (High) changes from −0.78 (0.60) percent to −0.78 (0.59) percent. The adjusted R<sup>2</sup>s are also similar across two models for the same test assets.

In Panel C, we employ the traded Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity factor, which produces more significant results. First, three out of 10 momentum portfolios (Portfolios 4, 5, and 7) have statistically significant loading on PS<sup>Traded</sup>. Second, the intercepts for four out of 10 momentum portfolios (Portfolios 3, 4, 5, and 7) become statistically insignificant as soon as PS<sup>Traded</sup> is included. The evidence therefore suggests that PS<sup>Traded</sup> may be more informative compared to PS.

In Panels D and E, we examine the liquidity factors of Dreshler *et al.* (2017) (DSS) and Bali *et al.* (2014) (BPST). As we can see, these alternative liquidity measures produce similar results as the Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity factor (PS and PS<sup>Traded</sup>). Panel E confirms this observations.
