
Hence, the joint PDF of \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) is

$$
g(x_1,x_2)=\frac{1}{2\pi|\Sigma|x_1x_2}\times\exp\left\{-\frac12\big[(\log x_1,\log x_2)-\mu\big]'\Sigma^{-1}\big[(\log x_1,\log x_2)-\mu\big]\right\}. \tag{85.32}
$$

From the property of the multivariate normal distribution, we have \(Y_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma_{ii})\). Hence, \(X_i\) is log-normal with

$$
E(X_i)=e^{\mu_i+\frac{\sigma_{ii}}{2}}, \tag{85.33}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=e^{2\mu_i}e^{\sigma_{ii}}\big[e^{\sigma_{ii}}-1\big]. \tag{85.34}
$$

Furthermore, by the property of the moment generating for the bivariate normal distribution, we have

$$
E(X_1X_2)=E(e^{Y_1+Y_2})=e^{\mu_1+\mu_2+\frac12(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22}+2\sigma_{12})}=E(X_1)E(X_2)\cdot\exp\big(\rho\sqrt{\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}}\big). \tag{85.35}
$$

Thus, the covariance between \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) is

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=E(X_1X_2)-E(X_1)E(X_2)=E(X_1)E(X_2)\cdot\big(\exp(\rho\sqrt{\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}})-1\big)=\exp\!\Big(\mu_1+\mu_2+\frac12(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22})\Big)\cdot\big(\exp(\rho\sqrt{\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}})-1\big). \tag{85.36}
$$

From the property of conditional normality of \(Y_1\) given \(Y_2=y_2\), we also see that the conditional distribution of \(X_1\) given \(X_2=x_2\) is log-normal.

The extension to the \(p\)-variate log-normal distribution is trivial. Let \(Y=(Y_1,\dots,Y_p)'\), where \(Y_i=\log X_i\). If \(Y\sim N_p(\mu,\Sigma)\), where \(\mu=(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_p)'\) and \(\Sigma=(\sigma_{ij})\). The joint PDF of \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) can be obtained from the following theorem.

### Theorem 1. (Anderson, 2003)

*Let the PDF of \(Y_1,\dots,Y_p\) be \(f(y_1,\dots,y_p)\), consider the \(p\)-valued functions*

$$
x_i = x_i(y_1,\dots,y_p),\ i=1,\dots,p. \tag{85.37}
$$

We assume that the transformation from the \(y\)-space to the \(x\)-space is one-to-one with the inverse transformation

$$
y_i = y_i(x_1,\dots,x_p),\ i=1,\dots,p. \tag{85.38}
$$

Let the random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) be defined by

$$
X_i = x_i(Y_1,\dots,Y_p),\ i=1,\dots,p. \tag{85.39}
$$

Then the PDF of \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) is

$$
g(x_1,\dots,x_p)=f\big(y_1(x_1,\dots,x_p),\dots,y_p(x_1,\dots,x_p)\big)J(x_1,\dots,x_p), \tag{85.40}
$$

where \(J(x_1,\dots,x_p)\) is the Jacobian of transformations

$$
J(x_1,\dots,x_p)=\operatorname{mod}\begin{vmatrix}\frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x_p}\\ \vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \frac{\partial y_p}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\frac{\partial y_p}{\partial x_p}\end{vmatrix}, \tag{85.41}
$$

where “mod” means a modulus or absolute value.

Applying the above theorem with \(f(y_1,\dots,y_p)\) being a \(p\)-variate normal, and

$$
J(x_1,\dots,x_p)=\operatorname{mod}\begin{vmatrix}\frac1{x_1}&0&\cdots&0\\0&\frac1{x_2}&\cdots&0\\\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\0&\cdots&&\frac1{x_p}\end{vmatrix}=\prod_{i=1}^p\frac1{x_i}, \tag{85.42}
$$

we have the following joint PDF of \(X_1,\dots,X_p\)

$$
g(x_1,\dots,x_p)=(2\pi)^{-p/2}|\Sigma|^{-1/2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^p\frac1{x_i}\right)
\times\exp\left\{-\frac12\big[(\log x_1,\dots,\log x_p)-\mu\big]'\Sigma^{-1}\big[(\log x_1,\dots,\log x_p)-\mu\big]\right\}. \tag{85.43}
$$

It is noted that when \(p=2\), equation (85.43) reduces to the bivariate case given in equation (85.32). Then the first two moments are

$$
E(X_i)=e^{\mu_i+\frac{\sigma_{ii}}{2}}, \tag{85.44}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=e^{2\mu_i}e^{\sigma_{ii}}[e^{\sigma_{ii}}-1]. \tag{85.45}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\exp\left(\mu_i+\mu_j+\frac12(\sigma_{ii}+\sigma_{jj})\right)\cdot\left(\exp\left(\rho_{ij}\sqrt{\sigma_{ii}\sigma_{jj}}\right)-1\right), \tag{85.46}
$$

where \(\rho_{ij}\) is the correlation between \(Y_i\) and \(Y_j\).

For more details concerning properties of the multivariate log-normal distribution, the reader is referred to Johnson and Kotz (1972).

## 85.6 The Normal Distribution as an Application to the Binomial and Poisson Distribution

The cumulative normal density function tells us the probability that a random variable \(Z\) will be less than some value \(x\). Note in Figure 85.1 that \(P(Z<x)\) is simply the area under the normal curve from \(-\infty\) up to point \(x\).

One of the many applications of the cumulative normal distribution function is in valuing stock options. A call option gives the option holder the right to purchase, at a specified price known as the exercise price, a specified number of shares of stock during a given time period. A call option is a function of the following five variables:

![](img/31ee7e6fa5107b22bbf7960ec96106a1_3065_0.png)

Figure 85.1: The cumulative normal density function.

- (1) current price of the firm’s common stock (\(S\));
- (2) exercise price (or strike price) of the option (\(X\));
- (3) term to maturity in years (\(T\));
- (4) variance of the stock’s price (\(\sigma^2\)); and
- (5) risk-free rate of interest (\(r\)).

The binomial option pricing model defined in equation (85.22) can be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
C &= S\left[ \sum_{k=m}^{T} \frac{T!}{k!(T-k)!} p'^k (1-p')^{T-k} \right]
- \frac{X}{(1+r)^T}\left[ \sum_{k=m}^{T} \frac{T!}{k!(T-k)!} p^k (1-p)^{T-k} \right] \\
&= SB(T,p',m) - \frac{X}{(1+r)^T} B(T,p,m),
\end{aligned}
\tag{85.47}
$$

and \(C = 0\) if \(m > T\),

where \(S\) is the current price of the stock, \(T\) is the term to maturity in years, \(m\) is the minimum number of upward movements in stock price that is necessary for the option to terminate “in the money,” \(p\) is \(\frac{R-d}{u-d}\) and \(1-p = \frac{u-R}{u-d}\), \(X\) is the option exercise price (or strike price), \(R\) is \(1 + r = 1+\) risk-free rate of return, \(u\) is \(1+\) percentage of price increase, \(d\) is \(1+\) percentage of price decrease, \(p' = (\frac{u}{R})p\), \(B(n,p,m) = \sum_{k=m}^n C_k^n p^k (1-p)^{n-k}\).

By a form of the central limit theorem, we showed in Section 85.7 as \(T \to \infty\), the option price \(C\) converges to \(C\) as

$$
C = S N(d_1) - X R^{-T} N(d_2), \tag{85.48}
$$

where

- \(C =\) Price of the call option,
- \(d_1 = \frac{\log\left(\frac{S}{X R^{-T}}\right)}{\sigma \sqrt{t}} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{t}\),
- \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{t}\).

\(N(d)\) is the value of the cumulative standard normal distribution, \(t\) is the fixed length of calendar time to expiration and \(h\) is the elapsed time between successive stock price changes and \(T = t/h\).

If future stock price is constant over time, then \(\sigma^2 = 0\). It can be shown that both \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\) are equal to 1 and that equation (85.48) becomes

$$
C = S - X e^{-rT}. \tag{85.49}
$$

Alternatively, equations (85.48) and (85.49) can be understood in terms of the following steps:

### Step 1: The future price of the stock is constant over time.

Because a call option gives the option holder the right to purchase the stock at the exercise price \(X\), the value of the option, \(C\), is just the current price of the stock less the present value of the stock’s purchase price. Mathematically, the value of the call option is

$$
C = S - X/(1+r)^T. \tag{85.50}
$$

Note that equation (85.50) assumes discrete compounding of interest, whereas equation (85.49) assumes continuous compounding of interest. To adjust equation (85.50) for continuous compounding, we substitute \(e^{-rT}\) for \(1/(1+r)^T\) to get

$$
C = S - X e^{-rT}. \tag{85.51}
$$

### Step 2: Assume the price of the stock fluctuates over time (\(S_t\)).

In this case, we need to adjust equation (85.49) for the uncertainty associated with that fluctuation. We do this by using the cumulative normal distribution function. In deriving equation (85.48), we assume that \(S_t\) follows a log-normal distribution, as discussed in Section 85.3.

The adjustment factors \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\) in the Black-Scholes option valuation model are simply adjustments made to equation (85.49) to account for the uncertainty associated with the fluctuation of the price of the stock.

We can now compute the values of \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\).

$$
N(d_1)=P(Z<d_1)=P(Z<0)+P(0<Z<d_1)
$$

$$
=P(Z<0.392)=0.5+0.1517=0.6517
$$

$$
N(d_2)=P(Z<d_2)=P(Z<0)+P(0<Z<d_2)
$$

$$
=P(Z<0.25)=0.5+0.0987=0.5987.
$$

Then the theoretical value of the option is

$$
C=(92.5)(0.6517)-\frac{(90)(0.5987)}{e^{(0.0435)(0.42)}}
$$

$$
=60.282-\frac{53.883}{1.0184}=\$7.373,
$$

and the actual price of the option on November 29, 1991, was $7.75.

## 85.7 Applications of the Log-Normal Distribution in Option Pricing

To derive the Black-Scholes formula, it is assumed that there are no transaction costs, no margin requirements, and no taxes; that all shares are infinitely divisible; and that continuous trading can be accomplished. It is also assumed that the economy is risk neutral and the stock price follows a log-normal distribution. Denote the current stock price by \(S\) and the stock price at the end of \(j\)th period by \(S_j\).

Then

$$
\frac{S_j}{S_{j-1}}=\exp[K_j]
$$

is a random variable with a log-normal distribution, where \(K_j\) is the rate of return in the \(j\)th period and is a random variable with normal distribution. Let \(K_j\) have the expected value \(\mu_k\) and variance \(\sigma_k^2\) for each \(j\). Then \(K_1+K_2+\cdots+K_t\) is a normal random variable with expected value \(t\mu_k\) and variance \(t\sigma_k^2\). Thus, we can define the expected value (mean) of \(\frac{S_t}{S}=\exp[K_1+K_2+\cdots+K_t]\) as

$$
E\left(\frac{S_t}{S}\right)=\exp\left[t\mu_k+\frac{t\sigma_k^2}{2}\right]. \tag{85.52}
$$

Under the assumption of a risk-neutral investor, the expected return \(E(\frac{S_t}{S})\) is assumed to be \(\exp(rt)\) (where \(r\) is the riskless rate of interest). In other words,

$$
\mu_k=r-\frac{\sigma_k^2}{2}. \tag{85.53}
$$
