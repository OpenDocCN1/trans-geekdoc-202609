Some classic puts on debt instruments can be viewed as calls with a concave payoff of the underlying interest rate since the bond price is a decreasing convex function of the interest rate. It is essential to understand the option bond of a call on bond. Hence, Henin and Pistre (1998) use second-order stochastic dominance arguments to study calls where the payoff is a concave function of the underlying asset. They derive bounds on option prices, whose exercise payoff is a concave function of the difference between the underlying asset and strike price. Later, Constantinides and Perrakis (2002) derive the tighter option bounds of European options by using stochastic dominance arguments. They utilize the zero-net cost portfolio which consists of a riskless bond, a stock, and European call (or put) options and assume the concave utility function for investor preference to derive the bounds of European option price. Their option bounds can be viewed as a generalization of single-period model to a multi-period economy since they are independent of the trading frequency.

Constantinides, Jackwerth, and Perrakis (2009) utilized the theoretical stochastic dominance method to test whether S&P 500 index options market is rational or not. In stochastic dominance framework, the investors are risk-averse and their preference is to maximize their expected utility that is concave and increasing function of the wealth and the wealth is monotone increasing in the stock return.

The development of linear programming is driven by its applications in economics and management. The standard form of a linear programming problem can be written as:

Maximize c^T x subject to A x ≤ b and x ≥ 0. (7.11)

In other words, linear programming is a technique for optimization of a linear objective function, subject to linear equality and linear inequality constraints. Any vector satisfying the constraints of the linear programming problem is called a feasible solution of the problem. To achieve narrower bounds, one can require additional assumptions. For example, in addition to investors preferring more wealth to less, Ritchken (1985) assumes that investors are risk-averse and there exists a set of ex ante consensus probabilities for the states:

e_i = π_i d_i,    i = 1, 2, 3, …, n,    (7.15)

where d_i is the state discount factor that is non-increase with respect to wealth and π_i is ex ante consensus probabilities that state i occurs. The bounds of a European call option are then obtained by solving the following linear programming problem:

Minimize (or Maximize) C_0 = Σ_{i=1}^n c_i π_i d_i,
subject to Σ_{i=1}^n s_i π_i d_i = S_0 and Σ_{i=1}^n π_i d_i = B_0
with d_1 ≥ d_2 ≥ d_3 ≥ … ≥ d_n ≥ 0,    (7.16)

where e_i is the current price of a pure state i security that pays out $1 at maturity date if state i occurs; s_i is the value of the underlying security in state i; c_i is the value of a European call option in state i; d_i is the state discount factor that is non-increase with respect to wealth; π_i is ex ante consensus probabilities that state i occurs; S_0 and B_0 are current values of the underlying security and a portfolio containing all state securities, respectively.

Following Ritchken (1985), Ritchken and Kuo (1988) propose the application of the single-period linear-programming option model to handle multiple periods. Ritchken and Kuo (1988) obtain identical upper bounds and tighter lower bounds than Ritchken (1985). Ritchken and Kuo (1989) further consider the additional preference restrictions on the investors with third-order or above stochastic dominance criteria. They also apply a decreasing absolute risk aversion (DARA) dominance rule in a state preference context. With the two restrictions, the closed form analytical solutions for the option bounds are not obtained, but a simple numerical example is presented.

The behavior of the DARA lower bound for the option price is not wholly explored by Ritchken and Kuo (1989). Basso and Pianca (1997) analyze

the general rule of DARA dominance. They solve the optimization DARA problem in the case of three states of nature, proving that the optimal solution satisfies the nonlinear constraint as equality. This solution is obtained through an exponential-like utility function having constant absolute risk aversion (CARA).

Basso and Pianca (1997) reconsider the following maximization and minimization problem:

$$\text{Minimize (or Maximize)}\quad C_0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i e_i,$$

$$\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{i=1}^{n}s_i e_i=S_0\ \text{and}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i=B_0\ \text{with}\ (e_1,e_2,e_3,\ldots,e_n)\in D, \tag{7.17}$$

where the set $D$ depends on the stochastic dominance rule adopted and is determined by the assumptions made on the utility function. For example, given utility function with decreasing absolute risk aversion (DARA) character, the set $D$ is constrained by:

$$e_i\ge 0,\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n$$

$$e_i\ge e_{i+1},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n-1$$

$$e_i e_{i+2}\ge e_{i+1}^{2},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n-2. \tag{7.18}$$

Recently, Peña, Vera and Zuluaga (2009) show that the problem of computing sharp upper and lower static-arbitrage bounds on the price of a European basket option, given the prices of other similar options, can also be cast as a linear program. The linear program formulations readily yield super-replicating (sub-replicating) strategies for the upper (lower) bound problem. The dual counterparts of the linear program formulations in turn yield underlying asset price distributions that replicate the given option prices, and the bound on the new basket option’s price. In the special case when the given option prices are those of vanilla options on the underlying assets, they show that the linear program formulations admit further simplifications. In particular, for the upper bound problem they derive closed-form formulas for the basket’s price bound and for the corresponding super replicating strategy. In addition, their linear program approach admits efficient modeling of additional features such as basket options with negative weights, bid-ask spreads, transaction costs, and diversification constraints.

## 7.3 Semi-Parametric and Non-Parametric Methods

The option bounds discussed so far are derived by “parametric” methods. Although Perrakis and Ryan (1984) obtain bounds for all stock price distributions with a non-negative beta property, the actual values of the bounds depend on the specific stock price distribution. Similar to Perrakis and Ryan (1984), the bounds of Levy (1985) obtained by using SSDR are also in parametric method. In addition, Ritchken (1985)’s linear-programming approach also requires parametric assumption of the state probabilities to calculate values for the bounds. In contrast to the previously discussed method, semi-parametric and non-parametric methods discussed in this section do not require strong assumptions in models and just need the historical data to derive option bounds.

### 7.3.1 Semi-parametric method

Different from parametric methods, Lo (1987) initiated a literature on “semi-parametric” bounds on option prices. The option bounds derived by Lo (1987) depend only on the mean and variance of the terminal stock price and not on its entire distribution, i.e., they are “semi-parametric”.

Relying on the mathematical inequality provided by Scarf (1958), Lo (1987) derives the boundary of option prices. For the underlying asset price, $S_t$, with constant mean $\mu$ and constant standard deviation $\sigma$, the upper bound of a European call option, $C_{\max}^E$, can be written as

$C_{\max}^E = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\mu^2 (\mu - K) + \mu \sigma^2}{\mu^2 + \sigma^2} & \text{if } K \le \dfrac{\mu^2 + \sigma^2}{2\mu} \\ \dfrac{1}{2} \left[ \mu - K + \sqrt{(\mu - K)^2 + \sigma^2} \right] & \text{if } K > \dfrac{\mu^2 + \sigma^2}{2\mu} \end{cases}$ (7.19)

The important feature of this equation is that the upper bound of the call option depends only on the first two moments of the price distribution.

Zhang (1994) sharpens Lo (1987)’s bounds by using the assumption that the underlying stock price is continuously distributed. Assume the terminal stock price $S_T$ with cumulative distribution $F(S_T)$ satisfying the moment conditions:

$\int_{0}^{\infty} dF(S_T) = 1,\ \int_{0}^{\infty} S_T dF(S_T) = \mu,\ \int_{0}^{\infty} S_T^2 dF(S_T) = \mu^2 + \sigma^2.$ (7.20)
