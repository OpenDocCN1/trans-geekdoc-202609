
Based on the previous literature, we use the debt-to-GDP ratio (D_GDP), corporate bond spread (Spread), and total public debt (TPD) as indicators of the macroeconomic attribute to the capital structure. We expect that D_GDP and TPD are negatively related to the leverage ratio, and the correlation between leverage ratio and Spread is negative.

## 35.2.1.3 Manager characteristics

Berger et al. (1997) build a measure of managerial entrenchment to investigate the agency problem between managers and shareholders; that is, managers would prefer to issue less debt to benefit their own private profits rather than pursue the optimal capital structure to benefit shareholders. Berger et al. (1997) find that the usage of debt decreases with the options and stocks held by a CEO, the log of number of directors and the percentage of outside directors, but increases with the length of tenure of a CEO. Graham (2000) utilizes the same variables from Berger et al. (1997) to measure the managerial entrenchment; the results are similar to Berger et al.'s (1997) finding that strong managerial entrenchment would lead to a decrease in the debt usage of a firm. The variables used to measure the managerial entrenchment are the stocks and options held by a CEO, the length of working years and tenure of a CEO, the log of number of directors, and the percentage of outside directors.

Bhagat et al. (2011) develop a dynamic capital structure model incorporated with taxes effect, bankruptcy costs and manager characteristics and investigate the effects of manager characteristics on the firm's capital structure. The researchers incorporate the concept of agency problems between manager and shareholders, and therefore, their model can be viewed as the application of trade-off theory and the agency conflict problem, which utilizes tax effects and bankruptcy costs as external factors and manager characteristics as internal factors to analyze financing decisions of a firm. The researchers find that manager characteristics are important determinants of capital structure decisions and that the manager's ability is negatively correlated to total debt ratio (total debt/total asset), and the results of their empirical work are consistent with the inference of their model. The variables, CEO cash compensation, CEO cash compensation to asset ratio, CEO tenure, CEO tenure divided by CEO age and CEO ownership (numbers of shares of common stock plus the number of options held by CEO), will be used as the proxies of CEO ability to test the influence of manager-shareholder agency conflicts on a firm's financing decisions.

## The Joint Determinants of Capital Structure and Stock Rate of Return

Lambrecht and Myers (2012) develop a theory of debt dynamics based on the relationship between dividend policy and managerial rents. In the researchers’ model framework, the target debt ratio may not be achieved because of smooth dividend payout policy. The researchers suggest using management compensation as the proxy of managerial rent to investigate how the agency costs affect payout, investment, and debt policies. In this chapter, we can investigate the influence of managerial rent on capital structure.

Here, we use CEO tenure over CEO age (Tenu_age), log of CEO tenure (log_Tenu), log of CEO total compensation (log_TC), CEO bonus (in millions) (Bonus), log of number of directors (log_Dir) and percentage of outside directors (Out_Dir) as indicators of manager characteristics. Since both the manager’s ability and strong managerial entrenchment would lead to the decrease of debt usage, the manager characteristics of a firm is expected to be negatively related to this firm’s leverage.

## 35.2.2 Joint determinants of capital structure and stock return

Marsh (1982) analyzes the empirical study in the financing decisions of UK companies and finds that their capital structures were heavily influenced by their stock prices.

Baker and Wurgler (2002) provide the empirical evidence that the capital structure of a firm is significantly related to its historical stock price. Firms prefer to issue equity when their stock prices are relatively high (market-to-book ratio high) and repurchase equity when the stock prices are relatively low. However, the regression equations used in Baker and Wurgler (2002) appear not very suitable for the description of the relationship between the capital structure and the stock price. The stock price and capital structure change simultaneously since the stock price will respond to the investors’ perspective on financing and investment decisions and managers will consider both reactions of stock prices and the firm’s long-term equity value when making financing decisions, and vice versa. Therefore, we can use the simultaneous structural equation to investigate the relationship between the capital structure and the stock price.

Welch (2004) investigates whether companies change their capital structure in accordance with the changes in stock prices or not. Welch (2004) finds that the stock price is a primary factor of dynamic capital structure. However, the firms do not readjust their capital structure to respond to the changes in stock prices. Jenter (2005) and Jenter et al. (2011) provide the different aspect of a firm’s financing activity affected by its stock price.

Jenter et al. (2011) demonstrate that managers attempt to take advantage of the mispricing of their firms’ equity through corporate financing activities. This behavior is called “time to market” under the agency problem between the manager and outside investors. The different beliefs between the manager and investors will cause market timing behavior (Jung and Subramanian, 2014). Yang (2013) estimates the influence of the difference in beliefs on firms’ leverage ratio and claim that market timing behavior has the significant effect on capital structure. The strong investor beliefs (higher stock price) lead to decreases in firms’ leverage.

Dittmar and Thakor (2007) propose a new theory called “managerial investment autonomy” to explain that a firm’s stock price and its capital structure are simultaneously decided. The “autonomy” means that the firm’s stock price is higher when the likelihood of investors’ disagreement with investment and financing decisions made from managers is lower, and vice versa. Since managers consider the response of shareholders to the investment and capital financing decisions, managers can use stock prices as a signal regarding whether investors agree or disagree with the capital budgeting decisions. The researchers’ empirical findings support the argument that a firm will issue equity rather than debt as external financing sources when its stock price is high.

For the stock rate of return, we use the annual close prices of a firm in accordance with its annual report release date to calculate the annual stock returns. Here, we add two attributes, liquidity and value, as attributes of stock returns. The indicators of liquidity are obtained from Pastor and Stambaugh’s (2003) innovations in aggregate liquidity (PS_Innov), level of aggregate liquidity (PS_Level), and traded liquidity factors (PS_Vwf). The indicators of value are excess return on the market (mktrf), small minus big (smb), high minus low (hml), risk-free interest rate used by the one-month T-bill rate (rf), and momentum (umd).5 In addition, the attributes of firm characteristics, growth and profitability are expected to affect the stock price directly. Therefore, we set these two attributes as the joint determinants of the stock return and the capital structure. All indicators and attributes are described in Table 35.3.

We summarize the relationship between the aforementioned attributes and the capital structure in Table 35.4. The associated theories describing how the attributes affect the capital structure are also presented.
