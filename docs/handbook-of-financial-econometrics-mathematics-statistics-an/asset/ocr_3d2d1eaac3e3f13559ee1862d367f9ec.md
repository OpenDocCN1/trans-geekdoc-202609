# 34.2.2.3  *Dynamics on options*

Chiras and Manaster (1978) disentangled information content on option prices. The starting point is the BS model when dividends are accounted for as initiated by Robert Merton. Although in Merton (1976) dividends are continuous, that does not conform to the reality as dividends are discrete in nature. Chiras and Manaster (1978) argue that prior studies on option pricing used historical standard deviation of returns on stocks (SDHIST) with pricing options and that it is one of the shortcomings of prior studies. Moreover, they state that the difference of observed and calculated option would be reflected in the difference of SDHIST and aggregated market volatilities. Marcato *et al.* (2018) illustrated the same phenomenon. In order to overcome that shortcoming, they estimated implied standard deviations (ISDs). They specifically weighted the ISDs. Although weighting has its own advantages, weighted implied standard deviations (WISDs) tend not to converge. Marcato *et al.* (2018) showed that when there is an iteration process during the calculation of implied volatilities, those implied volatilities tend to converge. The advantage of ISDs is that they are based on current market prices; therefore, ISDs reflect the current volatilities in an ideal environment. With historical volatilities, under (over) pricing of options is very common (Chiras and Manaster, 1978). That mispricing leads to arbitrage opportunities.

The central hypothesis in Chiras and Manaster (1978) is that implied volatilities are a better predictor of future stock returns than historical volatilities. The data was from CBOE from 23 monthly observations period beginning June 1973 and ending April 1975. During 1973–1974 there was a stock market crash and Dow Jones Industrial Average lost over 45% of its share price although in most studies share prices changes are quoted as value. The dividends are converted into continuous form in order for them to be consistent with the BS model. Results of Chiras and Manaster (1978) illustrate that SDHISTs explained approximately 26% of stock returns while WISDS explain 32% of stock returns. Moreover, volatilities decrease over time. That is consistent with volatility theory. Thus, initially investors have little information about the firm and that time volatilities tend to be high but with time investors have more information about that firm; therefore, volatilities decrease.
