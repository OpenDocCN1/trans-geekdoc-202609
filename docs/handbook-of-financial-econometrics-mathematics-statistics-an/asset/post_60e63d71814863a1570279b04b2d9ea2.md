There is a possibility that both dependent variable and independent variable are endogenously determined. The variables that explain market integration might explain diversification benefit. Driessen and Laeven (2007) argue country risk might be endogenous and thus apply legal origin (La Porta et al., 1998) as an instrument in their cross-sectional regression of an increase in Sharpe ratios. However, legal origin is not time varying and our regression is in the form of unbalanced panel data but not a valid instrument (Atanasov and Black, 2015). We use corruption, which is available for each country on an annual basis, as an instrument of country risk. We also use investment profile developed by ICRG as an instrument for R² and correlation. Investment profile captures the factors of investment risks that are not covered by other economic, political, and financial risk components. Lehkonen (2014) shows investment profile is the key determinant of market integration. Investment profile increases market integration. Both country risk and corruption are also available from ICRG. The results in columns 6 and 7 applying instruments are qualitatively similar to those in columns 4 and 5. Following Driessen and Laeven (2007), we perform an F-test of joint significance of the identifying instruments and the Hansen-Sargan test of over-identifying restrictions to examine the validity of our choice of instruments. The null hypothesis of the Hansen-Sargan test is that the instruments are uncorrelated with the error term and that the excluded instruments are correctly excluded from the estimated equation. If the Hansen-Sargan test is rejected, the choice of the instruments is questionable. Both tests support the use of corruption and investment profile as instruments for country risk and market integration. The F-test suggests corruption and investment profile explain much of the variation in country risk and market integration. The Hansen-Sargan test is not rejected at conventional significance levels. ## 13.5.2 *What determine the risk reduction of diversification benefits?*

Table 13.7 presents the empirical results of the diversification benefits measured by volatility reduction (*ε*). The results show both correlation and *R*² decreases diversification benefit, but the economic significance of *R*² is higher. One standard deviation increase in *R*² decreases diversification benefit by 30% whereas that in correlation decreases diversification benefit only by 18%. Controlling for other determinants and applying instruments, the results suggest an economy with high dividend yield, turnover, and domestic equity returns experiences the most reduction of volatility from diversification. The *p*-value at the bottom of Table 13.7 suggests *R*² is a more significant determinant of diversification benefit than correlation in all regression models. *F*-test of instrument and Hansen–Sargan test imply our choice of using corruption and investment profile as instruments for country risk, and market integration (and correlation), respectively is valid.

Theoretically, as the global market becomes *more integrated*, the global non-systemic volatility that can be offset by diversification diminishes. The global systemic risk of domestic assets will have stronger impact on the volatility of the global diversified portfolio and thus market integration
