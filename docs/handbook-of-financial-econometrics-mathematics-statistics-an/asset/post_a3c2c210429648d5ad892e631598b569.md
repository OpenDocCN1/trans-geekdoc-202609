In this regard, Bollen and Pool (2009) illustrate that when managers have the opportunity to choose to report a loss or gain, they overwhelmingly choose to report positive performance results and they illustrate this by showing that by using a cross-section of historical hedge fund returns, there is a discontinuity at zero. The researchers provide a few explanations for this discontinuity, which are as follows:
- they may optimistically value the illiquid securities in their portfolios, or
- they may overvalue their securities similar to how investors have been shown to be overly optimistic about their investments (Bollen et al., 2009).

Jorion and Schwartz (2014) question whether this kink in the distribution of returns is a result of purposeful manipulation by hedge fund managers and conclude that there is not enough proof because incentive fees may affect the performance results. Cassar and Gerakos (2011) added some further insight as to whether hedge fund managers purposefully smooth their returns and conclude that when it is difficult to verify pricing and hedge fund managers have more discretion over pricing, the hedge funds are likely to exhibit greater levels of smoothing; however, traditional controls, like oversight, do not lead to lower levels of smoothing.

On the other hand, Cumming and Dai (2010) claim that the legal structure of the hedge fund matters in terms of potential misreporting of returns and illustrate that there is a positive relationship between hedge funds structured to permit wrapper distributions and misreported returns. Likewise, Agarwal et al. (2011) found that hedge fund managers are likely to inflate their returns in December and fund manager incentives are positively related to this spike but find that these managers seem to under-report their returns throughout the year and only find weak evidence of borrowing returns from the ensuing year. Aragon and Nanda (2017) find evidence that hedge fund managers have a propensity to delay reporting negative performance results as hedge fund managers’ report performance results that exhibit clustered behavior and are typically reversals to poor performance results. Agarwal et al. (2013a) illustrate why it is important that hedge fund managers have some control over what they disclose and when they disclose it (i.e., they found that the confidential holdings of hedge fund managers reveal evidence of managerial skill) and that aggressive regulations that promote holding disclosure may allow other managers to free-ride on the hedge fund manager's talents.

In Table 91.4, Smith and Mumtaz (2017) provide a comparison to emphasize the fact that the conditional performance of hedge funds is not that different from standard and passive investment vehicles. There is evidence that hedge fund managers either smooth their returns or selectively release their returns, but the evidence that shows up in the reported performance results from hedge fund managers does not illustrate that any obvious and deceitful manipulation is occurring. Table 91.4 compares the conditional performance of publicly traded investment vehicles against the hedge fund return series and we can observe there is evidence that if we condition our next month's performance on whether the preceding month's returns were positive or negative, the ensuing month's returns are relatively depressed if negative and higher, if positive. In addition, we find that there are statistically significant differences between the conditional performances of half of our sample, which include publicly traded investment vehicles. Given that these investment vehicles are publicly traded, we cannot infer that they are being purposefully manipulated. Studies such as Cumming and Dai (2010) that explore the reported performances of hedge funds conditioned on the delay in their performance provide an interesting and particularly revealing examination as to whether hedge fund managers purposefully smooth their performance results.

## 91.3 Development of a Model of Hedge Fund Performance

In this section, we review the literature pertaining to hedge fund performance estimation. There are various goals that researchers have when attempting to model hedge fund performance, but the objective of this chapter is to provide a basis from which researchers might be able to begin to explore the modeling of hedge fund returns. Pertinent literature related to estimating hedge fund performance, modelling that performance, and cloning that performance is found in the following references: Lo (2010), Grummitt and Satchell (2011), Bali et al. (2014), Chen and Tindall (2014), Lee and Lo (2014), Getmansky et al. (2015), Payne and Tresl (2015), O'Doherty et al. (2015, 2017), Smith and Mumtaz (2017), and Stafylas et al. (2017) among others. In this particular section, the goal is to provide a basis to model hedge fund performance and an extension to this line of research would be to clone hedge fund returns.
