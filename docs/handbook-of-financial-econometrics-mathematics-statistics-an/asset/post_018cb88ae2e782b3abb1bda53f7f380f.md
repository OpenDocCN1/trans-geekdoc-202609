Just like power options, the Margrabe formula allows proper indexing, flexibility and proper parameterization (see Blenman and Clark, 2005). In this chapter, the Margrabe formula allows for inflexible parameterization and this has not been explored before. Those listed characteristics among other things make it interesting for one to incorporate incompleteness in the Margrabe formula because the original formula does not incorporate incompleteness.

Although continuous pricing is widely regarded, it has its limitations when pricing assets when markets are incomplete. One typical situation where continuous pricing will have limitations is when one prices options especially in incomplete markets. One of the reasons why it is interesting for one to correctly price assets in incomplete markets is that hedges are imperfect in incomplete markets. In a complete world, one can compute the option surface in full. The latter statement would imply that it is a challenge to price options accurately in incomplete markets. This chapter extends the Margrabe formula such that it is suitable for pricing options under incompleteness (i.e., Lévy process). Lévy process is a Brownian Motion (BM) process with a drift.

The issue of incompleteness when pricing options is not definitive; however, on general terms it implies either not all information relating to the price of asset is incorporated in the price or all possible products that can be written on that asset are currently not written. The advantage of using a Poisson process is that, should there be more than one jump in stock price at any given time, a Poisson process becomes a Cox process¹ — same as Hawkes process. On the other hand, Eraker (2004) states that the advantage of jumpy models is that they capture black swans (i.e., rare events) such as the crash in 1987, and that they are correctly parametrized. Jumps in prices of assets and positive skewness are common features of options in incomplete markets (see Lambrecht and Perraudin, 2003). Applebaum (2004: 1339) stated that “Gaussian and Poisson measures give rise to extreme points of the convex cone of all characteristic exponents”. Other advantages of pricing options in incomplete markets include finding equilibriums, expected return and time to exercise of options. Jumps are assumed to be normally distributed. The latter phenomenon can be traced back to Scott (1997).

Questions that arise from this study include does risk premium increase or decrease option values, are there robust pricing and hedging techniques for jumpy two-asset option and who benefits from this option, lastly, does this jump risk (i.e., kappa, κ) increase values of options? It is widely accepted that jumps illustrate inverse relationship between options and some of their parameters (Bates, 1996). The contribution of this chapter is precisely to take on those challenges. At the heart of the contribution, the Margrabe formula is extended such that it captures price jumps. The advantages of accounting for jumps when you price options are as follows;
- reduces model risk (see Cont, 2006)
- introduce incentives to limit excessive risk-taking (see Ronn and Verna, 1986)
- proper accounting of benefits and losses (see Lint and Pennings, 1998)
Generally, jumps occur in heterogeneous environment. Factors that cause stock jumps include earnings, price changes, returns, dividends, time, random disturbance and stocks risks. Some of those mentioned factors are synonymous with parameters of the Margrabe formula. Hence, it is interesting to extend the Margrabe formula such that it captures stock jumps. The Margrabe formula includes incorporating a Poisson process. Finally, the jumps that cause stock jumps can be induced by either political or financial events. Unlike Merton (1976) who assumed that jumps are diversifiable,² in this chapter jumps are assumed to be undiversifiable as those jumps will be “inherent” in the merged entity. Further, these jumps are discrete unlike Merton’s. Scott (1997: 419) stated that “to handle the extreme observations associated with the crash, one must use a model with discrete jumps”. On the other hand, Yan (2011) argues that jumps occur rarely and estimating them requires long-time series samples, which are difficult to find and large samples have their own error problems. In principle, the model is at-the-money time-type model. The reason for the latter statement is numerous models have been used to price options at different moneyness levels although, suitability at those moneyness levels has not been determined.

The study that is close to this chapter is one by Cheang and Chiarella (2011). They extend the Margrabe formula such that it is suitable when assets are driven by jump-diffusion (JD) process — introduced by Robert Merton in 1976. That is, Cheang and Chiarella (2011) use exchange option under JD process while this chapter explores exchange option under any randomness including JD process. Unlike Cheang and Chiarella (2011) who priced exchange option in a continuous environment, this chapter discretizes the environment so that the same Margrabe model can be used for hedging. Cheang and Chiarella (2011) also used money market account as numéraire environment. On a less scientific basis, it is hard to determine dependence of Poisson processes because in merger, certain events are related while others are unrelated. The latter statement is a point for future research. Merger jumps are unique from jumps of other events in so many ways including the fact that merger jumps are pre-determined in the sense that merging firms agree on merger before the merger is announced to the market.

## 34.2 Literature Review

What causes stock prices to jump varies from the actual events that occur in stock markets to the incompleteness of those stock markets. While there is no argument on events, which normally occur rarely, the incompleteness of stock markets tend to be a broad definition. Among the things that cause stock markets to be incomplete includes new information, asymmetry environment and non-availability of products that are supposed to be offered in those stock markets. For example, option products that are offered in South Africa are exclusively of index nature, i.e., single stock futures on equity indices. This implies that South African options market is incomplete in nature. Thus, both incompleteness and rare events cause stock prices to jump in stock markets. In criticizing prior studies, this chapter explores both literatures on incompleteness, rare events that cause stock price jumps and hedging in incomplete markets.

### 34.2.1 Pricing securities in incomplete markets

According to Smith, Jr. (1976), option formulas are either ad-hoc or equilibrium formulas. The exchange option formula falls within the equilibrium formulas group. In layman's terms, equilibrium models are models based on grounded theory. In exploring prior studies, this chapter focuses only on equilibrium option formulas given their similarity with the Margrabe formula. Benninga and Mayshar (2000), Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002), and Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) explored equilibrium option formulas when stock markets are incomplete.

Benninga and Mayshar (2000) explored options when there are multiple agents (i.e., heterogeneity) and different risk levels. One of the points put forward by Benninga and Mayshar (2000) is that investors do not hold identical wealth composition. They assumed that markets are Arrow–Debreu (AD) complete during a two-period. AD suggests that certain assumptions must be a set of prices such that aggregate supplies will equal aggregate demands for every asset in the economy. Given that every consumer has a different utility function, it seems impossible to mimic entire market prices. Benninga and Mayshar (2000: 11) stated that “as noted in the previous section, it is not possible to find a “representative consumer” who can mimic market prices for any possible set of aggregate endowments (Y_n); we therefore look for preferences which can mimic the prices in the given economy”.

Davis (1998) applied option pricing in incomplete markets given certain utility function. He derives initial principles from BS ones. In modeling, he proposes a certain utility function at time given a certain marginal rate of substitution. In order for the function to be defined, he proposes a lemma made up of a real function. At maximum point, the lemma is similar to Radon–Nikodym derivative. Then, Davis (1998) uses a standard discrete model which is independent of Wiener process. Thus, the model incorporates a Markov process. And the standard model takes an exponential shape. For advanced modeling, he extends a stochastic process to include Wiener process where the discrete process is underpinned by bond, stock and wealth. That resulted in a model with jumps due to the drift of the stock. When he incorporated transaction costs, stocks jumped further. A similar process will be illustrated in the formulation section of the chapter. Finally, arbitrage opportunities emerged due to the incompleteness of the environment.

In relation to pricing options when consumer economy is heterogeneous, Benninga and Mayshar (2000) stated that a less risk-averse consumer is not so much concerned with downside risk protection. And that corresponds to dominated consumption and consumption priced in high states. For pricing options with heterogeneous tastes, they state that the economy will generate prices similar to the homogenous economy. In the context of the option pricing theory (OPT), findings of Benninga and Mayshar (2000) are prevalent when one is invested in out-of-the-money options, especially in puts. They went further and calibrate their findings. Calibrated results illustrate that far out-of-the-money options are more expensive in a heterogeneous economy. Another reason why out-of-the-money options are expensive is that they are hardly traded. The volatility smile (i.e., implied volatility) of calibrated options is log-normally distributed; however, overall distribution curves are normally shaped. Further, the results illustrated that distribution curves have fat tails.

Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002), and Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) exemplify optimal equilibrium models when markets are incomplete.
