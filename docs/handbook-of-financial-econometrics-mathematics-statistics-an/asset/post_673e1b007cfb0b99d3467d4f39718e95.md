## 91.2 Hedge Fund Return Issues

### 91.2.1 Opacity

Opacity, when looking at hedge fund returns, occurs when the managers of the fund have some control over when their prices are displayed to the public and how they are displayed. This occurs in the hedge fund industry because hedge fund managers are able to invest in assets that may be privately held or are priced intermittently; therefore, the pricing at the fund level is not efficient. Bollen and Pool (2009), Cassar and Gerakos (2011), Cumming and Dai (2010), Agarwal et al. (2013a, 2013b), and Smith and Mumtaz (2017) provide a basis from which to explore the opaque nature of these types of investments. According to Asness et al. (2001), hedge fund returns are difficult to evaluate and the results of traditional methods used to evaluate performance are often difficult to interpret and contain significant methodological issues. More to the point, Asness et al. (2001) point out that because hedge funds hold illiquid securities that may not trade near the end of the month, managers often have the flexibility to interpret their pricing and performance. The relatively opaque nature of these investment instruments not only provides an opportunity for managers to add value but also provides opportunities for performance manipulation. Discussions of the relative opacity of hedge funds lead us into conversations about how managers might be incentivized to deceive investors by delaying the reporting of their performance. The following section on stale pricing provides us with a bridge to move from a conversation of a relatively opaque investment vehicle to an exploration of performance manipulation.

### 91.2.2 Stale Pricing

This section analyzes the nature of stale or smoothed pricing embedded in hedge fund returns and examines the literature on stale and smoothed pricing. Stale pricing occurs as a result of managers not fully integrating the impact of a market or another systematic factor on the returns of the fund. The reason why this is an important issue to contend with in the literature on hedge funds and hedge fund performance is that due to the relatively opaque nature of hedge funds and the managers’ ability to conceal their underlying holdings and positions, they have an opportunity to manipulate the returns associated with their underlying holdings to improve the perceived performance results of their funds if the underlying assets are priced intermittently. The works of Asness et al. (2001), Bollen and Pool (2008, 2009), Cumming and Dai (2010), Cassar and Gerakos (2011), Agarwal et al. (2011, 2013a), Jorion and Schwartz (2014), Aragon and Nanda (2017), and Smith and Mumtaz (2017) are discussed to explore stale pricing within the context of hedge fund performance.

Asness et al. (2001) introduced a very interesting and important point that practitioners and academics should understand when attempting to interpret the traditional metrics that are used in professional practice to assess the performance of hedge funds compared against standard benchmarks. Asness et al. (2001) contend that when evaluating hedge fund performance, researchers should consider both the current betas associated with the market’s influence on hedge fund performance and a lagged beta to attempt to understand the true systematic risk that investors are taking when investing in these products. This particular insight is important and somewhat underappreciated in professional practice; therefore, it deserves some attention. According to Asness et al. (2001), a good way to think about the systematic risk associated with hedge fund performance is to aggregate both the current systematic risk and lagged components associated with systematic risk, which is illustrated in the following formula:

$$R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{0i}R_{m,t} + \beta_{1i}R_{m,t-1} + \cdots + \beta_{3i}R_{m,t-3} + \varepsilon_{i,t},\qquad (91.1)$$

where $R_{i,t}$ is the return on an asset in time period $t$, $R_{m,t}$ is the return on the market proxy during time period $t$, $R_{m,t-1\,to\,t-3}$ are the lagged returns for the market proxy in time periods $t-1$, $t-2$, and $t-3$, $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ is an error term, and $\beta$ are the regression coefficients (i.e., $\beta_{0i}$, $\beta_{1i}$, and $\beta_{3i}$).

For instance, Asness et al. (2001) use this regression framework to illustrate how stale pricing could be used by managers to reduce the volatility experienced when investing in a particular type of hedge fund. Although the sample used to generate the regression results is from the 1994–2000 period, the illustration provides some meaningful insight, which is that hedge funds take on more systematic market risk than standard regression estimates would indicate.

In a more recent study, Smith and Mumtaz (2017) extended this analysis focusing specifically on the relationship between an aggregate hedge fund index with one modification, which was that the hedge fund returns were compared against a proxy for the global stock market (i.e., the MSCI World Index). In Smith et al. (2017), the results indicate that the ‘effect’ of the smoothing seems to last for two periods instead of three as identified in Asness et al. (2001) and the ‘summed beta,’ which is used for a proxy of total systematic risk, is 0.49, which is a fraction of the 0.84 estimate provided in Asness et al. (2001). However, this is still a significant increase in the level of systematic risk that the hedge fund is shouldering as compared against the traditional estimate of systematic risk (i.e., proxied for through $\beta_0$ in Table 91.1).

Bollen and Pool (2008) provided researchers with a slightly different way to think about performance smoothing in that they examined the conditional smoothing of hedge fund managers. Smith et al. (2017) made a simple adjustment to the Asness et al. (2001) formula presented in equation (91.1) and estimated regressions based on the following two equations to determine if there were any ‘conditional effects’ associated with positive or negative performance events:
