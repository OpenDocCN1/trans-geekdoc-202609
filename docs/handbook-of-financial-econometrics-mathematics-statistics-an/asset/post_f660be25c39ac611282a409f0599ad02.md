This study designs question items for each construct (i.e., cognitive/emotional biases and the disposition effect) based on behavioral finance literature and collects quantitative data through a questionnaire survey. The questionnaire uses a seven-point Likert-type scale, which ranges from one (strongly disagree) to seven (strongly agree). In addition, this study measures investment performance by classifying stock returns into seven buckets, which is consistent with a seven-point Likert-type scale. These question items are identified through a comprehensive review of behavioral finance literature and pre-tested by five senior managers of securities firms. As such, the questionnaire is implied to have face validity. All items are listed in the Appendix 16B.

This study adopts non-random sampling. The questionnaire was distributed in March 2009 through a securities firm to its branches all over Taiwan. Respondents with investment experience were asked to answer the relevant questions. This study obtained 805 responses, and the response rate is 89%. After excluding missing values, the final effective samples consist of 761 respondents. Of the 761 respondents, 55.5% and 45.5% are male and female, respectively. The marital status proportions with married and single are 53.4% and 46.6%, respectively.

These respondents are divided into two groups of early respondents and late ones, and the t-test is utilized to analyze their responses with the Likert scale questions for both groups. There is no difference found between the majority of the responses of both early and late respondents (Connors and Elliot, 1994). Thus, the non-response bias problem is mitigated in this study. In addition, this study conducts Harman’s one factor test to examine whether most of the question items jointly explain one general factor by the exploratory factor analysis (EFA). The findings show that a single factor accounts for a majority of the variance (36.04%), which is less than the common value of 50% (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Chen et al., 2012). The result of 1-factor CFA (confirmatory factor analysis) also shows that the incremental amount of Chi-square is significant (Δχ² = 929.32, Δdf = 3, p-value = 0.000) (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Chen et al., 2012). Therefore, common method variance is not a significant issue in this study.

### 16.4.2 Validity and reliability

The measurement model examines the factor loadings that observed variables reflect latent variables, and t-tests for the factor loadings are used to test the convergent validity and assess if each observed variable (i.e., question item) effectively reflects its latent variable (i.e., factor construct) (Anderson and Gerbing, 1988; Raine-Eudy, 2000). As shown in Table 16.2, each factor loading of the constructs shows highly significant t-statistics, thereby suggesting that all question items provide good measures of their respective construct in the measurement model. Average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) are employed to assess the construct reliability of the measurement model (Bagozzi and Yi, 1998). AVE evaluates the amount of variance captured by the construct, and CR reflects the internal consistency of the question items measuring each construct. As a result, all AVE values are over the recommended value of 0.5, and all CR values are over the common threshold of 0.7. In addition, Table 16.3 shows the discriminant validity is satisfied.
