where $U$ can be any real number not greater than 1. The optimal weights can be solved by applying the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions when the complementary slackness conditions, primal constraints, and gradient equations are fulfilled. Since there are multiple nonlinearities in the quadric programming, there is no equational form for the solutions. One can generate the Maximum Sharpe Ratio (MSR) portfolio among the selections on the constrained efficient frontier. Specifically,

$MSR = \max_{\{W_p\}} \{(W_p^T \mu)/(W_p^T V W_p)^{1/2} | W_p^T \in P_J\}. \quad (82.10)$

We use the monthly return data of the component stocks in the Dow Jones 30 Index between July 2008 and June 2018 to demonstrate an example. Our analysis prohibits short sales of asset and the asset allocation higher than a certain weight to ensure diversity of the portfolio. Since the large optimal weight among the assets when only short sales is about 42%, we set analyze the effect adding the upper bounds of portfolio weights by setting it as 20% and 10%.

Figure 82.3 exhibits the efficient frontiers that are constructed with various sets of weighting constraints. Among the optimal portfolio sets, the one that only prohibits short sales demonstrates higher mean–variance efficiency than the other two that include the upper bounds. The two curves with upper bounds are close to the no-short-sales efficient frontier in low risk–return regime and are feasible. Consistent with risk reduction effect suggested by Jagannathan and Ma (2003), the constraints that the weights of assets cannot be greater than 20% do not significantly change the low-volatility portfolio selections but eliminate risky investing opportunities. When the diversity increases as the weights cannot be higher than 10%, the mean–variance efficiency drops and the investing area also shrinks.

We report two characteristic portfolios on the efficient frontier. The first is the maximum Sharpe Ratio (MSR) portfolio that represents the highest mean–variance efficiency that can be achieved by the diversification. The second measure is the minimum-variance portfolio (MVP) as Elton, Gruber, Brown, and Goetzmann (2007) suggest that investors may seek to minimize the variance of a portfolio due to the lack of predictability of expected returns.
