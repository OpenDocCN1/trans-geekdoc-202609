In emerging markets — and the focus of this paper, Taiwan in particular — collateral is universally required for even small bank loans; consequently, firms have very little power to differentiate themselves based upon their willingness to offer collateral. As mentioned above, foreign banks do not possess the same soft information that is possessed by their domestic counterparts; hence, they face a larger adverse selection problem. Foreign banks offer borrowers a choice to either offer up more collateral and obtain a lower interest rate or put up less collateral and get a higher interest rate. High quality firms can signal their quality by their willingness to offer up their valuable assets as collateral for their foreign loans. A high quality firm might prefer to have a higher level of collateral — given that it has a lower likelihood of defaulting on its obligations — and lower interest rate; consequently, a separating equilibrium ensues. So, firms using foreign bank debt collateral may be signaling to the market that they are of high quality. For this reason, one might expect that stockholders may view collateral posted against foreign bank loans in a positive way, while viewing collateral posted against domestic bank loans as either conveying very little information or potentially as a negative signal of potential adverse selection problems.

As emerging markets begin to mature and outside foreign capital starts to flow into them, governments sometimes respond by initiating (often sweeping) financial reforms that increase the sophistication of the financial system. These reforms attempt, among other things, to ensure investor protection, allow for smooth transactions, improve competition, and protect against catastrophic systemic risk in the financial system. One of the benefits of this increase in the sophistication of the financial system is the acquisition of intellectual and human capital by domestic banks from their foreign counterparts. As a result, domestic banks become more sophisticated. Foreign banks, in contrast, begin to accumulate soft information and are better able to understand local market conditions. The results are consistent with arguments that geographic expansion lowers risk by reducing exposure to idiosyncratic local risks and inconsistent with arguments that expansion, on net, increases risk by reducing the ability of BHCs to monitor loans and manage risks. Goetz et al. (2016) finds that geographic expansion lowers bank risk by reducing overall exposure to idiosyncratic local risks. As a result, over time the differences between the information contained in local versus foreign banking relationships may diminish, and with them may diminish any existing differential impact of the use of foreign bank debt on firm performance.
