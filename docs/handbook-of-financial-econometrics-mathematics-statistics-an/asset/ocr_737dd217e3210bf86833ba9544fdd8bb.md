# (1) Existence of intelligent trading strategies better than the basic strategy of buy-and-hold

We know that technical analysis has a long history of about 120 years or longer. Since entering the times of information technology, technical trading has been lifted to the unprecedented level of quantitative trading. Rule-based trend following has been the primary trading strategy used by managed futures fund managers, also called CTA (commodity trading adviser), which has stood the test of the time and proved as still effective. In 1983, Richard J. Dennis, a famed commodities speculator recruited and trained 23 novice traders known as Turtles about a simple trend following system, trading a range of commodities, currencies and bond markets. After a trial period ended, he gave the few of them who had successfully traded the system, accounts ranging from $250,000 to $2 million to manage. Over four and a half years ever since, this group of selected Turtles earned an average annual compound rate of return of 80%. In 1982, James Simons, an award-winning mathematician, founded a hedge fund Renaissance Technologies LLC, which specializes in systematic trading using only quantitative models derived from mathematical and statistical analysis. Renaissance’s flagship Medallion fund is famed for one of the best records in investing history, returning more than 35% annualized over a 20-year span. Their very first trading strategy was based on Baum–Welch algorithm for finding the unknown parameters of the Hidden Markov Models (HMM), later on improved by algebraist James Ax, for market regime shift or switching and trend following. Dai et al. (2011) developed a model-based trend following strategy by expressing the shift coefficient μ(t) of the geometric Brownian motion as a Markov chain of two states — bull or bear. The long or short trading strategies are treated separately outside a no-action channel. The upper and lower bound of the no-action channel are derived by solving a HJB (Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman) equation. When the price breaks up through the upper bound of the channel, trading goes long, vice versa for going short. According to the test result reported in their paper, this model is quite effective for low-frequency trend following. The modeling approaches of this type originated by Hamilton (1989) formed a school generally called HMM (Hidden Markov Model), mainly used for modeling market regime shift. Liu and Deng (2014) provide a systematic interpretation of HMM for quantitative trading.
