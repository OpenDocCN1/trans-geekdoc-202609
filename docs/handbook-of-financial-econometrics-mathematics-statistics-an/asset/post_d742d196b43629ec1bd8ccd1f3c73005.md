## 13.4 Market Integration and Diversification Benefits

### 13.4.1 Are international markets more integrated over time?

Pukthuanthong and Roll (2009) consider levels of the world integration across a broad sample of countries. Specifically, they regress daily country index returns on their global principal components (PCs) constructed from countries that have data available in the Datastream since 1973. There are 17 of them representing the world market. For each member of the pre-1974 cohort of 17 countries, separate principal components are estimated for each country after it is excluded from the calculation. For example, when the subject country is Japan, the covariance matrix and principal components are computed only from concurrent daily returns for the 16 countries other than Japan plus lagged daily returns from the two North American countries.¹⁰ When Canada or the US is the subject country, the other 16 countries are used in the calculations but there is only one lagged return, that for the US or Canada, respectively. Excluding countries in the manner just described is intended to avoid any suspicion that a country’s return regressed on global factors is biased by that same country being heavily-weighted in the principal components. We apply their methodology and obtain the PC data up until 2011. With this approach, the R² measures the proportion of an index’s return that is explained by global factors.

To compare R² with correlation, we show t-statistics of slope from the regression of correlation of each country with the rest of the world on time trend. Fifteen of the twenty-one developed countries have positive t-statistics in excess of 2.0 with 4.21 of the mean time trend t-statistic. For the 13 emerging countries, seven have positive t-statistics in excess of 2.0, one has positive time trend significant at 10% level, three have positive but insignificant time trend, and two have negative time trends. The mean time trend t-statistic is 1.92. Overall, t-statistics of correlation time trend is lower than those of R² time trend. This evidence supports Roll (2013) and Pukthuanthong and Roll (2009)’s theoretical argument that the correlation can be conceivably quite low between markets; although, they are driven by the same common influences and are thus perfectly integrated or R² equal 1.0. The results suggest correlation and R² capture different dynamics of market integration.
