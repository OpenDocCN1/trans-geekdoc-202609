Supply chains, like all economic activities, strive to achieve economic efficiency, and with few exceptions (charities, non-profits), their primary driving force is financial gain. Thus, the financial underpinnings of supply chains are of paramount importance. Supply chain assets, liabilities, etc. often figure prominently in the firm’s financial statements, and can affect its market value. Supply chain assets (including inventories) and operations give rise to metrics that are used by analysts to assess a firm’s profitability and efficiency, as well as its liquidity and even solvency. In the wake of the recent credit crisis, cash management has evoked increased interest by firms and researchers, forcing firms to more carefully examine their sources and uses of cash, including net working capital.

There are many ways to measure the efficacy of working capital management on a firm’s financial performance. Key performance metrics are related to net profit and return on capital investment, among others. One metric, introduced by Richards and Laughlin (1980), which seems to be employed universally, is the cash conversion cycle (CCC), also known as the cash cycle or cash-to-cash cycle (C2C). The CCC is a duration metric, defined as the length of time (typically, the number of days) between the clearance of payment of a purchased item to the collection of payment for the sale of the same item. Like all financial managers, smart supply chain managers aim to speed up collection of receivables and delay disbursement of payables in order to take full advantage of financial float in their supply chain.

This chapter examines the issue of the optimal capital structure (choice of debt and equity) and inventory sizing via a Markovian cash conversion model in stochastic equilibrium (equilibrium, for short) for cash-constrained firms, in the sense that the firm is capital-rationed and credit-limited. Capital rationing means that for a period of time the firm has no access to additional external capital, while credit limiting means that the firm has access to a limited line of credit. In such cases, the firm often precludes the reinvestment of any internally generated profits (that is, it either does not have retained earnings or cannot reinvest them), and consequently, it is particularly sensitive to improvements in cash management. Instances of fully capital-rationed firms include start-up companies, firms in conservatorship, and wholly-owned subsidiaries of larger corporations. A subsidiary receives an allocation of funding from the parent company at the beginning of the period (quarter, month, etc.). Over the course of the period, the parent company injects no new capital into the subsidiary.
