To capture the determinants of capital structure within an optimal range of leverage ratios, the traditional linear regression analysis may not be a suitable methodology to investigate capital structure because the estimates of independent variables (determinants of capital structure) directly affect the dependent variables (leverage ratio) in regression. In other words, if the determinants are measured with errors, the estimated correlation coefficients will be biased. Leary and Roberts (2014) demonstrate that peer firms generally play an important role in determining capital structure and financial policies. Indeed, a firm’s financing decisions are responses to the financing decisions and, to a lesser extent, the characteristics of peer firms.

Moreover, most of the theoretical capital structure models proposed in previous studies are based on theoretical variables; however, these variables are often unobservable in the real world. Therefore, many studies use the accounting items from the financial statements as proxies to substitute for the theoretically derived variables. In the regression analysis, the estimated parameters from accounting items as proxies for unobservable theoretical attributes would cause problems. First, there are measurement errors between the observable proxies and the latent variables.¹ According to the previous theoretical literature in corporate finance, a theoretical variable can be formed with either one or several observed variables as a proxy. However, there is no clear rule to allocate the unique weights of observable variables as the perfect proxy of a latent variable. Second, because of unobservable attributes in the capital structure, researchers can choose different accounting items to measure the same attribute in accordance with the various capital structure theories and the economic interpretations. The use of these observed variables as theoretical explanatory latent variables in both cases will cause error-in-variable problems during the analysis of the determinants of the capital structure. Joreskog and Goldberger (1975), Joreskog (1977) and Joreskog and Sorbom (1981, 1989) developed the structural equation modeling (SEM) to analyze the relationship between the observed variables as the indicators and the latent variables as the attributes of the capital structure choice.

Titman and Wessels (1988) first utilize the LISREL system to analyze the determinants of the capital structure choice based on an SEM framework.

¹In statistics, latent variables (as opposed to observable variables), are variables that are not directly observed but are inferred (through a mathematical model) from other variables that are observed (directly measured).
