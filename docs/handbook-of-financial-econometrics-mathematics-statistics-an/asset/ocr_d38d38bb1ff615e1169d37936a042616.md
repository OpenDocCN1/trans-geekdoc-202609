On the other hand, balance sheet variables have been introduced in a number of other studies where their importance has been established although there are differences on the type of financial ratios that are found to be relevant for the pricing of bank CDS contracts. Chiaramonte and Casu (2012) relied only on balance sheet ratios in order to reveal their relevance for the CDS spreads. Their main results were that first, CDS spreads reflected the risk captured by balance sheet ratios and that their importance varied across time. Among the financial ratios, leverage and the Tier I ratio appeared insignificant in all three time periods that were examined while the liquidity factor became relevant only during the crisis and the post-crisis periods. Jenkins et al. (2016) tested the CDS market for being semi-strong efficient. They relied on data from 692 firms and were able to establish that announced earnings announcements and accruals were associated with systematic patterns of reaction in subsequent CDS returns during crisis periods while under stable economic conditions the semi-strong hypothesis could not be rejected. Drago et al. (2017) made use of both balance sheet and market determinants of a sample made up from 54 European and US large banks. They also found that a great number of balance sheet ratios expressing capital adequacy, leverage, asset quality, bank size and funding stability were important determinants of banks’ CDS spreads.

Finally, the sovereign debt crisis in Euro-zone has given impetus to a great number of studies which tried to unveil the interaction between sovereign and bank credit risk. De Bruyckere et al. (2013) investigated the impact of bank-specific factors for the bank/sovereign spillover risk. They found that banks with a weak capital base, weak funding structures and less traditional activities were more in danger of risk spillovers. Angeloni and Wolff (2012) fail to find strong evidence of correlation between Euro-zone banks’ asset holdings on peripheral sovereign debt and their stock market returns. Rather, bank stock prices appear to be more associated to the risk of the country these banks are located in. In a similar vein, Drago et al. (2017) show that sovereign risk rating is an important determinant of banks’ CDS spread. Arezki et al. (2011) find that downgrades of sovereigns have implications not only for the country involved but that they spillover to other markets and countries as well.
