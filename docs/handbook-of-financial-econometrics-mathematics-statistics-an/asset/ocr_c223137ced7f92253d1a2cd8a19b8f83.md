If we further assume d = 0, equation (58.13)' becomes equation (58.6) in that γ*_ij = γ_ij and φ*_j = φ_j; the VaR then is characterized by a conventional downside risk series.

Having applied the fractional filtering by applying the d_GPH derived from Table 58.4 to the downside risk series for each market, we estimate equations (58.12) and (58.13) twice. Panel A in Table 58.5 reports the estimates of the unrestricted model, while Panel B reports the results of equations (58.12)' and (58.13)' by imposing the restriction of λ*_i = 0 and λ*_j = 0, respectively. Columns 4 and 7 contain the parameters (γ*_ii and λ*_i) for equation (58.12) and columns 5 and 6 present the parameters (γ*_ij) for equation (58.13). The test results are summarized as follows. First, in Panel A, we find that the leverage effect (λ*_i) is negative, and only the coefficients on Hong Kong, Indonesia and Singapore are significant.21 The cross-market leverage effects (λ*_j) of most of the Asian countries on the US (column 8) and China (column 9) are negatively significant, implying that the lagged return shocks to Asian countries will subsequently increase the downside risk both in the local market and in other markets. This finding is consistent with the market integration hypothesis: the cross-market effect is not restricted to a big event; rather it occurs on a weekly basis. More important, estimated values for γ*_ii are highly significant, indicating a positive downside risk–return relation across various markets (except Taiwan),22 and confirming that higher excess stock returns compensate for a rise in downside risk, supporting the tradeoff hypothesis.

Second, a rather striking phenomenon is the evidence of cross-market downside risk, γ*_ij, regardless of whether it is from the US (column 5) or China (column 6). The estimated coefficients are consistently positive and statistically significant.23 Note that the negative sign between the US downside risk and the expected returns on Asian stock markets in Tables 58.2 (column 5) and 58.3 (column 2) now turns positive (column 5 in Table 58.5), as the long memory component in downside risk has been modeled. Consequently, without giving an appropriate treatment to the long memory, the test result may be misleading about the downside-risk and return relation.
