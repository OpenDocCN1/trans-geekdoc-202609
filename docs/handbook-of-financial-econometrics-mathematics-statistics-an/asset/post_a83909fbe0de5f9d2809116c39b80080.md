$r_t = \delta_0 + \delta_1^T x_t.$
(61.8)

In our estimation, we fix $\delta_0$ to be the unconditional mean of the three-month Treasury-bill rate. As regards to $\delta_1$, we obtain the coefficients $\delta_{11}$ corresponding to the macro factors from linear regressions of the three-month rate on the macro factors and leave $\delta_{12}$ corresponding to the latent factors to be estimated in the maximum likelihood estimation procedure. Finally, the market prices of risk are estimated through the following standard relation,

$\lambda_t = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 x_t.$
(61.9)

In this case, $\lambda_0$ is a 27-dimensional vector and $\lambda_1$ is a $27 \times 27$ matrix with affiliated parameters corresponding to the lagged macro factors being set to zero. This allows us to simplify the estimation procedure greatly, while still ensuring that risk in the macro and latent factors are priced. This means that the upper $2 \times 1$ row (corresponding to the current macro factors) and the bottom $3 \times 1$ row (corresponding to the latent factors) of $\lambda_0$ are free parameters. The upper-left $2 \times 2$ corner, upper-right $2 \times 3$ corner, lower-left $3 \times 2$ corner, and the lower-right $3 \times 3$ corner of $\lambda_1$ are also all free parameters. The aforementioned steps characterize the time-series dynamics of how the factors evolve through time in the model.

As regards the cross-sectional part of our maximum likelihood estimation procedure, we begin by assuming that $P_{t,n} = \exp (\bar{A}_n + \bar{B}_n^T x_t)$ (e.g., see Duffie and Kan, 1996). To derive the coefficients used for computing the recursive bond prices, we begin by noting that for an $n$-period bond,

$P_{t,n+1} = E_t[\xi_{t+1} P_{t+1,n}]$
$= E_t\left[ \exp\left\{ -r_t - \frac{1}{2} \lambda_t^T \lambda_t - \lambda_t^T \varepsilon_{t+1} + \bar{A}_n + \bar{B}_n^T x_{t+1} \right\} \right].$
(61.10a)

Since the expectation is taken at time $t$, the information is known at time $t$ and this expression can be evaluated without uncertainty. Thus, we take these known quantities out of the expectation as shown in equation (61.10b) and in subsequent steps,

$= \exp\left\{ -r_t - \frac{1}{2} \lambda_t^T \lambda_t + \bar{A}_n \right\} E_t[\exp\{-\lambda_t^T \varepsilon_{t+1} + \bar{B}_n^T x_{t+1}\}].$
(61.10b)

Then, we continue with the derivation by assuming the following dynamics for the state variable $x_{t+1} = \mu + \phi x_t + \Sigma \varepsilon_{t+1}$ and describe the state variable in the above expression with first order vector autoregressive dynamics

$=\exp\left\{-r_t - \frac{1}{2}\lambda_t^T\lambda_t + \bar A_n\right\} E_t\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda_t^T\varepsilon_{t+1} + \bar B_n^T(\mu + \phi x_t + \Sigma\varepsilon_{t+1})\right\}\right].$

(61.10c)

In the steps below, we simplify equation (61.10c) and then collect and match coefficients in order to demonstrate how the authors arrive at the state factor coefficients presented on page 760 of the paper (Ang and Piazzesi, 2003),

$=\exp\left\{-r_t - \frac{1}{2}\lambda_t^T\lambda_t + \bar A_n + \bar B_n^T\mu + \bar B_n^T\phi x_t\right\} E_t\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda_t^T\varepsilon_{t+1} + \bar B_n^T\Sigma\varepsilon_{t+1}\right\}\right]$
$=\exp\left\{-\delta_0 - \delta_1^T x_t - \frac{1}{2}\lambda_t^T\lambda_t + \bar A_n + \bar B_n^T\mu + \bar B_n^T\phi x_t\right\} E_t\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda_t^T\varepsilon_{t+1} + \bar B_n^T\Sigma\varepsilon_{t+1}\right\}\right]$
$=\exp\left\{-\delta_0 + \bar A_n + \bar B_n^T\mu + (\bar B_n^T\phi - \delta_1^T) x_t - \frac{1}{2}\lambda_t^T\lambda_t\right\} E_t\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda_t^T\varepsilon_{t+1} + \bar B_n^T\Sigma\varepsilon_{t+1}\right\}\right].$

Ang and Piazzesi (2003) further assume that $\varepsilon_{t+1} \sim N(0, I)$, thus, and in regards to the second term and the computation of its expectation

$E_t\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda_t^T\varepsilon_{t+1} + \bar B_n^T\Sigma\varepsilon_{t+1}\right\}\right]$
$= E_t\left[\exp\left\{(\bar B_n^T\Sigma - \lambda_t^T)\varepsilon_{t+1}\right\}\right]$
$= \exp\left\{\frac{(\bar B_n^T\Sigma - \lambda_t^T)(\bar B_n^T\Sigma - \lambda_t^T)^T}{2}\right\}$
$= \exp\left\{\frac{(\bar B_n^T\Sigma - \lambda_t^T)(\Sigma^T\bar B_n - \lambda_t)}{2}\right\}$
$= \exp\left\{\frac{(\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n - \bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_t - \lambda_t^T\Sigma^T\bar B_n + \lambda_t^T\lambda_t)}{2}\right\}$
$= \exp\left\{\frac{(\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n - 2\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_t + \lambda_t^T\lambda_t)}{2}\right\}$
$= \exp\left\{\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2} - \bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_t + \frac{\lambda_t^T\lambda_t}{2}\right\}$
$= \exp\left\{\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2} - \bar B_n^T\Sigma(\lambda_0 + \lambda_1 x_t) + \frac{\lambda_t^T\lambda_t}{2}\right\}$
$= \exp\left\{\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2} - \bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_0 + \bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_1 x_t + \frac{\lambda_t^T\lambda_t}{2}\right\}.$

Finally, plugging all this information back into the pricing equation and simplifying,

$P_{t,n+1}=\exp\left\{-\delta_0+\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T\mu+(\bar B_n^T\phi-\delta_1^T)x_t-\frac12\lambda_t^T\lambda_t\right\}\exp\left\{\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2}-\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_0+\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_1x_t+\frac{\lambda_t^T\lambda_t}{2}\right\},\tag{61.11}$

$P_{t,n+1}=\exp\left\{-\delta_0+\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T\mu+(\bar B_n^T\phi-\delta_1^T)x_t-\frac12\lambda_t^T\lambda_t+\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2}-\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_0+\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_1x_t+\frac{\lambda_t^T\lambda_t}{2}\right\},$

$P_{t,n+1}=\exp\left\{-\delta_0+\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T\mu+(\bar B_n^T\phi-\delta_1^T)x_t+\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2}-\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_0+\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_1x_t\right\},$

$P_{t,n+1}=\exp\left\{-\delta_0+\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T(\mu-\Sigma\lambda_0)+(\bar B_n^T\phi-\delta_1^T)x_t+\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2}+\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_1x_t\right\},$

$P_{t,n+1}=\exp\left\{-\delta_0+\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T(\mu-\Sigma\lambda_0)+(\bar B_n^T\phi-\bar B_n^T\Sigma\lambda_1)x_t+\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2}-\delta_1^Tx_t\right\},$

$P_{t,n+1}=\exp\left\{-\delta_0+\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T(\mu-\Sigma\lambda_0)+\bar B_n^T(\phi-\Sigma\lambda_1)x_t+\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2}-\delta_1^Tx_t\right\}.$

Thus, in matching the coefficients in the equation above with the one below, $P_{t,n+1}=\exp(\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T x_t)$, we find that

$\bar A_{n+1}=-\delta_0+\bar A_n+\bar B_n^T(\mu-\Sigma\lambda_0)+\frac{\bar B_n^T\Sigma\Sigma^T\bar B_n}{2}\tag{61.12}$

and

$\bar B_{n+1}=-\delta_1^T+\bar B_n^T(\phi-\Sigma\lambda_1).\tag{61.13}$

These recursive difference equations are solved with initial conditions $\bar{A}_1 = -\delta_0$ and $\bar{B}_1 = -\delta_1$ and are given by equation (61.17) of their paper. The continuously compounded zero coupon yield $y_t^n$ on an $n$-period zero coupon bond is given by

$$y_t^n = -\frac{\log p_t^n}{n} = A_n + B_n^T x_t.\tag{61.14}$$

Finally, $A_n = -\frac{\bar{A}_n}{n}$ and $B_n = -\frac{\bar{B}_n}{n}$. If we suppose that we have as many bonds available as the latent factors, which are three in the estimation. Then, and following much of the literature (e.g., Pearson and Sun (1994)), we can take the six-month, two-year, and 10-year zero coupon bond yield to be measured without error and use these yields to “invert” the model in order to arrive at the latent factors. This means that we can stack the equations for the three yields as $Y_t = (y_t^{6\text{mo}}, y_t^{2\text{yr}}, y_t^{10\text{yr}})^T$. Then, $Y_t = A + B x_t$ where A is a $3 \times 1$ vector and B is $3 \times 27$. If we partition B into $B = [B^o B^u]$ where $B^o$ is a $3 \times 24$ matrix associated with the observable factors and $B^u$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix connected to the latent variables, then, we can calculate the latent factors using the following relation, $Y_t = A + B^o x_t^o + B^u x_t^u$. Hence, we have that $x_t^u = B^u^{-1}(Y_t - A + B^o x_t^o)$ for the three yields that are measured without error as described above. The remaining four yields will be assumed to be measured with error and so we begin by defining $B^m$ as a $7 \times 4$ matrix of measurement error terms and $u_t^m$ is a 4-dimensional Gaussian white noise vector containing the measurement errors. From these assumptions and for the four remaining yields $(y_t^{1\text{yr}}, y_t^{3\text{yr}}, y_t^{5\text{yr}}, y_t^{7\text{yr}})$, we can write,

$$Y_t = A + B^o x_t^o + B^u x_t^u + B^m u_t^m.\tag{61.15}$$

Thus, the yields that are measured without error are used to solve for $x_t^u$ as described above and the yields that are assumed to be measured with error are used to solve for $u_t^m$ in an analogous calculation,

$$u_t^m = (B^m)^{-1}(Y_t - A + B^o x_t^o + B^u x_t^u).\tag{61.16}$$

Thus, in essence, we have transformed the model to estimate from a system with yields and observables into a system with observables and unobservable variables. The yields, which are expressed in terms of the state variables in closed form, can be used to obtain the unobservable factors from the yields. We are transforming $(Y_t, x_t^o)$ into $(x_t^o, x_t^u)$ and thus need the following Jacobian to facilitate this transformation

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial x_t^o}{\partial x_t^o} & \frac{\partial x_t^o}{\partial x_t^u} & \frac{\partial x_t^o}{\partial u_t^m} \\ \frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial x_t^o} & \frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial x_t^u} & \frac{\partial Y_t}{\partial u_t^m} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I_{(24\times24)} & 0_{(24\times3)} & 0_{(24\times4)} \\ B^o_{(7\times24)} & B^u_{(7\times3)} & B^m_{(7\times4)} \end{bmatrix}_{(31\times31)}.$$
