- (4) **Event-Based Active Trend Following (EATF):** Mature market economies of developed countries have developed a conventional information disclosure mechanism. Release of financial and economic policies or readings of economic indicators which may impact the financial markets are conventionally scheduled with exact dates and times, often as precise as to the minute. Therefore the market calendar has emerged. When and which market authorities will release what market-impacting information have already been arranged into the market calendar, although the polarity (negative or positive) of each information or data and the polarity of its market impact are unknown in advance. A trader needs to enter into the attention state, closely monitor the market activities before, during and after the release, take appropriate trading actions accordingly. This type of event-based trading strategies based on known market calendar but unknown polarity of information and that of market impact is feasible in developed countries where the market calendar exists. According to our long-term observation to international financial markets, about a half of profitable trading opportunities belong to this type of event-driven situations. As for emergent events with breaking news outside the market calendar, it is generally assumed in academics to be unpredictable. Or from a viewpoint of big science, prediction of emergent events outside the financial markets and economies requires far bigger and more complex models encompassing political, economic, financial, and environmental information. A group of European cross-disciplinary scientists were reported pushing for a grand project aiming to develop a Living Earth Simulator, which belongs to this type of big science prediction models. Apparently this type of complex models would require connecting to the technologies of the Internet, the Internet of Things, and deep intelligence. This is beyond the scope of this chapter.

The latter two types — PATF and EATF — belong to the category of expectation-based active strategies of trend following, which are more or less active, while the former two types — RPTF and MPTF — belong to the category of passive trend following.

## 43.6 Technical Feasibility of Strength Investing in the Framework of Intelligent Portfolio Management

The technical feasibility of strength investing for concentration with its risk controlled by the framework of intelligent portfolio management is determined mainly by the four conditions as follows:

### (1) Existence of intelligent trading strategies better than the basic strategy of buy-and-hold

We know that technical analysis has a long history of about 120 years or longer. Since entering the times of information technology, technical trading has been lifted to the unprecedented level of quantitative trading. Rule-based trend following has been the primary trading strategy used by managed futures fund managers, also called CTA (commodity trading adviser), which has stood the test of the time and proved as still effective. In 1983, Richard J. Dennis, a famed commodities speculator recruited and trained 23 novice traders known as Turtles about a simple trend following system, trading a range of commodities, currencies and bond markets. After a trial period ended, he gave the few of them who had successfully traded the system, accounts ranging from $250,000 to $2 million to manage. Over four and a half years ever since, this group of selected Turtles earned an average annual compound rate of return of 80%. In 1982, James Simons, an award-winning mathematician, founded a hedge fund Renaissance Technologies LLC, which specializes in systematic trading using only quantitative models derived from mathematical and statistical analysis. Renaissance’s flagship Medallion fund is famed for one of the best records in investing history, returning more than 35% annualized over a 20-year span. Their very first trading strategy was based on Baum–Welch algorithm for finding the unknown parameters of the Hidden Markov Models (HMM), later on improved by algebraist James Ax, for market regime shift or switching and trend following. Dai et al. (2011) developed a model-based trend following strategy by expressing the shift coefficient μ(t) of the geometric Brownian motion as a Markov chain of two states — bull or bear. The long or short trading strategies are treated separately outside a no-action channel. The upper and lower bound of the no-action channel are derived by solving a HJB (Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman) equation. When the price breaks up through the upper bound of the channel, trading goes long, vice versa for going short. According to the test result reported in their paper, this model is quite effective for low-frequency trend following. The modeling approaches of this type originated by Hamilton (1989) formed a school generally called HMM (Hidden Markov Model), mainly used for modeling market regime shift. Liu and Deng (2014) provide a systematic interpretation of HMM for quantitative trading.
