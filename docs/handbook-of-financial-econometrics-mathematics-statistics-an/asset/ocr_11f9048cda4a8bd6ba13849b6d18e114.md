# Abstract

This chapter attempts to explore the puzzle of post-earnings-announcement drifts by focusing on the revision of systematic risk subsequent to the release of earnings information. This chapter proposes a market model with time-varying systematic risk by incorporating ARCH into the CAPM. The Kalman filter is then employed to estimate how the market revises its risk assessment subsequent to earnings announcement. This chapter also conducts empirical analysis based on a sample of US publicly held companies during the five-fiscal year sample period, 2010–2014. After controlling for the revision of risk and isolating potential confounding effect, this chapter finds that the phenomenon of post-earnings announcement drifts, so well documented in accounting literature, no longer exists.

# Keywords

ARCH (autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity) • CAPM • Kalman filter • Post-earnings-announcement drifts.

# 53.1 Introduction

The phenomenon of the post-earnings-announcement drifts (PEADs) first observed in Ball and Brown (1968) has been extensively investigated by modern accounting research (Cartwright and Lee, 1987; Bernard and Thomas, 1989; Bhushan, 1994; Ke and Ramalingegowda, 2005; Garfinkel and Sokobin, 2006; Sadka, 2006; Francis et al., 2007 Ng et al., 2008; Ayers et al., 2011; Chung and Hrazdil, 2011; Louis and Sun, 2011; Shin and Park, 2018; Wang et al., 2018). However, whether this phenomenon is due to improper research design or is a sign of market inefficiency has been inconclusive.

Among those who can hardly argue that the stock market is inefficient, Ball (1978), Foster et al. (1984) (referred to FOS hereafter) and Ball et al. (1993) (referred BKW hereafter) attribute this phenomenon to the non-stationarity of systematic risk. They suggest that the systematic risk may not be fully adjusted, because the capital-asset-pricing model (CAPM) used to calculate abnormal returns is either incomplete or misspecified. As a result, the so-called abnormal returns represent fair compensation for risk bearing that is priced but not captured by the CAPM. Ball and Bartov (1996) further argue that predictable stock returns following earnings announcement remain anomalous and may be due to biases in sample selection or abnormal returns measurement. On the other hand, Bernard and Thomas (1989, 1990, referred to BT hereafter) contend that the inference of asset pricing model misspecification is unwarranted. After investigating several possible sources of the post-earnings-announcement drifts, BT conclude succinctly that the drifts is a damaging evidence against the belief in market efficiency.
