Firstly, we conduct standard unit root test procedures. In particular, we use the ADF (Dickey and Fuller, 1979) and Phillips and Perron (1988) approaches, which are some of the most commonly employed tests in the literature. In all the cases (no regressors, intercept, and an intercept with a linear trend) except for Cambodia, we can reject the null hypothesis of a unit root using the PP test, implying that the series can be considered stationary; however with the ADF test we find evidence of stationarity only in the cases of Malaysia, Myanmar, Thailand and Vietnam, while in the other cases we cannot reject the unit root null hypothesis. The p-values of both tests corresponding to each country can be found in Table 23.A1 in Appendix D.5.

In the context of fractional integration, we consider the following model,

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + x_t, \quad t = 1,2,\dots \tag{23.2}$$

$$(1 - L)^d x_t = u_t, \quad t = 1,2,\dots, \tag{23.3}$$

where $y_t$ is the observed time-series; $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are the coefficients corresponding, respectively, to the intercept and a linear time trend, and $x_t$ is supposed to be $I(d)$ adopting different forms such as white noise, Bloomfield-type autocorrelated disturbances, and given the monthly nature of the series examined, we also impose a seasonal monthly AR(1) structure for $u_t$.

We report the fractional differencing parameter d for the three standard cases examined in the literature, i.e., the case of no deterministic terms (i.e., $\beta_0 = \beta_1 = 0$ a priori in the undifferenced equation (23.2)), including only a constant ($\beta_0$ unknown, and $\beta_1 = 0$ a priori), and finally the case of a constant with a linear time trend ($\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ unknown).$^6$ These values were obtained by using a grid-search with the tests of Robinson (1994) and choosing the values of $d$ that produced the lowest statistics.$^7$ Together with the estimates, we also present the 95% confidence band of the non-rejection values of $d$, using Robinson's (1994) parametric tests. The results for the case of white noise disturbances are displayed in Table 23.2.

The first thing we observe in this table is that all the estimated values of $d$ (and all confidence bands) are higher than 0 and smaller than 1, providing thus evidence in favor of fractional integration. Focusing first on the deterministic terms, we observe that for half of the countries (Brunei, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar and Singapore) a linear time trend seems to be required (with a positive slope coefficient in the cases of Brunei, Malaysia and Myanmar, and a negative one in the other two, Laos and Singapore (see Table 23.2b)), while for the remaining five countries (Cambodia, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam) an intercept seems to be sufficient to describe the deterministic part. If we look at the estimated orders of integration for the selected models, we observe that the lowest degrees of integration are obtained in the cases of Myanmar (0.24), Singapore (0.31) and Brunei (0.35), while the highest values are obtained in the cases of Vietnam (0.77) and Cambodia (0.78). Since the orders of integration obtained in the 10 series examined are all below 1 we can conclude that these series present mean reverting behavior, and thus we can statistically predict that external shocks will not affect these series permanently. This can be considered as a first good indicator of homogeneity among the ASEAN countries.

> Note that even for these two countries (Vietnam and Cambodia) the unit root null hypothesis (d = 1) is rejected in favour of mean reversion (d < 1). This is based on the confidence bands reported in their corresponding tables.
