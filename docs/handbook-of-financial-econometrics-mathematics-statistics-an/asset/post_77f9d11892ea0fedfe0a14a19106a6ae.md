### Abstract

When evaluating the market risk of long-horizon equity returns, it is always difficult to provide a statistically sound solution due to the limitation of the sample size. To solve the problem for the value-at-risk (VaR) and the conditional tail expectation (CTE), Ho et al. (2016, 2018) introduce a general multivariate stochastic volatility return model from which asymptotic formulas for the VaR and the CTE are derived for integrated returns with the length of integration increasing to infinity. Based on the formulas, simple non-parametric estimators for the two popular risk measures of the long-horizon returns are constructed. The estimates are easy to implement and shown to be consistent and asymptotically normal. In this chapter, we further address the issue of testing the equality of the CTEs of integrated returns. Extensive finite-sample analysis and real data analysis are conducted to demonstrate the efficiency of the test statistics we propose.

### 72.1 Introduction

Risk management has been important in actuarial science, business, and finance. There are many measures proposed in the literature to quantify the risk. In this chapter, we focus on two important ones, the value-at-risk (VaR) and the conditional tail expectation (CTE). The VaR is a quantile-based risk measure that computes the amount of losses that might occur in a portfolio of assets over a specified time period with a specified small probability, and has been one of the most popular and well-established risk measures. Although VaR has been widely used because of its simplicity, it has also been criticized for its limitation of being unable to take the risk beyond VaR into account. To fill the gap, the CTE is designed to measure the average loss beyond the VaR.

The methods that have been proposed in the literature to compute the VaR can be divided into three categories, namely, non-parametric, semi-parametric and parametric. One typical non-parametric method is the historical simulation method, which does not assume any particular distribution nor the volatility model of the asset returns, but assumes implicit independence and identically distributed of the returns. Two examples of the semi-parametric approach are the extreme value theory used by Chan and Gray (2006) and the dynamic quantile regression CAViaR model proposed by Engle and Manganelli (2004). The parametric method assumes that the volatility of the return series follows a financial time series model (see, for example, Chan (2009a, 2009b)). There are in general two classes of volatility models depending on if the conditional variance is latent or not. Typical examples are ARCH model of Engle (1982) and the GARCH model of Bollerslev (1986) for the former and the stochastic volatility (SV) model of Taylor (1986) for the latter. Both these volatility models are shown to be capable of capturing the stylized facts of financial returns such as the fat tails and the volatility clustering (Taylor, 1986).

The literature on the three methods mentioned above are vast, and most studies are focused on simple returns or returns of short-horizon. However, for many important financial products in actuarial business, such as the equity-linked options issued by insurance companies, the contract duration typically lasts for 10–30 years, or more. In order for the insurance companies to manage the long-term market risk, it is of central importance to have an accurate and reliable estimate of the VaR for returns of long horizon. The VaR analysis for returns of long-horizon is much more difficult than that for simple returns because the number of observations reduces significantly as the horizon becomes very long. By using the bootstrap method, the properties of long-horizon US stock market returns are examined in Fama and French (2018). In particular, Fama and French (2018) found that distributions of continuously compounded returns converge to normal distributions if the horizon is stretched from 1 to 30 years. Indeed, the studies of long-horizon returns have received increasingly more attention recently (see, for example, Boudoukh, Richardson, and Whitelaw (2008), Neuberger (2012) and Lee (2013)) as many important statistical issues concerning returns of long-term investments need to be addressed.

Motivated by the fact that large institutional investors, such as insurance companies, pension funds, and sovereign funds, usually set a very long investment horizon for their portfolios, Ho et al. (2016) (as HCT-1) proposed a non-parametric estimator for the VaR of the integrated return. Considering the framework of a more general multivariate stochastic volatility model, Ho et al. (2018) (as HCT-2) focused on the CTE case and obtained two important results. First, an asymptotic formula for the CTE is derived when T is large. Second, HCT-2 used the formula to construct an estimator for the CTE of integrated returns, and showed that the estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal. In this chapter, we apply the results of HCT-2 to test the equality of two independent CTEs. The rest of this chapter is organized as follows. In Section 72.2, we briefly review the results of HCT-1 and HCT-2. The problem of the comparison between the CTEs generated from two samples of returns are considered in Section 72.3 followed by some simulation studies conducted in Section 72.4. Section 72.5 considers a simple application and Section 72.6 concludes.
