
where

$$\sum_i \equiv \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \sigma_v^2 & \sigma_v^2 & \cdots & \sigma_v^2 \\ \sigma_v^2 & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \sigma_v^2 \\ \sigma_v^2 & \cdots & \sigma_v^2 & \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \sigma_v^2 \end{bmatrix}\tag{28.29}$$

is $T \times T$ with $\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2$ on the principal diagonal and $\sigma_v^2$ elsewhere, and $\mathbf{i}$ is a $T$-vector of 1s.

To obtain the GLS estimates of $\beta$ we need to know $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ and $\sigma_v^2$, or at least their ratio since GLS estimation requires only that $\Omega$ be consistently specified up to a factor. So to obtain feasible GLS estimates, we need a consistent estimate of this ratio. Fortunately, it is always possible to find a consistent estimate of $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ by estimating the fixed effects model (28.24) because no matter how $N$ and $T$ may behave as $NT \to \infty$, there are $NT$ residuals. Dividing the sum of squared errors from (28.24), $SSE_{FE}$, by $NT - N - K$ gives an unbiased and consistent estimate of $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ since the error terms for this model are just the $\varepsilon_{it}$. But the natural estimator of $\sigma_v^2$, the sample variance of the elements of $\hat\gamma$ is not consistent unless $N \to \infty$ (so the RE estimator is problematic for small samples).

If $NT \to \infty$ as $N \to \infty$, $\sigma_v^2$ can be estimated consistently. Start with the regression

$$\mathbf{P_D y} = \mathbf{P_D X}\beta + \text{residuals},\tag{28.30}$$

where $\mathbf{P_D} \equiv \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{M_D}$ and $\mathbf{M_D}$ denotes the projection matrix $\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{D' D})^{-1}\mathbf{D'}$, to obtain the between-groups estimator:

$$\hat\beta_{BG} = (\mathbf{X' P_D X})^{-1} \mathbf{X' P_D y},\tag{28.31}$$

which is based on only $N$ observations since all variables are the same for every observation in each group. It only exists if $N > K$ since the matrix $\mathbf{X' P_D X}$ can have at most rank $N$. It is called the between estimator because it uses only the variation among the group means.

If the restrictions of the random effects model are not satisfied, the $\hat\beta_{BG}$ estimator, if it exists, is in general biased and inconsistent. To see this, unbiasedness and consistency require that the moment condition

$$E\big((\mathbf{P_D X})'_{it}(y_{it} - \mathbf{X}_{it}\beta)\big) = 0,\tag{28.32}$$

where $(\mathbf{P_D X})_{it}$ is the row labeled $it$ of the $NT \times K$ matrix $\mathbf{P_D X}$. Since $y_{it} - \mathbf{X}_{it}\beta = v_i + \varepsilon_{it}$ and $\varepsilon_{it}$ are independent of everything else in (28.32), this condition is equivalent to no correlation between the $v_i$ and elements of the matrix $\mathbf{X}$.

The variance of the error terms in the between-groups regression, (28.30), is $\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2/T$. Therefore, if we run it as a regression with $N$ observations, divide the resulting sum of squared errors by $N - K$, and then subtract $\sigma_\varepsilon^2/T$, we obtain a consistent estimate of $\sigma_v^2$. If this estimate is negative, Greene (2018), among others, provides details on alternative approaches for estimating the covariance components that can be tried but we should probably not be estimating a RE model in this case.

The FGLS estimator of the RE model is matrix-weighted averages of the within-groups (FE, equation (28.10)) and the between-groups estimators, (28.31). Fuller and Battese (1973, 1974) show that the GLS estimator of the RE model can be obtained by using OLS with the transformed data, i.e.,

$$(I - \lambda P_D) y = (I - \lambda P_D) X\beta + \text{residuals}, \tag{28.33}$$

where the scalar

$$\lambda \equiv 1 - (T\sigma_v^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2 + 1)^{-1/2} = 1 - (\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(T\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2))^{1/2} \tag{28.34}$$

and the consistent variance estimators developed above are used in (28.34). If $\lambda = 0$ then this estimator is just OLS and if $\lambda = 1$ this is the FE (within-groups) estimator. As long as $\lambda \in (0,1)$, the RE GLS estimator is more efficient than the FE estimator, (28.10), as well as the OLS pooled estimator.

## 28.12 DWH Test for FE Versus RE Models

In estimating the model, the effects are specified as either

- **Fixed Effects (FE)**: parameters to be estimated, can be estimated using dummy variables (the so-called LSDV estimator) or by group mean differencing and LS.
- **Random Effects (RE)**: The $e_{it}$ covariance matrix is specified as functions of the variances of the $u_t$, $v_i$, and $\varepsilon_{it}$, and FGLS is commonly used for estimation.

Joint hypothesis tests of the effects can be carried out with an F-test (for the FE model) or Wald, LR, or LM test (for the RE model). A DWH test can be used to choose between the FE and RE specifications of the model:

- If effects are uncorrelated with X, RE is consistent and efficient, FE is consistent but inefficient.

- If effects are correlated with X, then FE is consistent and efficient and RE is inconsistent.

Although the DWH test statistic in (28.35) is in the context of the RE and FE panel estimators, this is otherwise a relatively general form of the test.

$$m = (\hat{\beta}_{RE} - \hat{\beta}_{FE})' (\hat{\Omega}_{FE} - \hat{\Omega}_{RE})^{-1} (\hat{\beta}_{RE} - \hat{\beta}_{FE}) \sim \chi^2_K, \qquad (28.35)$$

where $\hat{\beta}_{RE}$ is a vector of RE parameter estimates, $\hat{\beta}_{FE}$ is a vector of FE parameter estimates, both of size $k$, and $\hat{\Omega}_{FE}$ and $\hat{\Omega}_{RE}$ are consistent estimates of the covariance matrices (or sub-matrices for the subset of the parameters being tested) for the FE and RE estimators. The DWH test statistic, $m$, has $K$ degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis $H_0$ : RE residuals are not correlated with X. Rejecting the null hypothesis implies that FE would be the preferred estimator because the RE parameter estimates are inconsistent (at least one of the explanatory variables is correlated with the random effects in the error structure). If the null is not rejected, while there is no preference between FE and RE in terms of consistency, RE would be preferred to FE because RE produces relatively efficient standard errors.

## 28.13 Example: DWH Test FE Versus RE Models

In an early test of the CAPM theory, Fama and MacBeth (1973) used a two-step estimation process:

- 1. Time series data for each asset was used to estimate separate regressions for each asset, which are used to estimate the $\beta_i$.
- 2. For each point in time, cross-sectional data was used to estimate

$$R_{it} - R_{ft} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 \beta_i + e_i, \qquad (28.36)$$

where $R_{it} - R_{ft} \equiv$ excess return on asset $i$ at time $t$, $\beta_i$ is the estimated beta, and $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$ are the parameters to estimate. Fama and MacBeth estimated this second stage separately for each time period and calculated the average of the parameter estimates to conduct hypothesis tests. However, one could also use a panel data approach to carry out this second stage analysis.

Data from Brooks (2014) on annual returns and estimated betas for 2,500 UK firms over 11 years are used to estimate FE and RE specifications of (28.36) and compare these estimates to those obtained using OLS on the pooled data, as well as each other. DWH tests are used to choose between these alternative specifications.
