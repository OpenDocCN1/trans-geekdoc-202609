## Abstract

This paper proposes a novel approach to rank analysts using their positions in a network constructed by peer analysts connected with overlapping firm coverage. We hypothesize that analysts occupying the network structural holes can produce higher quality equity research by a better access to their peer analysts’ wealth and diversity of information and knowledge. We report consistent empirical evidence that high-ranked analysts identified by network structural holes have greater ability to affect stock prices. Furthermore, those analysts tend to issue timely opinions, but not necessarily more accurate or consistent earnings forecasts. Analysts occupying structural holes tend to be more experienced, have a higher impact on stock prices when they work for large brokerages, and are rewarded with better career outcomes.

### 31.1 Introduction

Prior literature documents that equity analysts play an important role as information intermediaries in financial markets with an expertise in tracking industries and producing research coverage for selected firms. Analysts’ published opinions, such as earnings forecasts and investment recommendations, are documented to have a significant impact on prices and trading volume (e.g., Stickel, 1992; Womack, 1996; Chen et al., 2005; Bradley et al., 2014; Loh and Stulz, 2011; 2018). In addition, analyst coverage can change the information environment of stock trading and affect real operation of firms (e.g., Kelly and Ljungqvist, 2012; Cohen et al., 2013; Harford et al., 2017; Jia et al., 2017; Merkley et al., 2017).

Within the analyst community, some are recognized as star or lead analysts. For example, Institutional Investor magazine ranks analysts annually by soliciting votes from directors of research and chief investment officers at major money management institutions. Analysts selected to the roster of an All-Star analyst are well-compensated and highly regarded in the market place. Those highly ranked analysts, presumably with their comparative advantages in analyzing certain stocks based on their innate ability, industry-specific human capital, or access to information via an underwriting relation or social networks, will have more influence on the decisions of investors and have a greater impact on the prices and trading volume of stocks than other analysts do.

The literature has documented ways to rank analysts by comparing the stock price reaction to various characteristics of their earnings forecasts. Prior studies report that stock price reacts more to a forecast revision made by analysts with more accurate earnings forecasts (Park and Stice, 2000; Hong and Kubik, 2003; Jackson, 2005), with more timely forecasts (Cooper et al., 2001; Jegadeesh and Kim, 2010; Loh and Stulz, 2011), and with more consistent forecasts (Hilary and Hsu, 2013). However, ranking analysts by either accuracy, timeliness, or consistency based on forecast data has its own limitation. Ranking based on earnings forecast accuracy can be plagued by herding, as follower analysts can free ride on the information released by lead (high-ranked) analysts. It has also been pointed out that analysts can at times prefer to release timely or more consistent forecasts at the expense of forecast accuracy (Schipper, 1991; Clement and Tse, 2003). Alternatively, Cooper et al. (2001) propose to rank analysts by forecast timeliness. However, ranking forecast timeliness is highly model dependent with subjective parametric specifications. Finally, rankings based on forecast consistency can require data of long history of earnings forecasts, inherently biased against young and new analysts.

Our paper utilizes a new methodology of ranking analysts based on the network structure of the analyst coverage data. Our study focuses on the fact that analysts do not work in isolation, but rather with peer analysts producing information and competing for clients. These peer analysts can form a valuable network of knowledge and information that may improve an analyst’s performance and her ranking in the profession. We hypothesize how an analyst can produce higher quality equity research if she is in a network position with a better access to the peer analysts’ wealth of knowledge and information.

To test our hypothesis, we construct a network based on analyst coverage data by linking together analysts (nodes) who cover the same firm(s). When two analysts cover the same firm(s), they learn from each other’s evaluation and judgment on the company disclosures and the conversation with firm managers. Meanwhile, they also learn from each other’s access to the market, industry and economy wide information. From the perspective of each individual analyst, she will have access to and can benefit from aggregating information gathered from peer analysts covering the same firm(s) incremental to her own. Furthermore, there is a network effect. From the perspective of each individual analyst i, she not only can learn directly from the peer analyst j covering the same firm(s), but indirectly from the information of analyst j gathering from his peers. As a result, an analyst’s position in the network can matter. A network position with access to a set of diverse and rich information from peers will be helpful and instrumental for an analyst to make better and timely investment recommendations (Leone and Wu, 2007).¹ We follow Burt (2003) and use the measure of “structural hole” in measuring an analyst’s position to access complementary resources or information in the coverage network. As pointed out by Burt (2003), economic agents spanning structural holes in a network are in key positions of controlling flow of information and resources. Thus, if, as we hypothesize, there is an information sharing and learning among peer analysts, earnings forecasts released by those analysts occupying more structural holes should have greater impact on stock prices.

We report empirical results in line with our predictions. Analysts occupying more structural holes have more impact on stock prices when they release earnings forecast than other analysts. The impact is both economically and statistically significant. An increase in the structural hole (measured by network constraint) by one standard deviation will translate to an increase in the stock price reaction to an earnings forecast announcement by 21.6%. Further, we examine the relationship between the structural hole measure and the analyst performance measures of earnings forecast accuracy, timeliness, and consistency. While the structural hole measure has no obvious relation with forecast accuracy and consistency, it has a positive and significant correlation with forecast timeliness.

We proceed to examine some important attributes of high-ranked analysts by structural hole measure. Our results indicate that analysts who occupy more structural holes are more experienced. Moreover, analyst’s position in structural holes plays a more important role when analysts work at larger brokerages. As analysts working at small brokerages have to cover more firms out of necessity and have less supporting resources available, they will benefit less from their network positions. Ke and Yu (2006) report that corporate managers prefer to cooperate with analysts who work for larger brokerage firms, which make it much advantageous for those analysts working at large brokerages to access more information in the structural hole positions. Finally, our results indicate that analysts spanning more structural holes have better career outcome and are more likely to move to larger brokerages later in their career in the analyst profession.

¹ Alternatively, an analyst occupying the position of structural hole can simply benefit from the information she produces for her portfolio of covered firms. To account for such an effect, we control for the number of firms an analyst is providing coverage for in our regression analysis.
