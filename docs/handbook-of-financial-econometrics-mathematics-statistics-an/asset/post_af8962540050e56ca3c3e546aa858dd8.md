
There is a relationship between the estimated composite performance measures and the estimated risk proxies. However, the possible biases associated with estimated composite performance measures and their implications have not been carefully investigated.

The main purpose of this paper is to investigate the possible sources of the bias associated with the empirical relationship between the estimated Sharpe performance measure and its estimated risk proxy.^1^ It is shown that the sample size and the investment horizon are two important factors in determining the degree of empirical relationship between the estimated Sharpe measure and its estimated risk proxy. In addition, it is shown that the above-mentioned empirical relationships are generally not independent of the market conditions associated with the sample period selected for empirical studies.^2^ In the second section, the statistical relationship between the estimate of Sharpe’s measure and its estimated risk proxy is derived. It is found that the sample size and the market condition are two important factors in determining the empirical relationship between Sharpe’s measure and its risk proxy. The effect of this empirical relationship on the ranking of Sharpe’s performance measure is also explored. In the third section, the possible impact of the investment horizon on the bias associated with testing the theoretical relationship between the Sharpe measure and its risk proxy is developed. The implications of the *ex post* relationships between the estimated Sharpe performance measure and its risk proxy on portfolio management are also explored. Finally, the results of the paper are summarized.

## 69.2 Statistical Relationship Between the Estimated Sharpe Measure and its Risk Proxy

Following Sharpe (1966), the Sharpe performance measure can be defined as

$$SP = \frac{\bar{R}_i - R_f}{S_i},\tag{69.1}$$

where \(\bar{R}_i\) is the average rate of return on a portfolio (or an asset) \(i\), \(R_f\) is the risk-free rate of interest, and \(S_i\), the sample standard deviation of *ex post* data for portfolio \(i\), can be defined as

$$S_i = \left[\frac{\sum_{t=1}^n (R_{it} - \bar{R}_i)^2}{n}\right]^{1/2}.\tag{69.2}$$

Before deriving the empirical relationship between the estimated Sharpe’s measure and its risk proxy (\(S_i\)), the distributions of \(\bar{R}_i\) and \(S_i\) are first discussed briefly. If the rate of return of securities in the population is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\),³ then a set of sample rates of return for portfolio \(i\) can be considered as a random sample drawn from the normal population. Assume that the holding period coincides with the true investment horizon. The “investment horizon” concept will be explored in detail in section 69.3. It follows that the sampling distribution of the average rate of return, \(\bar{R}_i = \sum_{t=1}^n R_{it}/n\), is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2/n\), where \(n\) is the sample size. The random variable \(n S_i^2/\sigma^2\) has a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \((n - 1)\) degrees of freedom (see Hogg and Craig (1970)). For simplicity, \(\bar{R}\) and \(nS^2/\sigma^2\) denote \(\bar{R}_i\) and \(n S_i^2/\sigma^2\), respectively.

To investigate the relationship between the estimate of Sharpe’s measure and the estimated risk measure, the probability density function (p.d.f.) of the estimated risk (\(S\)) is derived first. Following Hogg and Craig (1970), the p.d.f. of the random variable \(n S^2/\sigma^2\) (denoted by \(y_1\)) can be defined as

$$f_1(y_1)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)2^{\frac{n-1}{2}}} y_1^{\left(\frac{n-1}{2}-1\right)} e^{-y_1/2}, & \text{for }0<y_1<\infty\\0, & \text{elsewhere}\end{cases}\tag{69.3}$$

Let \(y_2 = \sigma\sqrt{y_1}/\sqrt{n}\). Then \(y_2 = S\). The Jacobian of the transformation is \(dy_1/dy_2 = (2n/\sigma^2) y_2\). Thus, the p.d.f. of the estimated risk measure \(S\) is

$$f_2(y_2)=\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)2^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\left[\frac{n}{\sigma^2}y_2\right]^{\frac{n-1}{2}-1} e^{-\frac{n y_2^2}{2\sigma^2}}\cdot\frac{2n}{\sigma^2}y_2,$$

which can be simplified to

$$f_2(S) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\left(\dfrac{n}{\sigma^2}\right)^{1/2}}{\Gamma\left(\dfrac{n-1}{2}\right)2^{\frac{n-3}{2}}} \cdot \left(\dfrac{nS^2}{\sigma^2}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}-1} \cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{nS^2}{\sigma^2}\right)}, & 0 < S < \infty \\ 0, & \text{elsewhere} \end{cases}\tag{69.4}$$

Now, the covariance and the correlation coefficient between the estimated Sharpe measure and the estimated risk proxy are derived as follows:

- **(i)** The covariance is defined as

  $$\operatorname{Cov}\left(\frac{\bar{R} - R_f}{S}, S\right) = E(\bar{R} - R_f) \cdot \left[1 - E\left(\frac{1}{S}\right) E(S)\right] = d(R_f - \mu),\tag{69.5}$$

  where \(\mu = E(\bar{R}) =\) the expected market rate of return,

  $$d = E(S)E\left(\frac{1}{S}\right) - 1 = \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) \Gamma\left(\frac{n-2}{2}\right)}{\left[\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)\right]^2} - 1 > 0,\tag{69.6}$$

  $$E\left(\frac{1}{S}\right) = \left(\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}\right)^{1/2} \cdot \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-2}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)},$$
  $$E\left(\frac{1}{S^2}\right) = \left(\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}\right) \cdot \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-3}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)},\tag{69.7}$$

  and

  $$E(S) = \left(\frac{2\sigma^2}{n}\right)^{1/2} \frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{\Gamma((n-1)/2)}.\tag{69.8}$$

  The first equality of (69.5) is obtained by using the stochastic independence property between \((\bar{R} - R_f)\) and \(S\). The independence property can be justified methodologically. The joint p.d.f. of \((\bar{R} - R_f)\) and \(S\) can be derived, and it can be shown that the joint p.d.f. of \((\bar{R} - R_f)\) and \(S\) is the product of the marginal p.d.f. of \((\bar{R} - R_f)\) and the marginal p.d.f. of \(S.\)

- **(ii)** The correlation coefficient is defined as

  $$\rho\left(\frac{\bar{R} - R_f}{S}, S\right) = \frac{d(R_f - \mu)}{\sigma_{sp}\sigma_s},\tag{69.9}$$

  where

  $$\sigma_{sp}^2 = \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{\bar{R} - R_f}{S}\right)$$

  $$= \frac{\Gamma((n - 3)/2)}{2\Gamma((n - 1)/2)} + (\mu - R_f)^2\left(\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

  $$\times \left\{ \frac{\Gamma((n - 3)/2)}{\Gamma((n - 1)/2)} - \left[\frac{\Gamma((n - 2)/2)}{\Gamma((n - 1)/2)}\right]^2 \right\},\tag{69.10}$$

  $$\sigma_s^2 = \operatorname{Var}(S) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \left\{ (n - 1) - 2\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}\right]^2 \right\}.\tag{69.11}$$

Observing equations (69.5) and (69.9), one can see that the estimated Sharpe’s measure is uncorrelated with the estimated risk measure only when the risk-free rate of interest is equal to the mean rate, \(\mu\), of the return on the market portfolio or when the sample size \(n\) is infinite. The risk-free rate is generally not equal to the mean rate of return on the market portfolio and the sample size used in empirical work is generally finite. Therefore, the estimated Sharpe’s measure is in general correlated with the estimated risk measure (\(S\)). If the risk-free rate is less than the mean rate of return on the market portfolio, the estimated Sharpe’s measure is negatively correlated with the estimated risk measure. Conversely, the estimated Sharpe’s measure and the estimated risk measure will be positively correlated if the risk-free rate is greater than the mean rate of return on the market portfolio.^7^

The quantity \(d\) defined in (69.6) is a decreasing function of the sample size \(n\).^8^ For example, the value of \(d\) is 0.010 for a sample size of 50 and 0.005 for a sample size of 100. Thus, the covariance defined in (69.5) gets smaller as the sample size is increased. This implies that the bias (indicated by nonzero covariance) associated with the estimated Sharpe’s measure can be reduced by using a larger sample size. Hence, to reduce the bias in the empirical research, a large sample of the rates of return should be used to estimate the Sharpe measure.

**Notes**

1. The term “bias” used in this study refers to the deviation of the empirical relationship from the theoretical relationship. Theoretically, one-parameter performance measures are not expected to depend upon their risk proxies. However, it is empirically found that the estimated composite performance measures are generally highly correlated with their estimated risk proxies. To test the bias associated with the capital asset pricing theory, Black *et al.* (1972), Fama and MacBeth (1973) and others have conducted numerous empirical studies. Most recently, Roll (1977) has carefully re-examined these empirical tests.

2. Conceptually, the results for the Sharpe measure can also be applied to both the Treynor measure and the Jensen measure. The analytical results for both the Treynor and Jensen measures, without considering the problem associated with Roll’s (1977, 1978) critique of the CAPM, can be found in Chen and Lee (1979). These results were omitted based upon a suggestion by one of the referees.
