
Non-econometric evaluation shows that in 1993, the Israeli economy declined due to stock market bubble not terror attacks. And in the second half of the 1990s, the Israeli market became closely related to the United States of America (USA) because the USA is the largest trading partner of Israel. Thereafter, they used econometric analysis, and the Granger causality test confirmed positive correlation between S&P500 and TA100 indices. Central to the econometric analysis was the question “are all terror attacks alike?” for September 27, 2000, the Chow test confirmed structural breaks which can be interpreted “the general effect of the terror attacks on the stock market”. Further analysis illustrates that the Israeli economy declined due to deterioration of the economic environment. The terror attacks, especially Palestinian, had a permanent negative effect on the stock market but not on the foreign currency market. Moreover, terror attacks reduced firms’ expected profits. Finally, stock markets are efficient incorporating terror attack news.

Nikkinen and Vähämäa (2010) explored the impact of terrorism on stock market sentiments. Unlike prior studies, they focused on the behavior of implied option prices based on utility probability density functions. The latter principle will be adopted in hedging strategies in this chapter. To assess the impact of terrorism, Nikkinen and Vähämäa (2010) used implied density functions on the FTSE100 index around September 11, 2001 attacks in New York and Washington D.C., the March 11, 2004 attacks in Madrid and the July 7, 2005 attacks in London. The period of the data is from January 4, 2000 to December 30, 2005. The data covers 1,535 trading days. In order to reduce trading noise, firstly, they excluded options with less than five trading days, secondly, they used only at-the-money (ATM) and OTM options as in-the-money (ITM) options have the lowest liquidity. Just like Scott (1997), Nikkinen and Vähämäa (2010) agree that “noise” cannot be hedged.

For modeling, they used a discounted risk-neutral probability density function. Given that the equation in the latter statement is discrete, they transformed it into a continuous form by using a cubic spline. Moreover, the cubic spline is able to handle minimization problem. In presenting their results, firstly, they present basic descriptive statistics which illustrate that terrorist attacks are not remotely executed. Broadly, the implied FTSE option prices are negatively skewed with fat tails. The volatilities for those implied options soared. The probabilities were large and sharper for the key dates. The changes for the key dates (i.e., September 11, 2001, March 11, 2004 and July 7, 2005) were all negative and negatively skewed. To test the robustness of their results, Nikkinen and Vähämäa (2010) used a multiple regression, ARCH (0), ARCH (1) and GARCH (1). The results for all models on the FTSE100 were positive and statistically significant except for the attack day, which is negative and statistically significant. Thus, the attacks have adverse effects on implied option prices and the implied kurtosis tends to be revised upwards after the attacks.

## 34.2.2.3 *Dynamics on options*

Chiras and Manaster (1978) disentangled information content on option prices. The starting point is the BS model when dividends are accounted for as initiated by Robert Merton. Although in Merton (1976) dividends are continuous, that does not conform to the reality as dividends are discrete in nature. Chiras and Manaster (1978) argue that prior studies on option pricing used historical standard deviation of returns on stocks (SDHIST) with pricing options and that it is one of the shortcomings of prior studies. Moreover, they state that the difference of observed and calculated option would be reflected in the difference of SDHIST and aggregated market volatilities. Marcato *et al.* (2018) illustrated the same phenomenon. In order to overcome that shortcoming, they estimated implied standard deviations (ISDs). They specifically weighted the ISDs. Although weighting has its own advantages, weighted implied standard deviations (WISDs) tend not to converge. Marcato *et al.* (2018) showed that when there is an iteration process during the calculation of implied volatilities, those implied volatilities tend to converge. The advantage of ISDs is that they are based on current market prices; therefore, ISDs reflect the current volatilities in an ideal environment. With historical volatilities, under (over) pricing of options is very common (Chiras and Manaster, 1978). That mispricing leads to arbitrage opportunities.

The central hypothesis in Chiras and Manaster (1978) is that implied volatilities are a better predictor of future stock returns than historical volatilities. The data was from CBOE from 23 monthly observations period beginning June 1973 and ending April 1975. During 1973–1974 there was a stock market crash and Dow Jones Industrial Average lost over 45% of its share price although in most studies share prices changes are quoted as value. The dividends are converted into continuous form in order for them to be consistent with the BS model. Results of Chiras and Manaster (1978) illustrate that SDHISTs explained approximately 26% of stock returns while WISDs explain 32% of stock returns. Moreover, volatilities decrease over time. That is consistent with volatility theory. Thus, initially investors have little information about the firm and that time volatilities tend to be high but with time investors have more information about that firm; therefore, volatilities decrease. Still on volatilities, there are large discrepancies between ISDs and WISDs. This is expected as WISDs illustrated average volatilities while ISDs show volatilities at specific points in time. Chiras and Manaster (1978) did not confirm if the volatilities increases are statistically significant. A chi-square test would have confirmed whether the increase is statistically significant. Mutual correlation and auto-regression were ignored in this study. Some of the results were distorted, probably due to the effects of stock market crash.

Chiras and Manaster (1978) went further and investigated trading strategies during that particular period. And based on trading strategies, ISDs differed widely due to (i) non-simultaneous data, (ii) improper model specifications and market inefficiencies. On the other hand, market inefficiencies explain high arbitrage in this study. Moreover, they derived an equation that illustrated how many options should be “sold in order to offset an instantaneous change in stock price”. The trading Greeks were dynamic and changed constantly. Sebehela (2016) found similar results. Finally, information size has influenced the prices of options and related parameters.
