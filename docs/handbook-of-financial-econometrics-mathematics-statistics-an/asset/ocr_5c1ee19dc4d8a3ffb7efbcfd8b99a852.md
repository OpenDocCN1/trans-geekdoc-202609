Buzacott and Zhang (2004) incorporates asset-based financing into production decisions. The cash available in each period is modeled as a function of assets and liabilities that are periodically updated based on production planning. The importance of joint production and financing decisions is demonstrated in a start-up setting. Hu and Sobel (2007) studies a multi-echelon inventory model with the objective of optimizing the expected present value of dividends, and shows no optimal echelon base stock policy exists in the presence of financial constraints. Chao et al. (2008) studies a dynamic inventory control problem of a self-financing retailer, whose replenishment decisions are constrained by cash on hand. This paper finds the optimal inventory control policy, which maximizes the expected terminal wealth at the end of the planning horizon. Protopappa-Sieke and Seifert (2010) develops a model to determine the optimal purchasing order quantity under working capital restrictions and payment delays, and exhibits the interaction between payment delays, working capitals and the firm’s operational and financial costs. Chen and Wan (2011) investigates the impact of financing on the performance of a supply chain with one supplier and one budget-constrained retailer. The results show that credit financing can create value for the supply chain under study, and greater competition in financial markets leads to better financial performance. Gupta and Dutta (2011) formulates the problem of scheduling the payment day for each invoice, issued by upstream supply chain partners, as an integer programming model. Money received from downstream partners, but not used in payments, can be invested to earn interest. Heuristics are further developed to minimize the net present value of cash outflows. Luo and Shang (2015) studies supply chains owned by a single firm with two divisions, and develops an optimal joint inventory replenishment and cash retention policy. It further shows that in the presence of transaction costs, the firm may stock more inventory, even if holding costs increase.

When measuring the financial performance of supply chains, the CCC is a key metric that captures the efficacy of capital management (Farris and Hutchison, 2002), which is a strong indicator of companies’ success and profitability (Johnson and Soenen, 2003). In general, a firm with a shorter CCC is more efficient and profitable, everything else being the same. This result seems to hold worldwide and across small and large firms, even in emerging markets. To wit, such negative correlation between CCC and profitability is reported by empirical studies for a large cross-section of US firms (Jose et al., 1996), Belgian firms (Deloof, 2003), small and medium-sized Spanish firms (García-Teruel and Martínez-Solano, 2007), and Turkish merchandising and manufacturing companies (Uyar, 2009).
