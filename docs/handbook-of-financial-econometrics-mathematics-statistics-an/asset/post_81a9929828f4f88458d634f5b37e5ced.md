By applying the direct and reverse regression technique to test asset pricing models and other capital market research, one not only corrects for the EIV and measurement problem with observed asset returns, but also avoids grouping securities into portfolios which results in loss of information and data snooping biases.

## 75.3 Direct and Reverse Regression Technique

In a two-independent variable regression model of $Y$ on $X_1$ and $X_2$, three regression equations consisting of one direct and two reverse regressions (i.e., regressing each of the independent variables on the dependent variable and the other independent variable) are

$$Y = a_0 + a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + \varepsilon_t, \tag{75.1}$$

$$X_1 = b_0 + b_1Y + b_2X_2 + \mu_t, \tag{75.2}$$

$$X_2 = c_0 + c_1X_1 + c_2Y + \varsigma_t. \tag{75.3}$$

Reverse regression estimates are derived by solving equations (75.2) and (75.3) for the dependent variable $Y$ so that

$$Y = -b_0/b_1 + (1/b_1)X_1 - (b_2/b_1)X_2 - (1/b_1)\mu_t, \tag{75.4}$$

$$Y = -c_0/c_2 - (c_1/c_2)X_1 + (1/c_2)X_2 - (1/c_2)\varsigma_t. \tag{75.5}$$

The reverse regression estimates from equation (75.2) are $-b_0/b_1$ (intercept), $1/b_1$ for $X_1$, and $-b_2/b_1$ for $X_2$ and from equation (75.3) are $-c_0/c_2$ (intercept), $-c_1/c_2$ for $X_1$, and $1/c_2$ for $X_2$. The direct and reverse estimates together bound the true coefficients if certain conditions are met (Klepper and Leamer, 1984). If all the reverse regression estimates have the same sign as the direct regression estimate, they unconditionally bound the true estimate. The highest- and the lowest-valued estimates for each coefficient then provide the true coefficient’s maximum likelihood boundary.² On the other hand, if any of the reverse regression estimates has a sign different from that of the direct regression estimate, then not only is that coefficient estimate unbounded, but the coefficient estimates for all the variables are also unbounded (Cready et al., 2001).³ In such situations, one can make additional assumptions regarding measurement error to conditionally bound the coefficients. For example, if one is willing to assume that one of the variables is measured without error, then the direct and reverse regression estimates associated with that variable can be excluded from the set of estimates used to bound the coefficients for the other variables and sign differences in the set of coefficient estimates due to that variable (measured without error) would not be a source of unboundedness (Klepper and Leamer, 1984; Cready et al., 2001).

Klepper and Leamer (1984), however, point out that it is not necessary to assume that some of the explanatory variables are measured correctly in order to bound the set of maximum likelihood estimates. For the unbounded cases, they demonstrate that additional prior information about the seriousness of the measurement errors in the variables can be employed to bound the feasible region. Klepper and Leamer (1984) and Klepper (1988a, 1988b) developed a series of diagnostics to assess the sensitivity of regression coefficient estimates to measurement error in the regressors when the set of feasible values of the coefficients cannot initially be bounded. The diagnostics identify which measurement error variance(s) must be constrained, and by how much, to bound the set of feasible values of the coefficients to an acceptable range. Alternatively stated, the diagnostics indicate how prior constraints on the amount of measurement error in each regressor can be transformed into bounds on the true regression coefficients when all or some of the regressors are subject to the measurement error (Klepper, 1988a).

²Booth and Smith (1985) note that the conditions under which the estimated slope coefficients will consistently bound the true estimate can be easily applied, by extension, to bound the true intercept estimate. The importance of direct and reverse regression to bound the true intercept in capital market research cannot be emphasized enough since the intercept is often a measure of abnormal performance of a managed portfolio or pricing error in an asset pricing model.

³Cready et al. (2001) point out that unboundedness should not be interpreted as an indication of statistically insignificant coefficients. It simply implies that the procedure is unable to provide reliable inferences about coefficient signs and this may be attributed to the potentially severe effect of measurement error and EIV problem on the model estimation.

The diagnostics use two types of related information. One is bounds on the \(f_i\), the fraction of the variation in each of the regressors that is attributable to measurement error. The other is a bound on \(R_*^2\), the \(R^2\) of the direct regression if all the measurement error in the regressors were removed. The strategy of the diagnostics is to report measures that indicate how severe the bounds on the \(f_i\) and \(R_*^2\) need to be to bound the set of feasible values of the regression coefficients. In other words, one may place a limit on the relative magnitude of measurement error in a variable as a fraction of the overall error in the model and incorporate this information as a way of removing sign differences and bounding the coefficient estimates (Cready et al., 2001). This will allow the researcher to prioritize more serious measurement errors of one or more regressors and fix those before considering less serious measurement errors in other regressors. See Klepper (1988a) and Klepper et al. (1993) for details and illustrative examples.

## 75.4 Standard Error of Reverse Regression Estimates

We now derive the standard error of reverse regression estimates of the k-variable generalization of Klepper and Leamer (1984). The standard errors of direct regression estimates are readily available and can be used to test the statistical significance of these estimates. However, in order to test the statistical significance of reverse regression estimates, we need to derive their standard errors. Note that the derived reverse regression estimates are nonlinear functions of direct regression estimates of equations (75.2) and (75.3) and this causes some problem when estimating the standard error of reverse regression estimates.

Although there is no simple way of estimating the standard error of the reverse regression estimates from the standard error of direct regression estimates of equations (75.2) and (75.3), these standard errors can be obtained through approximation for a large sample size. Kmenta (1986) has suggested using an approximation formula in this case. The formula refers to the general case in which an estimator, say \(w\), is a function of \(k\) other estimators, \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k\); that is, \(w = f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k)\). The approximate variance of \(w\) can be obtained by using a Taylor series expansion for \(f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k)\) around \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k\), dropping terms of the order of two and higher, and then obtaining the variance by the standard formula. Following Klein (1953), the large sample variance of \(w\) can be approximated as

$$\operatorname{var}(w) \approx \sum_{i=1}^K \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^2 \operatorname{var}(x_i) + \sum_{i\neq j} \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\right) \operatorname{cov}(x_i, x_j), \tag{75.6}$$

where var is the variance and cov is the covariance.
