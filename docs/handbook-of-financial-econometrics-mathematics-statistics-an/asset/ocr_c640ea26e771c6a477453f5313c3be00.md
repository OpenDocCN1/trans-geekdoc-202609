This chapter focuses explicitly on the second vein of research because we feel that there have been some recent advances in how we account for aggregate IPO performance and the methods that we use to attempt to model aggregate-level IPO performance.

Historically, if we were to start at what some may call the beginning or pioneering studies that attempt to examine the cyclical nature of aggregate IPO performance, a good starting point would be Ibbotson and Jaffe (1975), in which the authors provide evidence of serial correlation in the residuals of IPO using correlational studies and the runs test. Ibbotson et al. (1975) found that there was a substantial correlation between the performance of the current month and subsequent month’s performance from 1960 to 1970. In addition, Ibbotson et al. (1975) used a regression framework and regressed volume of IPOs on the residual returns from the aggregate IPO performance adjusted for market performance and they identified that there was a strong relationship between the two variables but did not contend that it was causal due to the serial correlation issues. This set the stage for many researchers to question what the relationship between IPO performance and issuance was and what was causing both IPO performance and issuance.

Since this finding, research on IPO pricing and performance has become increasingly interesting and the influences on IPO pricing and performance through events or technological innovations have been noteworthy. For example, recently Liew and Wang (2016) examined the “tweet sentiment” from public Twitter accounts and found that this sentiment was indicative of IPO performance. Researchers such as Bajo et al. (2016) have shown that the role the lead IPO underwriter plays in the IPO process is significant and that the centrality of this lead underwriter in relation to other investment bankers affects the IPO’s offer price revisions, after-market valuations, initial returns, institutional holdings and analyst coverage, increases post-issue liquidity, and leads to better long-run performance. Similarly, Liu, Sherman and Zhang found that the media coverage of an IPO, pre-issuance, is positively related to the IPO’s long-term value, analysts’ coverage, and institutional investor ownership.
