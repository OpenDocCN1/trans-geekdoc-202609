Under the rent-seeking hypothesis, CEOs gain effective control of the pay-setting process so that they set their own pay with little oversight from shareholders and directors. The directors do not use the information in book-tax differences to undo the CEOs’ attempted earnings manipulation. Consequently, CEOs derive private benefits as their compensation is positively associated with book-tax differences.

We find that CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences. This provides evidence in support of the efficiency hypothesis: directors appear to use information in book-tax differences to set CEO compensation. This result is consistent across different compensation components such as salary, bonus and stock-based pay. We also investigate the implications of large book-tax differences on CEO compensation. Results indicate that CEO compensation is negatively associated with large positive book-tax differences and positively associated with large negative book-tax differences. This provides additional evidence supporting the efficiency hypothesis as the directors do not seem to reward CEO when firms have large positive book-tax differences (accounting income greater than taxable income). Furthermore, the directors appear to shield CEO compensation from the income-decreasing effect of large negative book-tax differences.

We also provide evidence that firms with strong corporate governance structure limit the CEO’s ability to capture the pay process. Specifically, we document that firms with high insider ownership and high proportion of outside directors on the board have lower CEO compensation when book-tax differences are large.

Our study contains several contributions. First, we contribute to the stream of research on using book-tax differences to infer earnings quality. While prior studies examine whether investors use the information in book-tax differences to assess earnings persistence, we provide evidence that the firm in general, and the board of directors in particular, use the information in book-tax differences to determine CEO compensation. Second, we complement prior studies that find directors shield CEO compensation from the negative consequences of income-decreasing activities (Dechow et al., 1994; Gaver and Gaver, 1998; Adut et al., 2003). Specifically, our results suggest that directors shield CEO compensation from the income-decreasing effect of large negative book-tax differences.
