
Domestic advisors can outperform their foreign counterparts. Hence, it remains a puzzle as to whether the role of financial advisors can influence deal outcomes in M&As. This study revisits this issue to examine the role of financial advisors in M&As. This study firstly examines what factors influence the choice of financial advisors for targets and bidders. Specifically, this study looks into what factors influence the choice of different types of financial advisors in terms of high and low-reputable financial advisors and domestic and foreign advisors. Secondly, this study explores the relationship between completion time and different types of financial advisors. Thirdly, the current study examines whether firms hired by high-reputable financial advisors can create higher value to their shareholders relative to those with low-reputable financial advisors and whether domestic advisors can outperform their foreign counterparts. This can shed lights on the importance of financial advisors in M&As.

Using the standard event study methodology with 333 targets and 949 bidders during the period of 1995–2011, the evidence reveals that firm size is an important determinant in the choice of financial advisors. When large firms involve themselves in M&As, firms are more likely to hire financial advisors. The evidence also shows that targets hired by low-reputable financial advisors spend more time in completing the deals. On the contrary, bidders hired by low-reputable financial advisors complete the deals faster than those with high-reputable financial advisors. In addition, bidders that hire domestic advisors can take longer time to complete the deals in comparison to those with foreign advisors. With regard to the impact of firm performance, the results reveal that targets hired by low-reputable financial advisors earn slightly higher announcement returns than those with high-reputable financial advisors. In addition, domestic advisors create higher gains to targets relative to foreign advisors. Taking into account the evidence for bidding firms, bidders hired by less prestigious financial advisors obtain higher gains around the announcement date relative to those with more prestigious counterparts. When looking at the post-announcement period, the results are reverse, showing that bidders hired by low-reputable financial advisors experience higher losses. Interestingly, I find that bidders hired by domestic advisors obtain higher announcement returns both around the announcement date and during the post-announcement period compared to those with foreign advisors. In the regression analysis, the results show that bidders obtain higher post-announcement returns when bidders hire domestic advisors.

This study contributes to the literature in several aspects. Firstly, the current study provides new evidence to determine the quality of financial advisors on the influence of deal outcomes in M&As. Secondly, the analysis takes into account the potential self-selection bias by controlling for inverse Mills ratio. This allows the current study to explore the relationship between announcement returns and the role of financial advisors precisely. More importantly, this study examines domestic and foreign advisors on the influence of deal outcomes in M&As. The empirical evidence indicates that domestic advisors can outperform their foreign counterparts leading to higher announcement returns to targets and bidders. This provides additional insights to reveal the importance of domestic and foreign advisors on M&As. Hence, the empirical evidence in this study enhances our understanding and knowledge to reveal the importance of the role of financial advisors on the influence of deal outcomes in M&As.

## 2.2 Literature Review

A number of prior studies have examined the performance of financial advisors in M&As (e.g., McLaughlin, 1992; Servaes and Zenner, 1996; Hunter and Jagtiani, 2003; Schiereck et al., 2009; Wang and Whyte, 2010; Golubov et al., 2012; Ma, 2013; Song et al., 2013). However, prior studies report inconclusive results and none of prior studies takes into account domestic and foreign advisors to examine the impact of deal outcomes in M&As. Servaes and Zenner (1996) argue that bidders are more likely to hire financial advisors when the transactions are more complex. Song et al. (2013) report that bidders are more likely to hire boutique advisors in hostile deals as hostile deals are more difficult to complete.

In addition, several studies have investigated the relationship between the quality of financial advisors and completion time in M&As. Hunter and Jagtiani (2003) and Walter et al. (2008) report that firms hired by more prestigious financial advisors can complete the deals faster as these advisors have more experience and expertise to advise the transactions. In a recent paper, Song et al. (2013) find that boutique advisors tend to take longer time to complete deals in that these advisors are more likely to be included in complex deals.

Furthermore, prior studies have examined the quality of financial advisors on firm performance in M&As. While financial advisors can assist targets to negotiate better terms during the process of the transactions, this can then create higher value to target shareholders. Studies, such as Walter et al. (2008) and Schiereck et al. (2009), document that targets hired by more prestigious financial advisors obtain lower returns. McLaughlin (1992) and Chahine and Ismail (2009) report that no significant relationship exists between the quality of financial advisor and target premia. On the contrary, Bowers and Miller (1990) report that targets gain more if either the target or bidder is advised by a first-tier advisor. Allen et al. (2004) find that targets gain more when targets employ their own banks as financial advisors. In a recent paper, Ma (2013) reports that targets obtain higher premiums and abnormal returns when targets hire top-tier investment banks.

With regard to the evidence for bidders, several studies have documented that bidders hired by first-tier financial advisors obtain lower returns (Bowers and Miller, 1990; McLaughlin, 1992; Servaes and Zenner, 1996; Rau and Rodgers, 2002; Hunter and Jagtiani, 2003; Allen et al., 2004; Walter et al., 2008). McLaughlin (1992) argues that high-reputable financial advisors tend to engage in more complex deals and then require higher premia. This may reduce the gains to acquiring firms. In a recent paper, Song et al. (2013) find that bidders obtain lower deal premiums when bidders hire boutique advisors. Chang et al. (2016) report that acquirers pay lower takeover premiums when acquirers employ targets’ ex-advisors. Chuang (2017) finds that bidders obtain higher announcement returns when hiring low-reputation financial advisors. However, Rau (2000) reports that bidders obtain higher gains when employing first-tier investment banks in tender offers. Golubov et al. (2012) similarly find that bidders in public acquisitions obtain higher gains when hiring top-tier advisors. Bi and Wang (2018) report that bidders that hire top-tier financial advisors can help improve the operating performance and reduce bid premiums. In sum, a number of existing literatures have examined the quality of financial advisors on the influence of deal outcomes in M&As. However, prior studies report mixed results and none of prior studies explores the impact of domestic and foreign advisors on deal outcomes in M&As. Thus, it is unknown as to whether firms hired by high-reputable financial advisors can create higher value to their shareholders and whether domestic advisors can outperform their foreign counterparts. As a result, this study revisits this issue to explore the role of financial advisors.
