We notice that, from Figure 36.15, the strongest wavelet correlation values are localized at a first time lag of zero and persist from the lowest time scale to an investment horizon of 64–128 days and with correlation values close to 1 for all investment horizons. This implies that the major world stock markets are almost perfectly integrated at a time lag equal to 0 and tend to move together. That can be interpreted as a result of growing economic and financial globalization in the world, which leads to reducing diversification opportunities between the global world stock markets and, consequently, the international diversification benefits suggested in the early works of Grubel (1968), Levy and Sarnat (1970), Solnik (1974) have largely disappeared in globalized world markets.

In addition, Figure 36.15 shows that France maximizes the multiple correlation against a linear combination of the rest of stock markets across all wavelet levels and, therefore, France has the biggest impact or is affected most by movements in other stock markets. This may be due to the fact that during the period under review, the French capital market has expanded with a dramatic increase in foreign investment flows as France is committed to encouraging foreign investments. In fact, investment regulation in France is simple and foreign investors are provided with a range of financial incentives. According to the “France investment climate statement, 2015” of the United States Department of State, France is one of the world’s leading destinations for foreign investments. The formal French investment regime is one of the least restrictive in the world, with no widespread screening of investments by non-French entities. Restrictions on foreign investments in France gradually disappeared in the 1990s. Since then, investment rules have been liberalized, firstly for business investments in the European Union, then for all foreign investments.
