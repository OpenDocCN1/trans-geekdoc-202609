
The reference point in the value function of prospect theory. It is often believed that what people really care about is not the absolute value of their consumption level, but the increase or decrease in their level of consumption. In other words, there exists a reference level of consumption in each consumer’s mind. In our model, the reference level is determined and updated based on the concept of habit formation. Following the concept in Abel (1990), habit formation updates the reference consumption level according to the aggregate consumption level per capita and the individual consumption level in the last period. Theoretically speaking, habit formation causes a time-varying risk aversion due to the change in consumption. This extra uncertainty could increase the equity premium. In addition, to be consistent with prospect theory, the ratio-form habit utility in Abel (1990) is replaced with the subtract-form habit utility in our model. Finally, note that when the power utility function is employed to implement the value function in prospect theory, the first derivative of this value function is not continuous and approaching infinity at the reference point. This non-differentiable issue at the reference point is a non-desired property for any asset pricing model. Therefore, we replace the power utility functions with exponential utility functions to construct our prospect utility function over consumption such that the first-order derivative of our prospect utility function is well-defined everywhere. Hence, we can readily apply it into the intertemporal consumption-based asset pricing model.

It is well known that Mehra and Prescott (1985) demonstrate that the traditional consumption-based asset pricing model with a proper risk aversion coefficient cannot generate a large enough equity premium. The observed real return in the US is 0.8% and the equity premium is 6%, but the largest premium that can be obtained with the traditional model is about 0.35%. This is the so-called equity premium puzzle.⁴ Since then, there have been many papers attempting to revise the consumption-based asset pricing model to solve this problem.

For instance, Constantinides (1990) applies habit formation in a production (rather than exchange) and continuous-time economy to solving the problem. In addition, Abel (1990) first introduces habit formation and “catching up with the Joneses” into the consumption-based asset pricing model to solve the equity premium puzzle. Adopting the parameter values as in Mehra and Prescott (1985), which used a two-point Markov process. Since the introduction of our model in Hung and Wang (2005), there are several papers trying to incorporate prospect theory (or merely loss aversion) into consumption-based asset pricing models. For example, Kőszegi and Rabin (2006) apply prospect theory to developing a reference-dependent, loss averse utility over consumption. They believe that the reference point that people use to compute gains and losses is their expectations. As a result, in addition to the traditional consumption utility stressed in economics, they incorporate another utility function from gains and losses compared with the expected consumption level, and this utility function also exhibits the characteristic of loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity proposed by prospect theory. In Kőszegi and Rabin (2007), they analyze risk attitudes of investors with such kind of utility function over consumption. Based on the same utility function, Kőszegi and Rabin (2009) derive the consumption plan associated with this utility function. However, Kőszegi and Rabin (2006, 2007, 2009) focus on theoretical analyses but do not provide any empirical evidence for the utility function they propose. Following this stream, Andries (2012) uses the expected consumption level as the reference point to construct a consumption utility, which exhibits loss aversion when future consumption levels are lower than their expected values. Different from prospect theory, investors described in Andries (2012) are always risk averse regardless of facing gains or losses in consumption. Similar to our results, she finds loss aversion enhances expected returns substantially and the asset pricing model with this utility shows superior performance in matching moments of asset prices.

## 48.2 Model Specifications and Asset Returns

### 48.2.1 *Our utility model*

We assume there exists a representative consumer in the economy, and he is with a time-additive utility function:

\( U_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \delta^j u(c_{t+j}, v_{t+j}) \), (48.1)

where δ is the discount factor, and in order to take prospect theory into account, the utility function \( u(c_t, v_t) \) is as follows:

\( u(c_t, v_t) = \begin{cases} 1 - e^{-\beta(c_t - v_t)} & \text{if } c_t - v_t \ge 0 \\ -\lambda[1 - e^{\beta/\lambda (c_t - v_t)}] & \text{if } c_t - v_t < 0 \end{cases} \) (48.2)

where β is the risk aversion coefficient. The consumption level at time t is c_t, and v_t is the consumption benchmark level at time t. When c_t - v_t ≥ 0, it means the consumer’s consumption level is better than the benchmark level, so we say he is in a “good state.” In this case, his utility is assumed to follow the exponential risk averse utility function 1 - e^{-β(c_t - v_t)}. Otherwise, when c_t - v_t < 0, the consumer’s consumption level is worse off than at the benchmark level. Under this scenario, we say he is in a “bad state,” and with the utility -λ[1 - e^{β/λ (c_t - v_t)}], where λ is the loss aversion coefficient and is set to be larger than 1. This means that when the representative consumer is in a bad state, he will become more sensitive to the relative consumption level than when he is in a good state. To deal with the determination and updating of the reference point, v_t, in prospect theory, the idea of habit formation is adopted. To include the features of habit formation and “catching up with the Joneses” into our model, the following form of the benchmark of consumption level v_t is adopted:

\( v_t = w c_{t-1} + (1 - w) \overline{C}_{t-1}, \quad 0 \le w \le 1. \) (48.3)

Note that v_t consists of the last period’s individual consumption level c_{t-1} and the last period’s aggregate consumption level per capita \overline{C}_{t-1}. The function of v_t in the utility function includes the concepts of both habit formation and “catching up with the Joneses.” When w = 1, v_t is influenced only by the last period’s individual consumption level, the utility function displays habit formation. Habit formation states that the utility level of the representative consumer depends only on the difference between today’s individual consumption and the consumption level in the last period. If w = 0, v_t only depends on the aggregate consumption level per capita in the last period. In this case, the utility function displays the phenomenon of “catching up with the Joneses,” which means that the utility level of the representative consumer is a function of the difference between today’s individual consumption and the aggregate consumption level per capita in the last period.

Relative to the original prospect theory, it should be noted in equation (48.2) of our model that when c_t − v_t < 0, we change the risk aversion coefficient from β to β/λ. Having made this small change, the first-order derivative of prospect utility function in our model will be continuous, which is as follows:

\( u'(c_t, v_t) = \begin{cases} \beta e^{-\beta(c_t - v_t)} & \text{if } c_t - v_t \ge 0 \\ \beta e^{(\beta/\lambda)(c_t - v_t)} & \text{if } c_t - v_t < 0 \end{cases} \)
