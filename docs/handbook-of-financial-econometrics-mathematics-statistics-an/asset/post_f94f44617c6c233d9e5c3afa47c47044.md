The interest rate for a deposit of one month was 5.14875% on that particular day. The only missing piece of information is the instantaneous variance of the stock price.

Several different techniques have been suggested for estimating the instantaneous variance. In this regard, the work of Latané and Rendleman (1976) must be mentioned; they derive standard deviations of continuous price-relative returns that are implied in actual call-option prices on the assumption that investors behave as if they price options according to the Black–Scholes model. In the example, the implicit variance is calculated by using the actual October 20, call price of an IBM option with an exercise price of $120 to solve for an estimate of the instantaneous variance. More specifically, a numerical search is used to approximate the standard deviation implied by the Black–Scholes formula with these parameters: stock price S = 116.67, exercise price X = 120, time to expiration T = 30/365 = 0.0822, riskless rate r = 0.0514875, and call-option price C = 2.00. The approximated implicit standard deviation is found to be σ = 23.82%. A simple spreadsheet like Excel for example can be used to calculate this “implied volatility”, using the “solver” or the “goal seek” tool. That way, one can simply write the Black–Scholes formula and set as “changing variable” the volatility, so that the “target variable” — the calculated option price — becomes equal to its market price.

After the clarifications are made, the example is this: given S = 116.67, X = 115, T = 0.0822, r = 0.0514875, and σ = 23.82%, we use equation (27.37) to compute C. Using equations (27.38) and (27.39) we calculate

$d_1 = \frac{\ln(116.67/115) + \left(0.0514875 + \frac{0.2382^2}{2}\right) \cdot 0.0822}{0.2382 \cdot \sqrt{0.0822}} = 0.30722,$

$d_2 = \frac{\ln(116.67/115) + \left(0.0514875 - \frac{0.2382^2}{2}\right) \cdot 0.0822}{0.2382 \cdot \sqrt{0.0822}} = 0.238922.$

Using the Excel function Normsdist ($d_i$) we can calculate the cumulative standardized normal distribution. Thus we calculate for the call option premium,

$C = 116.67 \cdot N(0.30722) - 115 \cdot \exp(-0.0514875 \cdot 0.0822) \cdot N(0.238922) = 4.34.$

The calculated call-option price of $4.34 is between the actual bid–offer for the call price of $4.30–$4.50 reported in Bloomberg.

This simple example shows how to use the Black–Scholes model to price a call option under the assumptions of the model. The example is presented for illustrative purposes only, and it relies heavily on the implicit estimate of the variance, its constancy over time, and all the remaining assumptions of the model. The appropriateness of estimating the implicit instantaneous variance is ultimately an empirical question, as is the entire Black-Scholes pricing formula. Boyer and Ananthanarayanan (1977) studied the implications of using an estimate of the variance in option-valuation models and showed that this procedure produces biased option values. However, the magnitude of this bias is not large. Chiras and Manaster (1978) compared implied volatilities from option prices with volatilities estimated from historical data and found the latter much better forecasts.

One additional remark must be made. The closeness in this example of the calculated call option price to the actual call price is not necessarily evidence of the validity of the Black–Scholes model. Earlier extensive empirical work that took place to investigate how market prices of call options compare with price predicted by Black-Scholes, showed positive evidence of the model’s robustness in the market (see MacBeth and Merville (1979) and Bhattacharya (1980)). For example, Chiras and Manaster (1978) also studied whether a strategy of buying low-volatility options and sell high volatility options produced excess returns. Their evidence was supportive of the Black-Scholes model.

However, the market crash of 1987 revealed its limitations in predicting abnormal market conditions. Indeed, the appearance of phenomena like the pronounced “volatility smile”,³ or daily price jumps that defied the assumption of a log-normal distribution for the asset returns, called for alternative models that explain in a more parsimonious and general way all market prices.

The search for these new models moved in the direction of the local volatility models (see for example Derman-Kani and Zhou (1996)), asset jump models (see Merton (1992)) or stochastic volatility models (Dupire (1994), Heston (1991)). Unfortunately, the larger scope of these models that is required in order to encompass for example the more general and more realistic — case of incomplete markets (see Pliska (1997), Britten-Jones and Neuberger (1996)) adds significantly to their complexity and thus obscure their intuitive content: instead of a single non-observable parameter — the volatility — on which the Black–Scholes model depends, these models require the estimation (“calibration”) of more parameters that relate to the price in usually untractable ways.

³Volatility smile is a pattern of consistently higher implied volatilities for strikes set far from the At-the-Money strike relative to the ATM implied volatility, that is, using the Black-Scholes equation in reverse, so as to recover the implied volatility from the market prices of options with different strikes, one would discover that each strike will yield a different number — see Das and Sundaram (1997) for an empirical study.

This fact alone, and considering the conceptual transparency and robustness of the Black–Scholes model, kept it to this day still very popular among practitioners in many option markets, especially in the more liquid ones that trade the simpler options in very large volumes. Traders that are aware of its limitations can be very often much more effective in dealing with their “out of model” unpredictable risks than the ones who can be lulled in false security behind a more sophisticated but much more obscure model. Taleb (1997) relates many cases that provide evidence of this fact.

Several textbooks such as Hull (2006), Chance (2006) and McDonald (2005) extend the discussion above of the Black–Scholes model and give numerous applications and examples.

## 27.7 Extending the Risk-Neutral Argument: The Martingale Approach

Following the works of Harrison–Kreps (1979), Harrison–Pliska (1981) and Cox–Ross (1976), a more general approach in pricing derivatives was devised than setting up each time a riskless replicating portfolio which we described in Section 27.6. Although the mathematics for the development of the Martingale method demand some basic knowledge of abstract measure theory that is outside the scope of this chapter, in this section we will attempt to give a simple and intuitive explanation of this approach.

We have already mentioned the concept of a Martingale process. Let’s assume that at every time t, we can collect the entire “flow of information” about the asset in question, that is, everything that is known up to time t. This flow of information is formally symbolized by the so-called filtration {F_t}_{t≥0}. A stochastic process X(t) is called a Martingale if it satisfies the relation

E[X(t)|F_s] = X(s). (27.63)

In other words, a process is a Martingale, if after collecting all the available information up to time s, the best forecast of the value of the process at any future time is no other than its current value. Of course, this fact is reminiscent of the main feature of the (weak form) of the efficient market hypothesis: past and present information is all included at the current price of an asset.

Now let's also assume that we have a stock whose price S(t) is the underlying of the derivative we wish to price. For simplicity, we let S(t) follow a Geometric Brownian motion described by the Itô stochastic differential equation

dS(t) = μ · S(t, w) · dt + σ · S(t, w) · dZ(t, w). (27.64)

Dividing by S(t, w) we get the elementary proportional change of its price equal to

dS(t, w)/S(t, w) ≈ d(ln S(t, w)) = μ · dt + σ · dZ(t, w). (27.65)

Assuming that we calculate its price in US dollars its expected proportional change (log-change) in price will be

E[ dS(t)/S(t, w) ] = μ · dt (27.66)

and its variance equal to

Var[ dS(t)/S(t, w) ] = σ² · dt. (27.67)

Also let's assume that we don't measure the stock price in current dollars, but we measure it relative to a tradable asset that grows deterministically in time. That could be for example a zero coupon bond that is traded today, it pays 1 US dollar at time T, has constant yield R and trades today at P(0, t).

Hence, instead of looking at the dollar stock price S(t) at time t we look at the normalized “stock per zero coupon bond price” S(t)/P(t,T), and the zero coupon bond is called the numeraire asset. The difference between measuring the stock price in zero coupon bonds instead of in dollars is the following: A dollar today is (almost certainly) more valuable than a dollar in the future time T, simply because they represent different cash flows in the time / cash flow two-dimensional space, in fact they are different assets. On the other hand, our zero coupon bond represents at all times until and including time T, the same claim at the same cash flow, that is at all times remains one single asset. Hence, in the first case we measure the same instrument relative to two different assets, whereas in the second case we measure it consistently relative to a single asset.
