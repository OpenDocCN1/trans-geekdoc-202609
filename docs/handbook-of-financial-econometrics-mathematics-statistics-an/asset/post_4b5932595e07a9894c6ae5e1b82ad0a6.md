## Abstract
This chapter presents the state of the art of the Intelligent Portfolio Theory which consists of three parts: the basic theory — principles and framework of intelligent portfolio management, the strength investing methodology as the driving engine, and the dynamic investability map in the confluence of business and market cycles and sector and location rotations. The theory is based on the tenet of “invest in trading” beyond “invest in assets”, distinguishing asset portfolio versus trading strategies and integrating them into a multi-asset portfolio which consists of many multi-strategy portfolios, one for each asset. The multi-asset portfolio is managed with an active portfolio management framework, where the asset allocation weights are dynamically estimated from a multi-factor model. The weighted investment on each single asset is then managed via a portfolio of trading strategies. Each trading strategy is itself a dynamically adapting trading agent with its own optimization mechanism. Strength investing as a methodology for asset selection with market timing focuses on dynamically tracing a small open cluster of assets which exhibit stronger trends and simultaneously follow trends of those assets, so to alleviate the drawbacks of single-asset trend following such as drawdown and stoploss. In the real world of global financial markets, the investability both in terms of asset selection and trade timing emerges in the confluence of business cycles and market cycles as well as the sector rotation for stock markets and location rotation for real estate markets.

## 43.1 Introduction
Along with the rapid development of information and communication technologies (ICT), the information infrastructure of the modern financial markets — stocks, bonds, futures and options — has made the financial markets of today far more efficient and complex than the age of industrial society about three decades ago where the fundamental cornerstones of the modern financial theory — portfolio theory, option pricing and arbitrage pricing — were established. The re-emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) with powerful capabilities of deep learning from big data has enabled individual and institutional participants of financial markets to use intelligent agents as new trading advisors or trading agents. This alone has an unprecedented implication to transform financial markets with new complexities and competitions. A serious consequence of the ICT and AI technological evolution is that the traditional financial theories, typically modern portfolio theory originated by Markowitz (1952) and followed up with many subsequent developments such as Capital Asset Pricing Models (CAPM), arbitrage pricing theory (APT), multi-period portfolio theories, active portfolio management, and so on, faces new challenges coming from more and more intelligent financial market participants and more and more complex market ecologies and dynamics.

Motivated by this kind of reality check and challenges both practical and theoretical, this author has proposed a basic theory of Intelligent Finance since 2004 (Pan, 2003–2016). As a cornerstone of intelligent finance, Pan (2015) outlined basic principles of an Intelligent Portfolio Theory including basic assumptions, principles, and framework of intelligent portfolio management. This chapter extends this theory with two more parts — strength investing as a methodology for asset selection, market timing and stronger trend following, and investability mapping in the confluence of business cycles and market cycles, as well as sector rotations and location rotations.

This chapter is organized as follows: Section 43.2 addresses outstanding problems with modern portfolio theory, pointing out the inevitable emergence of the Intelligent Portfolio Theory; Section 43.3 describes the basic principles and framework of the theory; Section 43.4 advances with strength investing as a new methodology for dynamic asset selection, concentrating investment on market strength by following assets with stronger trends; Section 43.5 outlines the investability mapping for asset selection and market timing in the confluence of business cycles and market cycles as well as sector and location rotation throughout these cycles. This chapter is theoretical and methodological in nature. However, pointers are given to many specialized developments in quantitative investment and trading, exhibiting the principles and technological possibilities of the proposed theory and methodology.

## 43.2 Evolution from Modern Portfolio Theory to Intelligent Portfolio Theory

### 43.2.1 Two outstanding problems with modern portfolio theory: Implementation and keeping pace with the times
Modern financial theory originates in western developed economies, typically the USA and the UK ones that have not been interrupted by the world wars throughout the last two centuries. The theory of financial investment with financial markets as the main objective of study has developed quantitative financial theories and models, of which the three most important fundamental theoretical innovations are the portfolio theory, the equilibrium pricing theory (the Capital Asset Pricing Model and the Arbitrage Pricing Model) and the option pricing models. Modern financial theory and its practice worldwide, both in developed and developing market economies, have all the way faced two difficult problems: the first is the problem of implementation of the theories, and the other is how the theories keep pace with the times.

This chapter studies the problem of the investment portfolio management and trading implementation in financial markets, the central theme is the modern portfolio theory. Obviously, implementation of portfolio management must be realized through trading. However, trading has always been the core problem of technical analysis and technical trading in financial industry, which is an area least attended and least studied by the financial academics. Naturally, in the last two decades, the financial academic community has started to re-examine technical analysis and technical trading from the perspective of behavioral finance, quantitative investment models and even the high-frequency trading systems, etc. We have realized that if a systematic scientific theory and models for technical trading is not well established, the portfolio theory cannot stand firmly up, or remains incomplete at least, or even it can be said the academic portfolio theory developed so far only addresses a small part of the financial investment problems.
