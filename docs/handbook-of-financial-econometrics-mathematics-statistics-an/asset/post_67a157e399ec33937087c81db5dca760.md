## 43.2.2 Inevitability of the intelligent portfolio theory to emerge

We think that the attribution of the application limitedness of modern portfolio theory to the implementation issues, a typical view of financial academics, corresponds only to the surface of the real problems. The missed essence of the problems is that the implementation issues of modern portfolio theory corresponds to a big problem domain, that is the theory, models and systems of technical trading. In this problem domain, by and large, the financial academics have had prejudice, are not good at, and are short of practical experience and systematic research. We believe a complete post-modern portfolio theory must comprise docking and integration of portfolio optimization and technical trading realization.

Most notably, after experiencing the recent global financial and economic crisis triggered by the US subprime mortgage crisis since 2008, it has become more and more pressing on modern financial theory to keep pace with the times. The main characteristics of the current times is that finance has entered into the era of Internet big data intelligent finance on the basis of computerization, explosive generation and accumulation of data, and the Internet going mobile and online. Consequently, the ecological environment and ecology itself of the financial markets worldwide have evolved accordingly. In stark contrast to the times of 1950s when the portfolio theory emerged and of 1970s when the option pricing models developed, today’s financial market participants have much more efficient information conditions, more comprehensive knowledge structure and more developed capabilities of investment gaming. Especially today, quantitative investment and intelligent trading systems have become an important type of market participants. It is reported that over 70% trading volume in the US stock markets is actually generated by program trading systems. The force of quantitative investment in China has also been in a quick growth in recent years.

Naturally, financial theory and technologies must keep pace with the times, i.e., evolving and advancing along with the changing and evolving financial systems and market ecology. We pay attention on the changes of the market ecology, which directly leads to the reflection and reconstruction of the fundamentals of financial theory such as the “adaptive market hypothesis” proposed by Lo (2004) and the “dynamic market hypothesis” proposed by Pan (2004, 2011), both hypotheses can be considered as deepening of or surpassing over the famous “efficient market hypothesis” of Fama (1965, 1970).

The necessity of keeping pace with the times can be clearly seen on modern portfolio theory. The mean-variance model of Markowitz is basically a static portfolio model. It was then further developed up to multi-period portfolio models which are a kind of mechanical dynamic portfolio models, and which are still to be lifted up to the level of adaptive intelligent dynamic portfolio models and systems, i.e., the “intelligent portfolio theory”. Even so, we recognize that the portfolio theory solely aiming at optimization of asset portfolio weights has its innate limitedness in its footing not yet on the real-time changing asset prices in financial markets. Here we believe that the complexity of financial market price dynamics has been severely overlooked if not underestimated deliberately. In the financial literature since Bachelier (1900), such complexity was simplified to stochastic process models, predominantly the random walk model (Brownian motion and geometric Brownian motion) and more sophisticated extensions such as Levy models with fat tails and jump-diffusion models for gapping or breaks in price process. Implementation of a portfolio in fact corresponds to technical trading in financial industry. No matter how precise, delicate or sophisticated the theoretical portfolio models are, the implementation still has to be realized with technical trading. If the complexity of trading is ignored, we are left with the simplest trading strategy — “Buy and Hold” (BoH) which is actually adopted with the classical static portfolio theory of Markowitz and with the multi-period dynamic portfolio models where in each period the assets are supposed to be bought with the allocated capital after portfolio weight optimization and then held until the end of the period. On the other hand, technical trading is an empirical science which has been formed along with empirical technical analysis widely practiced by market participants. Very useful technical trading strategies and systems, however, are treated as core technologies of institutional or private traders (such as Renaissance Technologies), so they are strictly protected, not directly available for academic studies. Due to their high economic value and market competitiveness, technical trading strategies and systems are rarely or incomprehensively reported in academic literature, thus it is difficult to form a scientific theoretical system of technical trading. With these considerations, we divide the complexity of portfolio management into two parts — the asset portfolio theory and the trading strategies, which are then integrated together into a unified framework of Intelligent Portfolio Theory (IPT). However, due to the foreseeable complexity of this theory, this chapter shall only focus on the principles and the general framework of intelligent portfolio management and trading, while more comprehensive mathematical and computational intelligence models, systems and testing with historical data are to be referred to other technical research papers. However, many published works by other researchers and developers in the literature correspond directly or indirectly quite well to various partial forms or implementations of this general theory.

## 43.2.3 A brief review on modern and post-modern portfolio theories

The notion of diversification in investment is long standing in financial industry. Empirical experiences in investment reveal that diversification of capital allocation on different assets can lower the fluctuation of the total equity, and thus reducing the investment risk but not necessarily lowering the investment return. This empirical insight was then reformulated into a quantitative portfolio theory by Markowitz in his PhD dissertation, first published in a paper in the Journal of Finance (1952), and then in a monograph book (1959). This work was then called the Mean–Variance Model (MVM) of the classical portfolio theory.
