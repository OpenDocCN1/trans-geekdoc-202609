
Empirically, Jensen (1968) specified a time series model as

R_{jt} - R_{Tt} = α_j + β_j(R_{mt} - R_{Tt}) + ε_{jt}, (62.2)

where α_j and β_j are assumed by Jensen to be time invariant, R_{Tt} is the rate of return for short-term Treasury Bills, and ε_{jt} is assumed to be N(0, σ_j^2) and to be identically and independently distributed over time (i.i.d.). Excess returns for the jth asset are also assumed to be proportional to excess return for the market portfolio at all times. These assumptions of intertemporal independence and the use of treasury bill rate as a proxy for R_f enable Jensen to use time series observations of R_{jt}, R_{mt}, and R_{Tt} to estimate α_j and β_j for different mutual funds. In addition, Jensen interpreted α_j as a measure of performance of the jth fund.

> “... if the model is valid, the particular nature of general economic conditions or the particular market conditions over the sample or evaluation time period has no effect whatsoever on the measure of performance...”

Jensen’s results have been criticized on the ground that the assumptions used to derive CAPM are not realistic and that errors of measurement in R_m and R_f can affect the results. Recently, Friend and Blume (1970) estimated α_j and β_j for many random portfolios. They then regressed the estimated α_j’s of different portfolios on the corresponding estimated β_j’s and found the regression coefficient b to be negative for the entire period 1960–1968 and also for the subperiod 1960–1964. They attributed these phenomena to the fact that one of the assumptions used in deriving CAPM — that investors can borrow at the riskless rate for infinite amounts — is violated in practice thus causing the estimated β_j’s to have a negative b. They further found that in the subperiod 1964–1968, b is positive. They attributed the latter phenomenon primarily to the unanticipated appreciation in price and not to the violation of the assumption on borrowing. Black, Jensen and Scholes (1972) and Miller and Scholes (1972) regressed average excess return on the estimated β_j’s and concluded that the second round regression coefficient is a downward biased estimator of the average excess market rate of return. BJS further attributed these phenomena to the fact that there exists no risk-free rate and advocated the use of zero beta factor.

## 62.2 Effects of Measurement Errors on α_j and β_j

In this chapter we will analyze equation (62.2), a form of CAPM used empirically by Jensen (1968), Friend and Blume (1970), and Miller and Scholes (1972). Using that to denote the sample estimate, the estimated regression line can be written as

$\widetilde{R}_{jt} - R_{Tt} = \widehat{\alpha}_j + \widehat{\beta}_j(\widetilde{R}_{mt} - R_{Tt}), \quad (62.3)$

where $R_{Tt}$ is used as a proxy for $R_{ft}$.

We will now derive the properties of $\widehat{\alpha}_j$ and $\widehat{\beta}_j$, when $R_{mt}$ and $R_{ft}$ are either with or without errors.

### Measurement Errors on $R_{ft}$:

Let us examine first the possible sources of measurement error on $R_{ft}$. As has been argued by Roll (1969) and Jen (1970), the treasury bill rate is only a proxy for the risk-free rate. We therefore postulate

$R_{ft} = R_{Tt} + \widetilde{U}_t, \quad (62.4)$

where $R_{Tt}$ is treasury bill rates used as a proxy for $R_{ft}$, $\widetilde{U}_t \sim N(0,\sigma_u^2)$ and is i.i.d.

In addition, it is well known that one of the unrealistic assumptions used to derive CAPM is that an investor can borrow freely at the riskless rate.

Allowing for the fact that the borrowing rate is higher than the riskless rate, Brennan (1971) showed that the relationship between return and systematic risk of a capital asset is still linear. He further showed that the only difference between the traditional CAPM and this version is to replace $R_f$ by $R_b$, the latter represents a weighted average of market’s lending and borrowing rate. After considering the traditional element of the borrowing rate, Brennan derived this new form of CAPM.

$E(R_j) - R_b = \beta'_j[E(R_m) - R_b]. \quad (62.5)$

Following (62.2) and (62.5), we obtain a new regression model as

$R_{jt} - R_{bt} = \alpha'_j + \beta'_j(R_{mt} - R_{bt}) + w_{jt}, \quad (62.6)$

It is clear that Jensen’s regression equation can be regarded as the correspondent of the form in (8) with error in the measurement of $R_b$. Further, the measurement error of $R_b$ is decomposed into the constant part, B, and the random part, $e_{bt}$.

Using standard econometric methods we can express $\widehat{\alpha}'_j$ and $\widehat{\beta}'_j$ as estimated parameters in Jensen’s equation in (62.3) when the true model should be that in (62.8) as

$$\widehat{\beta}'_j = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \{(r_{jt} - r_{bt} + e_{bt})(r_{mt} - r_{bt} + e_{bt})\} / \sum_{t=1}^{n} (r_{mt} - r_{bt} + e_{bt})^2, \tag{62.9a}$$

$$\widehat{\alpha}'_j = (\bar{R}_j - \bar{R}_b + B) - \widehat{\beta}'_j (\bar{R}_j - \bar{R}_b + B), \tag{62.9b}$$

where $r_{jt} = R_{jt} - \bar{R}_j$, $r_{mt} = R_{mt} - \bar{R}_m$, $r_{bt} = R_{bt} - \bar{R}_b$, and $\bar{R}_j$, $\bar{R}_m$ and $\bar{R}_b$ are arithmetic means of $R_{jt}$, $R_{mt}$ and $R_{bt}$, respectively. Following Johnson (1972) and Cramer (1946), the components of the sample estimates $\hat{\beta}'_j$ and $\hat{\alpha}'_j$ can be written as$^4$

$$\widehat{\beta}'_j = \beta'_j + \{(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_e^2)\} + s_j, \tag{62.10a}$$

$$\widehat{\alpha}'_j = \alpha'_j - \{(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_e^2) + s_j\} (\bar{R}_m - \bar{R}_b) - \{(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_e^2) + s_j + (\beta'_j - 1)\} B, \tag{62.10b}$$

where $\widehat{\beta}'_j$ and $\widehat{\alpha}'_j$ are the population coefficients under the specification of (62.6); $\beta_j$ and $\alpha_j$ are the population coefficients under the specification of (62.2). Further, $\beta'_j = \beta_j$ and $\alpha_j = \alpha'_j + (1 - \beta_j)B$. $\sigma_M^2$ is the variance of $(R_{mt} - R_{bt})$. $s_j$ is the sample variance of $\beta_j$ and is distributed with zero mean and finite variance $\sigma_s^2$.

Equation (62.10a) implies that the sample estimate of the systematic risk can be decomposed into the population value $\beta'_j$, sample variance $s_j$, and the bias caused by the measurement error of $R_b$, i.e., $(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_e^2)$. Similarly, as in equation (62.10b), the sample estimate of the Jensen performance measure $\alpha'_j$ can be decomposed into three components. The effects of the components other than $\alpha'_j$ and $\beta'_j$ are usually ignored. Thus, the magnitude of $\beta'_j$ will affect both the signs and the magnitude of $E(\hat{\beta}'_j)$ and $E(\alpha'_j)$. In general, $E(\hat{\beta}'_j)$ will be larger (or smaller) than $\beta'_j$ when $\beta'_j$ is smaller (or larger) than unity.

### Measurement Errors on R_mt:

We will now investigate the possible source of measurement error of R_mt. As Roll (1969) has pointed out, “... A New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) average measures R_mt with errors because it only includes a subset of the portfolio...” Indeed, conceptually, we can consider the “market” portfolio to consist of at least three types of assets, i.e., (1) equity assets, (2) real assets, and (3) debt assets. Equity assets further consist of equities listed on the NYSE and American Stock Exchange, and unlisted stocks. Hence, using the NYSE stock average only to measure the return of the entire market portfolio (R_mt) will induce error in measurement. To take the errors into account, we can postulate the following relationship

R_mt = R'_mt + τ + η_t, (62.11)

where R'_mt is the NYSE average used as a proxy for R_mt, the true rate of return of the market. τ and η_t are the constant and random measurement error of R_mt, respectively, and η_t is distributed with zero mean and finite variance σ_η².

Substituting (62.11) into (62.8), we have this theoretical form of CAPM

R_jt - (R_Tt + B + e_bt) = α''_j + β''_j[(R'_mt + τ + η_t) - (R_Tt + B + e_bt)] + w_jt, (62.12)

where (e_bt - η_t) ~ N(0, σ_e² + σ_η²)

w_jt ~ N(0,σ_w²), E(e_bt,η_t)=0

e_bt and η_t are independent of the true value R_jt, R_mt, and R_bt, and α'_j = α''_j and β_j = β'_j = β''_j.

Equation (62.12) is the most general form of the one-factor CAPM. Since we can generally only observe R_Tt and R'_mt instead of R_bt and R_mt, we can decompose β̂''_j and α̂''_j as β̂''_j = β''_j + ψ_j + S_j, (62.13a) α̂''_j = α''_j - (ψ_j + S_j)(R̄_m - R̄_b) - {ψ_j + S_j + β_j}(τ - B) + B, (62.13b) where ψ_j = (1 - β''_j)(σ_e² + σ_η²)/(σ_M² + σ_e² + σ_η²).
