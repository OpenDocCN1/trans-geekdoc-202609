## 47.6 Summary and Concluding Comments

I examine a subset of the infinite number of SMLs that ensure the market portfolio is MV efficient both at a point in time and over time. The SMLs are chosen either to be consistent with economic arguments or to minimize a variety of distances between the sample means and sets of SML means at a point in time. Focusing on whether the market is MV efficient and on raw rather than excess returns provides insights, both with respect to the form of the tests and results not readily available otherwise.

The GRS multivariate test is based on excess returns where the intercept of the SML is equal to zero and the slope is the average excess return on the market. I focus on the corresponding SML in raw return form where the intercept is the average risk-free rate and slope is the average excess return on the market. In this case, the Hotelling's T² test employed in this chapter is more directly related to the efficient set mathematics. The difference in the squared Sharpe ratios is more transparent. And the market portfolio is guaranteed to be on the minimum-variance frontier.

Consistent with other parts of the CAPM literature, some results provide strong support for the CAPM. Others provide strong evidence against it. An infinite number of SMLs at a point in time would seem to make it more likely that the CAPM holds. Yet, with some specifications of the SML, the risk-free rate exceeds the market portfolio's equilibrium mean, which is clearly inconsistent with CAPM theory. Moreover, while statistical tests are the gold standard in empirical work, they are far from fool proof. Somewhat paradoxically, in high interest rate periods, statistical tests may not reject SMLs, even though other MV criteria may indicate they are not economically reasonable. Conversely, in low interest rate periods, statistical tests may reject the SMLs even though other MV criteria may indicate they are economically reasonable.

As with the traditional SML tests, the results are sensitive to the way securities are grouped into portfolios — the size and book-to-market dataset provides strong statistical evidence against the CAPM, while the size dataset provides strong support for it. Finally, the results are sensitive to estimates of sample means, CAPM means, and betas based on a fixed 120-month or an expanding window.
