This paper makes four contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the stream of research on using book-tax differences to infer earnings quality (Clemons and Shevlin, 2016; Blaylock et al., 2017). While prior studies examine whether investors use the information in book-tax differences to assess earnings persistence, we focus on whether the firm in general, and the board of directors in particular, use the information in book-tax differences to determine CEO compensation. Understanding the linkage between CEO compensation and book-tax differences is important because there is increasing criticism that CEOs personally enrich themselves by opportunistically managing accounting income upwards to increase their own compensation at the expense of shareholders. Second, our study also complements prior research that find compensation committees do intervene in setting executive compensation (Dechow et al., 1994; Gaver and Gaver, 1998; Duru et al., 2002; Adut et al., 2003). This stream of research documents that compensation committees shield executive compensation from the adverse effect of current period earnings of potentially firm-value-enhancing activities such as corporate restructurings and research and development. Our finding that CEO compensation is positively associated with large negative book-tax differences suggests that compensation committees shield CEO compensation from the effect of income-decreasing book-tax differences. Moreover, our result that CEO compensation is negatively associated with large positive book-tax differences suggests that compensation committees are not narrowly fixated on income-increasing book-tax differences. Third, we extend Core et al. (1999) finding that firms with excessive CEO compensation have weak corporate governance structure. Our result that strong corporate governance structure strengthens the negative relation between CEO compensation and book-tax differences suggests that firms with strong corporate governance are more likely to use the information in book-tax differences to mitigate excessive CEO compensation. Finally, we also contribute to the body of research on the determinants of compensating CEOs using pre-versus after-tax earnings (Newman, 1989; Atwood et al., 1998; Dhaliwal et al., 2000; Phillips et al., 2003). These studies find that firms with greater tax planning opportunities are more likely to use after-tax performance measures. We extend this body of research by examining the association between CEO compensation and book-tax differences. We show that different compensation components (such as salary, cash compensation and stock-based compensation) are negatively associated with book-tax differences.
