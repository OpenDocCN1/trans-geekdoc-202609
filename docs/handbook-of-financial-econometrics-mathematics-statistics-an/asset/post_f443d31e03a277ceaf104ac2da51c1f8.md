
Recall that we consider a stock to move from \(S\) to \(uS\) with probability \(p\) and to \(dS\) with probability \(1-p\). The mean and variance of the continuously compounded rate of return for this stock are \(\tilde{\mu}_p\) and \(\tilde{\sigma}_p^2\), where

\[
\tilde{\mu}_p=p\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)+\log d \quad\text{and}\quad \tilde{\sigma}_p^2=\left[\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)\right]^2p(1-p). \tag{102.32}
\]

From equation (102.25) and the definitions for \(\tilde{\mu}_p\) and \(\tilde{\sigma}_p^2\), we have

\[
\frac{j-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}=\frac{\log\left(\frac{S^*}{S}\right)-\tilde{\mu}_p n}{\tilde{\sigma}_p\sqrt{n}}. \tag{102.33}
\]

Also, from the binomial option pricing formula we have

\[
a-1=\frac{\log\left(\frac{X}{Sd^n}\right)}{\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)}-\varepsilon=\frac{\log\frac{X}{S}-n\log d}{\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)}-\varepsilon. \tag{102.34}
\]

where \(\varepsilon\) is a real number between 0 and 1.

From the definitions of \(\tilde{\mu}_p\) and \(\tilde{\sigma}_p^2\), it is easy to show that

\[
\frac{a-1-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}=\frac{\log\left(\frac{X}{S}\right)-\tilde{\mu}_p n-\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)}{\tilde{\sigma}_p\sqrt{n}}. \tag{102.35}
\]

Thus from equation (102.31) we have

\[
1-B_2(a;n,p)=\Pr\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{S^*}{S}\right)-\tilde{\mu}_p n}{\tilde{\sigma}_p\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{\log\frac{X}{S}-\tilde{\mu}_p n-\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)}{\tilde{\sigma}_p\sqrt{n}}\right). \tag{102.36}
\]

We have checked the condition given by equation (102.29) in order to apply the central limit theorem. In addition, we have to evaluate \(\tilde{\mu}_p n\), \(\tilde{\sigma}_p^2 n\), and \(\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)\) as \(n\to\infty\). \(\tilde{\mu}_p n\to\left(\log r-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t\), which can be derived from the property of the lognormal distribution that \(\log E(S^*/S)=\mu_p t+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2t\), and \(E(S^*/S)=[pu+(1-p)d]^n=\hat{r}^n=r^t\). It is also clear that \(n\tilde{\sigma}_p^2\to\sigma^2t\) and \(\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)\to0\).

Hence, in order to evaluate the asymptotic probability in equation (102.30), we have

\[
\frac{\log\left(\frac{X}{S}\right)-\tilde{\mu}_p n-\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)}{\tilde{\sigma}_p\sqrt{n}}\to z=\frac{\log\left(\frac{X}{S}\right)-\left(\log r-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}. \tag{102.37}
\]

Using the fact that \(1-N(z)=N(-z)\), we have, as \(n\to\infty\), \(B_2(a;n,p)\to N(-z)=N(x-\sigma\sqrt{t})\), where \(x=\frac{\log(X/S)-rt}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{t}\). A similar argument holds for \(B_1(a;n,p')\), and hence we completed the proof that the binomial option pricing formula as given in equation (102.23) includes the Black–Scholes option pricing formula as a limiting case.

Lyapunov’s condition requires that \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) are independent and uniformly bounded with \(E(X_i)=0\), \(Y_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n\), and \(s^2=E(Y_n^2)=\mathrm{Var}(Y_n)\). However, rates of return are generally not independent over time and not necessarily uniformly bounded by the condition required. This is the potential limitation of the proof by Cox et al. (1979). We found that the derivation methods proposed by Rendleman and Bartter (1979), which will be discussed in the next section, are not so restrictive as the proof discussed in this section.

### 102.4.2 Rendleman and Bartter method

In Rendleman and Bartter (1979), a stock price can either advance or decline during the next period. Let \(H_T^+\) and \(H_T^-\) represent the returns per dollar invested in the stock if the price rises (the \(+\) state) or falls (the \(-\) state), respectively, from time \(T-1\) to time \(T\) (maturity of the option). Let \(V_T^+\) and \(V_T^-\) be the corresponding end-of-period values of the option.

Let \(R\) be the riskless interest rate. They showed that the price of the option can be represented in a recursive form as:

\[
W_{T-1}=\frac{W_T^+(1+R-H_T^-)+W_T^-(H_T^+-1-R)}{(H_T^+-H_T^-)(1+R)}. \tag{102.38}
\]

Equation (102.38) can be applied at any time \(T-1\) to determine the price of the option as a function of its value at time \(T\).⁷ By using recursive substitution as discussed in Section 102.2.1, they derived the binomial option pricing model as defined in equation (102.39)⁸:

\[
W_0=S_0 B_1(a;T,\varpi)-\frac{X}{(1+R)^T}B_2(a;T,\varphi). \tag{102.39}
\]

where the pseudo probabilities \(\varpi\) and \(\varphi\) are defined as

\[
\varpi=\frac{(1+R-H^-)H^+}{(1+R)(H^+-H^-)}, \tag{102.40}
\]

\[
\varphi=\frac{1+R-H^-}{H^+-H^-}. \tag{102.41}
\]

⁷Note that notation \(T\) used here is the number of periods rather than calendar time.  
⁸Note that some of the variables used in this section are different from those used in Section 102.2.1 and Section 102.4.1.

Note that \(\varphi\) and \(\varpi\) are identical to \(p\) and \(p'\), respectively, which are defined as \(p=\frac{r-d}{u-d}\) and \(p'\equiv\frac{u}{r}p\) in Section 102.2.1.

\(a\) denotes the minimum integer value of \(i\) for which \(S_0H^{+i}H^{-T-i}>X\) will be satisfied. This value is given by⁹

\[
a=1+\mathrm{INT}\left[\frac{\ln(X/S_0)-T\ln(H^-)}{\ln H^+-\ln H^-}\right], \tag{102.42}
\]

where \(\mathrm{INT}[\cdot]\) is the integer operator.

\(B_1(a;T,\varpi)\) and \(B_2(a;T,\varphi)\) are the cumulative binomial probabilities. The number of successes will fall between \(a\) and \(T\) after \(T\) trials, and \(\varpi\) and \(\varphi\) represent the probability associated with a success after one trial.

In each period, the stock price rises with probability \(\theta\). We assume the distribution of returns, which is generated after \(T\) periods, will follow a log-binomial distribution. Then the mean of the stock price return is

\[
\mu=T[h^+\theta+h^-(1-\theta)]=T[(h^+-h^-)\theta+h^-], \tag{102.43}
\]

and the variance of stock price return is

\[
\sigma^2=T(h^+-h^-)^2\theta(1-\theta), \tag{102.44}
\]

where

\[
\theta=\text{probability that the price of the stock will rise}, \quad h^+=\ln(H^+), \quad h^-=\ln(H^-). \tag{102.45}
\]

Note that in Cox et al. (1979), they assume a log-binomial distribution with mean \(\mu t\), and variance \(\sigma^2 t\). Apparently, Rendleman and Bartter (1979) assumed that \(t=1\). Therefore, the Black–Scholes model derived by them is not exactly identical to the original Black–Scholes model. The implied values of \(H^+\) and \(H^-\) are then determined by solving equations (102.42)–(102.45), shown as equations (102.46) and (102.47), respectively.

\[
H^+=\exp\left(\frac{\mu}{T}+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}\right), \tag{102.46}
\]

\[
H^-=\exp\left(\frac{\mu}{T}-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}\right). \tag{102.47}
\]

As \(T\) becomes larger, the cumulative binomial density function can be approximated by the cumulative normal density function. When \(T\to\infty\), the approximation will be exact, and equation (102.38) evolves to equation (102.48).

\[
W_0\sim S_0 N(Z_1,Z_1')-\frac{X}{(1+R)^T}N(Z_2,Z_2'). \tag{102.48}
\]

In this equation, \(N(Z,Z')\) is the probability that a random variable from a standard normal distribution will take on values between a lower limit \(Z\) and an upper limit \(Z'\). According to the property of the binomial probability distribution function, we have

\[
Z_1=\frac{a-T\varpi}{\sqrt{T\varpi(1-\varpi)}}, \quad Z_1'=\frac{T-T\varpi}{\sqrt{T\varpi(1-\varpi)}}, \quad Z_2=\frac{a-T\varphi}{\sqrt{T\varphi(1-\varphi)}}, \quad Z_2'=\frac{T-T\varphi}{\sqrt{T\varphi(1-\varphi)}}.
\]

Thus, the price of the option when the two-state process evolves continuously is presented as

\[
W_0=S_0 N\left(\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_1,\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_1'\right)-\frac{X}{\lim_{T\to\infty}(1+R)^T}N\left(\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_2,\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_2'\right).
\]

Let \(1+R=e^{r/T}\) reflect the continuous compounding of interest, then \(\lim_{T\to\infty}(1+R)^T=e^r\). It is obvious that \(\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_1'=\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_2'=\infty\), therefore, all that needs to be determined is \(\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_1\) and \(\lim_{T\to\infty}Z_2\) in the derivation of the two-state model under a continuous time case.
