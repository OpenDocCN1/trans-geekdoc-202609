- If no state transition occurs in the system or a demand arrives and is not filled, then the state of the system does not change.
- Whenever a demand arrives and is filled, the inventory size is decreased by one PEMU and one PEMU is added to the receivables pool.
- Whenever a product unit arrives as replenishment, the inventory size is increased by one PEMU, and the available funds are decreased by one PEMU.
- Whenever a receivable is paid up, the receivables pool is decreased by one PEMU and the available funds are increased by one PEMU.

In view of equation (46.5), we readily conclude that the bivariate process { (I(t),C(t)) : t ≥ 0 } is an equivalent Markovian cash conversion process with a finite state space of integer pairs of the form (n,k), since the state of the receivables pool, j = G + H − n − k, is determined by each such pair. However, the trivariate process { (I(t),C(t),R(t)) : t ≥ 0 } is more transparent, and will be the preferred one in this chapter. Accordingly, the state space of the trivariate process is

𝒮 = { (n,k,j) : 0 ≤ n ≤ S, 0 ≤ k ≤ G + H − n, j = G + H − n − k }, (46.6)

and its transition rates are given by

q((n,k,j), (n−1,k,j+1)) = 1̄_{0}(n) 1̄_{G+H}(j) λ,
q((n,k,j), (n+1,k−1,j)) = 1̄_{S}(n) 1̄_{0}(k) L_{n,k} μ,
q((n,k,j), (n,k+1,j−1)) = 1̄_{G+H}(k) 1̄_{0}(j) j ν. (46.7)

In equation (46.7), the first line corresponds to a demand arrival at the inventory causing a PEMU to transition from the inventory to the receivables pool, provided the inventory is not empty and the receivables pool is not full; the second line corresponds to a replenishment arrival at the inventory causing a PEMU to transition from the available funds pool to the inventory, provided the available funds pool is not empty and the inventory is not full; and the third line corresponds to the arrival of a pending payment (receivable) at the available funds pool causing a PEMU to transition from the receivables pool to the available funds pool, provided the available funds pool is not full and the receivables pool is not empty. All other transition rates equal 0.

### Corollary 46.1:

Given rate parameters λ, μ, ν > 0, the equilibrium probability vector π depends only on the inventory base stock level, S, and the firm's available funds maximum, A = G + H, and not on the individual values of the capital investment G and credit limit H.

### Proof.

The state space of {(I(t),C(t),R(t)) : t ≥ 0} is determined by S and A. The corollary follows since for any fixed λ, μ, ν, S, and A, the transition rate matrix remains unchanged regardless of the values of G and H, as long as their sum is fixed.

Next, the balance equations corresponding to equation (46.7) in the unknown equilibrium probability vector π = [π(n,k,j)], (n,k,j) ∈ S, can be written as the following system of equations:

+ 1̄_{0}(k)1̄_{G+H}(j)π(n,k−1,j+1)(j+1)ν  (c)
=  (46.8)
1̄_{0}(n)1̄_{G+H}(j)π(n,k,j)λ  (d)
+ 1̄_{S}(n)1̄_{0}(k)π(n,k,j)L_{n,k}μ  (e)

Here, line (a), (b) and (c) correspond to transitions into state (n,k,j) ∈ S due to demand arrival at the inventory, replenishment arrival at the inventory and receivable arrival at the available cash pool, respectively, while lines (d), (e) and (f) correspond to transitions out of state (n,k,j) ∈ S due to the same events, respectively.

### Lemma 46.1:

Equation (46.8) has a unique solution, which is the equilibrium probability pmf of the Markov process {(I(t),C(t),R(t)) : t ≥ 0}.

### Proof.

The equilibrium solution is unique because all states (n,k,j) ∈ S are (1) irreducible, namely, all states communicate, and (2) positive recurrent and non-null since the state space is finite. It follows that there exists a unique equilibrium pmf.

## 46.4.3 Cash conversion model with S < G + H

For the generalized case of cash conversion model with S < G + H, the solution of the balance equations (46.8) provides the equilibrium pmf of the system. However, solving these equations analytically is much harder compared to the balance equations (46.10) for the case S = G + H, since for S < G + H, L_{n,k} = min{S − n, k} depends on both n and k. Consequently, we resort to a numerical solution approach. More specifically, we will use equation (46.4) to approximate the jump chain’s equilibrium pmf π^J, and then derive an approximation for the requisite equilibrium pmf π using the approximation of π^J. To this end, we first prove some properties of the jump chain corresponding to the cash conversion process.

### Lemma 46.3: The jump chain of the Markovian cash conversion process {(I(t),C(t),R(t)) : t ≥ 0} is irreducible, positive recurrent, non-null and periodic with period 3.

Proof. See Chen et al. (2017, proof of Lemma 2).

### Theorem 46.2: The Markov process {(I(t),C(t),R(t)) : t ≥ 0} has a unique equilibrium probability vector π, given by

π(s) = π^J(s) / [ q(s) Σ_{u∈S} π^J(u)/q(u) ],  s ∈ S,  (46.13)

where the equilibrium probability vector π^J of the corresponding jump chain is unique, and for any probability vector ψ over the state space S, π^J is given by

π^J = ψ (P + P^2 + P^3)/3 lim_{n→∞} (P^3)^n .  (46.14)

Proof. The existence and uniqueness of π and π^J follow from Lemma 46.3. Equation (46.13) follows readily from equation (46.3), while equation (46.14) follows from equation (46.4) as a special case for period K = 3.

Theorem 46.2 provides the basis for a numerical solution of equation (46.10) as described in Algorithm 46.1.

### Algorithm 46.1: (Numerical Solution of the Balance Equations of the Cash Conversion Process).

- 1. Construct the transition matrix Q over the state space S of {(I(t),C(t),R(t)) : t ≥ 0}.
- 2. From Q construct the transition matrix P of equation (46.2) of the associated jump chain.
- 3. Set an initial probability vector ψ over S, and compute the probability vector π₀ᴶ = ψ (P+P²+P³)/3.
- 4. The computation proceeds recursively. At step n, compute πₙᴶ = πₙ₋₁ᴶ P³.
- 5. The computation stops when two successive vectors, πₖ₋₁ᴶ and πₖᴶ are “sufficiently close” in some sense, yielding the approximation π̂ᴶ = πₖᴶ for the equilibrium pmf πᴶ of the jump chain.
- 6. Compute an approximate equilibrium pmf π̂ of the equilibrium pmf π by substituting π̂ᴶ into equation (46.13).

A number of implementation details for Algorithm 46.1 are worth mentioning: (1) the transition matrix Q is typically a sparse matrix, and thus the implementation can use sparse matrix techniques; (2) in trying out initial probability vectors in Step 3 of Algorithm 46.1, we settled on ψ = (1,0,...,0) which had the best computational performance; (3) the stopping criterion in Algorithm 46.1 was to stop when the maximum norm of πₖᴶ − πₖ₋₁ᴶ dropped below 10⁻⁶ for the first time.

## 46.5 Cash Conversion Cycle

In our model, the CCC is the sojourn time of a PEMU from the moment it transitions from the available funds node to the inventory node up until the same PEMU transitions from the receivables node back to the available funds node. Thus, the CCC is the duration random variable T_C, defined by

T_C = T_I + T_R,  (46.15)

where T_I is the random sojourn time of the PEMU in the inventory node and T_R is the random sojourn time of the PEMU in the receivables node. We assume that PEMUs are queued FIFO in the inventory node and move sequentially in that order to the receivables node, while in the receivables node, PEMUs move independently in parallel. We now proceed to obtain the equilibrium densities f_{T_I} and f_{T_R}, and then derive the statistics of T_C.

To compute f_{T_I}, we need to derive the equilibrium pmf of the embedded inventory size just after a replenishment. In our case, the cash conversion process {(I(t),C(t),R(t)) : t ≥ 0} is embedded at the occurrence times {τ_i^Θ : i ≥ 1} of transitions of the form (u,s) = ((n−1,k+1,j),(n,k,j)). Accordingly, define the corresponding transition set by
