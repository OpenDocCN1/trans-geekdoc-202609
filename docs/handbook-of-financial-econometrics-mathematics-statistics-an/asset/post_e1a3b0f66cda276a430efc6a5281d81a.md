Bates (1996) developed a model to price American forex options on stochastic volatility/jumps diffusion processes under systematic jump and volatility risk. The options analyzed in Bates (1996) are Deutsche Mark (DM) options over the period from January 1984 to June 1991, on page 79 from Philadelphia Stock Exchange (PHLX). He argues that prior studies on forex options show that there are conditional and unconditional moments. Moreover, ‘unconditional distribution of log-differenced exchange rates is leptokurtic’. And when the relationship is inverse; normally there is excess kurtosis.

He proposes a stochastic volatility/jump-diffusion model. In that model, skewness is due to correlations between exchange rate or volatility shocks or due to non-zero average jumps. For kurtosis, it arises either from volatile volatility or substantial jump component. The stochastic model in Bates (1996) has square root variance and Bates (1996) state that the advantages of the square root variance are (i) it allows for systematic volatility risk, and (ii) the process generates analytical solution for pricing forex options. He went further and presented terminal payoffs under the risk-neutral probability measure and a call option which follows a constant-volatility geometric BM for the exchange rate. The latter model is similar to the model derived in this chapter and it will be shown later in the chapter. From the date of 62500 Deutsche Marks, the final data consisted of 19689 transactions (11952 calls; 7737 puts). For estimate, some parameters he used unconstrained implicit parameters which include (i) BS model for implicit volatility for all maturities on a given day, (ii) deterministic volatility model (DV) for daily downward or upward sloping term structure of implicit volatilities, (iii) deterministic volatility/jump-diffusion model (DVJD) for implicit skewness and excess kurtosis inversely related to option maturity, (iv) stochastic volatility model (SV) skewness and excess kurtosis directly related to option maturity, and (v) stochastic volatility/jump-diffusion model (SVJD) which allows mixed maturity effects on skewness and excess kurtosis. The first three models are ad hoc and/or non-equilibrium models according to Bates (1996). The non-general equilibrium models are not grounded based on the theory; therefore, their results should be read in a cautious manner. Further, Bates (1996) state that in non-equilibrium models imperfect synchronization with appropriate underlying futures suggests that there is independent heteroskedastic residuals.

The results illustrate that when DM model is relaxed in relation to BS model, term structure flattens. Bates (1996: 85) stated that “the tendency for the term structure of implicit volatilities to be upward (downward) sloping for low (high) short-term implicit volatilities was qualitatively consistent with the postulated volatility mean reversion”. In the presence of skewness and/or leptokurtic distributions DVJD, SV and SVJD “specifications further improved the model’s ability to fit option prices”. Based on non-nested DVJD and SV models, stochastic volatility models exemplify a “strong inverse relationship characteristic of jump models”. Conditional standard errors are relatively large. ## 34.2.3 Hedging in incomplete markets

Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) priced volatility smiles pre- and post-1987 stock market crash. They opined that deterministic volatility models accommodated flexible volatility surface and no additional risk factors are introduced. On one hand, that trait of deterministic volatility is suitable for hedging but when there are jumps deterministic volatility models might be unsuitable for dynamic hedging. Prior studies on hedging argue that the BS model should be relaxed such that it is suitable for hedging in different scenarios. Buraschi and Jackwerth’s (2001) study focus loosely on two approaches; (i) deterministic volatility and (ii) stochastic models. This chapter combines traits from two approaches. The elegance of the latter approach is that it is flexible and dynamic, moreover, it can be used for hedging of most securities. On deterministic volatilities, Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) state that they are advantageous for the following reasons; (i) market dynamism is complete in these models, (ii) practically, they capture time-varying volatility and correlation between parameters and/or assets, and (iii) are easy to fit smiles and to calibrate deterministic volatilities.

In modeling volatilities, first Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) present martingale situation for option prices. Thus, they allow for no-arbitrage environment. The objective is to meet a world represented by a probability space. In defining no-arbitrage environment, they state that it implies law of one price and positive kernels exist. The latter phenomenon leads to a log-normal process. The log-normal process (ξ_T) has a mean of zero and variance equal to μ². Then, kernel-replicating portfolio

is made up of underlying asset and cash account B_t, with B_{t+Δt} = e^{rΔt} B_t. R*_{t+Δt} is the two-dimensional vector of log returns that constitutes the basis. Another advantage of the kernel-replicating portfolio is that it has a conditional expectation. The advantage of a conditional expectation on one hand, it is an expected value and on the other hand, it is probabilistic nature (see Sebehela, 2015a). Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) argue that when a jump is priced in the kernel-replicating portfolio, that jump would be an additional risk factor. This chapter shows that besides the fact that the jump is an additional risk factor, the jump is a risk premium which increases the overall return of an option.

The data used in Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) is Berkeley Options Database containing all minute by minute European option quotes and trades on the S&P 500 index traded on CBOE from April 2, 1986 to December 29, 1995. The goal of the authors was to identify (i) index, (ii) risk-free interest rate, (iii) several options with different strike price/index level ratios (moneyness) and (iv) constant maturity. The index is typically used by traders as a benchmark, interest rate is one key input for option pricing, and options with different maturities are used for extrapolating deterministic and stochastic volatilities. While for arbitrage violations, they excluded any options more than 200 cents for options and 20 cents for futures. Moreover, they restrict to options which are not more than 4% away from-the-money. The findings are as follows. First, options show linear pattern except for ones that are deep-out-of-the-money. They argue that it is because deep-out-of-the-money are very liquid.
