More important than examining (and controlling for) the impact of the number of banking relationships on firm performance, we are really interested in examining whether there is any differential impact on performance due to the use of domestic bank debt relative to foreign bank debt. If the more sophisticated foreign banks possess superior credit screening and monitoring skills, and lend primarily on hard information rather than the soft information that is often relied up by domestic banks, then the market might find some valuable information in the fact that a firm was able to secure foreign bank debt. Thus, we expect a positive relationship between the use of foreign bank debt and firm performance.

We are also interested in measuring the impact on firm performance of the concentration of a firm’s debt that comes from any given bank on average. Sharpe (1990), Rajan (1992), or Greenbaum et al. (1989) argue that there are “hold up” costs that can occur when too much of a firm’s debt comes from one lender; specially when the concentration comes from lender having a long relationship with the firm. These hold up costs are value-destroying quasi-rents that the lender extracts from the borrower. We try to capture the concentration of bank lending with the variable CONCENT, the ratio of bank debt over total assets, divided by the number of banking relationships. Our expectation is that there will be a negative relationship between the concentration of a firm’s debt that comes from any given bank and the firm’s performance.

As reported in Table 25.1, 84% of domestic Taiwanese bank loans are collateralized. Collateral provisions are used extensively in emerging markets, and they are prevalent even in developed markets. Avery et al. (1998) document that, in the US, ~90% of small business loans (in dollar value) are secured by collateral (see 1998). Berger and Udell (1998) report that 92% of bank loans to US small businesses are backed by collateral, and they suggest that collateral is used to help reduce credit risk and increase the efficiency of the loan. As large as these ratios are in the developed world, these ratios approach 100% in emerging markets. Because of this, emerging market firms have very little power to differentiate themselves based upon their willingness to offer collateral to local banks.
