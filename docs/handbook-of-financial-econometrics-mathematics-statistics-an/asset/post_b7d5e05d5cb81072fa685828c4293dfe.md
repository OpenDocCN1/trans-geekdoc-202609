## 23.4 Methodology

In the analysis of time-series, the issue of stationarity is crucial in order to make statistical inference. However, most macroeconomic time-series display non-stationarity in their behavior. The traditional approaches to remove the non-stationarity were usually based on deterministic functions of time or alternatively, taking first (or second) differences or a combination of the two. In fact, differencing (with or without deterministic components) became a standard practice, especially after the seminal paper by Nelson and Plosser (1982). In that paper, these authors found that, using tests of Fuller (1976) and Dickey and Fuller (1979) all except one of the 14 US macroeconomic series examined contained a unit root. Later on, many other more powerful test statistics were developed for the analysis of unit roots (Phillips and Perron, 1988; Elliott et al., 1996; Ng and Perron, 2001; etc.), including in some cases one or more structural breaks (Zivot and Andrews, 1992; Perron, 1997; Lumsdaine and Papell, 1997; Lee and Strazicich, 2003; etc). Within this context, it became common practice to differentiate series with unit roots in order to render stationarity. The natural generalization of the unit root models to the multivariate case is through the concept of cointegration, initially developed by Engle and Granger (1987) and later extended by Johansen (1991, 1995) and others.

In this paper we make use of some of the above approaches along with other more recent developments in time-series analysis that permit us to consider non-integer degrees of differentiation. Thus, the above models become simply particular cases of other more general specifications, where the number of differences required to render a series stationary I(0) is not an integer (usually 1) but a fractional value. In such a case, we say that the time-series is fractionally integrated or I(d) where the differencing parameter d can be any real value.¹

Fractional integration processes were introduced by Granger (1980), Granger and Joyeux (1980) and Hosking (1981) showing that many economic series present spectral density functions with a large value at the zero frequency, suggesting that differentiation is required. However, taking first differences, the spectrum displays a value close to zero at the zero frequency indicating overdifferentation.

A process $x_t$ is said to be I(d) if it can be represented as

$(1 - L)^d x_t = u_t, \quad t = 0, \pm1, \dots \tag{23.1}$

with $x_t = 0$ for $t \le 0$, and $d > 0$, where $L$ is the lag-operator ($L x_t = x_{t-1}$) and $u_t$ is $I(0)$ defined, for the purpose of the present work, as a covariance stationary process with spectral density function that is positive and finite. In the case where the parameter $d$ is not an integer, the polynomial in the left hand side in (23.1) can be expanded in terms of its binomial expansion, such that, for all real $d$:

$(1 - L)^d = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j L^j = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \binom{d}{j} (-1)^j L^j = 1 - dL + \frac{d(d - 1)}{2} L^2 - \dots ,$

and thus

$(1 - L)^d x_t = x_t - d x_{t-1} + \frac{d(d - 1)}{2} x_{t-2} - \dots .$

In this context, $d$ plays a crucial role as an indicator of the degree of dependence in the series. Thus, the higher the value of $d$, the higher the level of association is between the observations. Processes with $d > 0$ in (23.1) display the property of long memory, so-named because of the strong degree of association between observations distant in time. These processes are also characterized by autocorrelations decaying hyperbolically at a slow rate and with a spectral density function unbounded at the origin.² If d = 0, the series is integrated of order 0, I(0). If 0 < d < 0.5, the series exhibits long memory and stationarity, if d ≥ 0.5, the series is then non-stationary³ and if d = 1 the series is integrated of order 1 or I(1). It is important to note that if the time-series is I(d) with d < 1, the series is “mean-reverting”, in the sense that shocks affecting the series will disappear in the long run. On the contrary, if the series is I(d) with d ≥ 1 it is not mean reverting and shocks could eventually remain in the series forever.

Robinson (1978) and Granger (1980) justified the use of these models based on the aggregation of heterogeneous autoregressive (AR) processes. See also Chambers (1998). Gil-Alana and Hualde (2009) present an updated review of fractional integration and its applications in economic time-series.

Non-stationary in the sense that the variance of the partial sums increases with d.

Several methods exist for estimating and testing the fractional differencing parameter d. Some are parametric while others are semiparametric and can be specified in the time or in the frequency domain. In this paper, we use first a parametric frequency domain Whittle estimation approach (Dahlhaus, 1989) along with a testing procedure (Robinson, 1994), which is based on the Lagrange Multiplier (LM) principle and that also uses the Whittle function in the frequency domain. This latter method has the advantage that it remains valid even in non-stationary contexts (i.e., d ≥ 0.5) and its functional form is presented in Appendix A. In addition we also conduct the analysis with a semiparametric method, where no functional form is imposed on the I(0) error term. Here we use a local Whittle estimate in the frequency domain, based on a band of frequencies that degenerates to zero. This method (Robinson, 1995a) is described in detail in Appendix B.

### 23.4.1 Fractional cointegration

The multivariate extension of the above I(d) model refers to the concept of fractional cointegration. In a broad sense, given two real numbers d, b, the components of the vector zt are said to be cointegrated of order d, b, denoted zt ~ CI(d,b) if:

- (i) all the components of zt are I(d),
- (ii) there exists a vector α ≠ 0 such that st = α'zt ~ I(γ) = I(d-b), b > 0.

Here, α and s_t are called the cointegrating vector and error, respectively. Here we conduct the following strategy: We first estimate individually the orders of integration of the series using, in addition to the previous methods, the log-periodogram-type of estimator as devised by Robinson (1995b), Kim and Phillips (1999, 2006), Velasco (1999b) and others.⁴ Next, we test the homogeneity of the orders of integration in the bivariate systems (i.e., H₀: d_x = d_y), where d_x and d_y are now the orders of integration of the two individual series, by using an adaptation of Robinson and Yajima (2002) statistic to log-periodogram estimation. The functional form of the test statistic can be found in Gil-Alana and Hualde (2009). In the final step, we perform the Hausman test for no cointegration of Marinucci and Robinson (2001) comparing the estimate d̂_x of d_x with the more efficient bivariate one of Robinson (1995b), which uses the information that d_x = d_y = d_* (see Appendix C).

These methods are generalizations of the one proposed earlier by Geweke and Porter-Hudak (GPH, 1983) and based on log-periodogram regressions.

We carried out the mentioned cointegration analysis since most of the trade carried out by the members of the ASEAN is performed within the boundaries of this community. We believe that further studies should be made with the aim of finding similar relationships among the ASEAN+3 countries, which are the 10 member countries from the ASEAN Economic Community plus China, Japan and South Korea.
