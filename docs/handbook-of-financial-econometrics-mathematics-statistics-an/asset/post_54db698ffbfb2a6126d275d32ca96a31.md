This field of study attracts more and more researchers. It has led to an increasing number of studies and published empirical studies. Scholars have examined different factors and IT adoption theories affecting mobile banking adoption commonly. However, most studies are based on three main adoption theories: Technology Acceptance Model (Koenig-Lewis et al., 2010; Mortimer et al., 2015; Koksal, 2016), Innovation Diffusion Theory (Chen, 2013; Koksal, 2016; Laukkanen, 2016), Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (Zhou et al., 2010; Baptista and Oliveira, 2015).

Actually, some factors that drive mobile banking adoption are more widespread in the literature than others. Ha et al. (2012) found perceived usefulness, risk, compatibility and cost as the most occurring ones in empirical studies. Later, Shaikh and Karjaluoto (2015) reported that the most frequent factors were perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness followed by trust, social influence, perceived risk and self-efficacy. In the network of constructs and their relationships that are discussed in mobile banking studies, the most important constructs included intention, use, perceived ease of use, perceived risk, perceived usefulness, social influence, trust (Baptista and Oliveira, 2016).

The extant study has tried to explain mobile banking adoption using these theories, but the forms of mobile banking are changing. However, “virtually no studies address the use of m-banking applications via smartphones or tablets or consider the consequences of such usage” (Shaikh and Karjaluoto, 2015, p. 129). Later, Munoz-Leiva et al. (2017) who studied the determinants of the intention to use m-banking apps in Spain argued that this area is totally underexplored and only a scarce number of relevant papers are available (Hsu and Lin, 2016; Hew et al., 2015) existed. In turn, we try to fill this gap in the literature by focusing on mobile banking via special apps for smartphones and tablets in the case of an emerging market (Russia). This market is characterized by the lack of quick and easy access to banking services by half of customers, so mobile users have a great potential to increase the level of financial inclusion (Plaksenkov and Korovkin, 2015).

## 71.2 Literature Review

The term “mobile banking” could be interpreted in different ways. It is often used to describe different kinds of interactions between banks and customers via mobile phones. For example, mobile phones could be used to make calls to call-centers or personal consultants so that they could be a part of telephone banking. One of the oldest types of m-banking which certainly requires mobile phones is based on the use of short message services (SMS). The development of technology has allowed banks to develop separate applications for smartphones and tablets (Shaikh and Karjaluoto, 2015). Actually, it would be suitable to study smartphone and tablet banking separately in countries with mature mobile commerce. However, in Russia, only a few of the largest banks offer special apps for tablets while most of tablet owners just use the same apps as smartphone owners do (NBJ, 2015).

As the main conceptual framework, the lion’s share of m-banking studies considers IT adoption theories. Actually, the three most widespread IT adoption theories are Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT), Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Unified Theory of the Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). In practice, these models are empirically tested with the main dependent variable which reflects the intention to use mobile banking rather than real usage. It is one of the biggest challenges of such models because even high intention to use does not guarantee the real usage of the technology. However, such a simplification is widely used because of the difficulties in measuring real usage. Moreover, as shown in Baptista and Oliveira (2016), the intention to use is the best predictor of the real usage, so such simplification appears not to cause a significant bias.

Chronologically, IDT could be considered as the first adoption theory. Rogers initially developed this theory in 1962 and has since been amended by him. According to IDT, the intention to use mobile banking arises mainly from five factors or innovation attributes: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, observability and trialability (Rogers, 2003).

Another model, TAM, includes two key factors, “perceived usefulness” and “perceived ease of use”, which influence the intention to use mobile banking; another intermediary variable is “attitude” (Davis, 1989, Davis et al., 1989). However, in empirical explorations, this intermediary variable is often dropped from TAM for simplification. Actually, this model was criticized for simplicity, and TAM was extended in 2000 by adding some determinants of “perceived usefulness” like “subjective norms” or “job relevance”, and this new model was called TAM2 (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000). Moreover, Venkatesh and Bala built TAM3 model in 2008 which also included some constructs that influenced another core factor: “ease of use” as “computer anxiety” or “perceived enjoyment”.

An alternative model, UTAUT, was developed in 2003 as an attempt to create the most powerful IT adoption model by the integration of eight models, including TAM and IDT. This model operates on four factors: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence and facilitating conditions (Venkatesh et al., 2003). In 2012, Venkatesh et al. modified this model by adding three new constructs: hedonic motivation, price value and habit. The researchers also held only three of the four moderating variables: age, gender and experience. This model was called UTAUT2.

Actually, this tends to build the extensions of well-known models (TAM2, TAM3 and UTAUT2) that originated from empirical studies. A wide stream of the literature shows that integrated and extended models seem to be more powerful than basic ones. Thus, all three core basic models (TAM, IDT, UTAUT) were found to be more powerful while combining extra factors or integrated with each other.

For example, Luarn and Lin (2005) extended TAM by adding three new factors, found all additional factors significant and reported a higher explanatory power of the extended model. Later, Wang, Luarn and Lin (2006) respecified and validated this model. Their results supported the previous findings. In addition, new relationships between the existing chains were also found. They reported the significance of security and privacy for mobile banking, and found that knowledge and financial resources were also important. Later, Gu et al. (2009) added new variables to the integrated model, such as determinants of the two core factors of TAM, and trust as the third core construct. Most of the new factors were significant in the model, and the risk-side factor or trust also seemed to be important as an incentive to use mobile banking.

Recently, Hanafizadeh et al. (2014) tested a model with six factors that are not covered in TAM. The empirical data supported all their hypotheses. The authors not only showed the influence of core factors (perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use) on the intention to use mobile banking but also the importance of other factors: social factors such as the need for personal interactions, or economic factors such as the perceived risk.

IDT-based models were also reported to achieve better estimations when such models were extended with new factors or combinations of factors.
