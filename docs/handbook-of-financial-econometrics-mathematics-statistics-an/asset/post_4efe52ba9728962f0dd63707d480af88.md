LOGNB is negative for all but Q5 and is monotonically decreasing across all the Q quantiles. It is statistically significant for Q50, Q75 and Q95. This result suggests that there may be a value-destroying free-rider monitoring problem as the number of banking relationships increases. The problem is especially visible among High Q firms.

Moving to the control variables, the results are as expected. The coefficient on AGE is negative, significant, and monotonically decreasing in Q. The older firms in our sample tend to underperform their younger counterparts. Size, as proxied for by the log of total assets, has a statistically positive but economically weak impact on value for Q25, but has a substantial economic impact in quantile 95. For the highest Q firms, a larger asset base is associated with better performance. As expected, the coefficient on CAPEX/TA and R&D expenditure are positive, significant, and monotonically increasing in Q. The coefficient on the ratio of fixed assets to total assets is everywhere negative, statistically significant, and monotonically decreasing in Q. The coefficient on the dividends to total asset ratio is positive and significant for all quantiles except the 75th and 95th. It is negative and significant for the 95th quantile. Lastly, the coefficient on the managerial ownership percentage is positive and significant for every quantile except Q5, and its square is everywhere negative but only significant for Q25 and Q50.

Table 25.5 demonstrates that Tobin’s Q is concave and monotonically decreasing in the leverage ratio. For Q5, which are the low growth firms, the coefficient on the leverage ratio is positive and significant, but it quickly turns negative for Q25, and then negative and significant for all the other quantiles. The relationship in Q95 is particularly economically important. The market seems to reward low growth firms that use more debt in their capital structure, but punish severely the most highly growing ones. Similarly, the coefficient on the ratio of public debt to total debt is also concave and monotonically decreasing by quantile. It is positive and significant for Q5 and Q25, not significant for Q50, and negative and significant for Q75 and Q95.

We find convincing evidence that foreign bank monitoring adds value to shareholders. The coefficient on the ratio of foreign bank debt to total debt is everywhere positive, monotonically increasing, and is significant for all quantiles except for the extreme quantiles, Q5 and Q95. It seems that foreign bank debt — controlling for the existence of local, public and non-public debt — is performance enhancing.

Lastly, the coefficient on domestic bank collateral is not significantly different from zero for every quantile except for Q95. This implies that the use of collateral for domestic bank debt carries with it little information about the firm’s performance, except for the highest growth firms. This is not surprising since collateral is nearly universally required by domestic Taiwanese banks. The opposite, however, is true for the coefficient on foreign bank collateral. Here the coefficient is positive and significant for every quantile except Q95. High quality Taiwanese firms use collateral for their foreign bank borrowing as a way to signal their quality.

## 25.5 The Factors Associated with the Use of Different Types of Debt

We next examine the factors that impact the firm’s decision to use different kinds of debt in its capital structure: domestic bank debt, public debt, non-bank private debt and foreign bank debt. The dependent variables are the percentage of each type of debt as a fraction of the total debt held by the firms in our sample. Since the sum of all proportions of the different kinds of debt must sum to one, we employ a seemingly unrelated regression analysis. To this end, we specify the system of equation (25.2) as

$$\%\text{ each type of debt}_{it}=\alpha_0+\alpha_1AGE_{it}+\alpha_2LOGTA_{it}+\alpha_3LEVERAGE_{it}$$
$$\quad+\alpha_4ROA_{it}+\alpha_5FA/TA_{it}+\alpha_6GROWTH_{it}$$
$$\quad+\alpha_7CF/TA_{it}+\alpha_8NWC/TA_{it}+\alpha_9CAPEX/TA_{it}$$
$$\quad+\alpha_{10}RD/SALES_{it}+\alpha_{11}EBITDA_{it}$$
$$\quad+\alpha_{12}DIV/TA_{it}+\alpha_{13}COLLATDB_{it}$$
$$\quad+\alpha_{14}COLLATFB_{it}+\varepsilon_{it}, \tag{25.2}$$

where $\sum_{i=1}^{4}\text{ Proportion Of Each Type Of Debt}_{it}=1$

Table 25.6 presents the results of the seemingly unrelated regression analyses for each of the proportions. There are substantial differences. There is a positive association between age and the propensity for the firm to use a greater proportion of public debt; however, the opposite is true for the proportion of domestic debt, and there seems to be little impact on either non-bank private debt or foreign bank debt. Larger firms tend to use both more public debt and foreign bank debt, but tend to employ a smaller percentage of domestic bank debt. High leverage ratio firms have a greater tendency to use more public debt and non-bank debt, but have a lower tendency to use both domestic and foreign debt. Firms that have a lot of fixed assets have a greater tendency to use local bank debt and a lower tendency to use public debt. Firms that have a greater percentage of net working capital to total assets tend to use less local and foreign bank debt but more public debt. Firms with more earnings volatility tend to use a greater percentage of local bank debt, but a lesser percentage of public debt and foreign bank debt.
