The purpose of the diversification optimization is to determine the set of capital allocation weights on assets. There are two alternative objective functions for this optimization: to maximize the expected return of the portfolio given a maximum level of risk, or to minimize the portfolio risk given a minimum level of expected return. The optimization problem has a closed-form analytical solution, corresponding to a hyperbola the upper part of which is called the efficient frontier where each point corresponds to an efficient portfolio, i.e., an optimal portfolio with the maximum expected return for a given level of risk. According to today’s knowledge and literature, the limitations of Markowitz classical portfolio theory are obvious: (1) the use of Normal distributions to model asset returns, (2) the correlations between asset returns are fixed and constant forever, (3) the use of variance as risk measure which penalize not only down-side risk but also the up-side returns, (4) implementation of a viable portfolio construction procedure is plagued by technical difficulties stemming from the instability of the optimization problem with respect to the available data.

Follow-on researchers on portfolio theory have advanced with more and better optimization functions and computational implementation approaches, but still sticking to the general principles of the portfolio theory. Nowadays, financial academics call Markowitz’s MVM the classical version of the modern portfolio theory, while the later developments with more elaborate risk measures and optimization objective functions and procedures as the post-modern portfolio theory, or the general portfolio theory.
