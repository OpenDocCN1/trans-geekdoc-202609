## 47.5.1 Sample means, equilibrium means, and betas

Figure 47.1 shows the time-series plots of the sample means and seven sets of equilibrium means estimated from an expanding window in the size and book-to-market dataset. Each panel includes a plot of the risk-free rate of interest, the mean of the market portfolio, and the means of the 25 portfolios.

A simple visual inspection shows that the alternative ways of specifying the SML means are in fact different. The differences in the time-series behavior of the equilibrium means are quite marked. For example, the 25 portfolios’ equilibrium means increase in the first 460 months when the market risk premium is constant and decrease over the same period when the market portfolio’s expected excess return to variance ratio is constant. More importantly, the CAPM does not hold if the risk-free rate exceeds the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean. The risk-free rate peaks at 1.35% per month in June 1981 (month 461 in the plots). At this point in time, the risk-free rate exceeds the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean when the market’s expected return is constant; in the two cases when the market’s equilibrium mean is equal to its sample mean, i.e., when the intercepts and slopes of the SMLs are θ_Int = r̄_ft and θ_Slt = μ_mt^s − r̄_ft, and θ_Int = r_ft and θ_Slt = μ_mt^s − r_ft, respectively; and when the slope and intercept of the equilibrium means are set by the GLS regression. The risk-less rate exceeding the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean is not consistent with MV theory, even though the time-series of equilibrium means closely track the sample means in the latter case. On the other hand, the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean exceeds the risk-free rate when the market portfolio’s risk premium and expected excess return to variance ratio are constant, and when the GLS regression of sample means on betas is constrained so that the intercept is greater than or equal to the risk-free rate and the slope is positive. While this is consistent with MV theory, these equilibrium means are far from their sample counterparts.

The means and betas estimated from the expanding and fixed 120-month windows may be startlingly different, especially the sample means, and the equilibrium means based on the unconstrained and constrained GLS regressions. Figure 47.2 shows that, with the fixed 120-month window, the means are both more variable over time and less variable at a point in time. It is much easier to see differences in the cross-section of betas, shown for the 10 size portfolios dataset in Figure 47.3. The betas estimated from the expanding window, in the top panel, are reasonably stationary, although the spread narrows over time.⁹ By way of contrast, the betas estimated from the 120-month fixed window, in the lower panel, are highly variable. In most of the months, the small (large) size portfolio has the highest (lowest) beta. Yet, in and around month 250, the pattern reverses itself.

⁹The results are similar in the other two datasets. Moreover, the betas estimated from the time-series regressions in equation (47.7) are similar, but do not show quite as much variability.

## 47.5.2 GRS and Hotelling’s T² tests

We examine the statistical results first at two points in time, June 1981, the high risk-free rate date, and in May 2012, a low risk-free rate date. Then, we examine results at each of the 852 months in the sample period. There are problems with the CAPM means when the expected excess return on the market and the expected excess return on the market to variance ratio are constant. In 1981, the SML means associated with these two specifications plot *above* all the assets’ sample means. In 2012, the SML means associated with them plot *below* all the assets’ sample means. Therefore, to save space we drop these two specifications from further analysis.

Table 47.2 shows the results for the GRS and Hotelling’s $T^2$ tests of four specifications of the $(\Sigma,\mathbf{x}_m)$-compatible means in June 1981 and May 2012. The differences in the $p$-values across datasets and estimation periods are quite striking. With an expanding window in the 25 size and book-to-market portfolios dataset, the GRS test and the Hotelling’s $T^2$ test of four specifications of the SMLs reject the CAPM. With a fixed 120-month window, the tests cannot reject the CAPM in 1981, but reject it in 2012. With three exceptions, the tests cannot reject the CAPM in the 10 size portfolios dataset. With a fixed 120-month window in the 12 industry portfolios dataset, the CAPM cannot be rejected. For the most part, the CAPM is more likely to be rejected when estimates are based on an expanding window estimation.

The market and tangency portfolios are MV efficient and the slope of the SML is positive. However, the average risk-free rate (the zero-beta rate in this case) is less than the risk-free rate, which is not consistent with MV theory.¹⁰
