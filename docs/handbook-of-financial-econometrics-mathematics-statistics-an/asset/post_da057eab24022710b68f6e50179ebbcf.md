## Abstract

Stochastic volatility models of option prices treat variance as a variable. However, the application of such models requires calibration to market prices that often treats variance as an optimized parameter. If variance represents a variable, option pricing models should reflect measure-invariant features of its historic evolution. Alternatively, if variance is a parameter used to generate desired features of the implied volatility surface, stochastic volatility models lose their connection to the historic evolution of variance. This chapter obtains evidence that variance in stochastic volatility models is an artificial construct used to confer desired properties to the generated implied volatility surface.

### 33.1 Introduction

The dynamic nature of volatility was recognized in the seminal work of Engle (1982) and manifested itself in ARCH models. Furthermore, research in the field of options markets made it natural to consider variance as a price process, and, thus, a stochastic process in its own right. Contributions of Hull and White (1987), Scott (1987), and Wiggins (1987) demonstrated how postulating stochastic volatility may advance our understanding of option prices. Stochastic volatility models have become the workhorses of financial engineering since at least the introduction of the Heston (1993) model. Notable variations of pure stochastic volatility models include the works of Heston and Nandi (2000), Christoffersen et al. (2009). Understanding of their features and limitations is, thus, crucial for accurate pricing and risk management of derivative positions. This chapter focuses on whether the variance process in stochastic volatility models represents (i) a model for the unobserved true variance or (ii) serves as an artificial construct to imbue the theoretical model prices with the features of observed market prices, thus, allowing the model to be calibrated to observed implied volatility surfaces. The first explanation of the role of variance in stochastic volatility models makes it a variable, while the second makes it a parameter. Thus, the paper compliments the existing literature on the performance of option pricing models, represented by prominent works of Bates (1996), Bakshi et al. (1997), and Dumas et al. (1998), by focusing on the mechanics of stochastic volatility models. Specifically, Bates (1996) and Bakshi et al. (1997) point out the lack of consistency in stochastic volatility models. The current chapter augments their results in two ways. First, it relies on trajectories of daily recalibrated model parameters and rolling window econometric estimates
