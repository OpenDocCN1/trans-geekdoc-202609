In so doing, it enables the researcher to decompose the correlations between each pair of variables into the different effects that flow from the causal variable(s) to the dependent variable. These effects may be either direct (e.g., increased market share will lead directly into more sales to customers) or channeled indirectly through other variables (the same increased share could come at the expense of profit margins). Thus one may examine both the direct and various indirect effects of a firm's decision making and their customer generation measures and assess the impact of each.

In path analysis, the variables examined are broken into two types, exogenous or endogenous, based upon whether or not they appear as dependent variables in any of the system of equations. It is customary to develop a diagram with single or double-sided arrows indicating the predicted direction of causation among the exogenous and endogenous. These predicted paths are then tested using a system of simultaneous equations, processed iteratively, to determine whether the predicted relationships hold. The coefficients generated are standardized regression coefficients (betas), showing the direct effect of an independent variable on its dependent variable in the path diagram. Thus, when the model has two or more causal variables, path coefficients are partial regression coefficients that measure the extent of the effect of a causal variable and its dependent in the path model controlling for other prior variables. The path analysis typically uses standardized data or a correlation matrix as an input. The resulting methodology allows for simultaneously addressing issues of factor identification and endogeneity.

The path analysis methodology presented in this chapter could be easily applied to a number of areas of accounting research. In particular, it could be used to improve variable measurement by providing guidance on how best to breakdown accounting numbers into their components and on examining the impacts of accounting and non-financial data on firm valuations. It could allow for better amortization schedules by eliminating more transitory elements of these variables from those that should be capitalized. Similarly, path analysis could be used to develop better (possibly more recursive) accruals models by isolating the effects accounting variables have on each other. This could lead to better measures of non-discretionary versus discretionary accruals. In addition, this framework could be used to isolate and test the effects of more or less permanent components of earnings, while simultaneously giving researchers the ability to control for decisions made by managers on when to recognize such items as write-offs. In fact, consistent with its use in other literatures, path analysis may improve the specification and interpretability of results whenever competing incentives might influence decision making (e.g., for managerial decision making, actions by auditor, and/or analyst forecasting).
