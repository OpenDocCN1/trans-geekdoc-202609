Columns (4) and (5) include other determinants of diversification benefits documented by the literature. Market integration and correlation are still highly significant, but the first is more significant. Inflation, TED spread, turnover, and the relative performance of the local portfolios in the world market (RSR) have significant explanatory power on the benefits of mean-variance efficiency. Specifically, an economy that has high inflation, high global credit risk or TED spread, high unemployment, low turnover, and low equity market return benefits more from diversification.

There is a possibility that both dependent variable and independent variable are endogenously determined. The variables that explain market integration might explain diversification benefit. Driessen and Laeven (2007) argue country risk might be endogenous and thus apply legal origin (La Porta et al., 1998) as an instrument in their cross-sectional regression of an increase in Sharpe ratios. However, legal origin is not time varying and our regression is in the form of unbalanced panel data but not a valid instrument (Atanasov and Black, 2015). We use corruption, which is available for each country on an annual basis, as an instrument of country risk. We also use investment profile developed by ICRG as an instrument for R² and correlation. Investment profile captures the factors of investment risks that are not covered by other economic, political, and financial risk components. Lehkonen (2014) shows investment profile is the key determinant of market integration. Investment profile increases market integration. Both country risk and corruption are also available from ICRG. The results in columns 6 and 7 applying instruments are qualitatively similar to those in columns 4 and 5. Following Driessen and Laeven (2007), we perform an F-test of joint significance of the identifying instruments and the Hansen-Sargan test of over-identifying restrictions to examine the validity of our choice of instruments. The null hypothesis of the Hansen-Sargan test is that the instruments are uncorrelated with the error term and that the excluded instruments are correctly excluded from the estimated equation. If the Hansen-Sargan test is rejected, the choice of the instruments is questionable. Both tests support the use of corruption and investment profile as instruments for country risk and market integration. The F-test suggests corruption and investment profile explain much of the variation in country risk and market integration. The Hansen-Sargan test is not rejected at conventional significance levels.
