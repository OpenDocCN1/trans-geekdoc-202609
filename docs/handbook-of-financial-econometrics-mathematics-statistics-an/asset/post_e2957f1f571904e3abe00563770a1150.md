To examine this issue, we require data from an emerging market that has both sufficient foreign bank penetration and has undergone substantial financial reforms. Our market of choice is Taiwan. Taiwan is unique in that it offers access to trustworthy, detailed credit data that is not available in many other emerging markets.³ Taiwan, even in the early years of our data sample, also has enough foreign bank penetration for a meaningful exploration. Moreover, in 1994, the Taiwanese government set in motion a strategy that would slowly begin the transformation of Taiwan from an emerging financial market into a regional Asian financial center. To that end, the government proposed the strategy “Manufacturing Industry is the Root and Financial Industry is the Leaf”, which gradually introduced reforms into the financial sector.⁴ As helpful as the 1994 reforms were, it was the Taiwanese banking reforms of 2002 and 2004 that were the most critical events in the transformation of Taiwan from an emerging market into a developed one. Since the implementation of the 2002 and 2004 financial reforms, Taiwan has evolved into one of the world’s most important economies and a significant regional financial center. In 2003, the Taiwanese Ministry of Finance placed a moratorium on the de novo creation of bank branches. It is no surprise that the second Taiwanese banking reform in 2004 was focused on mergers and acquisitions. The financial reform had a fourfold purpose. The first was to consolidate the Taiwanese banking sector so that there were three large Taiwanese banks with an individual market share of over 10%. The second was to reduce by 50% the number of state-owned banks. The third was a major reduction in the number of financial holding companies. The fourth was the fostering of an environment that facilitated an increase in the amount of cross-border investment by foreign banks into Taiwanese banks.

Although there were four goals, there are nine pillars upon which the Taiwanese Financial Reform was built. The first pillar was the creation of the Financial Supervisory Committee: an independent surveillance agency that is responsible for the oversight of Taiwanese financial institutions. The second pillar was the creation of the Financial Reform Task Force, also known as the Executive Yuan, whose responsibility it was to propose reforms in banking, capital markets, insurance companies, and defining and qualifying what constituted financial crimes. The purpose of these reforms was to enhance competition within Taiwan’s financial sector. The third pillar was, as discussed above, an initiative to improve the overall asset quality of Taiwan’s financial institutions. Between 2002 and 2003, the Taiwanese government managed to cut the ratio of non-performing loans by nearly 50%. The fourth pillar started as the Merger Law of Financial Institutions (2000) and then culminated in the Financial Holding Company Act that allowed an unprecedented wave of industry consolidation through the formation of Taiwanese financial holding companies. In total, over 68 acquisitions occurred. As a result, Taiwanese banks were getting bigger and concentration was increasing. The fifth pillar was a relaxation of capital controls.⁸ The Taiwanese government removed many of the restrictions placed on foreign investors interested in investing in Taiwan’s securities markets. They did away with the outdated Qualified Foreign Institutional Investor system and began the deregulation of securities markets. The goal was to encourage foreign investment in Taiwan’s securities markets. By the end of the first reform, foreign investment inflows topped $66 billion, nearly 2.5 × higher than they were pre-reform. The sixth pillar of Financial Reform was the creation of trading

## 25.3 Data

The data employed are drawn from three separate sources: the *Taiwan Economic Journal* (TEJ) financial reports database, the TEJ lending information database, and the TEJ ownership structure database. Our sample covers a 15 year period from 1991 to 2005. The observations are comprised exclusively of non-financial firms listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange (TSE). We exclude financial firms because their asset and liability structures differ substantially from those of the non-financial firms. We also eliminate those firms with obvious errors in their financial reports, such as firms with negative debt or negative sales. We also exclude firms with missing data in relation to short-term and long-term debt or other required variables during our sample period. The final total is 8,877 effective year-firm observations from a potential starting figure of 9,690 observations.

For each firm in the sample, the TEJ provides an income statement, balance sheet and information pertaining to borrowing and lending as of the end of each fiscal year.
