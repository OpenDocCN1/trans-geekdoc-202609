
## An Integral Equation Approach for Bond Prices with Applications

Under the risk-neutral measure Q, the value of the firm follows the dynamics:

\[
dV_t = V_t \left[ r dt + \sigma dW_t + d\left( \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} U_i - \lambda \nu t \right) \right], 
\tag{110.3}
\]

where \(W = (W_t)\) is a standard Brownian motion, \(N = (N_t)\) is a Poisson process with intensity \(\lambda\), and \(\{U_i\}\) is a sequence of independent jump sizes with a common distribution on \((-1,0) \cup (0,\infty)\) and has mean \(\nu\). Also we assume that \((W_t)\), \((N_t)\), and \(\{U_i\}\) are independent. Furthermore, following Longstaff and Schwartz (1995) and Zhou (2001), we assume that the payoff at \(T\) is determined by a function of the firm value at the time of default if there is default or simply the bond pays the full face value if there is no default. In other words, the payoff of the zero coupon bond is given at time \(T\) by

\[
1_{\{\tau(T)>T\}} + [1 - \psi(\log(V_{\tau(T)}/K_{\tau(T)}))]\,1_{\{\tau(T)\le T\}}.
\tag{110.4}
\]

Here the writedown function \(\psi(\log x)\) reports the writedown fraction for the bond that is lost due to bankruptcy costs or the others. Actually, Zhou (2001) considered \(\psi\) of the affine-exponential form: \(\psi(y) = a - b e^y\). In the literature, \(1 - \psi\) is also referred to as the recovery rate. It is worth noting that in empirical studies even for the same class of bond issues, the recovery rate \(1 - \psi\) differs significantly over different time periods and different firms. See Altman (1995) and Franks and Torous (1994). Based on the choice of write-down functions, Zhou (2001) provided a Monte Carlo simulation scheme. With his simulation results, he found that by manipulating the parameters, various shapes of credit spreads, default probabilities, and other characteristics of risky bonds found in empirical studies can be recovered. On the other hand, Hilberink and Rogers (2002) generalized Leland's model (see Leland (1994)). They assumed that the firm's log asset value process is the sum of a Brownian motion with drift and a downward jump compound Poisson process. Although there was no closed-form solution for the price of the perpetual debt, they found the Fourier transform of it. By inverting the Fourier transform, they also found numerically the result that credit spreads do not tend to zero as time to maturity goes to zero.

Note that the unique (up to indistinguishability) solution to (110.3) is given by

\[
V_t = V_0 \exp \left\{ \left(r - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 - \lambda \nu \right) t + \sigma W_t \right\} \prod_{j=1}^{N_t} (1 + U_j).
\]

Set \(X_t = \log(V_t/K_t)\) and put \(\tau(T)=\inf\{0\le s\le T: X_s\le 0\}\). Then we have

\[
X_t = \log(V_0/K_0) + \left(r - \frac12\sigma^2 - \lambda\nu - \kappa\right) t + \sigma W_t - Z_t
= X_0 + c t + \sigma W_t - Z_t,\quad t \in \mathbb R_+,
\tag{110.5}
\]

where \(X_0 = x = \log(V_0/K_0)\), \(c = r - \frac12\sigma^2 - \lambda\nu - \kappa\), and \(Z_t \triangleq \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} -\log(1+U_n)\). It follows from (110.4) that the no-arbitrage price of the bond is given by

\[
D(V_0,T)= e^{-rT} - e^{-rT}E_x[\psi(X_{\tau(T)})1_{\{\tau(T)<\infty\}}] = e^{-rT} - e^{-rT}\Phi(x,T),
\tag{110.6}
\]

where

\[
\Phi(x,T) \triangleq E_x[\psi(X_{\tau(T)})1_{\{\tau(T)<\infty\}}].
\tag{110.7}
\]

Here \(E_x\) denotes the expectation conditioning on \(X_0 = x\) under the risk-neutral measure \(Q\). We also write \(E\) for \(E_0\).

In this paper, instead of trying to derive a closed-form solution for the bond price, we derive an integral equation for the function \(\Phi\) in (110.7). The elements of the integral equation are in explicit forms. (See Theorem 110.4 below.) Using these elements, we study the analytic properties of the bond prices. In particular, under mild conditions on the jump distribution and writedown function, we show that the credit spread has a strictly positive lower bound as time to maturity tends to zero. It is interesting to compare our theoretical results with the simulation results obtained earlier by Zhou (2001) and Hilberink and Rogers (2002) (in which only some specific jump distributions and writedown functions were considered). As another application of the integral equation, we derive an infinite series expression for the bond prices. (Note that in Merton's jump diffusion model, if \(1+U_j\) is lognormal, there is an infinite sum of Black–Scholes type expressions for the price of the risky zero coupon bond; see Bingham and Kiesel (2004, Theorem 9.3.1).)

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section 110.2, we use integral equation techniques to take a first look at the bond price. Based on results in Section 110.2, we study in Section 110.3 properties of bond prices and investigate the behavior of the credit spreads as the time to maturity tends to zero. In the Appendix, we justify an infinite series expression for the bond prices.

## 110.2 Integral Equation for Φ

For notational convenience, from now on, we will write \(X_t = X_0 + X_t^c - Z_t\) with \(X_t^c = c t + \sigma W_t\). For any constant \(x \in \mathbb R\), write \(\hat{x} = \frac{x}{\sigma}\), and let \(J_k\) denote the \(k\)th epoch time of the compound Poisson process \(Z\) with interarrival time \(S_k\), that is \(J_k = \sum_{j=1}^k S_j\). Furthermore, if \(G_1,\ldots,G_n\) are random variables on \(\mathbb R\), we write \(F_{G_1,\ldots,G_n}\) for their joint distribution. We reserve \(F\) for the distribution of \(-\log(1+U_j)\).

We use an integral-equation technique to take a first look at the function \(\Phi\). To begin with, write

\[
\{\tau(T)\le T\} = A \cup B \cup C \cup D,
\tag{110.8}
\]

where

- \(A = [J_1 > T \ge \tau(T)]\) — No jump up to maturity and default is caused by diffusion.
- \(B = [T > J_1 > \tau(T)]\) — Jump occurs up to maturity and default occurs before \(J_1\).
- \(C = [T > \tau(T) = J_1]\) — Jump occurs up to maturity and default occurs at \(J_1\).
- \(D = [T > \tau(T) > J_1]\) — Jump occurs up to maturity and default occurs after \(J_1\).

Note that \(\{A, B, C, D\}\) is a partition of \(\{\tau(T)\le T\}\). With these, we define \(G_A(x,T)=E_x[\psi(X_{\tau(T)});A]\) and similarly for \(G_B\), \(G_C\), and \(G_D\). Before stating our results, we recall some facts about the joint distribution of Brownian motion with drift and its maximum process. For details and proofs, see Shreve (2003).

**Theorem 110.1.** Let \(\alpha \in \mathbb R\), \(T > 0\), \(W(t;\alpha)=\alpha t+W(t)\), and \(M(T;\alpha)=\max_{0\le t\le T}W(t;\alpha)\). Then the joint density of \(M(T;\alpha)\) and \(W(T;\alpha)\) is given by

\[
f_{M(T;\alpha),W(T;\alpha)}(m,w)=\begin{cases}
\frac{2(2m-w)}{T\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{\alpha w-\frac12\alpha^2T-\frac1{2T}(2m-w)^2}, & w \le m,\ m \ge 0,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\tag{110.9}
\]

where the last equality follows from the symmetry of standard Brownian motion. By (110.11), we have

\[
\begin{aligned}
P[\max_{s\le T}(-\hat{c}s + W_s) \ge \hat{x}]
&= 1 - P[\max_{s\le T}(-\hat{c}s + W_s) \le \hat{x}] \\
&= 1 - \left[ N\left(\frac{\hat{x} + \hat{c}T}{\sqrt{T}}\right) - e^{-2\hat{c}\hat{x}} N\left(\frac{-\hat{x} + \hat{c}T}{\sqrt{T}}\right) \right] \\
&= N\left(\frac{-\hat{x} - \hat{c}T}{\sqrt{T}}\right) + e^{-2\hat{c}\hat{x}} N\left(\frac{-\hat{x} + \hat{c}T}{\sqrt{T}}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{110.16}
\]

This completes the proof of (110.12).

We next turn to the proof of (110.13). Again by the independence of \(\{W_t; t \in \mathbb R_+\}\) and \(J_1\), and the symmetry of standard Brownian motion, we get

\[
\begin{aligned}
G_B(x,T)
&= \psi(0) P\left[ \min_{s\le J_1} (x + cs + \sigma W_s) \le 0,\ J_1 \le T \right] \\
&= \psi(0) \int_0^T P\left[ \min_{s\le t} (x + cs + \sigma W_s) \le 0 \right] dF_{J_1}(t) \\
&= \psi(0) \int_0^T P\left[ \min_{s\le t} (-\hat{c}s + W_s) \ge \hat{x} \right] dF_{J_1}(t).
\end{aligned}
\]

Then replacing \(T\) with \(t\) in (110.16), we get (110.13).

&= \int_0^T dF_{J_1}(t) \int_0^\infty dF(y)\,
E\left[ \psi(x + X_t^c - y)\,

\end{aligned}
\]
