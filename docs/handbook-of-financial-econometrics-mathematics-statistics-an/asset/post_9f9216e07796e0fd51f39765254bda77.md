## Abstract

This chapter investigates the characteristics of implied risk-neutral distributions derived separately from call and put options prices. Differences in risk-neutral moments between call and put options indicate deviations from put–call parity. We find that sentiment effect is significantly related to differences between call and put option prices. Our results suggest there is differential impact of investor sentiment and consumer sentiment on call and put option traders’ expectations. Rational and irrational sentiment components have different influence on call and put option traders’ beliefs as well.

## Keywords

Implied risk-neutral distribution • Put–call parity • Investor sentiment • Consumer sentiment • TAIEX options.

## 42.1 Introduction

Put–call parity in options is a no-arbitrage relation in which a stock payoff is equivalent to the payoff for a portfolio that is long a call and short a put option. Kamara and Miller (1995) and Nisbet (1992) suggest that transaction costs, dividend payments, and the early exercise value of American options can cause call and put option prices to deviate from put–call parity. Another potential reason for deviations from put–call parity is that informed investors with private information trade in the options market before they trade in the underlying stock market. Therefore, Atilgan (2010) and Cremers and Weinbaum (2010) show that deviations from put–call parity measured by the volatility spread between call and put options contains information concerning future abnormal stock returns. Arbitrage restrictions also prevent the convergence of call and put option prices. When put option prices are sufficiently higher than the prices of underlying stock and corresponding call options, deviations from put–call parity arise in the presence of short sale constraints on the underlying stocks (Lamont and Thaler, 2003; Ofek and Richardson, 2003; Ofek et al., 2004). Szu et al. (2011) find different patterns for the implied volatility curves between Taiwan Stock Exchange Capitalization Weighted Stock Index (TAIEX) call and put option prices provided by the Taiwan Futures Exchange (TAIFEX). The underlying asset of TAIEX options is futures, which have no restrictions on short sales. Therefore, in addition to short sale constraints, this chapter studies another

possible explanation of the divergence between TAIEX call and put option prices.^1^

TAIEX options are actively traded by individual investors, who are more susceptible to sentiment. The unpredictability of individual investors’ beliefs increases arbitrage risk and informed traders may suffer a loss if they engage in arbitrage against individual investors (DeLong *et al.*, 1990; Shleifer and Summers, 1990). For more impediments to arbitrage, the sentiment effect becomes a more important determinant of index option prices. Moreover, the index options market is segmented, with out-of-the-money put options primarily traded by hedgers and out-of-the-money call options mostly traded by speculators (Constantinides *et al.*, 2011). Deviations from put–call parity reflect differences in time values between call and put options with identical underlying assets, strike prices, and maturity. Therefore, we study whether investor and consumer sentiment effects differ on call and put option traders’ expectations about underlying asset prices. Furthermore, the sentiment proxy is related to two components: aggregate error and rational updating in investors’ or consumers’ beliefs concerning future returns. We explore the manner in which rational and irrational sentiment influence call and put option prices. Finally, the link between sentiment effect and the divergence between call and put option prices is investigated.

To investigate the sentiment effect under different market conditions, we employ the optimization method proposed by Rubinstein (1994) to separately derive the risk-neutral distributions from call and put option prices across two subperiods of the 2008 global financial crisis. Higher moments of the risk-neutral distribution extracted from option prices, which is model independent, provide credible information concerning future returns and investors’ price expectations (Bali and Hovakimian, 2009; Birru and Figlewski, 2012; Conrad *et al.*, 2013; Diavatopoulos *et al.*, 2012). The differences in higher risk-neutral moments between call and put options show that, in both the pre-crisis and crisis periods, call and put option traders have significantly different beliefs concerning underlying asset prices. ## 42.2 Data and Variables

### 42.2.1 Option data

We obtain the daily prices of TAIEX call and put options from the TAIFEX. The sample period is from January 3, 2005, through December 31, 2010, including 1,493 trading dates, 39,119 call options, and 40,959 put options. The TAIEX options are European options and the expiring month can be in the next three calendar months or the next two calendar-quarter months (March, June, September, or December). TAIEX options that expire in the current month are characterized by higher liquidity and heavier trading volume than options that expire the following month. This study therefore uses concurrent-month TAIEX options. When the options approximate the maturity dates, implied volatility is sensitive to option prices and the trading volumes of the option contracts for the following month subsequently increase, which is often referred to as the maturity effect. To prevent sampling option prices from being distorted by the maturity effect, we use the option contracts of the following month for the last three trading dates

of the current-month option contracts.³ Because the maturity dates and determination of the final settlement prices at maturity of TAIEX futures are indifferent to those of TAIEX options, TAIEX futures taken from the TAIFEX represent the underlying assets of TAIEX options in this chapter.⁴ We use the daily interest rate of savings accounts from the First Bank between 2005 and 2010 as a measure of the risk-free interest rate.
