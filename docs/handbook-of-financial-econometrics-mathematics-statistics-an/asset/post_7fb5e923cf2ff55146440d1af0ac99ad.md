## 67.4 Events that Affect IPO Performance and Issuance

Benninga, Helmantel, and Sarig (2005) and Alti (2005) liken IPO performance to waves and cascades. This seems to be a good general description of IPO performance and volume; however, when we attempt to evaluate or estimate these cycles, researchers (i.e., Brailsford, Heaney, Powell, & Shi, 2001 and Guo, Brooks, & Shami, 2010) seem to have focused in on these two types of markets (i.e., hot and cold) as defined previously in the literature (e.g., Ibbotson, 1975; Ritter, 1984; Helwege & Liang, 2004) and have not questioned whether the market’s cycles are more complex. Hu and Wang (2013) seemed to have broken out of this mold and considered a three-regime change model, which spurred Smith and Mumtaz (2018) to consider whether we should constrain ourselves to three types of markets or whether we should attempt to isolate the effects of events on IPO performance and volume and then attempt to identify the cyclical nature of IPO performance.

In Ritter (1984), the researcher challenged the general idea of whether hot markets just pop up and are best explained through cycles or if there is a better description of these hot market cycles. Ritter (1984) concluded that, in reference to the hot market of the 1980s, it was better described as an industry effect related to outperformance in IPOs that specializes in natural resources, and most other industries did not experience the same hot market. Helwege et al. (2004) found in their analysis of IPO performance and issuance from 1975 to 2000 that “surges in issuance do not reflect special types of IPO firms in special types of business” (p. 566) and that “hot markets are not accurately characterized by the clustering of a single industry, but rather, times when many industries have hot markets” (p. 566). In conclusion, Helwege et al. (2004) determined that hot markets emerge during periods in which investors are willing to buy more high-growth companies, whether these periods are caused by irrationality or on an assessment of the market potential of these firms at that time is a question that is left for the reader to ponder.

Pastor and Veronesi (2005) leverage the work of Helwege et al. (2004) and suggest, in response to a question posed by Ritter and Welch (2002), that the reason why IPO volume drops precipitously after the general stock market declines is that firms that are private wait for market conditions to become more accommodative to issuing new shares. These market events are critical determinants of IPO issuance and performance. In addition, we believe that to create an accurate picture of the cyclical nature of IPO performance we need to remove events that are likely to affect general market conditions. As such, we illustrate how Smith et al. (2018) removed the effects of the financial collapse of 2007 or 2008, the introduction of the JOBS Act in 2012, and the eventual change in the type of securities that entered the market as a result of the initiation of the JOBS Act because these events fundamentally changed the types of securities that were issued into the market and the market’s issuance and performance.

It is challenging for anyone to argue that an event similar to the financial collapse of 2007-2008 would not have a significant impact on IPO volume and performance; therefore, we question whether an event like this, which has such a significant effect on the market, should be considered a component of the hot or cold market or should it be dealt with separately. Similar to how Ritter (1984) questioned whether a hot market in one industry should be categorized as a hot market for all industries, we question if a global financial crisis should be categorized as a cold market or should this type of event receive its own categorization when we attempt to explain the cyclical nature of IPO issuance and performance.

To continue this point, we question whether there are other events that are related to policy initiatives that should be controlled for before examining the perceived hotness or coldness of the market and attempting to generate any theoretical model that we could use to predict IPO issuance and performance without the influence of these events. As an example, the JOBS Act was initiated in 2012 as an attempt to stimulate economic growth and encourage smaller companies to issue their shares on public exchanges. Dambra, Field, and Gustafson (2015) estimated that the implementation of the JOBS Act in April of 2012 led to an annual 25% increase in IPO issuance from May 2012 to March 2014 as compared against pre-JOBS Act levels. As illustrated in Table 67.2, Smith et al. (2018) contend that the influence of the JOBS Act did not end in March of 2014, the volatility associated with the average IPO increased markedly from April 2014 to December of 2015 and the proportion of IPOs that went public during this time were more heavily skewed to small biotechnology firms. Incorporating public policy initiatives and major events into the process of modeling IPO issuance and performance should provide researchers and practitioners with a better understanding of the cyclical nature of IPO issuance and performance.
