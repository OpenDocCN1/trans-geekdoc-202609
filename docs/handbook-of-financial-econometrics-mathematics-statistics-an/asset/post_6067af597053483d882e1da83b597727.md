Whether we classify IPO performance as bubbles, hot and cold markets, or sequential learning, or provide some other form of classification for their performance, there are some key elements about the process of issuance and the subsequent performance that researchers should be cognizant of prior to attempting to gauge this performance. The following subsections of this chapter will identify those areas specifically and provide the reader with some insight on how to contend with these issues, before attempting to classify or identify a cycle.

## 67.3 Hot and Cold IPO Market Performance

Ibbotson et al. (1975) initially identified hot issue markets for IPOs as “periods in which the 1st-month performance (or aftermarket performance) of new issues is abnormally high” (p. 1027). In their initial specification of “hot” and “cold” markets, Ibbotson et al. (1975) used the median market-adjusted (i.e., S&P 500 Index) as a sorting device and based on whether the average IPO performance in any given month was above or below the median performance of all monthly market-adjusted IPO performances in the sample, they were categorized as hot or cold IPOs, respectively. In a comparative study, Ritter (1984) examined data from 1977 to 1982 and illustrated that in the 15 months, which included January 1980–March 1981, the average monthly market-adjusted performance generated by IPOs was 48.4%, he compared this estimate to his entire sample that had an average market-adjusted performance of 16.3%, and categorized the period lasting from January 1980 to March 1981 as the hot market and the 1977–1979 period as the cold market. After identifying hot and cold markets, Ritter (1984) was able to look more specifically at the factors that caused this “hot” market performance to occur and contended that this “hot market” was an industry-specific phenomenon, specifically the natural resources industry, and if researchers were to take the performance of this particular industry out of their analysis, they may find that “a hot issue market is barely perceptible” (Ritter, 1985, p. 215).

One of the primary goals of studies that attempt to identify abnormal performance in studies of aggregate IPO performance and issuance is to identify factors that influence issuance and performance and the identification and specification of hot and cold IPO markets is an excellent starting point. One general estimation issue in the literature that had not been examined until recently (i.e., Smith and Mumtaz, 2018) is that hot and cold market cycles are identified ex post, or after the entire sample is drawn and retroactively applied to the entire sample, as in Helwege and Liang (2004). Helwege et al. (2004) provided researchers and investors with a first critical review of hot and cold IPO markets. Their analysis reviewed hot and cold equity markets from an issuance perspective, a dominant industry perspective, and a performance or underpricing perspective using a sample that was drawn from 1975 to 2000.

Helwege et al. (2004) defined hot and cold markets as periods of issuance and performance in which the centered three-month average resides in the top quartile and the bottom quartile of the entire sample, which seems to be a significant improvement from segmenting the market into just two segments (i.e., hot and cold markets) and this technique includes a period that could be defined as normal performance. This advance in the way that we perceive hot and cold market activity led to additional studies (e.g., Guo et al., 2010; Hu and Wang, 2013) in which researchers attempted to characterize IPOs in more than just two buckets, which were hot and cold, but to allow for a classification of a number of performance and issuance classifications. All of these methods used to classify cold, normal, and hot markets are only able to be classified once the researchers have obtained the entire sample that they are attempting to categorize issuance and performance.

Smith et al. (2018) offered a comparison, or a different way to think about market heat and coolness, and is compared specifically against the performance of the Helwege et al. (2004) study in a subsequent section. The Smith et al. (2018) model could, similarly, be used as a comparison model for studies in which other models were used to identify hot and cold markets such as Ibbotson et al. (1975), Ritter (1984), Guo et al. (2010), and Hu et al. (2013). In Smith et al. (2018), the researchers use the trailing 10 and 20-month average issuance and performance as proxies for the relative heat or coolness of the market for IPO performance and issuance. Unlike the methods used in Ibbotson et al. (1975), Ritter (1984), Guo et al. (2010), and Hu et al. (2013), the methodology of Smith et al. (2018) is able to be applied to estimate the heat of the market without having an idea about what the future performance and issuance might be.
