On the other hand, technical trading is an empirical science which has been formed along with empirical technical analysis widely practiced by market participants. Very useful technical trading strategies and systems, however, are treated as core technologies of institutional or private traders (such as Renaissance Technologies), so they are strictly protected, not directly available for academic studies. Due to their high economic value and market competitiveness, technical trading strategies and systems are rarely or incomprehensively reported in academic literature, thus it is difficult to form a scientific theoretical system of technical trading. With these considerations, we divide the complexity of portfolio management into two parts — the asset portfolio theory and the trading strategies, which are then integrated together into a unified framework of Intelligent Portfolio Theory (IPT). However, due to the foreseeable complexity of this theory, this chapter shall only focus on the principles and the general framework of intelligent portfolio management and trading, while more comprehensive mathematical and computational intelligence models, systems and testing with historical data are to be referred to other technical research papers. However, many published works by other researchers and developers in the literature correspond directly or indirectly quite well to various partial forms or implementations of this general theory.

# 43.2.3 A brief review on modern and post-modern portfolio theories

The notion of diversification in investment is long standing in financial industry. Empirical experiences in investment reveal that diversification of capital allocation on different assets can lower the fluctuation of the total equity, and thus reducing the investment risk but not necessarily lowering the investment return. This empirical insight was then reformulated into a quantitative portfolio theory by Markowitz in his PhD dissertation, first published in a paper in the Journal of Finance (1952), and then in a monograph book (1959). This work was then called the Mean–Variance Model (MVM) of the classical portfolio theory. More than half a century since then has passed, virtually all the follow-on research works in portfolio theory can be considered as deepening and extensions of the classical portfolio theory.
