Arce (2013) interprets DFA in the context of moral hazard and adverse selection in that banks are constrained to write contracts that have the consequence of attracting employees with social preferences that benefit firm performance and employee remuneration. Banks become simultaneously market- and socially-driven under DFA. This research supports this finding by proposing that social conscious employment compensation policies are driven, in part, by improved governance transparency required by SOX. This approach is complemented by Chemla and Hennessy (2014) and Stiglitz (2010) who observe that lender expertise is required for successful DFA implementation. Compliance provides incentives to banks to retain loan originators, thereby reducing the moral hazard experienced by the investor. However, such a tactic facilitates the application of lender expertise in a new type of originator-based adverse selection that increases lending to be more profitable, potentially increasing the number of riskier loans. Augmented loan loss provisions occur to offset this consequence of employee retention.

Governance climate and employee skill affect bank loan loss provisioning. This suggests complementarity between SOX and DFA. Banks are motivated and experience oversight, which reduces the risk of their loan portfolio, causing loan loss provisions to decline after the passage of DFA. Alternatively, if DFA is designed to lower the risk of default, then banks should increase their loan volume, consistent with the “too big to fail” adverse selection hypothesis and loan loss provision should increase to make sure that financial institutions have enough reserves to cover any losses.

# 131.3.2 Empirical methods

To examine this new regulatory environment, an empirical model is presented to analyze how DFA is associated with loan loss provisions and whether SOX requirements for governance quality are still relevant with respect to loan loss provisions even after controlling for DFA. The model detects the consequences of regulatory change on loan loss provision behavior by community banks. A fixed effects panel model is employed to estimate the relationship between the loan loss provision, increased regulatory and audit scrutiny following the implementation of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX), operational changes associated with implementation of the Dodd-Frank Act of 2008 (DFA), and other internal firm factors and external market factors.
