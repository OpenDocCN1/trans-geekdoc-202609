Brennan and Lo (2010) suggested that positively weighted MV efficient portfolios are even more unlikely. They defined an “impossible frontier” as a frontier on which all portfolios have at least one negative weight. They proved that for randomly drawn covariance matrices the probability of obtaining an impossible frontier approaches one as the number of assets grows.

Levy and Roll (2010, 2012) painted a very different picture, arguing that the market portfolio may be MV efficient after all. They extended the reverse-engineering approach to find a minimum distance between the sample means and standard deviations and a set of equilibrium means and standard deviations required to ensure that the market is MV efficient. And, they conducted univariate and multivariate tests of these variations. Surprisingly slight variations in parameters, well within estimation error bounds, suffice to make the market efficient.

These arguments aside, the results from the statistical analysis are equally contradictory. The cross-sectional tests of Fama and MacBeth (1973) provided strong support for the CAPM. Twenty years later, Fama and French (1992) rejected the CAPM because the relation between beta and average return is flat and statistically insignificantly different from zero.²

Yet, Roll and Ross (1994) showed that the “market” portfolio can be almost efficient when slope of an OLS regression of means on betas is zero. Kandel and Stambaugh (1995) showed that the “market” portfolio can be grossly inefficient when securities plot almost exactly on the SML. If the index portfolio is inefficient, then the coefficients and R² from an OLS regression of means on betas can equal essentially any values and bear no relation to the index portfolio’s MV location. That location does determine the outcome of a mean–beta regression fitted by GLS.

Grauer and Janmaat (2009) showed that cross-sectional tests have little power to reject the hypothesis that the slope of the SML is equal to zero. The lack of power is related to grouping, which does not produce a wide range of betas. Grauer and Janmaat (2010) provided a simple way to alleviate the problem by repackaging the data with zero-weight portfolios that increased the spread of betas. In 14 standard datasets, the results provide little or no support for the CAPM. In the zero-weight portfolio datasets, GLS regressions provide strong support for it.

## 47.2 (Σ, x_m)-Compatible, Equivalently SML, Equilibrium, or CAPM Means

The central ideas for specifying the family of CAPM means can be traced to the portfolio theory. In the traditional MV problem, the mean vector and covariance matrix are given, and we solve for the portfolio weights. Let x_m and ι be n-vectors containing portfolio weights and ones, respectively, and Σ be an n × n covariance matrix of returns.³ Best and Grauer (1985) reverse engineered the MV problem. Taking Σ and x_m as given, they constructed an infinite number of sets of (Σ, x_m)-compatible means that ensure that x_m is the optimal solution. x_m may be any portfolio containing positive and negative weights, if the weights sum to one. When x_m is the market portfolio, the (Σ, x_m)-compatible means are equivalently SML or equilibrium means. More specifically,

μᵉ = θ₁ι + θ₂Σxₘ  (47.1)

where μᵉ is the n-vector of equilibrium means, θ₁ and θ₂ are scalar constants, and Σxₘ is an n-vector whose jth element is the covariance of the return on the jth asset with the return on the market portfolio. When the slope is positive, the market portfolio is MV efficient.⁴

Although the CAPM is a single-period model, it is treated as if it holds period-by-period in a multiperiod setting. When time enters the analysis, the equilibrium means are

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^e = \theta_{Int}\boldsymbol{\iota} + \theta_{Slt}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{mt}, \tag{47.2}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{mt} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_t\mathbf{x}_{mt}/\sigma_{mt}^2$ is an $n$-vector containing the betas, $\sigma_{mt}^2 = \mathbf{x}_{mt}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_t\mathbf{x}_{mt}$ is the variance of the return on the market portfolio, $\mu_{mt}$ is the expected return on the market portfolio, $\theta_{Int} = r_t$ is the intercept, $\theta_{Slt} = (\mu_{mt} - r_t)$ is the slope, and $r_t$ may be either the riskfree rate $r_{ft}$ or zero-beta rate $r_{zt}$. Writing the equilibrium means in terms of betas rather than covariances is in keeping with the testing literature.

In this chapter, we specify the intercepts and slopes of the SMLs in two ways. First, we hold either the market expected return, market risk premium, market mean excess return to variance ratio, or market mean excess return to standard deviation ratio constant over time.$^5$ Second, we minimize a variety of distances between the sample means and different sets of SML means at a point in time. A convenient framework for analyzing the distance sets considers them as special cases of either OLS or GLS regressions. Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^s = \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t + \boldsymbol{\mu}_t^e$ be the $n$-vector of sample means at time $t$, where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$ are the deviations from the equilibrium means $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^e$. Consider OLS and Aitken's GLS cross-sectional regressions of the sample means on the betas, i.e., $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t^s$ on $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{mt}$. The intercept $\theta_{Int}$ and slope $\theta_{Slt}$ from these regressions are estimates of the intercepts and slopes of the CAPM means in equation (47.2). Let $\mathbf{W}$ be the weighting matrix for an Aitken GLS regression. The general linear model may be written as $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta} + \mathbf{e}$. The OLS and GLS coefficients are $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{y}$, respectively, where $\mathbf{X}=(\iota:\boldsymbol{\beta}_{mt})$ and $\mathbf{y} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t^s$.

The OLS intercept and slope minimize the Euclidean norm $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t'\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$. The Aitken's GLS intercept and slope minimize $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t'\mathbf{W}_t^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$. When $\mathbf{W}_t = \boldsymbol{\Omega}_t$, where $\boldsymbol{\Omega}_t$ is the covariance of residual returns from the time-series regressions in equation (47.4), the GLS estimator minimizes $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t'\boldsymbol{\Omega}_t^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$.
