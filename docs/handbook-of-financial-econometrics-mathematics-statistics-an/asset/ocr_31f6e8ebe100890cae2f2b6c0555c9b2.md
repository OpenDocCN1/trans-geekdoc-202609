However, it can promote synchronization of economic variables across the nations such as business cycles and asset prices (Beine et al., 2010; Wälti, 2011; Asgharian et al., 2013; Ductor Leiva-Leon, 2016), which affects the stock market dynamic interdependence and promotes the transmission of financial crises or the so-called crisis contagion.

The last three decades have witnessed a series of financial crises with significant impact on the regional and global economic performance: the 1994 Mexican devaluation peso crisis, the 1997 East Asian crisis, the 1998 Russian collapse, the 1999 Brazilian devaluation, the 1998-2002 Argentine economic crisis, the 2008 USA Subprime mortgage crisis and the 2015 Chinese stock market crash. Figure 36.1 shows that for example: (i) In 1994, the Mexican stock market crashed; this decline rapidly spread to other markets: European, American and Asian stock markets. (ii) In 1997, the collapse of the Hong Kong market quickly affected the world's major stock markets. (iii) At the end of 2001, the Argentina market had cratered; this plunge was rapidly reflected in the main global markets. (iv) In September 2008, the global stock markets declined in the wake of the Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy. (v) The 2015 turmoil period in China affected major stock markets around the globe.

These brief observations show that market crashes are rapidly spreading from one stock market to another around the world. The central questions that this study focuses on are: are these linkages temporary or permanent
