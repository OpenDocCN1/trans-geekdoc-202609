The purpose of the diversification optimization is to determine the set of capital allocation weights on assets. There are two alternative objective functions for this optimization: to maximize the expected return of the portfolio given a maximum level of risk, or to minimize the portfolio risk given a minimum level of expected return. The optimization problem has a closed-form analytical solution, corresponding to a hyperbola the upper part of which is called the efficient frontier where each point corresponds to an efficient portfolio, i.e., an optimal portfolio with the maximum expected return for a given level of risk. According to today’s knowledge and literature, the limitations of Markowitz's classical portfolio theory are obvious: (1) the use of Normal distributions to model asset returns, (2) the correlations between asset returns are fixed and constant forever, (3) the use of variance as risk measure which penalizes not only down-side risk but also the up-side returns, (4) implementation of a viable portfolio construction procedure is plagued by technical difficulties stemming from the instability of the optimization problem with respect to the available data.

Follow-on researchers on portfolio theory have advanced with more and better optimization functions and computational implementation approaches, but still sticking to the general principles of the portfolio theory. Nowadays, financial academics call Markowitz’s MVM the classical version of the modern portfolio theory, while the later developments with more elaborate risk measures and optimization objective functions and procedures as the post-modern portfolio theory, or the general portfolio theory.

From the perspective of the chronological context, the general post-modern portfolio theory includes the following major movements:

- 1. General equilibrium models of capital markets derived from the classic portfolio theory, mainly the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) (Sharpe, 1964) and the Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) (Ross, 1976).
- 2. Mean-Risk Models (MRM) for portfolio management based on better risk measures (Markowitz, 1959; Roy, 1952; Fishburn, 1977; Bawa, 1978; Ogryczak and Ruszczynski, 1999; G-30 1993; Artzner et al., 1999; Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000; Konno et al., 1993; Dentcheva and Ruszczynski, 2006; Roman et al., 2006; Roman and Mitra, 2009).
- 3. Dynamic Portfolio Theories, including momentum, multi-period, Bayesian learning and Knightian uncertainty, etc. (Mossin, 1968; Li et al., 1998; Li and Ng, 2000; Yu and Hiroshi, 2007; Black and Litterman, 1992; Hoeting et al., 1999; Brandt, 2010; Avramov and Zhou, 2010; Wachter, 2010; Dow and Werlang, 1992; Epstein and Schneider, 2010; Guidolin and Rinaldi, 2010).
- 4. Active Portfolio Theories, including fundamental theorem of active investing, style switch, etc. (Grinold, 1989; Grinold and Kahn, 1994, 1999; Clarke, 2002, 2006; Qian and Hua, 2004; Buckle, 2004; Sorensen et al., 2004; Zhou, 2008; Chincarini and Kim, 2006; Zhou and Jain, 2014).
- 5. Other developments in portfolio theory, including robustness considerations, behavior finance, considerations on liquidity, non-utility-based, family asset allocation and family wealth management, etc. (Fabozzi et al., 2010; Fabozzi and Markowitz, 2011).

From now on, we shall call the modern and post-modern portfolio theories, including dynamic and active portfolio theories and all related branches, the Quantitative Portfolio Theory (QPT). The Intelligent Portfolio Theory (IPT) is the so-called relative to the existing QPT, and it is actually motivated and driven by the outstanding problems existent in the QPT.

## 43.2.4 Outstanding problems in quantitative portfolio theory

Looking back, the Quantitative Portfolio Theory (QPT) has started from Markowitz’s Mean-Variance Model (MVM), and then evolved through CAPM, APT, various mean-risk models, multi-period dynamic portfolio models, active portfolio management, and extended to robust portfolio implementation, considerations on liquidity, as well as behavior finance, etc. Finally the QPT has entered into a passive versus active bifurcation. The passive approach of portfolio management follows the efficient market theory (EMT), and tracks a benchmark index with a market-averaging portfolio. The active approach seems to break off the constraints of the EMT, but actually has still entered into the zero-sum game of capital markets. Therefore, it is still not conclusive whether the passive approach or the active one is superior. We believe it is hard to have a conclusion because the competition among the investors in the financial markets always exist.

We have found out, however, that irrespective of the passive or the active approach in portfolio management approach or models, there exists a few seemingly simple but actually extremely complex and critical problems outstanding:

- Problem 1: Division and integration of portfolio management and trading strategy implementation (divide and conquer of the complexity).
- Problem 2: Periodic or just-in-time adjustment or rebalance of the portfolio.
- Problem 3: Time frame or scale or horizon of portfolio management.
- Problem 4: Long-only portfolio with long-short trading.
- Problem 5: Multi-source financial information fusion in the big data era.
- Problem 6: Context-dependence and market ecology underlying portfolio management.

These outstanding problems provide motivations to the construction and development of the Intelligent Portfolio Theory (IPT), the principle and framework of which are presented in the following section.

## 43.3 Basic Intelligent Portfolio Theory — Principles and Framework of Intelligent Portfolio Management

The proposal of the Intelligent Portfolio Theory (IPT) is motivated by and is meant to address the two strategic issues — implementation of the portfolio theory and keeping pace with the times — and the six research problems as listed in the section 43.2.4. The separation and then integration of portfolio management and trading strategies lead to providing a natural framework for these issues and problems, and to open new possibilities for further research and development.

### 43.3.1 Hypotheses and principles of the intelligent portfolio theory

### H1: Intelligent Market Hypothesis (IMH)

All financial market participants as decision makers and trade executors, whether institutional investors or risk managers, individuals, or automated trading system software, are intelligent trading agents. The intelligence of a trading agent is exhibited in its ability or skills to process or understand market information, to make investment decisions and to realize trade execution. A financial market as a whole is a set of interacting trading agents, its behaviors must exhibit more or less intelligence such as ability to process and comprehend information and to adapt to the changing economic conditions. Due to differences in the abilities of the agents, the market can hardly reach and maintain efficient modes, either in strong form, or weak form or semi-strong form of efficiency. The adaptability of the agents will drive the market to swing between the efficient and inefficient modes. This kind of market efficiency swing provides opportunities for intelligent trading agents to gain excessive returns.

Note that this Intelligent Market Hypothesis (IMH) is a deepening extension of the “Adaptive Market Hypothesis” of Lo (2004), and of the “Dynamic Market Hypothesis” of Pan (2004, 2011) (previously also termed “Swingtum Market Hypothesis” in Pan (2004)). Lo’s Adaptive Market Hypothesis mainly focuses on the adaptability of the market, which is actually a consequence of the intelligence ability of the market. However, intelligence ability includes not only adaptability, but also learning, competition, gaming, as well as technical advancement. Pan’s Dynamic Market Hypothesis does point out that the market tends to swing between efficient and inefficient modes, which, however, does not mention the underlying market structure — the intelligent trading agents. It is thus not difficult to see that both the Adaptive and Dynamic Market Hypotheses are logical derivations from the Intelligent Market Hypothesis.

### H2: Limited Rationality Hypothesis

> *Any trading strategy (a trading agent with its decision logic and control mechanism) is rational, profit-seeking and risk-averse. However, the rationality — the intelligence level or ability — of any trading strategy is limited, meaning that there is simply no trading strategy in existence which is always profitable in all market conditions or over all periods of time.*

We see that any investment approach or portfolio model must be implemented on trading individual assets. A general process of trading a single asset involves mainly three stages: (1) entry conditions, timing, and execution, (2) position management after entry, including setting and managing profit targets and stoploss levels or conditions, adding to or reducing positions, (3) exit conditions, timing (either profit taking or stoploss triggered), and execution. This process is not just for a single trade, but rather a repeated loop of condition matching and trade execution, typically a rule-based system. Taking care of the terminology used by the financial traders, the term “trading strategy” so called in this chapter refers to a complete trading logic and its implementation, which actually corresponds to a self-contained autonomous decision-making and trade-executing trading agent. In default with no specific mentioning of its implementational form, a “trading agent” can be a human trader, or an investment institution (such as a fund), or an autonomous trading program (software) which directly operates in the market.
