To lend further credibility to the above determination of dates for structural breaks and to attempt to link them to alternative economic environments, we collect data on the real gross domestic product over our sample period put out by the US Bureau of Economic Analysis and obtained from FRED. We present this plot in Figure 61.2. In the Figure 61.2 plot, the grey bars represent recessionary periods. From this chart, we can see clear delineation of changes in real GDP. In early 2000 and perhaps due to the tech boom, changes in real GDP are very strong and then decline until it is near zero in the fourth quarter of 2001 indicative of a recession. By the second quarter of 2002, it appears that the changes stabilized and increased into the third quarter. Implementation of the Chow F-test led us to conclude that our first structural break occurred during the third quarter of 2002 during which changes in real GDP indicated we were pulling into a new business cycle characterized by stronger growth. We also observe that overall the first period appears to be most closely associated with a “normal” economic environment, which does have up and down swings in cycles. The tech boom caused an unusually large upswing early in the first quarter, but real GDP began to normalize by 2001 and then slowly moved back up into 2002. As we note above, pulling into the third quarter of 2003, we see that the economy is associated with very strong growth and this strong upward trending growth continues into 2004. However, by the third quarter of 2006, it appears that the economy has begun to slow down and return to the level of changes observed during our “normal” economic environment. The implementation of the Chow F-test confirms the finding of a structural break in the third quarter of 2006. Putting all the above information together, we call the second sub-period an economic “boom” period. As we move into 2007, although we see an initial upswing in real GDP, we start to see a precipitous decline changes in real GDP as we hit the global financial crisis of 2007-2010. Real GDP hits its low point in the second quarter of 2009. Gust et al. (2017) find that the ZLB accounted for about 30% of the contractions that occurred in 2009. The Chow test supports that there was a structural change in performance during the fourth quarter of 2008, specifically in November. The Fed began to keep the target rate near zero in this quarter. We call this third sub-period the “recession” period. Finally, in 2009, the economy starts to pull out of the recession and as the real GDP data indicate, it remained stable.
