In this regard, Bollen and Pool (2009) illustrate that when managers have the opportunity to choose to report a loss or gain, they overwhelmingly choose to report positive performance results and they illustrate this by showing that by using a cross-section of historical hedge fund returns, there is a discontinuity at zero. The researchers provide a few explanations for this discontinuity, which are as follows: (a) they may optimistically value the illiquid securities in their portfolios, or (b) they may overvalue their securities similar to how investors have been shown to be overly optimistic about their investments (Bollen et al., 2009). Jorion and Schwarz (2014) question whether this kink in the distribution of returns is a result of purposeful manipulation by hedge fund managers and conclude that there is not enough proof because incentive fees may affect the performance results. Cassar and Gerakos (2001) added some further insight as to whether hedge fund managers purposefully smooth their returns and conclude that when it is difficult to verify pricing and hedge fund managers have more discretion over pricing, the hedge funds are likely to exhibit greater levels of smoothing; however, traditional controls, like oversight, do not lead to lower levels of smoothing.

On the other hand, Cummings and Dai (2010) claim that the legal structure of the hedge fund matters in terms of potential misreporting of returns and illustrate that there is a positive relationship between hedge funds structured to permit wrapper distributions and misreported returns. Likewise, Agarwal et al. (2011) found that hedge fund managers are likely to inflate their returns in December and fund manager incentives are positively related to this spike but find that these managers seem to under-report their returns throughout the year and only find weak evidence of borrowing returns from the ensuing year. Aragon and Nanda (2017) find evidence that hedge fund managers have a propensity to delay reporting negative performance results as hedge fund managers’ report performance results that exhibit clustered behavior and are typically reversals to poor performance results.
