Typical examples are ARCH model of Engle (1982) and the GARCH model of Bollerslev (1986) for the former and the stochastic volatility (SV) model of Taylor (1986) for the latter. Both these volatility models are shown to be capable of capturing the stylized facts of financial returns such as the fat tails and the volatility clustering (Taylor, 1986).

The literature on the three methods mentioned above are vast, and most studies are focused on simple returns or returns of short-horizon. However, for many important financial products in actuarial business, such as the equity-linked options issued by insurance companies, the contract duration typically lasts for 10–30 years, or more. In order for the insurance companies to manage the long-term market risk, it is of central importance to have an accurate and reliable estimate of the VaR for returns of long horizon. The VaR analysis for returns of long-horizon is much more difficult than that for simple returns because the number of observations reduces significantly as the horizon becomes very long. By using the bootstrap method, the properties of long-horizon US stock market returns are examined in Fama and French (2018). In particular, Fama and French (2018) found that distributions of continuously compounded returns converge to normal distributions if the horizon is stretched from 1 to 30 years. Indeed, the studies of long-horizon returns have received increasingly more attention recently (see, for example, Boudoukh, Richardson, and Whitelaw (2008), Neuberger (2012) and Lee (2013)) as many important statistical issues concerning returns of long-term investments need to be addressed.

Motivated by the fact that large institutional investors, such as insurance companies, pension funds, and sovereign funds, usually set a very long investment horizon for their portfolios, Ho et al. (2016) (as HCT-1) proposed a non-parametric estimator for the VaR of the integrated return. Considering the framework of a more general multivariate stochastic volatility model, Ho et al. (2018) (as HCT-2) focused on the CTE case and obtained two important results. First, an asymptotic formula for the CTE is derived when T is large. Second, HCT-2 used the formula to construct an estimator for the CTE of integrated returns, and show that the estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal. In this chapter, we apply the results of HCT-2 to test the equality of two independent CTEs. The rest of this chapter is organized as follows. In Section 72.2, we briefly review the results of HCT-1 and HCT-2. The problem of the comparison between the CTEs generated from two samples of returns are considered in Section 72.3 followed by some simulation studies conducted in Section 72.4. Section 72.5 considers a simple application and Section 72.6 concludes.
