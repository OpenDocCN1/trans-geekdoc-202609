
where \(E[R_{i,t}]\) is the expected rate of return for the individual hedge fund return series in time \(t\), \(R_{\text{Micro}}\) is the return on the Wilshire Micro Cap Index, \(R_{\text{Global Stocks}}\) is the return of the MSCI World Index, \(R_{\text{NASDAQ}}\) is the return associated with the NASDAQ index, \(R_{\text{SmallCap}}\) provides the returns associated with the 2,000 smallest stocks based on market capitalization in the Russell 3000 Index, \(R_{\text{Gold}}\) is the return associated with the S&P GSCI Gold Index, \(R_{\text{GlobalHY Bonds}}\) is the return associated with the Bank of America/Merrill Lynch Global High Yield Index, \(R_{\text{CrudeOil}}\) is the return associated with the S&P GSCI Crude Oil Index, \(R_{\text{Commodities}}\) is the return associated with the Goldman Sachs (GS) Commodities Index, \(R_{\text{GlobalCorpBonds}}\) is the return series associated with the Bank of America/Merrill Lynch Global Corporate Bond Index, \(R_{\text{USAggBond}}\) is the return series associated with the Barclays U.S. Aggregate Bond Index, \(R_{\text{GlobalAggBond}}\) is the return series associated with the Barclays Global Aggregate Bond Index, \(\beta_1\)–\(\beta_{11}\) are the regression coefficients associated with each independent variable, \(\alpha_i\) is the slope coefficient associated with the regression model, and \(\varepsilon_i\) is an error term associated with the estimated regression.

To transition into our discussion of extreme bounds analysis (EBA), the general form regression could be rewritten following Mumtaz et al. (2016) as follows:

\[
R_i = \alpha_0 + \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j X_{j,i} + \varepsilon_i. \tag{91.10}
\]

where \(R_i\) is the return on the hedge fund index, \(\alpha_0\) is the intercept in a regression framework, \(\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j X_{j,i}\), which should read the sum of all explanatory variables' returns multiplied by their coefficient, which is the estimated relationship between variable \(j\) and the dependent variable, and \(\varepsilon_i\) is a standard error term. When applying this to evaluate the robustness and sensitivity of the variables used to describe hedge fund performance, the Mumtaz et al. (2016) model is modified as follows:

\[
R_i = \alpha_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{n} \delta_j X_{j,i} + \beta Q_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n} \gamma_j Z_{j,i} + \varepsilon_i. \tag{91.11}
\]

As indicated in Mumtaz et al. (2016), the \(X_{j,i}\) term is the \(j\)th variable of interest that explains the individual hedge fund return series that is indicated to have a statistically significant relationship with hedge fund performance, the \(Q_i\) variable is a potential variable of interest for which the robustness is examined, and \(Z_{j,i}\) are potentially important explanatory variables. When using this method, hundreds of regressions are run to test the robustness and sensitivity of the \(Q_i\) variable to changes in the model. According to Mumtaz et al. (2016), “if the value of the coefficient has the same sign and its extreme value remains statistically significant, then it is a ‘robust’ variable; otherwise, it is reported as a ‘fragile’ variable” (p. 76).

Before attempting to replicate a return series, it is critical to have a clear understanding of what is driving hedge fund performance. In this section, we have illustrated that hedge fund returns are replicable using assets that are readily investable and have provided a basic well-specified and robust model of hedge fund performance.

## 91.4 Concluding Remarks

In this chapter, we have highlighted the general problems that researchers and practitioners face when they attempt to model and understand hedge fund performance. At the forefront, they have to deal with the opaque nature of hedge fund returns, but as we have previously indicated that opacity enables hedge fund managers to generate returns that feel different from traditional asset classes. As we provided illustrations of models aimed at estimating hedge fund returns, however, we illustrated that even with this opacity, hedge fund managers seem to be relying on instruments that are investable in the marketplace but accessing these risk exposures in a different manner. The research that we have reviewed for this chapter indicates that taking the opaque shell away from hedge fund managers will likely have an impact on the returns that these managers are able to produce.

The staleness of pricing related to hedge fund returns seems to be a very complicated issue and its impact is felt both in the explanations of hedge fund performance and attempts to model hedge fund performance. In the model of hedge fund returns that was presented in this chapter, the lagged previous month’s hedge fund returns had a statistically significant impact on the current month’s performance results. This finding, in similar studies, has led to a barrage of studies that attempt to illustrate why hedge fund pricing and performance are stale. Studies have claimed that managers purposely misreported performance results and used the serial correlation in hedge fund performance results as evidence that they are misleading investors. This general argument does not alone prove that hedge fund managers are maliciously deceiving investors and, as we have shown, serial correlations of similar magnitudes are found in publicly traded investment vehicles. Studies that aim to evaluate the performance of hedge funds based on the delay in reporting performance results seem to be a useful pursuit to add to the body of literature on hedge fund performance. Moreover, in terms of estimating or identifying the determinants of hedge fund performance, it is useful to start by examining the relationship between hedge fund performance and investment vehicles that are investable. This chapter reviewed examples of attempts to model hedge fund returns and illustrated why it is helpful to use an approach that focuses on developing a model that employs investable indices. Using these model estimates as a basis, investors and researchers could explore more complicated replication strategies in an attempt to construct passive hedge fund indices.

The current body of literature on hedge fund performance provides a solid basis to think about hedge fund returns and how to interpret the returns reported by hedge fund managers. The ultimate goal to replicate hedge fund returns and passively exploit investment factors that automate the hedge fund experience should start with an exploration of determinants of hedge fund performance that can be obtained using passive and investable indices. Once that basis is provided, as this chapter illustrates, investors and practitioners may be able to experiment with advanced replication strategies to attempt to generate a similar experience with less cost, which would lead to an improved investment experience. In conclusion, this chapter offers key suggestions for financial market participants such as hedge fund managers, portfolio managers, risk managers, regulatory bodies, financial analysts, and investors in regard to their evaluation and interpretation of hedge fund performance. In addition, this critical review chapter can benefit investors, portfolio managers, and researchers in the establishment of a yardstick for the assessment of hedge fund performance and the performance of assets that have stale pricing and are relatively opaque.
