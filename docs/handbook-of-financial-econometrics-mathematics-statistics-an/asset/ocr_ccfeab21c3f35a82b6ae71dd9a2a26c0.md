The later two types — PATF and EATF — belong to the category of expectation-based active strategies of trend following, which are more or less active, while the former two types — RPTF and MPTF — belong to the category of passive trend following.

# 43.5 Investability Map in the Confluence of Business and Market Cycles and Sector and Location Rotations

Now we have two constructs: The Intelligent Portfolio Theory provides a framework of risk diversification through two-level portfolios — a multi-asset portfolio of multi-strategy portfolios; the methodology of strength investing provides a driving force and engine for concentration on strong markets, strong sectors and strong assets (e.g., stocks) through a mechanism of hierarchical optimal focus of attention. Now still the third construct is needed in order to put the theory and the methodology into practical implementation and applications, which addresses where to find the market strength or investability in the global, national or regional financial markets, including stocks, commodities, forex, bonds, as well as real estate.

In a bigger sense, what is needed is a dynamic map of investability in terms of market trend strength including the quality, reliability and phase of trend. Theoretically, the opportunities of investment will emerge in the confluence of two types of cycles — business and market, and two types of rotations — sector and location. In general, stock markets, commodities, currencies, and real estate are large asset classes, between them there must also be money flows. However, we limit the concept of sector rotation to the stock market sectors, and we may only marginally mention real estate markets.

Business cycle has been one of the most important areas of academic research in macro-economics, we shall not delve into this topic here. A key aspect of relevance we want to point out is that there is a time delay or phase shift between the stock market cycle and the business cycle for a national economy, and in general the stock market cycle lead the business cycle with about half a year lead time as shown in Figure 43.3. Sector rotation refers to the phenomenon that the strength (lead–lag order) of the sectors rotate one after another. As a theory of stock market trading patterns, sector rotation recognizes three facts: First, whatever sector is hot (has done well recently) should continue to outperform; Second, these sectors will eventually rotate so that whatever was once out of favor will be in favor. Third, these movements are somewhat predictable, and connected with the business cycle. Erickson
