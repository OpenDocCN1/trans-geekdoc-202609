In this chapter, we shall focus on a cash conversion system that resides in a subsidiary of a parent company, and assume that the parent never goes bankrupt, and therefore, neither does the subsidiary. The treatment in this chapter is based on Melamed and Rogers (2015) and Chen et al. (2017).

Why might a parent company refrain from investing excessive cash in a subsidiary and/or force the subsidiary to pay out all of its earnings, rather than retain some of them? One potential answer lies in agency theory. Managers have a choice: they can either efficiently invest funds in activities that create value for the parent company, or waste the funds on activities that benefit them personally. An example of such a wasteful choice is excessive perquisite consumption. Jensen (1986) shows that when monitoring is imperfect, then the accumulation of cash and other very liquid assets permits managers to pursue their own interests rather than working in the interests of the parent company or shareholders. Yafeh and Yosha (2003) finds that relatively high levels of cash suggest that a manager is unable to locate profitable projects in which to invest. Han and Qiu (2007) documents the motivations for financially constrained firms to hold precautionary cash. Yu et al. (2015) verifies that managerial incentives and board monitoring are substitutes for each other, and the substitution effect is pronounced when firms have poorly incentivized managers. In light of the agency-theoretic reason why firms might be capital rationed, and that capital-rationed firms are the most sensitive to having an inadequate amount of cash to fund working capital, it is of interest to study their optimal cash policy.

The approach we take in this chapter in computing financial performance metrics of the system under study is Equilibrium Rate Analysis (ERA); see Melamed and Rogers (2015). In the context of supply chain management, ERA adopts two key elements that facilitate the evaluation and optimization of attendant metrics: first, it assumes a mathematical setting where the underlying system is in equilibrium; and second, it computes rate-oriented metrics instead of cumulative (time-additive) ones. The first element takes advantage of major computational simplifications afforded by equilibrium settings (that is, replacing time-dependent metrics with time-invariant counterparts). The second element brings about conceptual simplifications from which cumulative time-dependent metrics are readily obtained for both undiscounted and discounted cases. In fact, the time invariance of equilibrium rates leads trivially to the same optimized solutions for both the undiscounted case as well as its discounted counterpart. For more details, refer to ibid.
