
## 131.6.1 Regulation and discretionary loan default risk management

The SOX and DF legislation affects the risk management decisions made in banks, and each piece of legislation affects loan loss expense volume distinctly. Loan loss provisioning with current regulatory controls has been deterred. Increased interest income and successful collections or delinquency reduction reduce loss provision expense. Increased specialization in lending to specific industries tends to increase loss provision expenses, but the advent of the DFA offsets much of this finding for commercial loans. This observation presumes that DFA regulations affect bank manager proclivity to accept more risky loan opportunities. This in itself will result in a reduced allocation for risk reserves. The reduced risk reserve cushion is complemented by a reduction in loan loss provision expenses associated with improved governance activities as required by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.

Banks experience a complementary effect between SOX and DFA on loan loss provisions. Improved governance procedures to establish policy responses to non-performing loans result in one of the largest estimated coefficient values in the statistical model while still providing incentives for growth. Yet DFA affects loan loss provisions distinctly, with estimated coefficients for DFA having the opposite signs when compared to corresponding SOX counterparts for delinquent loan volume and for asset growth. This argues for careful policymaking which generates, or enforces, attention to risk as part of the lending culture of a bank. Indeed, these results indicate increased lending activity reduces loan loss provisions only when making relatively profitable loans regardless of the regulatory environment. The distinct roles of controls impact non-performing loan volume management. Incentives for firm growth, income, capital, and loan specialization under the two regulatory environments make the loan risk management process more complex and under increasing influence by federal banking regulation.

## 131.6.2 Regulation and market structure

A model has also been provided to discuss potential consequences of structural change on the competitive interactions of banks and other financial institutions, with emphasis on non-metro areas. Managers in non-metro financial institutions need to be cognizant of the competitive alignment within the local market sphere. The presence of financial organization types will impact the firm’s responsiveness to market forces, product mixes and operating efficiencies.

As financial service markets within the non-metro sector shift with other competitive market entrants, managerial awareness to alter consumer offerings is necessitated. The ability of the non-metro financial institution to improve efficiencies while adapting to competition is critical to firm performance. Given that many non-metro financial markets have a varying set of competitive organizations, market awareness is a key to the successful service array delivery and consumer product identification. X-efficiency within a competitive environment encourages financial institutions to optimize the overall cost function in an effort to achieve optimum outputs, in this case banking products and services. Community banks develop market-based products based on the firms’ existing cost structure.
