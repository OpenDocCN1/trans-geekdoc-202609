
## Time-Frequency Wavelet Analysis of Stock-Market Co-Movement

Yang et al., 2003; Glezakos et al., 2007; Fraser and Oyefeso, 2005; Psillaki and Margaritis, 2008; An and Brown, 2010; Palamalai et al., 2013; Guidi and Ugur, 2014; Patel, 2017; Vo and Ellis, 2018).

The researches cited above exemplify the relationship between stock markets in the time domain. However, taking into account the frequency domain in this area of research makes the co-movement analysis more comprehensive and useful for investors. Indeed, according to Müller et al. (1997), the stock markets consist of heterogeneous investors operating at different time scales, and their investment horizons vary from a short to long term. Thus, the assessment of co-movement in terms of frequency is very crucial for investors when choosing a short- or long-term profile in investment strategies (Smith, 2001). Short-term investment horizons are related to speculative activities while long-term investment horizons are related to investment activities. To understand the relationship between stock markets in both time and frequency representations, we refer to the econophysics domain which is a discipline that deals with economic systems using the thinking from physics.¹ This chapter employs the wavelet approach to carry out the examination.² This approach has the advantage of showing the variation of a time series over the time domain without losing the frequency domain. Thus, the wavelet analysis takes both domains into account and provides a unified framework to examine co-movement in the time–frequency domains.

The aim of this chapter is to study the relationship of stock markets across intra and inter regional blocs in the time–frequency domains by means of wavelet correlation, wavelet coherence and wavelet multiple cross-correlation. The wavelet correlation investigates the degree of dependency between two assets. The wavelet coherence investigates the degree of similarity between two series and it reveals the phase and the lead–lag relationship. The cross-correlation function considers two or more series not only simultaneously, but also with a time shift. The wavelet multiple cross-correlation studies the correlation within multiple assets and determines which market has a potential to lead or follow the other markets. The study investigates the stock market co-movement between 13 developed EU stock markets, two developed North American capital markets, six developing Latin American equity markets, 10 developing Asian stock markets, one developed Asian stock market, two developed European asset markets and another developed stock market. The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows: Section 36.2 presents the data, the descriptive statistics, and introduces the wavelet transform, the notions of wavelet coherence, correlation, cross-correlation and multiple cross-correlation. In Section 36.3, we present the results of the wavelet computation. Finally, Section 36.4 concludes with a summary of the main findings.

### 36.2 Data Description and Methodology

#### 36.2.1 Descriptive statistics

Our data set consists of daily stock indices of 13 developed EU equity markets (France, Germany, Spain, Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, Greece, Austria, Denmark, Finland, Portugal, United Kingdom and Sweden), two developed North American stock markets (United States and Canada), six developing Latin American capital markets (Argentina, Mexico, Chile, Peru, Colombia and Venezuela), one developed Asian stock market (Japan), 10 developing Asian equity markets (Hong Kong, Singapore, India, Indonesia, South Korea, Thailand, China, Taiwan, Sri Lanka and Malaysia), two developed European stock markets (Norway and Switzerland) and another developed stock market (Australia).³

The sample consists of 5674 daily observations covering the period from December 31, 1993 to September 30, 2015. The data are extracted from the DataStream database, Quandl Financial, Economic Data and Investing.com. We use the log price change of stock price indices to resolve the problem of non-synchronous trading:

r_t = Log(P_t / P_{t-1}), (36.1)

where P_t and P_{t-1} represent the stock price index at time t and t − 1, respectively.

To examine the index return distributions, we begin with descriptive statistics.⁴ During the period under study, except Greece, Japan and Thailand, all other markets showed positive average daily returns. The highest average daily return within the group came from Venezuela (0.001185) followed by Argentina (0.000498), Mexico (0.000492), India (0.000379), Denmark (0.000371), Indonesia (0.000347), Colombia (0.000327), Finland (0.000309), Sweden (0.000308) and Sri Lanka (0.000307). The lowest average daily return is recorded for Greece (−4.954 × 10^-5), Japan (−2.946 × 10^-7), Portugal (2.873 × 10^-5), Malaysia (4.228 × 10^-5), Austria (5.165 × 10^-5), Singapore (5.615 × 10^-5), Taiwan (6.793 × 10^-5), Italy (8.513 × 10^-5) and Hong Kong (9.899 × 10^-5). These statistics suggest that the developing Latin American stock markets outperformed the other regional stock markets.

The most volatile stock market is Argentina, with a standard deviation of 0.0217 followed by Greece (0.0199), China (0.0196), Thailand (0.01909), Finland (0.01901), Sweden (0.0179), South Korea (0.0172), Venezuela (0.0167), Hong Kong (0.0162) and Austria (0.0161). The least volatile market is Chile, with a standard deviation of 0.0086, followed by Australia (0.0096), Colombia (0.0101), Canada (0.0105) and Peru (0.0106). Furthermore, except Venezuela, Mexico, Chile, Sweden, Hong Kong, Thailand, China, Sri Lanka and Malaysia, all the other stock returns are negatively skewed. This means that the tremendous negative returns of equities are more evident than the enormous positive returns. The highest skewness belongs to Venezuela (2.3200) while the kurtosis statistics show that all stock return series are leptokurtic. The highest excess kurtosis is witnessed by Malaysia (55.069) followed by Venezuela (43.189) and China (23.975). The distribution of the returns series is not normal with a kurtosis exceeding three in all cases and showing a skewness different from zero. The Jarque–Bera statistics and p-values, on the other hand, strongly reject the null hypothesis of normal distribution.

The correlation matrix for the stock market returns shows that the correlation coefficients across all the stock market returns are positive. The strongest correlation is between France and Netherlands (0.8905), followed by France and Germany (0.8630), France and Spain (0.8428), France and Italy (0.8256), USA and Canada (0.7171), Canada and Mexico (0.5248), and Hong Kong and Japan (0.4791). The weakest correlation is between Venezuela and Sri Lanka (0.0036) followed by Venezuela and Greece (0.0203), Venezuela and India (0.0256), Venezuela and Denmark (0.0293), Venezuela and Austria (0.0372), Venezuela and USA (0.0529) and Venezuela and Belgium (0.0658). So, we notice that Venezuela is weakly correlated with most of the world stock markets. Furthermore, we notice that the correlation within regional blocs is high. Indeed, the developed EU stock markets present the highest correlation coefficients (from 0.3032 to 0.8905), followed by the developing Asian stock markets (from 0.2511 to 0.4671) and the developing Latin American stock markets (from 0.1934 to 0.4387). The correlation between regional blocs is also high. In fact, the correlation coefficients range from 0.1473 to 0.5560.

#### 36.2.2 Methodology

In this study, we use the wavelet approach to examine the linkage between the global stock market returns. In this section, we give a brief overview of the wavelet analysis approach. The fundamental idea of this approach is to decompose a time series into time-frequency domains. There are generally two ways of decomposition under the wavelet method: the discrete wavelet transform (DWT) and the continuous wavelet transform (CWT). The application of DWT in economics and finance was done by Gençay et al. (2001a, 2001b, 2005), Gallegati and Gallegati (2007), Gallegati (2008), Ozun and Cifter (2010) and Habimana (2017), among others. Recently, the CWT was used for the purpose of economic and financial data analysis by Aguiar-Conraria et al. (2008), Vacha and Barunik (2012), Vacha et al. (2013), Aguiar-Conraria and Soares (2014), Sousa et al. (2014), Filip et al. (2016), Kristoufek et al. (2016) and Das et al. (2018), among others. In this research, to transform the stock market return series, we use the CWT (wavelet coherence analysis) and the DWT (wavelet correlation and wavelet multiple cross-correlation). Before introducing wavelet correlation, wavelet coherence and wavelet multiple cross-correlation, we begin by providing basic definitions of wavelet.
