Table 53.3 reports the empirical results of the Chow Test. For each firm, we divide the sample period into pre- and post-announcement periods. We then perform the Chow test to test if there is a structural shift in the systematic risks before and after the announcement of earnings. There are 452,408 out of 987,347 observations (45.82%) exhibiting a structural shift at a 5% confidence level. Further, there are 700,354 out of 987,347 observations (67.57%) exhibiting a structural shift at a 10% confidence level. This evidence indicates that the CARs reported in Table 53.2 and plotted in Figure 53.1 may be misleading because of the failure to take into account structural changes in the market model. Although these empirical results are consistent with previous findings in literature.

The observations in the pre- and post-announcement periods are used to perform the Chow test for testing the hypothesis that there is no structural shift in the systematic risks before and after the announcement of earnings. The test statistic is computed as:

```
F = (CSSSE-(SSE1+SSE2))/K / ((SSE1+SSE2)/(N1+N2-2K))
where SSE1 is the sum of squared errors for the OLS regression using the observations in the pre-announcement period, SSE2 is the sum of squared errors for the OLS regression using the observations in the post-announcement period, CSSSE is the sum of squared errors of the combined OLS regression. K is the number of parameters, N1 is the number of observations in the first separate OLS regression, and N2 is the number of observations in the last separate OLS regression.
```

Based on the model developed in Section 53.2, one may be interested in how the market revises its assessment of risk on earnings announcement in the presence of conditional heteroscedasticity. Figure 53.2 plots the time-varying systematic risk of Portfolio 1 (firms released the most unfavorable earnings) and Portfolio 5 (firms released the most favorable earnings news) following the announcement of earnings by applying the Kalman filter algorithm as illustrated in Appendix 53A. It is also consistent with prior findings that the systematic risk for firms announcing good news are higher than those announcing bad news. However, the mean systematic risks for both portfolios drift upward subsequent to the announcement of earnings. This phenomenon indicates that the CARs estimated with OLS are biased upward for both portfolios.
