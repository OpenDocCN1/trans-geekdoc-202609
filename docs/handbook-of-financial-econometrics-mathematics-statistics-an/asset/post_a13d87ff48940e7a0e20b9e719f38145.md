As a methodology, strength investing focuses attention on leading market forces, concentrates investment on markets or assets which show stronger trends than the majority of the other markets or assets. ## 43.4.2 A methodology of strength investing

Strength investing is based on four assumptions (A):

- **A1:** There is a complete universe of investable assets (or markets).
- **A2:** The totality of attention of the market participants in this asset universe is always preserved. This assumption is referred to as attention preservation.
- **A3:** At any time there is always at least one asset in bull (or bear) market.
- **A4:** Technically it is always possible to shift investment into the leading bull (or bear) markets without waiting for stoploss to be triggered from other invested assets (or markets).

In reality, we can only accept A1 is relatively true, because there is no absolute complete universe of assets where the attention of all the market participants is bounded, so investors only consider to put their capitals within this universe. Examples of such universe are many. Chinese A-shares is typical as Chinese stock investors only consider the universe of A-shares without distraction to commodity futures or foreign exchanges (forex). On the other hand, futures or forex traders also form their own universes of trading.

An operable methodology of strength investing in any given national stock market as illustrated in Fig. 43.2 can be formulated in a procedure as follows:

- **Step 1:** Stock classification or clustering with one or two or three of these approaches: (1) conceptual sectors such as blue chips, growth, small cap stocks, etc; (2) standard industrial classification such as mining, telecom, real estate, etc; (3) stock clustering through statistical or data mining tools, typically using price correlation or cointegration.
- **Step 2:** Tracing leaders (or laggers) in the conceptual sectors, industrial sectors and stock clusters respectively in terms of price strength of each sector relative to a common reference time which should be a market bottom. Note in principle, there can be 1–3 levels of sector classification or clustering.
- **Step 3:** Selecting leaders (or laggers) among the stocks of selected sectors or clusters, still in terms of price strength of each stock relative to a common reference time.
- **Step 4:** Grouping the selected stocks with price strength in a multi-stock portfolio.
- **Step 5:** Constructing a multi-strategy portfolio for each selected stock, so in total, a multi-stock multi-strategy portfolio is constructed.
- **Step 6:** Along with the market moving forward, the comparative strength of each sector or cluster and each stock will change, and even the clustering will produce new clusters. Therefore, there is a need to reiterate the whole process by going back to Step 1.

This procedure is hierarchical in nature, so the stocks with strength, i.e., showing stronger trends are selected with higher quality and reliability of trend because the multi-level strengths from sectors or clusters to individual stocks confirm the trend of the selected stocks with larger background market forces which may come from value appreciation, strategic intent of strategic investors, or herding. The principle here is to stay always with the stronger market forces, so big mistakes or gross errors can be avoided.

This methodology of strength investing can also be called stronger trend following, which is strategically superior than traditional single (asset or market) trend following with following advantages: (1) it shifts investment dynamically to assets (or markets) with stronger trend so the stoploss on the existing positions need not to be triggered, and so the loss due to stoploss triggered is either avoided or minimized; (2) it always concentrates capital on the market leaders with a minimal portfolio, so superior return of investment can be automatically realized.

## 43.4.3 Four trading strategies of trend following

Strength investing technically takes the form of stronger trend following. Apart from strength comparison and selection, it still trades the selected strong assets (or markets) with trend following strategies, except the stoploss, though used as a risk control measure, will much less frequently be triggered, because the capital may be shifted to stronger trending assets before the loss due to stoploss being triggered.

### (1) Rule-Based Passive Trend Following (RPTF):
Basically this type comes from discretionary trading methods and experiences of professional traders. Typical trading strategies of this type are three: (1) Use break out of the price across the upper bound or break down across the lower bound of a price channel as the entry conditions; (2) Use the cross up or down of a fast moving average of a shorter period through a slow moving average of a longer period as the entry or exit conditions; (3) Use the resonance of two- or three-level trends as the entry conditions while using the divergence of these trends as the exit conditions. Often the reciprocal of the entry conditions may be used as the exit conditions. Naturally, and what is often overlooked is that the proper matching or pairing of the entry versus exit conditions for long or short positions is the key to the establishment and effectiveness of the trading strategy. Often, the entry conditions are tougher, including more comprehensive conditions, such as filtering of noise or fake breakout, while the exit conditions may be simpler, which may be triggered by stoploss or take-profit. From the point of view of intelligent finance, the combinations of various entry and exit conditions for long or short trading form a complicated space of patterns, which reflects the limitation of human discretionary trading, and where the deep intelligence trading comes into play.

### (2) Model-Based Passive Trend Following (MPTF):
This type of strategy is based on some stochastic dynamic model of the market price. A typical form is based on geometric Brownian motion and regime-switching models. Dai et al. (2011) developed an optimal trend following trading rule in a bull–bear switching market, where the drift of the stock price switches between two parameters corresponding to an uptrend (bull market) and a downtrend (bear market) according to an unobservable Markov chain. They showed that the optimal trading strategy is a trend following system characterized by the conditional probability in the uptrend (or downtrend) crossing two threshold curves. The thresholds can be obtained by solving the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations. Naturally, this type of model-based trend following can be extended to more realistic models such as Levy process with heavy tails, stochastic volatility, and jump-diffusion process.
