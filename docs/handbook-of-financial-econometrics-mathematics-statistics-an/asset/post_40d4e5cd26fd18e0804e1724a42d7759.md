Chinn and Coibion (2014) examine whether futures prices are unbiased and/or accurate predictors of subsequent prices, by analyzing four commodities including gold. The results show that gold is a poor predictor of subsequent price changes.

In practice, such deviations may be due to asymmetric information as the price may reflect the information earlier in the futures market than the spot market. Evidence from hedging practices shows that firms would hedge only when an expected unfavorable event may occur. In markets with severe information asymmetry in which hedgers are having superior knowledge on supply and demand, such as futures prices, traders would selectively hedge price movements, causing sharp price adjustments upon resolution of information asymmetry (Knill, et al., 2006).

Prior literature related to the gold spot and futures prices are analyzed in fewer studies. Ruan et al. (2016) employ the detrended cross-correlation analysis (DCCA) to examine the correlations between gold spot and futures returns and find that the cross-correlations are significant for all lagged orders and are weakly persistent. Other extant literature mainly focuses on the forecast of gold prices, including the reports of Koutsoyiannis (1983), Baker and Van Tassel (1985), and Pindyck (1993). Other research examining gold related issues is tremendous, including studies on both the linear and nonlinear relationships of gold and oil prices, exchange rates, or the macroeconomic effects (Sherman 1982, 1983; Ariovich, 1983; Fortune, 1987; Dooley et al., 2004; Wang and Lee, 2011; Lee and Zeng, 2011). However, few of them provide a more complete view of possible causal relationships once variables are outside the mean, non-normally distributed or have extreme values. Similarly, Michaud et al. (2006) examined the correlations between gold and other major commodities and reported the low correlations between gold and other commodities. Worthington and Pahlavani (2007) employ a co-integration approach and confirm the effectiveness of gold as an inflation hedge in the short and long run. Wang et al. (2011) apply the threshold models to examine similar issues and conclude that gold acts as an effective inflation hedge instrument as well.

More recently, Adrangi et al. (2000) employed the quantile co-integration to estimate the relationship of gold and silver, and suggested that silver prices respond more strongly to gold prices if silver prices are high. Ciner et al. (2013) also show that the dependencies between the gold and other assets change along with the different quantiles. Pierdzioch et al. (2015, 2016) indicate that the quantile-based approach has an improving performance in forecasting the gold returns due to accounting for the model uncertainty. Schweikert (2018) applies a quantile co-integration model to gold and silver prices and to prices of the corresponding futures contracts. The results find an asymmetric dependence between silver prices and gold prices that a stronger response of silver prices to gold price changes is observed when silver prices are at a relatively high level and vice versa.

In other words, the value of quantile co-integrating coefficients reflects shocks to the prices received in each period. Under these conditions, the possible prices of gold spot and futures are nonlinearly co-integrated with a time-varying and quantile-dependent co-integration, while those of the conventional co-integration analysis may not be detected. Motivated by these works of literature, this study attempts to answer the short- and long-term dependence on the gold spot and futures by employing a novel quantile co-integrating approach developed by Xiao (2009).

Gold has had its dual role both as a currency and a commodity, although after 1978, the gold standard was no longer obtained, and the monetary role of gold slowly faded. Gold is still regarded as an important reserve currency as the monetary properties of gold are vital in affecting its price in case of a credit bubble or other volatile situation. The major gold markets, including London, Zurich, Hong Kong, Tokyo, and New York, constitute a 24/7 cycle of buying and selling, making gold a powerful hedge tool to quickly respond to broader market conditions.

This study contributes to the literature by modeling the state- and time-dependence of the short- and long-run relationships between gold spot and futures prices, and attempts to explain if the gold spot and futures move together in the long-run. The contributions of this study are in several ways. First, we use the quantile co-integration model to examine the relationships of gold spot price and various maturities of gold futures, which allow the co-integrating coefficients to vary over the conditional distribution of gold spot prices. This approach can avoid the coefficient biases once the variables are non-normally distributed or have extreme values. We find that the co-integration of gold spot and futures contracts within six months increases with the quantiles, indicating that the co-integration at high quantile is greater than those observed at low quantiles. As such, the traditional Johansen co-integration would underestimate the co-integration for high quantile memberships. Second, we also find that at the same quantile, the co-integration of short-run gold futures and the gold spot is greater than those observed in the long-run gold futures. ### 92.2.2.1 Model

The concept of co-integration is mainly applied to non-stationary time series variables that produce stationary residuals in models. We apply the quantile co-integrating regression developed by Xiao (2009) to examine the contemporaneous spot and futures gold prices. To avoid endogeneity, we adopted the lead-lag terms in the traditional co-integration model. The equation is specified as follows:

LnGold_{spot_t} = α + β LnGold_{future_t} + Σ_{i=-K}^{K} ω_i Δ LnGold_{future,t-i} + ε_t; (92.1)

where LnGold_{spot} is the spot gold price taken in natural logarithm, LnGold_{future} is futures gold price taken in natural logarithm, and ε denotes a residual. The number K is the length of the lead–lag terms. Here, we set K to be three.¹ In this study, we replace LnGold_{future} with Ln1m, Ln2m, Ln3m, and Ln6m to represent four different maturities of gold futures contracts of 1-, 2-, 3-, and 6-month and the following equations go along with this setting.
