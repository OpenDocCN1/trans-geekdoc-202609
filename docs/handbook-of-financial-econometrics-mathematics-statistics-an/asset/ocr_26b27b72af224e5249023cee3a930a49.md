By the third assumption, the covariance of the random effect and the deviation of the index return from its mean are zero; also, the expected value of the squared errors is equal to the variance of the random effect. Thus,

$$\sigma_i^2 = b_i^2 E(R_{It} - r_I)^2 + E(e_{it})^2$$
$$= b_i^2 \sigma_I^2 + \sigma_{ei}^2. \tag{81.5}$$

This is equivalent to saying that the variance of the returns on a security is made of some adjusted quantity of the variance of the market (usually referred to as systematic risk) plus the variance of the random effects exclusive to that particular security (unsystematic risk).

The last result to be investigated from these assumptions involves a minor step into abstraction in which the possibility of interaction between two securities and the market index is considered. It is suggested that because variations in the returns of two different securities are not interrelated but only connected to the market index, the covariance between the two securities can be derived from the twice-adjusted variance of the market index (once by the b coefficient of the first security with the market and again by the b coefficient of the second security with the market). The investigation starts with a statement about the covariance of the two securities:

$$\sigma_{ij} = E[(R_{it} - r_i)(R_{jt} - r_j)]$$
$$= E[((a_i + b_i R_{It} + e_{it})(a_i + b_i r_I))$$
$$\times ((a_j + b_j R_{It} + e_{jt})(a_j + b_j r_I)]$$
$$= E[(b_i (R_{It} - r_I) + e_{it}) \times (b_j (R_{It} - r_I) + e_{jt})]$$
$$= b_i b_j E(R_{It} - r_I)^2 + b_i E(e_{jt}(R_{It} - r_I))$$
$$+ b_j E(e_{it}(R_{It} - r_I)) + E(e_{it} e_{jt}). \tag{81.6}$$

Since according to the third and fourth assumptions, the last three terms of the last summation are equal to zero:

$$\sigma_{ij} = b_i b_j \sigma_I^2. \tag{81.7}$$
