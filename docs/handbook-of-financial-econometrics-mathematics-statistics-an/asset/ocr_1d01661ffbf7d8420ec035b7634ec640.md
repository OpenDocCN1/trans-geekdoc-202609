# **Abstract**

This chapter investigates the characteristics of a subset of the infinite number of Security Market Lines (SMLs) that ensure the market portfolio is mean–variance efficient both at a point in time and over time. The analysis employs raw rather than excess returns. With some specifications of the SML, the riskfree rate exceeds the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean, which is inconsistent with CAPM theory. At a point in time, a Hotelling’s T² test may reject most of the SMLs or none of them, although other mean–variance criteria may indicate some are economically reasonable and others are not.

# **47.1 Introduction**

The debate as to whether the Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) mean–variance (MV) capital asset pricing model (CAPM) holds is both lengthy and contentious — with strong evidence both for and against the model. Tests of the model are legion. The clear majority concentrates on whether securities plot on the Security Market Line (SML) in mean–beta space. A minority concentrates on whether the market portfolio plots on the efficient frontier in mean–standard deviation space. However, I take a different approach focusing on the weights in the market portfolio. I examine characteristics of a subset of the SMLs that insure the changing-weights market portfolio is MV efficient both at a point in time and over time.¹ I extend the analysis in Best and Grauer (1985), who employed a reverse-engineering approach to show that there are an infinite number of SMLs from a two-parameter family that ensure the market portfolio is MV efficient at a point in time. I specify the two parameters — the intercepts and slopes of the SMLs — in two ways. First, like Best and Grauer, I hold either the market expected return, market risk premium, market mean excess return to variance ratio, or market mean excess return to standard deviation ratio constant over time. Second, I extend the analysis minimizing a variety of distances between the sample means and different sets of SML means at a point in time. Cross-sectional tests of the CAPM focus on whether the slope of a regression of sample means on betas is statistically significantly different from zero.
