## Abstract

This study investigates the role of financial advisors on the influence of deal outcomes in M&As. In particular, this study examines whether firms hired by domestic financial advisors can outperform those by foreign counterparts. Using 333 targets and 949 bidders from 1995 to 2011, the results show that targets take more (less) time to complete the deals when hiring low-reputable (foreign) financial advisors. When bidders hire low-reputable financial advisors and foreign advisors, bidders can complete the deals faster. In addition, the evidence indicates that low-reputable financial advisors create higher gains to both targets and bidders around the announcement date. However, bidders hired by less prestigious financial advisors suffer larger losses during post-announcement period. Interestingly, when hiring domestic advisors, both targets and bidders obtain higher announcement returns. The regression analysis further reveals that bidders obtain higher post-announcement returns when bidders hire domestic advisors. Hence, this study reveals that domestic advisors play an important role in M&As.

### 76.1 Introduction

Merger and acquisition (M&A) activities significantly increase during the last decade. The increase of M&As derives from globalization and industry consolidation. A large number of M&A activities provide relatively large incentives to investment banks. Investment banks can offer their expertise to advise their clients during the process of the transactions in return for advisory fees. Kale et al. (2003) argue that financial advisors have more abilities to identify potential firms and can then create synergies to their clients in M&As. Cao and Madura (2013) argue that financial advisors can reduce information asymmetry between targets and bidders, and can accelerate the matching process. Schiereck et al. (2009) and Wang and Whyte (2010) also argue that investment banks provide their knowledge and expertise to evaluate the transactions and then increase the speed of deal completion. Hence, the presence of financial advisors can assist their clients to facilitate the transactions and negotiate better terms.

A growing body of existing literatures has examined the quality of financial advisors on firm performance in M&As (e.g., Servaes and Zenner, 1996; Kale et al., 2003; Wang and Whyte, 2010; Golubov et al., 2012; Ma, 2013; Song et al., 2013; Chang et al., 2016); Chuang, 2017; Bi and Wang, 2018). Prior studies report mixed evidence to determine the quality of financial advisors on firm performance in M&As. More importantly, none of prior studies examines domestic and foreign advisors on the impact of firm performance in M&As. It is unknown as to whether firms hired by domestic advisors can outperform those with foreign counterparts. With regard to the empirical evidence, Walter et al. (2008) report that targets hired by more prestigious financial advisors obtain lower returns. On the contrary, Ma (2013) reports that targets hired by top-tier investment banks earn higher premiums and abnormal returns. However, McLaughlin (1992) and Chahine and Ismail (2009) report no significant relationship between the quality of financial advisor and target premiums. With respect to the evidence for bidding firms, Golubov et al. (2012) find that bidders obtain higher returns when hiring top-tier advisors in public acquisitions. Instead, Da Silva et al. (2004) report that bidders hired by first-tier advisors obtain lower announcement returns around the announcement date in comparison to those using second- and third-tier advisors. Song et al. (2013) document that bidders obtain lower deal premiums when bidders hire boutique advisors.

This study focuses on the Australian market to examine the role of financial advisors on the influence of deal outcomes in M&As. In particular, the current study looks into whether firms hired by domestic advisors can outperform those with foreign counterparts. Unlike the US takeover market, the Australian takeover market is less competitive. In addition, the market structure in Australia can differ from that of the US market. Shams et al. (2013) argue that the Australian takeover market is dominated by publicly traded bidding firms that acquire private and subsidiary target firms. The transactions involved in private and subsidiary targets can possibly be smaller than those of public targets. This provides an opportunity to domestic advisors to compete with foreign advisors as foreign advisors are more likely to be involved in relatively large transactions. While in the domestic advisors may be better known in the domestic market, these advisors may possibly have advantages to obtain superior information. Domestic advisors can then have more ability to identify potential firms and to evaluate synergies. Hence, domestic advisors can create a higher value for their clients in comparison to their foreign counterparts. Alternatively, foreign advisors may possibly have more international experience in M&As. The use of foreign advisors can complete the deals faster and create higher gains for their clients.
