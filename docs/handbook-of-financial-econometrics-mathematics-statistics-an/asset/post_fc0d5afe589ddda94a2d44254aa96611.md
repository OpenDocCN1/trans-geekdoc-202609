The CBOE volatility index is derived based on the model-free volatility expectation shown in (40.14). The key assumption required to derive VIX is that the stochastic process for the underlying asset price is continuous and follows the GBM and IL. When there are relatively small jumps in the stock price process, Jiang and Tian (2005) and Carr and Wu (2006, 2009) show that this model-free approach is an excellent approximation of the risk-neutral, expected quadratic variation of the logarithm of the asset price. However, if there is an appreciable risk of a large jump, then the approximation error can be very significant. In Theorem 40.2, we show that without any diffusion assumption of the underlying asset return generating process, the model-free VIX expression is in fact a moment-combination of log-returns to its underlying portfolio.

### Theorem 40.2.

Let $V_T = E(r_T^2)$, $W_T = E(r_T^3)$, and $X_T = E(r_T^4)$ be the second, third and fourth ex ante return-moments to an asset. Without any modeling specification about the stochastic process of returns, the formulation of CBOE volatility index (VIX) is equal to a linear combination of return-moments such that

$$VIX = \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sqrt{\left[ V_T + \frac{W_T}{3} + \frac{X_T}{12} + o(X_T) \right] } - 2[(e^{rT} - 1) - rT],$$
$$\lim_{T\to0}[(e^{rT} - 1) - rT] = 0 \quad \lim_{T\to0}[(e^{rT} - 1) - rT] = 0. \tag{40.19}$$

Equation (40.19) can be directly derived from Theorem 40.1, Corollary 40.1 as well as equations (40.14) and (40.15). Theorem 40.2 identifies the invalidity of VIX as a volatility index unless high moments of the return distribution neither exist nor have impact on the return generating process. Therefore, any approximation error of VIX to the true volatility could intuitively be due to the impact of high return-moments.

Bakshi et al. (2003) show that without imposing any structure on the underlying forcing process, the distributional moments, $V_T$, $W_T$, and $X_T$ can be extracted from prices of OTM option portfolios. This indicates that the expected value of ex ante volatility can also be estimated without any specification of the underlying stochastic process. We extend the Bakshi et al. (2003) approach and propose a direct estimation of ex ante return-volatility in the following theorem.

f(S_T) = ln²(\frac{S_T}{S_0}), and let a = K_0. Then,

$$\ln^2\left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right) = \ln^2\left(\frac{K_0}{S_0}\right) + 2\ln\left(\frac{K_0}{S_0}\right)\left(\frac{S_T - K_0}{K_0}\right)$$
$$+2\int_{K_0}^{\infty}\frac{\left[1 - \ln\left(\frac{K}{S_0}\right)\right]}{K^2}(S_T - K)^+ dK$$
$$+2\int_{0}^{K_0}\frac{\left[1 + \ln\left(\frac{S_0}{K}\right)\right]}{K^2}(K - S_T)^+ dK. \quad (40.23)$$

Further, by taking the risk-neutral expectation for both sides of equation (40.23), we have equation (40.22). Our derivation of (40.22) provides similar results as shown in Bakshi et al. (2003). Theorem 40.3 formulates a generalized mechanism to extract fair value of ex ante volatility from OTM option portfolios. In Theorem 40.4, we show that the CBOE's VIX is just a special case of the proposed volatility index (GVIX).

### Theorem 40.4. CBOE's VIX is a special case of GVIX in that they are equal only if the stochastic process of asset's return follows Geometric Brownian Motion and Ito's Lemma as shown in (40.11) and (40.12).

The proof of the above theorem is straightforward. Based on the assumption of Geometric Brownian Motion and Ito's Lemma,

$$E\left[\ln^2\left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right)\right] = \left[\left(\mu - \frac12\sigma^2\right)T\right]^2 + \sigma^2 T,$$
and $$E\left[\ln\left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right)\right] = \left(\mu - \frac12\sigma^2\right)T.$$

From (40.13), (40.14), and (40.19), $$GVIX^2 = \frac1T\left\{E\left[\ln^2\left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right)\right] - \left\{E\left[\ln\left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right)\right]\right\}^2\right\}$$
$$= \sigma^2 = \frac1T\int_0^T \sigma^2 dt = E(V) = VIX^2.$$
Thus, if the diffusion assumptions of (40.11) and (40.12) hold, then GVIX \equiv VIX. (Q.E.D.)

Finally, according to equations (40.19) and (40.20), it is clear that the deviation of VIX² from the ex ante variance of log-returns (GVIX²) could be primarily driven by the high moments of returns in that $$VIX^2 - GVIX^2 \approx \frac1T\left(\mu^2 T + \frac{W_T}{3} + \frac{X_T}{12}\right). \quad (40.24)$$

The equality of (40.24) indicates that the spread of GVIX and VIX (GV-Spread) is determined by the ex ante mean return of the underlying asset as well as the ex ante high moments of the return distribution.

In practice, the calculation of our generalized volatility index is as simple as that of VIX (equation (40.17)). No additional information is necessary, and one is able to compute the GVIX based on three aspects: option prices (Q_K), arbitrage-free forward price of the underlying asset (F0 = S0 e^{rT}) and risk-free rate of interest (r) such that

mu_T hat = [ln(K0/S0) + (F0/K0 -1)] - e^{rT} sum_i 1/K_i^2 Q(K_i) Delta K, (40.25)

V_T hat = [ln^2(K0/S0) +2 ln(K0/S0)(F0/K0 -1)] +2 e^{rT} sum_i 1/K_i^2 [1+ln(S0/K_i)] Q(K_i) Delta K_i, (40.26)

GVIX = 1/sqrt(T) sqrt(V_T hat - (mu_T hat)^2). (40.27)

Furthermore, to be consistent with CBOE's VIX estimation, we adjust the GVIX index on a 30-day basis. Following the same interpolation procedures as VIX estimation (see equation (40.18)), we first identify option contracts of the near-term and next-term, denoted by T1 and T2, accordingly. Then, one calculates two GVIX indexes according to T1 and T2, respectively. Finally, by taking a weighted average, one could calculate the annualized GVIX index based on 30-day market returns as follows:

GVIX_a = sqrt( { [N(T2)-N(30)]/[N(T2)-N(T1)] (T1 * GVIX1^2) + [N(30)-N(T1)]/[N(T2)-N(T1)] (T2 * GVIX2^2) } (N(365)/N(30)) ). (40.28)
