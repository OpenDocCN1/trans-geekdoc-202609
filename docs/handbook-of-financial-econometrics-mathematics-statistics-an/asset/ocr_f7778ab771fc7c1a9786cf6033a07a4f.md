The ICA model attempts to maximize the nongaussianity through alternative measures. In our estimation of the independent components, we use a fixed-point algorithm that measures nongaussianity using the negentropy in the data and maximize it by choosing the mixing matrix. Negentropy measures the randomness given in an observed variable. The more unpredictable and unstructured the data are (i.e., no immediately discernable patterns in its structure), the larger is its entropy. In information theory and among all random variables, random variables that follow a Gaussian distribution have the largest entropy among all that have equal variance. We use this idea to measure nongaussianity through the employment of differential entropy (the difference between the entropy of a Gaussian random variable of the same correlation and covariance as the actual data and the data itself) also known as negentropy. Although the entropy of a random variable has a proper definition, it is widely regarded as a theoretical quantity that is computationally very challenging to estimate in practice as it involves the probability density function of the observable random variable. Fortunately, approximations do exist in practice that allow us to estimate it. We approximate negentropy using the classic method involving polynomial density expansions of the bond yields with a cubic power. Hyvärinen, Karhunen, and Oja (2001) note that these polynomial density expansion-based approximations are possibly coarse in practice.¹⁴ Hence, each time we implement the ICA model, we generate a slightly different set of bond yields and so we generate N = 10,000 sets of independent components. Then, we use the first three of them as the factors in constructing bond yields following the JSZ scheme.
