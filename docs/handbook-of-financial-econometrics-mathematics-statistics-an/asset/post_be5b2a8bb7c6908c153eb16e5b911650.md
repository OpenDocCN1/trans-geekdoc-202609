## 36.1 Introduction

Towards the end of the Second World War, world markets were characterized by many barriers to the free movements of goods, services and capital. Deregulation policies, which began in the developed countries in 1970 and in developing countries in the second half of 1980, removed the obstacles to trade and investment flows across the regions. Since the formation of the General Agreement on Trade and Tariffs (GATT), which was replaced on January 1, 1995 by the World Trade Organization (WTO), many governments have upheld the elimination of the traditional trade barriers. The economic and financial market liberalization has resulted in growing international stock market integration (Bekaert and Harvey, 2002; Forbes and Chinn, 2004; Chambet and Gibson, 2008; Phuan et al., 2009; Beine and Candelon, 2011; Paramati et al., 2015, 2016; Chuang and Karamatov, 2018). Furthermore, technological innovations have reduced transaction costs related to foreign assets and, consequently, have led to an increase in cross-border financial flows, which have contributed to an increase in the international stock market integration (Zimmermann et al., 2003; Sharma and Seth, 2012; Theodorou, 2017). The formation of regional trading blocs such as the European Union (EU), the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) and the Asia Pacific Economic Council (APEC) have also increased the international stock market integration (Kim et al., 2005; Bartram et al., 2007; Hooy and Goh, 2014; Kaur et al., 2017). The forces cited above (market liberalization, advanced technology and regional economic bloc), coupled with multinational corporations and Global Social Networks, have rapidly contributed to the globalization of markets. Globalization, which can be defined as the market integration throughout the world, leads to an increase in production and incomes, and enlarges the possibility of an international portfolio diversification. However, it can promote synchronization of economic variables across the nations such as business cycles and asset prices (Beine et al., 2010; Wälti, 2011; Asgharian et al., 2013; Ductor and Leiva-Leon, 2016), which affects the stock market dynamic interdependence and promotes the transmission of financial crises or the so-called crisis contagion.

The last three decades have witnessed a series of financial crises with significant impact on the regional and global economic performance: the 1994 Mexican devaluation peso crisis, the 1997 East Asian crisis, the 1998 Russian collapse, the 1999 Brazilian devaluation, the 1998-2002 Argentine economic crisis, the 2008 USA Subprime mortgage crisis and the 2015 Chinese stock market crash. Figure 36.1 shows that for example: (i) In 1994, the Mexican stock market crashed; this decline rapidly spread to other markets: European, American and Asian stock markets. (ii) In 1997, the collapse of the Hong Kong market quickly affected the world's major stock markets. (iii) At the end of 2001, the Argentina market had cratered; this plunge was rapidly reflected in the main global markets. (iv) In September 2008, the global stock markets declined in the wake of the Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy. (v) The 2015 turmoil period in China affected major stock markets around the globe.

These brief observations show that market crashes are rapidly spreading from one stock market to another around the world. The central questions that this study focuses on are: are these linkages temporary or permanent in nature? Do the major world stock markets present high degree of interdependence in turmoil and tranquil periods and do they consequently tend to move together over time? As the world becomes more and more globalized each day, are there still global diversification opportunities for international investors? Does the degree of co-movement vary across investment horizons?

International equity market co-movement is a major issue in finance and has important implications for international portfolio diversification (Rua and Nunes, 2009). As a result of globalization, diversification strategies around the world have been growing and investors are interested in international diversification rather than in domestic investment portfolio. The objective of diversification according to Williams (1938) and Markowitz (1952) is to reduce the portfolio risk by holding combinations of equities that are not positively correlated. The total risk of any portfolio is made up of both systematic (market risk) and unsystematic (idiosyncratic) risks. Sharpe (1964) explained in his seminal paper that domestic diversification can remove the specific risk from the portfolio. So, only systematic risk will remain. However, international diversification is able to reduce the systematic risk and consequently the total risk of a portfolio made up of foreign assets as well as domestic assets is reduced (Grubel, 1968; Grubel and Fadner, 1971; Solnik, 1974; Odier and Solnik, 1993; Hui, 2005; Solnik and Mcleavey, 2009; Berger et al., 2011, 2013; Stepien and Su, 2012; Syamala and Wadhwa, 2016).

The co-movement between domestic and foreign stock markets is the key determinant of the benefits of international portfolio diversification. Thus, the international investors need to understand the degree of interdependence between stock markets in order to better assess the potential gains in regional and global diversification. Ever since the seminal paper of Grubel (1968) on the benefits of international diversification based on the interdependence between two assets, several researchers have been interested in the international stock market co-movement. Some works in this area have analyzed the correlation between national stock markets (Longin and Solnik, 1995, 2001; Forbes and Rigobon, 2002; Brooks and Del Negro, 2004; Chelly-Steely, 2005; Kearney and Poti, 2006; Carrieri et al., 2007; Blackburn and Chidambaran, 2011; Mark, 2014; Chiang et al., 2016; Teti et al., 2017).
