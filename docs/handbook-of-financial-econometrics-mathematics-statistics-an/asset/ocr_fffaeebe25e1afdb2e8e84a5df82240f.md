Another plausible explanation is that with a liquid secondary market, investors may find it easier to invest in productive though illiquid long term projects (Levine, 1991; Bencivenga, Smith, and Starr, 1995; Levine and Zervos, 1998).

The strong evidence that stock market liquidity is a leading indicator of business cycles supports the idea that its variation could have a systematic impact on expected stock returns. Among others (Hasbrouck and Seppi, 2001; Huberman and Halka, 2001), Chordia, Roll, and Subrahmanyam (2000) find empirical evidence of commonality in stock market liquidity, likely due to the trading-induced co-movements in inventory fluctuations across individual stocks. The commonality in liquidity, therefore, implies the unanticipated changes in market liquidity pose a non-diversifiable priced risk.⁵ If so, a stock whose return is more positively correlated with liquidity shocks shall require a higher rate of return.

Indeed, it has been well documented that time variation in aggregate market liquidity affects the cross-section of stock returns.⁶ Pastor and Stambaugh (2003) construct a market wide liquidity measure based upon order-flow-induced return reversals of individual stocks.
