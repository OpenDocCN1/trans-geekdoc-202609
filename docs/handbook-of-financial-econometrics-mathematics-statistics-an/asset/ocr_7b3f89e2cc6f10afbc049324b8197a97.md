Consequently, “banks” here refers to “commercial banks” from which firms borrow each year. “Foreign banks” means that a commercial bank is registered, located or operated in a foreign country in order to provide more services to its multinational corporate customers. These banks are obligated to follow the regulations of both their home and host countries. “Foreign bank debt” means that one of the firms in our sample borrows funds (loans) from one of these foreign banks in Taiwan.

The lending database reports the identity of the firm’s creditors, the amount of credit, and whether or not the loans are secured by collateral in a given firm-year. Compared to the extant research, our data provide more dimensions of information than those of Sheard (1989), Hoshi et al. (1990), and Kang and Shivdasani (1997), who consider only the largest creditor. In addition to this richer set of bank-relationship data, the percentage shareholdings of managers, boards, and large shareholders are also included in this research for further tests. We define large shareholders as those whose shareholdings exceed 3% of total outstanding shares, according to Taiwan’s Security Transactions Law, 1990.
