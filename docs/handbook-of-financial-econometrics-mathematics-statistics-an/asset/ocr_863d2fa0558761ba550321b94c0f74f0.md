$$\begin{bmatrix} R_{i,t+1} \\ V_{i,t+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \gamma_{ii}\phi_i \\ 0 & \phi_i \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} R_{i,t} \\ V_{i,t} \end{bmatrix} + \boldsymbol{u}_{i,t+1}$$

and

$$\boldsymbol{u}_{i,t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \gamma_{ii} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{i,t+1} \\ v_{i,t+1} \end{bmatrix}. \tag{58.5}$$

The above system implies that equation (58.1), $A_i(L) = I_2 - A_i L$, where $A_i = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \gamma_{ii}\phi_i \\ 0 & \phi_i \end{bmatrix}$. In addition, $\boldsymbol{u}_{i,t+1}$ is a matrix that contains both errors, $\varepsilon_{i,t+1}$ (noise in returns) and $v_{i,t+1}$ (innovation to downside risk), and these errors are not necessarily independent, since they are correlated with the downside risk aversion parameter, $\gamma_{ii}$.$^9$ As a result, efficient estimation of this model would require the simultaneous estimation of both equations, since the system errors in $\boldsymbol{u}_{i,t+1}$ are contemporaneously correlated. The maximum likelihood method is applied here to efficiently and simultaneously estimate the parameters in this system. After obtaining the parameters in the $A_i$ matrix using the maximum likelihood method, $\gamma_{ii}$ can be calculated through $\gamma_{ii} = A_{i,12} / A_{i,22} = \gamma_{ii}\phi_i / \phi_i$.$^{10}$

However, if our interest is on investigating a “cross-market” intertemporal downside risk–return relation, the specification in equation (58.5) can be modified as

$$\begin{bmatrix} R_{i,t+1} \\ V_{j,t+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \gamma_{ij}\phi_j \\ 0 & \phi_j \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} R_{i,t} \\ V_{j,t} \end{bmatrix} + \boldsymbol{u}_{ij,t+1} \quad \text{and}$$

$$\boldsymbol{u}_{ij,t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \gamma_{ij} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{ij,t+1} \\ v_{j,t+1} \end{bmatrix}. \tag{58.6}$$

The cross-market downside risk aversion parameter, $\gamma_{ij}$, measures the degree to which investors in country $i$ expect higher stock returns to compensate for the higher downside risk from country $j$.$^{11}$ This specification helps us to capture the contagion effect or a spillover effect induced by the risk from country $j$.$^{12}$

$^9$Without losing generality, we suppress the constant term to keep equation (58.1) simple.

$^{10}$The asymptotic standard errors here are calculated by the delta method.

$^{11}$We may call this a cross-country effect, since there is only one stock market in a particular country. An exception is that Hong Kong’s market cannot be treated as a country.

$^{12}$This can be more significant if it arises from a dominating country in global financial markets or a close trading partner. This cross-market risk effect is important for small emerging markets in Asia, since most stock markets in this region are relatively thin and vulnerable; their price volatility is subject to the global markets’ fluctuations, especially the contagion effect originating from the US market.
