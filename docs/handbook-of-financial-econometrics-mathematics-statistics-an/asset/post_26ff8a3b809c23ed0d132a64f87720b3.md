Several models for assessing deposit insurance premium can be grouped into three main categories: bank failure prediction approach, reduced-form approach, and a structural approach. The advantage of the bank failure prediction approach is to determine what variables should be used in a risk-based deposit insurance system (Sinkey, 1975; Avery et al., 1985; Calomiris and Jaremski, 2016). Under this framework, the logit model estimates the probability of bank failure. Hwang et al. (1997) utilize logistic regressions to identify financial ratios significantly contributing to bank failures. They find the financial ratios related to equity capital, profitability and liquidity have significant influences on the probability of bank failure. The fair deposit insurance premium then can be calculated by the probability of failure times the liquidation costs. Thomson (1991) indicates that the ratio of non-deposit liability to cash and investment securities significantly affect the bank failure. However, Thomson (1991) states that the logit estimation is not sensitive to an uneven sampling frequencies problem. Thomson (1992) applies two-step logit regressions to model the bank regulator’s closure decision. The first step is the regression of the bank’s market value on accounting data, and the second step is logistic regression to predict bank failure based on the estimated market value of the bank in the first step and the constraints on the FDIC’s ability to close insolvent banks.

With regard to the reduced-form methodology, Duffie et al. (2003) propose the approach based on reduced-form to price deposit insurance and claim this approach need not treat the exact form of the insurance contract because it does not depend on the liability structure or auditing process of the bank. It is difficult to capture regulations of an insurance contract within the bank’s capital structure, especially for banks that are not publicly traded (Falkenheim and Pennacchi, 2002). Duffie et al. (2003) point out that the fair-market deposit insurance rates can be the product of a bank’s short-term credit spread and the ratio of the insurer’s expected loss at failure per dollar of assessed deposit to the bond investors’ expected loss at failure per dollar of principal. Although the reduced-form model can solve the influence of the deposit insurance contract on evaluation of the deposit insurance premium, there are too many parameters to be estimated that can potentially influence the accuracy of results.

Most studies estimate fair-market FDIC insurance premiums by a structural approach, which typically bases the firm’s asset and the volatility of its asset on its equity price (Black and Scholes, 1973; Black and Cox, 1976; Merton, 1977; Leland, 1994; Anderson and Sundaresan, 2000; Brockman and Turle, 2003; Episcopos, 2008).

## Pricing Fair Deposit Insurance: Structural Model Approach

Merton (1977) first gives the insight into the relationship between the deposit insurance and put option. If bank assets cannot meet the amount of deposits, the bank is insolvent and receives nothing. All remaining assets belong to debt holders, that is, depositors. When insolvency of a bank occurs, the insurer of deposit insurance should pay the difference of the bank assets and the deposits to depositors. Therefore, the contract of deposit insurance can be viewed as a put option written on bank assets with the strike price equal to deposits. Marcus and Shaked (1984) implement Merton’s model to price insurance premium with constant proportional dividends. Their empirical results show the overpriced deposit insurance premiums charged by the FDIC.

Deposit insurance premiums are evaluated by barrier option models (Brockman and Turle, 2003; Episcopos, 2008). However, the barrier option model used in previous studies neglects the restrictions of the deposit insurance contracts, therefore, the structural model approach in terms of the barrier option model and the Stair Tree model is proposed to deal with bankruptcy costs, the limited indemnification for depositors, discretely monitoring banks’ situations and the adjustment of the insurance premium in different financial institutions based on a risk-based assessment system. We are then able to build a fair insurance premium system and calculate the reasonable implied barrier critical points to determine whether FDIC's supervisory policy is strict or forbearance. The simulation results suggest that insurers should adopt a forbearance policy instead of a strict policy for closure regulation to avoid losses from bankruptcy costs. In addition, an appropriate deposit insurance premium can alleviate potential moral hazard problems caused by a forbearance policy.

At the beginning, the BT model shows the classical structural model, barrier option closed form, which views the value of equity as a down-and-out call (DOC)

$$\text{DOC}(H,X)=V N(a)-X e^{-rT} N\left(a-\sigma\sqrt{T}\right)-V\left(\frac{H}{V}\right)^{2\eta} N(b)$$ $$+X e^{-rT}\left(\frac{H}{V}\right)^{2\eta-2} N\left(b - 2\sigma\sqrt{T}\right),\qquad (15.1),$$

where $V$ is the current market value of bank asset, $X$ is the promised payment to depositors in $T$ years, $H$ is closure barrier, $N$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, and $r$ is the risk-free rate of interest.

Where

$a = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{\ln(V/X)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} & X \ge H\\
\dfrac{\ln(V/H)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} & X < H
\end{cases}$

$b = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{\ln(H^2/VX)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} & X \ge H\\
\dfrac{\ln(H/V)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} & X < H
\end{cases}$

$\eta = r/\sigma^2 + 1/2.$

According to capturing bank’s equity by DOC, the residual bank’s asset would be equal to

$$V - \text{DOC}(H,X)=V - C(X)+\text{DIC}(H,X)$$ $$=X e^{-rT}-P(X)+\text{DIC}(H,X),\qquad (15.2),$$

where $C(X)$ is a European call option and $P(X)$ is a European put with exercise price $X$; DIC $(H,X)$ is a down-and-in call (DIC) with barrier $H$. The first term is the present value for the insurance (the depositors) under the full insurance; the last two terms are the total value of FDIC which profits DIC $(H,X)$ by taking over the failure bank, but loses $P(X)$ due to responsibility of insurance payment for depositors at maturity date.

However, BT model is difficult to catch up all real extent of bank regulation, thus simplifying assumptions, for example, no dividends, no tax, no bankruptcy cost, no insurance premium, constant volatility and continuous monitoring. For example, the contract of the FDIC deposit insurance is determined quarterly. According to the quarterly financial statement of the financial institutions, the FDIC will receive their insurance premium by assessing the level of risk-based assessment system. Therefore, the continuous assumption of the structural model doesn’t seem suitable for the evaluation of deposit insurances. Another example of the contract’s limitation elaborated in a practical provision is that the FDIC protects the depositors to a certain extent. Deposit insurances don’t cover other financial products when a bankruptcy occurs.

Taking the aforementioned into consideration, we provide a novel discrete structural model based on the Stair Tree model and the concept of BT model to price the FDIC in a barrier option framework. In other words, our extended structural approach can modify the BT model by adding regulation limitations. This model not only allows the adjustment of the insurance premium in different financial institutions based on a risk-based assessment system, but also takes account of the loss from bankruptcy costs and the limited indemnification for depositors. We are then able to build a fair insurance premium system and calculate the reasonable implied barrier critical points to determine whether FDIC’s supervisory policy is strict or forbearance.

The bank asset is discretely monitored for the regular declaration of financial reports and significant announcements. Therefore, we combine the techniques for constructing the Stair Tree (see Dai, 2009) and its generalization (see Dai and Lyuu, 2010) to monitor the bank asset at the time of auditing and to deal with bank failure when its asset meets the closure barrier.
