Similarly, Banerjee et al. More recently, researchers have leveraged explanations about the general market environment to illustrate why IPOs may experience hot or cold cycles. This vein of inquiry seems to date back to the Ritter (1984) update of Ibbotson et al. (1975) paper that first identified market cycles and in Ritter (1984) the researcher concluded that if you take away all of the natural resource stocks from the hot period that he was examining, the market would not have been considered hot. This trend continues with work done by Colak et al. (2017) in which the researchers provide localized examples of cyclical patterns in IPO performance and illustrate that uncertainty surrounding the results of US gubernatorial elections leads firms to delay their IPOs and that firms obtain lower IPO prices when dealing with uncertainty associated with these contests.

## 67.2 Identifying Cycles

As mentioned in the introduction, Ibbotson et al. (1975) introduced the idea of the "hot" IPO market, determined that there is serial correlation in IPO performance, and that the relationship between IPO performance and volume is somewhat related (i.e., the two variables are correlated); since then, academics and practitioners have spent a great deal of time attempting to model IPO volume and performance in an attempt to understand the underlying forces that lead to increases in issuance and performance, but also to profit off of that understanding. Research on the cyclical nature of IPO performance and issuance that is relevant for this section includes Ibbotson and Jaffe (1975), Ritter (1984), Ibbotson et al. (1994), Loughran et al. (1994), Pagano et al. (1998), Stoughton et al. (2001), Benveniste et al. (2002), Lowry and Schwert (2002), Lowry (2003), Helwege and Liang (2004), Purnanandam and Swaminathan (2004), Alti (2005), Benninga et al. (2005), Derrien (2005), Pastor and Veronesi (2005), Yung et al. (2008), Gao et al. (2013), Hu and Wang (2013) and Smith and Mumtaz (2018). Our goal, in this section, is to explore the cyclical nature of IPO issuance.

When we think about cyclicality or hot and cold markets, an interesting next step in the literature would be to evaluate what Ritter (1984) found in his follow-up study to Ibbotson et al. (1975). Ritter (1984) focused on the average performance of IPOs issued during the 15-month period starting in January 1980, a performance of 48.4%, which was far greater than the aggregate performance of the IPO market during the Ibbotson et al. (1975) 1977–1982 time period (i.e., that performance was 16.3%). In the end, Ritter (1984) concluded that there was a "market" factor that led to this outperformance. This factor, according to Ritter (1984), was a boom in the performance of natural resources stocks issued during this time and the "hot" market was contained mostly in this particular industry in which underwriters would substantially discount the shares of the firms that had small market capitalizations and were issuing their shares at this time. Namely, according to Ritter (1984), if the firms that were issuing their shares during this 15-month period had less than $500,000 in sales, their average initial return was 140.4%.

Lowry (2003) focused more on the volume of IPO shares issued over time, but used the initial returns generated by IPOs as a determinant of volume. The sample Lowry (2003) used was a significant increase in the sample size from previous studies, which was used to measure the potential causes of the cycles; the sample data ran from 1970 to 1996. Lowry (2003) determined that the demand for capital and investor sentiment leads to increases in IPO volume and that temporary overvaluations by investors are also associated with increases in IPO volume. Interestingly enough, Ritter (1984) initially tested something like this temporary overvaluation hypothesis, but referred to it as the speculative bubble hypothesis, and although he determined that there was not sufficient evidence to conclude that a bubble had burst, a review of the data indicates that the returns on the IPOs initiated during this period suffered a lot more than the returns on the comparison index (i.e., there was a difference of about 20 percentage points between the two indices in the post-IPO performance comparisons). Whether or not these are bubbles, may be debatable; however, it seems as though this idea of bubbles or periods of misvaluation caused partly because investors have irrational expectations about the potential opportunities that the companies issuing shares have in front of them have led to temporary disequilibrium in the marketplace.

Lowry and Schwert (2002) tied these two ideas together and state that "periods of high initial returns appear to represent avoidable bubbles" (p. 173); however, they further indicate in their analysis from 1985 to 1997, that the "cycles in the initial returns predominantly reflect investment bankers’ learning process. Because the registration periods of many IPOs overlap, the information that underwriters learn during one firm’s registration period contributes to the first-day returns of many IPOs, thereby causing the returns to be serially correlated" (p. 1173). This idea of a learning process is interesting and leads us to a discussion about the conditional offerings associated with these shares, which is similar to what Ritter (1984) seemed to observe in the 1980s as natural resource firms brought their shares to the market in a somewhat concerted effort.

(2002), a "hot" market occurs as a result of cascades or firms that follow pioneers of an industry that issue their shares and introduce investors to an idea or ownership in a certain type of company.
