Lowry (2003) focused more on the volume of IPO shares issues over time, but used the initial returns generated by IPOs as a determinate of volume. The sample Lowry (2003) used was a significant increase in the sample size from previous studies, which was used to measure the potential causes of the cycles; the sample data ran from 1970 and 1996. Lowry (2003) determined that the demand for capital and investor sentiment leads to increases in IPO volume and that temporary overvaluations by investors are also associated with increases in IPO volume. Interestingly enough, Ritter (1984) initially tested something like this temporary overvaluation hypothesis, but referred to it as the speculative bubble hypothesis, and although he determined that there was not sufficient evidence to conclude that a bubble had burst, a review of the data indicates that the returns on the IPOs initiated during this period suffered a lot more than the returns on the comparison index (i.e., there was a difference of about 20 percentage points between the two indices in the post IPO performance comparisons). Whether or not these are bubbles, may be debatable; however, it seems as though this idea of bubbles or periods of misvaluation caused partly because investors have irrational expectations about the potential opportunities that the companies issuing shares have in front of them have led to temporary disequilibrium in the marketplace.

Lowry and Schwert (2002) tied these two ideas together and state that “periods of high initial returns appear to represent avoidable bubbles” (p. 173); however, they further indicate in their analysis from 1985 to 1997, that the “cycles in the initial returns predominantly reflect investment bankers’ learning process. Because the registration periods of many IPOs overlap, the information that underwriters learn during one firm’s registration period contributes to the first-day returns of many IPOs, thereby causing the returns to be serially correlated” (p. 1173). This idea of a learning process is interesting and leads us to a discussion about the conditional offerings associated with these shares, which is similar to what Ritter (1984) seemed to observe in the 1980s as natural resource firms brought their shares to the market in a somewhat concerted effort.

Benveniste et al. (2002) provided some initial evidence of industry clustering and why it occurs. According to Benveniste et al. (2002), a “hot” market occurs as a result of cascades or firms that follow pioneers of an industry that issue their shares and introduce investors to an idea or ownership in a certain type of company.
