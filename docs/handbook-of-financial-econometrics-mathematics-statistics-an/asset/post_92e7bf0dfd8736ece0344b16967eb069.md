
into the product of the conditional variance and correlation matrices

$H_t = D_t R_t D_t,$
(123.44)

where the conditional variance $D_t$ is defined in equation (123.32). The $n \times n$ conditional correlation matrix $R_t$ is not constant unlike that of the CCC-GARCH. The diagonal elements of $H_t$ are formulated as

$\bar h_t = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^q A_i \bar\varepsilon_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^p B_j \bar h_{t-j},$ 
(123.45)

where $\bar h_t = (h_{11,t}, \ldots, h_{nn,t})'$, and $\bar\varepsilon_t = (\varepsilon_{1,t}^2, \ldots, \varepsilon_{n,t}^2)'$ are $n \times 1$ vectors. This formulation is identical to the CCC-GARCH. Defining the normalized error term vector as

$u_t = D_t^{-1/2} \varepsilon_t,$
(123.46)

the conditional correlation matrix of $\varepsilon_t$ is given by $R_t = E(u_t u_t' | I_{t-1})$. We specify an innovation of the conditional correlation matrix as

$Q_t = (1 - a - b)\bar Q + a u_{t-1} u_{t-1}' + b Q_{t-1},$ 
(123.47)

where $\bar Q$ is a matrix of location parameters. If $a \ge 0$, $b \ge 0$, $a + b < 1$ and $\bar Q$ is positive definite, then $R_t$ is also positive definite. Hence, $R_t$ can be defined in terms of $Q_t$ as

$R_t = \text{diag}(q_{11,t}, \ldots, q_{nn,t})^{-1/2} Q_t \text{diag}(q_{11,t}, \ldots, q_{nn,t})^{-1/2}.$ 
(123.48)

The $i$th and $j$th elements of $R_t$ can be written as

$\rho_{ij,t} = q_{ij,t} \{q_{ii,t} q_{jj,t}\}^{-1/2}$
$= \{(1 - a - b)\bar q_{ij} + b q_{ij,t-1} + a u_{i,t-1} u_{j,t-1}\}$
$\times \{((1 - a - b)\bar q_{ii} + b q_{ii,t-1} + a u_{i,t-1}^2)((1 - a - b)\bar q_{jj} + b q_{jj,t-1} + a u_{j,t-1}^2)\}^{-1/2},$ 
(123.49)

which implies that the conditional correlations are dynamically driven by the process of $Q_t$. The conditional covariances are obtained accordingly as $h_{ij,t} = \rho_{ij,t} \{h_{ii,t} h_{jj,t}\}^{1/2}$. We note that the correlation coefficients are nonlinear functions of two unknown parameters $a$ and $b$. The full set of parameters for the DCC-GARCH$(p,q)$ model consists of $\theta = (\mu, \Gamma_1, \ldots, \Gamma_k; \omega, A_1, \ldots, A_q; B_1, \ldots, B_p; a, b, \bar Q)$.

Engle (2002) specifies the process of the conditional correlation matrix $(R_t)$ using equations (123.47) and (123.48), while most of multivariate GARCH models before Engle (2002) directly specify the process of $n(n+1)/2$ different elements of $H_t$. The construction of $R_t$ using (123.48)

automatically satisfies the positive definiteness condition as long as $Q_t$ is positive definite. The number of parameters required for specifying $H_t$ in this model is $s = n + n^2(p + q) + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} + 2$, and exceeds that of the CCC-GARCH by only two. This model is also flexible for allowing the conditional correlations to change over time. The DCC-GARCH model ingeniously compromises two contrary requirements for constructing a model: sufficiently flexible to catch behaviors of actually observed data process, and sufficiently parsimonious for statistical analysis in practice.

## 123.4.2 Estimation of the DCC-GARCH(p, q) model

We can employ an estimation procedure analogously to that of the CCC-GARCH model. The (negative) quasi log-likelihood function is given apart from constant terms as

$l_T(\theta) = l(\theta; Y_1, \dots, Y_T) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{t=1}^{T} l_t,$ (123.50)

where $l_t = \log \det(\tilde{H}_t) + \tilde{\varepsilon}'_t \tilde{H}_t^{-1} \tilde{\varepsilon}_t$. We obtain the QMLE by minimizing the (negative) quasi-log-likelihood (123.50) with respect to $\theta$. However, for the empirical studies in Section 123.5, we employ the “one-step maximum likelihood” method proposed by Bauwens and Laurent (2005). They replace $\bar{Q}$ by its empirical counterpart as in Engle (2002), before minimizing the (negative) log-likelihood function of (123.50). Once an estimate $\hat{\theta}$ is obtained, we are able to compute $\hat{H}_t$, $\hat{R}_t$, and $\hat{\Phi}_t$ as functions of $\hat{\theta}$. Engle (2002) claims that the DCC model can be inefficiently but consistently estimated by using a “two-step approach”, in which $\bar{Q}$ is estimated by the sample second moment of the standardized returns. The two-step estimator relies on the conjecture that $\bar{Q}$ is the second moment of $u_t$, i.e., $\bar{Q} = E[u_t u'_t]$.

The DCC model has been controversial among researchers. Caporin and McAleer (2013) present several caveats about applications of the DCC model. Aielli (2013) provides a thorough investigation of the properties and estimation methods of the DCC model. Pointing out that Engle’s conjecture about $\bar{Q}$ is not correct, i.e., $\bar{Q} \neq E[u_t u'_t]$, Aielli (2013) proves the inconsistency of the two-step estimator of Engle (2002).$^7$ He also suggests a correction of the DCC model to a more tractable one called the cDCC model. This model admits strict stationarity and ergodic solution under certain explicitly stated

$^7$This criticism of Aielli (2013) is also valid to the one-step ML method of Bauwens and Laurent (2005). The one-step ML estimator may not be consistent.

regularity conditions. He proposes the cDCC estimator and proves the consistency of this estimator. The discussion of Aielli (2013) signifies theoretically as well as numerically the contribution and limitation of Engle (2002).

Despite some theoretical disadvantages as pointed out by Aielli (2013) and Caporin and McAleer (2013), the DCC model of Engle (2002) and its extensions are now among the most popular approaches to the modeling of multivariate volatility. The numerical behaviors of the DCC model are almost identical to those of the cDCC model for the typical parameter range of $a$ and $b$ ($a+b\leq 0.990$ and $a\leq 0.04$) indicated by Aielli (2013). Comparing the performances of various popular MGARCH models by way of Monte Carlo experiments, de Almeida *et al.* (2018) report that specification of the models is more important than choice of estimators, and DCC models generally show good performances.

## 123.4.3 Dynamic conditional variance decomposition

We assume that the volatility spillover effects are unidirectional along the order of variables $Y_{1t}$ through $Y_{nt}$ (i.e., from $Y_{1t}$ to $Y_{2t}$ and from $Y_{2t}$ to $Y_{3t}$ until $Y_{nt}$) and that there is no reverse spillover. We can uniquely decompose the conditional covariance matrix $(H_t)$ by the Cholesky method⁹ as

$$H_t=\Phi_t\Sigma_t\Phi_t',$$                                                (123.51)

where $\Phi_t$ is a lower triangular matrix with diagonal elements of ones and $\Sigma_t=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2_{1,t},\ldots,\sigma^2_{n,t})$ is a diagonal matrix. We note that $\Phi_t$ in (123.51) changes over time.

In order to decompose the unexpected returns into the idiosyncratic shocks, we transform the error term vector $\varepsilon_t$ into a new random vector as follows¹⁰

$$\widetilde{\varepsilon}_t=(\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{1,t},\ldots,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n,t})'=\Phi_t^{-1}\varepsilon_t,\ \text{or equivalently,}\ \varepsilon_t=\Phi_t\widetilde{\varepsilon}_t,$$                                                (123.52)
