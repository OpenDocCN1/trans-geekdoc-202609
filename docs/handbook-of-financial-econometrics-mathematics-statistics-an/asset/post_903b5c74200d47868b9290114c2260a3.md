$P_{SS+DV(U)} = \{W_p \in S: \quad 0 \le W_i \le U, \quad i = 1,2,\dots,N\}, \quad (82.9)$

where $U$ can be any real number not greater than 1. The optimal weights can be solved by applying the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions when the complementary slackness conditions, primal constraints, and gradient equations are fulfilled. Since there are multiple nonlinearities in the quadratic programming, there is no equational form for the solutions. One can generate the Maximum Sharpe Ratio (MSR) portfolio among the selections on the constrained efficient frontier. Specifically,

$MSR = \max_{\{W_p\}} \{(W_p^T \mu)/(W_p^T V W_p)^{1/2} | W_p \in P_J\}. \quad (82.10)$

We use the monthly return data of the component stocks in the Dow Jones 30 Index between July 2008 and June 2018 to demonstrate an example. Our analysis prohibits short sales of assets and asset allocations higher than a certain weight to ensure diversity of the portfolio. Since the largest optimal weight among the assets when only short sales is prohibited is about 42%, we set out to analyze the effect of adding the upper bounds of portfolio weights by setting them at 20% and 10%.

Figure 82.3 exhibits the efficient frontiers that are constructed with various sets of weighting constraints. Among the optimal portfolio sets, the one that only prohibits short sales demonstrates higher mean–variance efficiency than the other two that include the upper bounds. The two curves with upper bounds are close to the no-short-sales efficient frontier in low risk–return regime and are feasible. Consistent with risk reduction effect suggested by Jagannathan and Ma (2003), the constraints that the weights of assets cannot be greater than 20% do not significantly change the low-volatility portfolio selections but eliminate risky investing opportunities. When the diversity increases as the weights cannot be higher than 10%, the mean–variance efficiency drops and the investing area also shrinks.

We report two characteristic portfolios on the efficient frontier. The first is the maximum Sharpe Ratio (MSR) portfolio that represents the highest mean–variance efficiency that can be achieved by the diversification. The second measure is the minimum-variance portfolio (MVP) as Elton, Gruber, Brown, and Goetzmann (2007) suggest that investors may seek to minimize the variance of a portfolio due to the lack of predictability of expected returns. ## 82.4 Treynor-Measure Approach with Short Sales Allowed

Using the single-index market model, Elton et al. (1976) define

σ_p = (∑_{i=1}^n W_i^2 β_i^2 σ_m^2 + ∑_{i=1}^n ∑_{j=1, j≠i}^n W_i W_j β_i β_j σ_m^2 + ∑_{i=1}^n W_i^2 σ_{εi}^2)^{1/2}.

Substituting this value of σ_p into equation (82.3),

L = (∑_{i=1}^n W_i (\bar{R}_i - R_f)) / σ_p. (82.11)

In order to find the set of W_i s that maximizes L, take the partial derivative of the above equation with respect to each W_i and some manipulation. Then we can obtain

W_i = H_i / ∑_{i=1}^n H_i, (82.12)

where H_i = (\bar{R}_i - R_f)/σ_{εi}^2 - ( σ_m^2 ∑_{j=1}^n (\bar{R}_j - R_f)/σ_{εj}^2 ) / (1 + σ_m^2 ∑_{j=1}^n β_j^2/σ_{εj}^2) * (β_i^2/σ_{εi}^2). (82.13)

The procedure of deriving equations (82.12) and (82.13) can be found in Appendix 82B. The $H_i$s must be calculated for all the stocks in the portfolio. If $H_i$ is a positive value, this indicates the stock will be held long, whereas a negative value indicates that the stock should be sold short. This method is called the Treynor measure approach. The argument will be clearer when the case of portfolio selection with short sales not allowed is discussed.

To determine the optimum portfolio from the $H_i$s (such that 100% of funds are invested), the weights must be scaled. One method follows the standard definition of short sales, which presumes that a short sale of stock is a source of funds to the investor; it is called the *standard method of short sales*. This standard scaling method is indicated in equation (82.12). In equation (82.13), $H_i$ can be positive or negative. This scaling factor includes a definition of short sales and the constraint:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} W_i = 1.$$

A second method (Lintner's (1965) *method of short sales*) assumes that the proceeds of short sales are not available to the investor and that the investor must put up an amount of funds equal to the proceeds of the short sale. The additional amount of funds serves as collateral to protect against adverse price movements. Under these assumptions, the constraints on the $W_i$s can be expressed as

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} |W_i| = 1,$$

and the scaling factor is expressed as

$$W_i = \frac{H_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} |H_i|}.$$

(82.14)
