Consequently, “banks” here refers to “commercial banks” from which firms borrow each year. “Foreign banks” means that a commercial bank is registered, located or operated in a foreign country in order to provide more services to its multinational corporate customers. These banks are obligated to follow the regulations of both their home and host countries. “Foreign bank debt” means that one of the firms in our sample borrows funds (loans) from one of these foreign banks in Taiwan.

The lending database reports the identity of the firm’s creditors, the amount of credit, and whether or not the loans are secured by collateral in a given firm-year. Compared to the extant research, our data provide more dimensions of information than those of Sheard (1989), Hoshi et al. (1990), and Kang and Shivdasani (1997), who consider only the largest creditor. In addition to this richer set of bank-relationship data, the percentage shareholdings of managers, boards, and large shareholders are also included in this research for further tests. We define large shareholders as those whose shareholdings exceed 3% of total outstanding shares, according to Taiwan’s Securities Transactions Law, 1990.

9. Our bank relationship data is collected from the Taiwan Securities Exchange Council (TSEC). Taiwanese listed firms, like firms in other countries, are required to release their balance sheets, income statements, and cash flow statements to the stock exchange commission. While releasing these three public financial statements, listed firms in Taiwan are further required to attach a “long-styled” financial statement, describing the detailed information about how each item in these three statements is compiled. This long-styled financial statement, albeit not available to the public, can nevertheless be obtained by application and Xeroxing. A complete long-style financial statement records the information on each loan transaction, such as loan rate, lending bank name, loan size, and loan period. As such, we are able to count the number of banks a listed firm borrows from over our sample periods. Of particular note, the items reported in this supplementary report are not uniform. Some firms provide the detailed information as they can, while some of them disclose the information in general without specifying individual bank loans, although disclosing the lending bank name.

The average number of banking relationships is 5.59, but similar to the results for Q, there is a significant difference between those firms that use foreign debt and those that do not use it. The average number of banking relationships for firms that borrow exclusively from Taiwanese banks is 4.42, while the corresponding number for firms that also borrow from foreign banks is 7.97 (significantly higher).

The average firm in our sample has a leverage ratio of 25%, and the average ratio of bank debt to total debt is 61%. Firms that borrow only from domestic banks have a slightly higher average bank debt to total debt ratio (65%) than firms that also borrow from foreign banks (52%). The opposite is true for public debt. Although the average ratio of public debt to total debt is 24%, firms that borrow from foreign banks have a greater proportion of their debt coming from public markets (28% relative to 22%). Non-bank private debt is on average a much smaller component of the capital structure of the firms in our sample. The average percentage of non-bank private debt to total debt is only 10%. Once again, we see a difference in this ratio between firms that borrow from foreign banks and those that do not do so. The average percentage of non-bank private debt to total debt is 13% for firms that do not borrow from foreign banks, while it is only 3% for firms that do borrow from foreign banks. The average ratio of foreign bank debt to total debt is 6%, but one needs to interpret this number with some care, since for firms that have no foreign bank debt the ratio is obviously zero. For those firms that do have foreign bank debt, the average percentage of foreign bank debt to total debt is 17% — a non-trivial percentage. The fact that foreign bank debt is not easily obtainable by all Taiwanese firms suggests that the use of such debt might be a signal of quality from those firms that are able to obtain it.

Collateral seems to be used almost universally by the firms in our sample: 84% of the firms in the sample use collateral in their local banking relationships. This ratio is remarkably stable regardless of whether firms do or do not borrow from foreign banks. The average age of the firms in the sample is 21.18 years, with very little differentiation between firms that do not borrow from foreign banks (20.79 years) and those that do (21.67 years). The average log of total assets is 6.50, with firms that borrow from foreign banks being slightly larger (6.60) relative to those that borrow only from local banks (6.43).
