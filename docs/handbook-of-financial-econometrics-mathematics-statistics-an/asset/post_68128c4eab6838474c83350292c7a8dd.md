## Abstract

We study the heteroskedasticity and jump behavior of the Thai baht using models of the square root stochastic volatility with or without jumps. The Bayesian factor is used to evaluate the explanatory power of competing models. The results suggest that in our sample, the square root stochastic volatility model with independent jumps in the observation and state equations (SVIJ) has the best explanatory power for the 1996 Asian financial crisis. Using the estimation results of the SVIJ model, we are able to link the major events of the Asian financial crisis to jump behavior in either volatility or observation.

**Keywords**
Heteroskedasticity ● Jump ● Stochastic volatility model with independent jumps ● Asian financial crisis ● Bayesian factor.

### 52.1 Introduction

There are two contributing factors to the exchange rate crises in developing countries. The first factor is the behavior of the central bank, and the second is the strength of the foreign exchange reserves. Before 1997, the Thai central bank adopted a high interest rate and stable exchange rate policy; the Thai baht was pegged to the US dollar. However, due to the high interest rate, a great deal of capital flowed into Thailand, which resulted in the bubbles of speculative markets. When international speculators attacked the Thai financial market and there were insufficient foreign exchange reserves, the stable exchange rate policy collapsed. In this chapter, we study the development of the 1997 Asian financial crisis through stochastic volatility (SV) models that allow for potential jump behaviors in the exchange rate and its volatility.

The Asian financial crisis was caused by speculators attacking the Thai baht and the currencies of other Asian countries. The Thai baht situation started deteriorating in May 1997 but only began to receive public attention after the baht depreciated 17% on July 2, 1997. Thailand’s government turned to the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and other countries, including Japan and the US, for help. While Thailand’s government was coping with this challenge, the crisis spilled over into other southeast Asian countries. The Thai baht did not stop depreciating until early January 1998.

Jump behavior in the US stock market is clearly identified in Eraker et al. (2003). They found that jump in return and volatility significantly increases the curvature of implied volatility curves. Bayesian factors are used in this study to confirm the speculation that time series models with jumps have better explanatory power than index return data.

There are several variations in modeling jumps. Eraker (2004) proposed a model with correlated jumps in observation and state equations. Li et al. (2008) adopted a return dynamics models with Levy jumps that added a variance-gamma jump component to the return equation to capture small jumps in equity index. Kou et al. (2017) proposed to replace normally distributed jumps with double-exponential jumps in the observation equation. They reported results in favor of the double-exponential distribution using the Standard and Poor’s (S&P) 500 and the NASDAQ-100 daily returns from 1980 to 2013.

In this study, we adopted the log stochastic volatility (log SV) and square root SV models, with or without jumps in the observation and state equations. The MCMC method is used to estimate the models. Compared to other estimation methods, MCMC has three advantages (Johannes and Polson, 2010): it estimates state variables (volatility and jump size in our models) as a byproduct, accounts for estimation risk, and requires only conditional posterior distributions of parameters. The latter are much easier to derive than an unconditional posterior distribution, as required in other likelihood-function-based methods.

The remainder of this chapter is arranged as follows. Section 52.2 introduces and derives the square root SV model, and Section 52.3 summarizes the empirical results. Model diagnosis is given in Section 52.4, and Section 52.5 discusses a potential connection between the estimated jump behavior and the events of the Asian financial crisis. The conclusion is presented in Section 52.6.
