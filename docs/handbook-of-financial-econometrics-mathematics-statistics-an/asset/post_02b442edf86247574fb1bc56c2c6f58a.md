The detailed procedure of mixture copula-based risk integration is shown in Algorithm 38.1, which consists of three Stages, with a total of seven Steps. Stage 1 prepares marginal risk distributions and the copula for Monte Carlo simulation. Stage 2 simulates total risk distribution by Monte Carlo simulation. Stage 3 calculates VaR from total risk distribution. Assume there are n types of individual risks (r1, r2, ..., rn) to be integrated and the number of Monte Carlo simulations is N. Next, each step is explained in detail, as follows.

### Algorithm 38.1: Procedure of mixture copula-based risk integration.

- **Stage 1: Preliminaries**
  - Step 1: Estimate marginal risk distributions (F1, F2, ..., Fn)
  - Step 2: Estimate mixture copula C
- **Stage 2: Monte Carlo simulation**
  - Step 3: Draw a joint sample of uniform random variables (u1, u2, ..., un) from the mixture copula C
  - Step 4: Transform the sample (u1, u2, ..., un) into the sample of risk losses (s1, s2, ..., sn) = (F1⁻¹(u1), F2⁻¹(u2), ..., Fn⁻¹(un))
  - Step 5: Sum all the losses from Step 4 to derive integrated loss L = s1 + s2 + ⋯ + sn
  - Step 6: Repeat Step 3 to Step 5 N times to derive N simulated integrated losses L1, L2, ..., LN
- **Stage 3: Calculation**
  - Step 7: Calculate VaR from integrated losses L1, L2, ..., LN

Stage 1 is the preliminary stage, comprising Steps 1 and 2. Step 1 estimates the marginal distributions (F1, F2, ..., Fn) for risk types (r1, r2, ..., rn). Generally, a goodness-of-fit test such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (KS test for short) is used to find the most proper distributions for empirical risk data series, and maximum likelihood estimation is used to estimate the parameters. Step 2 uses the most suitable copula C to model the dependence structure across risk types (r1, r2, ..., rn). Similar to Step 1, there are many types of copulas, so first, the goodness-of-fit test is needed to determine the best fit for the dependence structure. Popular test methods for mixture copula include the χ² test, Cramer-von Mises test and Anderson-Darling test (Hu, 2006; Kole et al., 2007; Genest et al., 2009; Grundke and Polle, 2012; Weiß, 2014). Moreover, the expectation maximization (EM) algorithm of Dempster et al. (1977) is usually used to implement the maximum likelihood estimation for the mixture copula (Cai et al., 2008).

Stage 2 is the Monte Carlo simulation stage, comprising Steps 3–6. In Step 3, a joint sample of uniform random variables (u1, u2, …, un) is drawn from the mixture copula C. Then, Step 4 transforms the sample (u1, u2, …, un) into a sample of simulated risk losses (s1, s2, …, sn) by using the inverse functions of marginal risks (F1⁻¹, F2⁻¹, …, Fn⁻¹). Step 5 sums all the simulated risk losses (s1, s2, …, sn) to obtain the total loss L. Step 6 repeats Step 3 to Step 5 N times to derive N simulated total losses L1, L2, …, LN.

When the number of simulations reaches a certain threshold, the simulated empirical distribution and the actual distribution are very close. The larger the number of simulations is, the closer the simulated total risk distribution is to the actual distribution, and the longer is the computational time required. To balance simulation accuracy and time cost, it is generally accepted that simulation times N should conform to equation (38.21).

N × α × (1 − α) ≥ 50, (38.21)

where α is confidence level. For risk management purpose, the confidence level is generally set larger than 90%. BCBS (2006) considers that the banks should hold enough economic capital to resist a magnitude of loss that may come around only once in a millennium and recommends 99.9% as a proper confidence level. Therefore, the number of simulations is generally set at 100,000 in many studies (Jorion, 2007; Li et al., 2015).

Stage 3 is calculation stage, comprising only Step 7. Step 7 calculates VaR from total losses L1, L2, …, LN. Plenty of total losses L1, L2, …, LN from Monte Carlo simulations construct empirical distribution of the total loss, based on which VaR or other risk measures can be calculated. VaR measures the maximum loss at a specified probability over a given time horizon. Pioneered by J.P. Morgan, it has become a standard measure used in financial risk (Jorion, 2007; Hull, 2012). Given a stationary distribution of the losses, VaR at confidence level α ∈ (0,1) is defined as the smallest number l such that the probability of loss L exceeding l is not larger than (1 − α):

VaR = inf{ l : P(L ≥ l) ≤ (1 − α) }. (38.22)

## 38.4 Empirical Study

In this empirical study, the constructed mixture copulas are applied to describe the dependence structure between the credit, market and operational risks of Chinese banks and to integrate these three types of risks into the total risk of the Chinese banking industry. The results of mixture copulas are compared with seven other major risk integration approaches to verify their effectiveness.

### 38.4.1 Data descriptions

The data used in this empirical study are credit risk, market risk and operational risk data of Chinese banks. Covered in Pillar I of the Basel Accord, credit, market and operational risks are the three major risks faced by banks (BCBS, 2006). However, the lack of these risk data has always been an obstacle. Many studies seek risk data from academic studies, expert judgments, surveys and external databases (Rosenberg and Schuermann, 2006; Aas et al., 2007; Li et al., 2015). However, usually only scattered risk statistics or data for a single risk type can be obtained. Therefore, some other studies have tried to look for proxies for these risks (BCBS, 2010; Li et al., 2018). Financial statements present a relatively complete picture of a bank's performance, in which all earnings and losses are recorded and summarized into an income statement periodically. Therefore, a growing number of studies are mapping the income statement items to these risk types.

Market risk refers to the potential losses arising from changes in the value or price of an asset, such as those resulting from fluctuations in interest rates, currency exchange rates, stock prices and commodity prices (BCBS, 2001), which is nicely captured in income statements as “trading revenue” in American banks (Rosenberg and Schuermann, 2006; Inanoglu and Jacobs, 2009). However, unlike American banks, there is no direct “trading income” item in financial statements of Chinese banks. The sum of net gains from financial investments, net gains from foreign exchange and changes in fair value of investment properties is used as the proxy of market risk instead. Credit risk is defined as the potential loss caused by borrower’s or counterparty’s failure to meet its obligations (BCBS, 1999), which is usually considered close to the meaning of provisions (Kuritzkes and Schuermann, 2007). Operational risk is defined as the risk of direct or indirect loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal process, people and systems or from external events (BCBS, 2006). Non-operating expenses is a relatively proper proxy for it (Inanoglu and Jacobs, 2009).

Compared with the former approaches, this type of approach is able to obtain data of various bank risk types, thereby offering a relatively comprehensive dataset. However, it is noteworthy that financial statements are disclosed in quarterly or biannual frequency, so the data of a single bank usually cannot support accurate model estimation in the empirical study. A common solution for this problem is to gather risk values of different banks together to study the general condition of the entire banking industry. For example, Rosenberg and Schuermann (2006) gathered the quarterly data of 17 banking holding companies from 1994Q1 to 2002Q4. Kuritzkes and Schuermann (2007) collected data of all banks in Y-9C regulatory reports from 1986Q2 to 2005Q1. Therefore, in line with these studies, we also gather the risk values of different banks together to construct our empirical dataset.

Because all the risk proxies are from financial statements, the sample in this empirical study includes only listed Chinese banks that release financial reports regularly. Although there are only 16 listed banks in China, their total assets accounted for 53% of the entire Chinese banking industry in 2016. The 16 Chinese listed banks and their abbreviations are shown in Table 38.3. Most of these banks were listed after 2007, and a new accounting standard was also put into effect in the same year, so the collection of our data starts from 2007. Moreover, to eliminate the scale effect, the data of earnings or losses are divided by risk-weighted assets, which are updated every half year. Ultimately, the data consist of all observations from the 16 banks, spanning from 2007 to 2016, in semi-yearly frequency.
