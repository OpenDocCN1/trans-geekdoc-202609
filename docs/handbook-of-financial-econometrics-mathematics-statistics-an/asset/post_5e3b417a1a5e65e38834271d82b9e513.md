Y is either non-interest expense to assets (NIEA) or non-interest income (NIEI), α is the average NIEA or NIEI value for all observations over time, and ε is the idiosyncratic error term for all observations. The same model is used for NIEA or NIEI. The variables RELL, ROA, and CR are ratios. The variable Asset is measured in natural logarithm and its associated coefficient is the elasticity of the non-interest ratio to changes in assets.

The dependent variable NIEA is the ratio of non-interest expense to total assets. The dependent variable NIEI is the ratio of non-interest to the sum of interest income and non-interest income. To the extent that relative financial institutions have adopted compensation or other expenses in response to DFA, these should be observed as statistical relationships between the NIEA and NIEI variables and others if present.

RELL is the variable depicting the ratio of outstanding residential real estate loans a financial institution has or the ratio of first mortgage loans a bank has outstanding as a portion of all outstanding loans. The variable DF is a dummy variable that takes a value of one for observations from the years 2010–2014 and a zero for other years before the Dodd–Frank Act in this data series, 2007–2009. Interacting DF with RELL allows for the detection of whether the time period after the passage of DFA created a different relationship between the ratio of non-interest expenses to assets, or income, and the fraction of loans generated. It is anticipated that this value will be positive, indicating that, all else equal, when the fraction of outstanding loans increases within a financial institution then the incremental expenses of compliance will increase as well.

The variable Asset is the total assets held by a financial institution and represents firm size. It is further anticipated that banks will experience economies of scale in Dodd–Frank compliance and expect this variable to be negative in both models. The variable ROA is the ratio of after-tax net income to total assets and serves as a proxy for profitability of community banks. It is assumed that the ROA variable would be negative, indicating that more profitable institutions are more cost efficient, including DFA compliance, relative to less profitable firms.

An increase in NIEI suggests this increase in firm level revenues is more rapid than income expansion, and may indicate that revenue generated from loans have low margins associated with these products. A decline in NIEI can be explained by revenues of community banks increasing faster than their income in the period after DFA was enacted.

A statistically significant increase is observed in the ratio of non-interest expenses-to-income when the real estate loans-to-total loans ratio increases, and a statistically significant decrease is observed in the ratio of non-interest expense to assets when the real estate loans-to-total loans ratio increases. In the model where NIEI is the dependent variable, an increase in the ratio of real estate loans-to-total loans increases the ratio of non-interest expenses to total income. When NIEA is the dependent variable, an increase in the real estate-to-total loan ratio decreases the non-interest expense ratio. Regardless of model, prior to the Dodd–Frank Act financial institutions with relatively large shares of real estate loans were more cost efficient than other firms, all else equal, but became less cost efficient after the passage of the Act. When NIEI is the dependent variable, this indicates an increase in the ratio of real estate loans-to-all loans tended to increase expenses faster than income. This suggests an inclination by lenders seeking to provide loans to local residents, thereby growing the bank’s assets, with regard to maintaining relationships.

Results also imply economies of scale for any size lender when increasing the level of assets within the institution is present. Deliberate attention to exploiting economies of scale may be a strategic objective since the NIEI ratio is elastic with respect to organizational assets. If, on the other hand, service provision is the objective, the inelasticity of the NIEA response to assets suggests little interest to expanding product offerings requiring additional compliance expense.

When the capital ratio coefficient is evaluated it appears to be positive and significant in the NIEA model and negative in the NIEI model. This indicates a combination of adequate capital is an important consideration when businesses begin to incur compliance expenses. Capital must be combined with financial institutions of a minimum size. Capital can withstand the increased compliance expenses but the institution must be large enough to afford the expense. Non-interest expenses, however, decline as capital increases. This suggests that cost efficiency improves, all else equal, when capital increases since institutions obtain this by reducing their compliance or other operations cost. Estimating this equation using a sample of community banks suggests the cost-saving feature reflected by increasing capital is the more economically important effect.

Asset size is an important determinant of the NIEI version of the model. Hence, the effect of size differs based on the asset size of the lender. This observation also indicates that lender size can offset the positive effect of increasing the specialization needed for product offerings when the institution is of a minimum size, with the offsetting effect upon expenses monotonically increasing with larger asset sizes. Asset size is also an important determinant in the NIEA model, implying the effect of size on the non-interest cost ratio differs based on firm asset size. The apparent non-interest expense savings from specializing is offset based on size. These results suggest that, after the passage of DFA, the smallest financial institutions have opportunity to impact their cost efficiency through lending activity and may explain not making, or not beginning to make, expenditures associated with Dodd–Frank compliance. Dodd–Frank regulations would appear to be a barrier to entry for community banks not currently dedicating resources to developing compliance with these regulations within specific product segments.

## 131.5 Regulation and Market Structure

Community banks compete with financial institutions of various corporate types in non-metro markets. Given that relatively fewer deposits are available in these regions, comparatively lesser numbers of financial institutions are likely to be present. As a result, entry and exit by financial institutions into a non-metro area may signal extraction of monopoly rents after eliminating other competitors (Christophers, 2018).

Overlooked in these studies is the role of institutional type in the competitive outcomes. Banks, regardless of size, and credit unions may operate with different objectives. Bank investors seek financial returns in proportion to their equity ownership. On the other hand, a credit union is a cooperative financial institution owned by depositors. The objective of credit unions has been modeled variously as profit maximization (Flannery, 1974; Hempel and Yawitz, 1977; Keating and Keating, 1975), value maximization (Keating and Keating, 1989; Rubin et al., 2013), cost minimization, and service maximization—whether for savers or borrowers (Smith, 1982, 1984, 1988; Smith et al., 1981). These are likely to be prominent in certain non-metro markets where they operate.
