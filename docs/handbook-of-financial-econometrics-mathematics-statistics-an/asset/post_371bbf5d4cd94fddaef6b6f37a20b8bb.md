
SOX and the DFA were both passed to curb business fraud and corruption by reducing the risky behavior of financial institutions, requiring certification and disclosure of various elements of internal information. SOX (2002) requires management to certify its financial reports are free from errors and weaknesses. Secondly the results of an externally conducted audit of the internal control environment have to be reported. DFA was passed in 2010 with the goal of imposing additional market-based discipline on the risk management behavior in the financial services, including requirements for protection of investors and consumers from risky bank practices as well as providing stakeholders with information about the link between executive compensation and bank performance. The intended benefits of SOX and DFA are to reduce informational asymmetry between financial institutions and their stakeholders which should decrease firm risk. Critics claim that uniform application of the regulations associated with this legislation has resulted in various, negative, unintended consequences which may increase systemic risk among financial institutions.

Regulatory compliance, as measured by traditional indicators includes capital requirements, changes in credit risk expenses, differential size effects. Another major component of financial institution performance is net non-interest expense as a percentage of assets, or the net non-interest expense as a percentage of income. Compliance requirements impose non-interest expenses, reflected as changes in overhead expense, in general, and overhead expense, in particular.

In order to meet the compliance obligations of the regulations extending from this legislation, changes in overhead expense, in general, and personnel expense in particular have occurred. Community banks appear to have changed their total workforce, experiencing a 14.4% decrease in non-interest expense between 2002 and 2011, and then a 55.4% increase between 2011 and 2017. Compensation expenses at these institutions increased 66.1% between 2011 and 2017. These increases outpaced changes in assets in community banks, which grew 23.8% between 2002 and 2011 and then 65.4% after 2011. Therefore, increases in personnel expense are an important component in the change in performance of community banks, resulting in changes in product offerings, costs, and efficiency for individual firms (Table 131.2.2).

## 131.3 Regulation and Discretionary Loan Default Risk Management

Section 131.2 demonstrated the ways legislation has affected the competitive environment of community banks. This section describes the consequences of these changes on a single parameter: loan and lease loss expenses. An important component of the earnings quality for financial institutions is the loan loss provision, an annual expense set aside to create an allowance for loan and lease losses for uncollected loans and loan payments. It is often used as an accounting measure of risk management as well as a surrogate for portfolio quality. Loan loss provisioning is a key financial statement item that reveals management’s responses to changes in loan portfolio risk. The higher the risk of non-collection, the higher the loan loss provision should be. Operational responses to recent regulation have made loss provisioning more timely and transparent (Molyneux and Wilson, 2017), improving the transparency of financial statements to investors and reflective of risk management culture changes in financial institutions.

Taken collectively, the effects of the SOX and DFA legislation indicate that substantive regulatory change in governance (SOX) and monitoring of internal controls (DFA) by bank regulators can result in an operating environment that economizes on loan loss provision expenses, which provides a measure of management’s perception of loan risk. However, with the passage of the DFA, financial and depository institutions had to make specific changes to business processes, which changed risks, and therefore altered the internal controls and loan loss provisions of banks. These led to the development of regulations whose purpose is to generate information that informs investors and other stakeholders about control weaknesses in the firm that could lead to fraud. Disclosure requirements support both risk management practices and safety and soundness of operations within the banking sector.

### 131.3.1 Requirements of DFA and SOX for risk management

The combined effects of improved governance and enhanced lending expertise facilitate a change in optimal risk taking in the banking industry; SOX and DFA affected banking industry production costs. DFA imposed new duties and obligations on loan originators. Among the new residential mortgage loan origination standards imposed by Dodd-Frank are the following. Mortgage loan originators (MLOs) must be licensed as described by applicable law. Dodd-Frank attempts to eliminate originators from having borrowers pursue unsustainable loans that are sold to the secondary market. Originators are prohibited from receiving compensation that varies based on any component of the loan or from the amount of principal. MLOs cannot receive compensation from the consumer and a third party. Dodd-Frank prohibits originators from steering consumers towards residential mortgage loans that the borrower lacks the ability to repay as well as not allowing originators to direct the consumer toward loans that erode equity. Dodd-Frank reduces excessive fees during the loan origination process, does not allow for loan conditions that are abusive to the consumer, steer consumers to non-qualified mortgages and away from a qualified mortgage, mischaracterize a consumer’s credit history.

Arce (2013) interprets DFA in the context of moral hazard and adverse selection in that banks are constrained to write contracts that have the consequence of attracting employees with social preferences that benefit firm performance and employee remuneration. Banks become simultaneously market- and socially-driven under DFA. This research supports this finding by proposing that social conscious employment compensation policies are driven, in part, by improved governance transparency required by SOX. This approach is complemented by Chemla and Hennessy (2014) and Stiglitz (2010) who observe that lender expertise is required for successful DFA implementation. Compliance provides incentives to banks to retain loan originators, thereby reducing the moral hazard experienced by the investor. However, such a tactic facilitates the application of lender expertise in a new type of originator-based adverse selection that increases lending to be more profitable, potentially increasing the number of riskier loans. Augmented loan loss provisions occur to offset this consequence of employee retention.

Governance climate and employee skill affect bank loan loss provisioning. This suggests complementarity between SOX and DFA. Banks are motivated and experience oversight, which reduces the risk of their loan portfolio, causing loan loss provisions to decline after the passage of DFA. Alternatively, if DFA is designed to lower the risk of default, then banks should increase their loan volume, consistent with the “too big to fail” adverse selection hypothesis and loan loss provision should increase to make sure that financial institutions have enough reserves to cover any losses.
