## 13.5 Explaining the Time-Variation in Diversification Benefits

Thus far, we have shown investors still benefit from international diversification though markets are increasingly integrated. In this section, we further evaluate the extent to which economic factors impact the potential gains from international investments. Specifically, we are interested to ascertain how inflation, economic growth, risks, and dynamics of domestic and international financial markets affect the potential values of diversification. Moreover, we compare the impact of R² and correlation measuring market integration on the benefits of diversification. In this section, we use a regression framework to analyze the impact of economic and financial variables on the diversifying benefits.

Table 13.5 shows statistics for each time-series. Due to overlapping returns in estimating efficient frontiers, each assessment of diversifying gains is positively correlated with its one-period lag at 1% significant level. For the lags greater than one period, the economic and the statistical significance of auto-correlations are trivial. The statistics of Phillips–Perron (PP) and Dickey–Fuller (DF) tests suggest that the unit-root hypothesis should be rejected for each time-series of the Sharpe ratio benefits. These findings suggest that a one-period lag should be considered in regression.

### 13.5.1 What determines the size of Sharpe ratio benefits?

The mean-variance diagram masks the benefits of diversification since it implicitly compares a fixed domestic portfolio with the global efficient frontier. Instead, the domestic SRs are time-varying and, therefore, the global MSR portfolio needs to be rebalanced periodically. Because the change in return and the change in volatility brought by diversification vary in different scenarios, we only can determine the size of benefits by empirical tests.

We first analyze the mean-variance efficiency benefit (δ). Since investors can rebalance portfolios to have the same risk profile in a multi-factor world, diversification depends only on the idiosyncratic risk that remains unexplained by the factors after re-weighting. Specifically, we use the PP unbalanced panel data and consider the following regression models:

$\delta_{j,t} = \alpha + \beta' \delta_{j,t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma' x_{j,i,t} + \xi_{j,i,t}, \qquad (13.11)$

and

$\varepsilon_{j,t} = a + b' \varepsilon_{j,t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{N} c' x_{j,i,t} + \zeta_{j,i,t}, \qquad (13.12)$

where $\delta$ is an increase in Sharpe ratio and $\varepsilon$ is a decrease in volatility defined in equations (13.4) and (13.6), respectively. $\alpha$ and $a$ are constant, and $x_i$ is the $i$th independent variable of country $j$ that are described in Table 13.2. We estimate the model for all countries jointly with unbalanced panel data ordinary least squares (OLS) but account for cross-sectional dependence by clustering the standard error across country indices. The full set of independent variables is shown in Table 13.2. The variables included, in general, are highly correlated and thus an estimation of the full model creates a large amount of insignificant independent variables and generates noise to the results. We apply general-to-specific algorithm (see Bekaert et al., 2011; Bekaert et al., 2012; Hendry and Krolzig, 2005) to reduce the number of variables that explain the variation of diversification benefit. This algorithm includes multiple steps to eliminate variables with coefficients that are insignificant. In detail, we estimate models (13.11) and (13.12) with all independent variables and then eliminate the least statistically significant variables that do not reach 15% of significance level. We set up a high significance level to be conservative in retaining potentially important variables. We continue estimating the model and excluding the individual variables while simultaneously testing whether already excluded variables should be included. Considering the results displayed in Table 13.5, a one-period lag is included in all regressions to control the autoregressive property of diversifying gains.
