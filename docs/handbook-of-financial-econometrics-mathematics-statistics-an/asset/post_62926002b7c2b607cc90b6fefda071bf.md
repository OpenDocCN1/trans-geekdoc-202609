
defined as⁵

$$\psi_{\tau,s}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|}}\psi\left(\frac{t-\tau}{s}\right),\quad s,\tau\in R, s\neq0,\tag{36.2}$$

where $s$ is a scale parameter (dilation factor) that controls the length of the wavelet and $\tau$ represents the time domain (translation parameter) which controls the location of the wavelet.

The mean of the wavelet function has to be zero; the integral of its square is equal to one and it has to satisfy the admissibility condition.

## 36.2.2.2 Continuous wavelet transform

The continuous wavelet function of a time series $x(t)$ is defined as

$$W_x(\tau,s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s|}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\psi^{*}\left(\frac{t-\tau}{s}\right)dt,\tag{36.3}$$

where $*$ denotes a complex conjugate.

In empirical applications, one has to make a choice about the mother wavelet. In this chapter, the mother wavelet used is the Morlet wavelet first introduced by Goupillaud et al. (1984), and which is the most common complex wavelet used in continuous wavelet analysis. It is defined by the following equation for a central frequency value superior or equal to five:

$$\psi_{\eta}(t)=\pi^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{i\eta t}e^{\left(-\frac{t^2}{2}\right)},\tag{36.4}$$

where $i=\sqrt{-1}$ is an imaginary number and $\eta$ denotes the central frequency of the wavelet.

In this study, we use the Morlet wavelet with $\eta=6$, which, for a feature extracting purpose, is a good choice because it provides a good balance between time and frequency localizations (Rua and Nunes, 2009).

## 36.2.2.3 Wavelet coherence

The wavelet coherence (WC) used in this study is a powerful tool to depict the interaction between two time series and examine their co-movements in
the joint time–frequency domains. The first step in deriving the WC is the calculation of the cross-wavelet transform (CRWT). The CRWT of two time series x(t) and y(t) is defined as follows (Liu, 1994):

$$W_{xy}(\tau,s)=W_x(\tau,s)W_y^*(\tau,s)\tag{36.5}$$

where $W_x$ and $W_y$ are the continuous wavelet transforms of the original series $x(t)$ and $y(t)$, respectively, and the symbol * indicates complex conjugate.

In order to depict the local covariance of two time series in both time and frequency domains, Liu (1994) defines the cross-wavelet power (CRWP) as follows:

$$CRWP_{xy}=|W_{xy}(\tau,s)|\tag{36.6}$$

Having the CRWT and CRWP defined, the squared wavelet coherence (SWC) can be defined as the squared absolute value of the smoothed cross-wavelet spectrum, normalized by the product of the smoothed individual wavelet power spectra of each time series (Torrence and Compo, 1998):

$$R_{xy}^2(\tau,s)=\frac{|S(s^{-1}W_{xy}(\tau,s))|^2}{S(s^{-1}|W_x(\tau,s)|^2)S(s^{-1}|W_y(\tau,s)|^2)}\tag{36.7}$$

where $S$ is a smoothing operator.

According to Grinsted et al. (2004), $S$ is defined as follows:

$$S(W)=S_{\text{scale}}(S_{\text{time}}(W(s)))\tag{36.8}$$

where $S_{\text{scale}}$ is smoothing along the wavelet scale and $S_{\text{time}}$ stands for smoothing in time.

The SWC coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, and it can be considered as a squared local correlation coefficient between two time series in the time–frequency domains. A high coefficient of SWC indicates a strong co-movement between two time series.

## 36.2.2.4 Phase

The square WC is unable to distinguish between positive and negative correlations and identify the lead–lag relationship between two time series. For this reason, Torrence and Webster (1999) defined the WC phase difference:

$$\phi_{xy}(\tau,s)=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\Im\{S(s^{-1}W_{xy}(\tau,s))\}}{\Re\{S(s^{-1}W_{xy}(\tau,s))\}}\right)\tag{36.9}$$

where $\Im$ and $\Re$ are, respectively, an imaginary part operator and a real part one.

The phase difference is represented by arrows on the WC figures. A zero phase indicates that two series move together. If the arrows point to the right (left), the time series are in-phase (anti-phase), i.e., they are positively or negatively correlated, respectively. If the arrows point up and right (left), this indicates that the study series are in-phase (anti-phase) and the first (second) time series leads the second (first) one (Aguiar-Conraria and Soares, 2014).

## 36.2.2.5 Wavelet correlation

To investigate the correlation and the cross-correlation between stock market returns in different investment horizons, we apply the DWT to decompose stock market return series on a scale-by-scale basis. The DWT employs two sets of functions: the father or the scaling function which represents the long scale smooth component of the time series and the mother or the wavelet function which represents the deviation from the smooth component. The following equations, respectively, define these functions:

$$\varphi_{j,t}(x)=\frac{1}{(2)^{j/2}}\varphi\left(\frac{x-t}{2^{j}}\right),\tag{36.10}$$ $$\psi_{j,t}(x)=\frac{1}{(2)^{j/2}}\psi\left(\frac{x-t}{2^{j}}\right).\tag{36.11}$$

The father and mother functions are, respectively, associated with low-pass and high-pass filters:

$$\varphi(t)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}g(k)\varphi(2t-k),\tag{36.12}$$ $$\psi(t)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}h(k)\varphi(2t-k),\tag{36.13}$$

where $g(k)$ is the low-pass filter, $h(k)$ is the high-pass filter, $\varphi(2t-k)$ is the father wavelet and $N$ is the length of the time series.

The $j$th level wavelet coefficients ($W_{j,t}$) and scaling coefficients ($V_{j,t}$) of DWT are defined as

$$W_{j,t}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_j-1}h_{j,\ell}X_{2^{j}(t+1)-1-\ell\mod N},\tag{36.14}$$ $$V_{j,t}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_j-1}g_{j,\ell}X_{2^{j}(t+1)-1-\ell\mod N},\tag{36.15}$$

where $L$ is the length of the filter and $L_j=(2^{j}-1)(L-1)+1$.

The DWT presents some drawbacks. For example, the sample size of the time series processed by the DWT into J level should be an integer multiple of 2^J, and this method is not translation invariant. The maximum overlap discrete wavelet transform (MODWT) removes the DWT limitations since it can be applied to all sample sizes and it is translation invariant (Percival and Walden, 2000). The jth level wavelet coefficients (W_{j,t}) and scaling coefficients (V_{j,t}) obtained from MODWT, are defined as follows:

$$\tilde{W}_{j,t}= \sum_{\ell=0}^{L_j-1} \tilde{h}_{j,\ell} X_{(t-\ell)\bmod N}, \tag{36.16}$$

$$\tilde{V}_{j,t}= \sum_{\ell=0}^{L_j-1} \tilde{g}_{j,\ell} X_{(t-\ell)\bmod N}, \tag{36.17}$$

where L is the length of the filter and L_j = (2^j -1)(L -1)+1, \tilde{h}_{j,\ell}= h_{j,\ell}/2^{j/2}, \tilde{g}_{j,\ell}= g_{j,\ell}/2^{j/2}, h is the high-pass filter and g is the low-pass filter.

In this chapter, in order to estimate the wavelet correlation, we perform the time-scale decomposition analysis by means of the MODWT with the Daubechies (1988, 1992) least asymmetric filter of length L = 8, commonly denoted as LA(8) which appears to be the most favored one in economic and financial applications (Ranta, 2010).

The wavelet variance of a stochastic process {X} with level j and at the scale \lambda_j = 2^{j-1} is defined as follows (Percival, 1995; Gençay et al., 2001; Gallegati and Gallegati, 2007; Gallegati, 2012):

$$\sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \sigma_X^2(\lambda_j)= \sigma_X^2, \tag{36.18}$$

where \sigma_X^2(\lambda_j) represent the contribution to the total variability of the process due to changes at scale \lambda_j.

This relation implies that the wavelet variance decomposes the variance of a time series into variance components associated with different scales. The wavelet variance for scale \lambda_j is the variance of the j-level wavelet coefficients:

$$\sigma_X^2(\lambda_j)= \operatorname{var}\{\tilde{W}_{j,t}^2\}. \tag{36.19}$$

An unbiased estimator of the wavelet variance based on MODWT is

$$\tilde{\sigma}_X^2(\lambda_j)= \frac{1}{\tilde{N}_j} \sum_{t=L_j-1}^{N-1} \tilde{W}_{j,t}^2, \tag{36.20}$$

where $\tilde N_j = N - L_j + 1 > 0$ is the number of maximum overlap coefficients at scale $\lambda_j$ and $L_j = (2^j - 1)(L - 1) + 1$ is the length of the wavelet filter for level $j$.

The wavelet covariance between two stochastic processes {$X$ and $Y$} at scale $\lambda_j$ with level $j$ is defined as the covariance between wavelet coefficients of {$X$ and $Y$}, that is

$\gamma_{XY}(\lambda_j) = \mathrm{Cov}\left(\tilde W_{j,t}^X, \tilde W_{j,t}^Y\right). \tag{36.21}$

An unbiased estimator of the covariance is (Whitcher et al., 1999, 2000; Gallegati, 2012):

$\tilde\gamma_{XY}(\lambda_j) = \frac{1}{\tilde N_j} \sum_{t=L_j-1}^{N-1} \tilde W_{j,t}^{(X)} \tilde W_{j,t}^{(Y)}. \tag{36.22}$

The wavelet correlation between two stochastic processes {$X$ and $Y$} at scale $\lambda_j$ with level $j$ is defined as the ratio of the wavelet covariance, $\gamma_{XY}(\lambda_j)$, and the square root of their wavelet variance, that is

$\rho_{XY}(\lambda_j) = \frac{\gamma_{XY}(\lambda_j)}{\nu_X(\lambda_j) \nu_Y(\lambda_j)}. \tag{36.23}$

where $\nu_X^2(\lambda_j)$ and $\nu_Y^2(\lambda_j)$ are the wavelet variances of $X$ and $Y$, respectively, and $|\rho_{XY}(\lambda_j)| \le 1.$

An unbiased estimator of the wavelet correlation is

$\tilde\rho_{XY}(\lambda_j) = \frac{\tilde\gamma_{XY}(\lambda_j)}{\tilde\nu_X(\lambda_j) \tilde\nu_Y(\lambda_j)}. \tag{36.24}$
