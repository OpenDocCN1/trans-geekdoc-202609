## Abstract

This chapter proposes a mixture copula framework for integration of different types of bank risks, which is able to capture comprehensively the nonlinearity, tail dependence, tail asymmetry and structure asymmetry of bank risk dependence. We analyze why mixture copula is well-suited for bank risk integration, discuss how to construct a proper mixture copula and present detailed steps for using mixture copula. In the empirical analysis, the proposed framework is employed to model the dependence structure between credit risk, market risk and operational risk of Chinese banks. The comparisons with seven other major approaches provide strong evidence of the effectiveness of the constructed mixture copulas and help to uncover several important pitfalls and misunderstandings in risk dependence modeling.

## 38.1 Introduction

Bank risk integration is always a great challenge for both researchers and practitioners because correlations between various types of bank risks are highly complicated (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, BCBS, 2010; Baker and Filbeck, 2015). Commercial banks face many types of risks, such as credit risk, market risk and operational risk (BCBS, 2006). These risks are not independent; on the contrary, they are intrinsically related to each other (Breuer et al., 2010; Imbierowicz and Rauch, 2014). This becomes evident very early in banks’ day-to-day business and was realized fully when the subprime crisis started in 2007 (Hartmann, 2010; Gagliardini and Gourieroux, 2013). Therefore, when integrating different risk types, accurate descriptions of the dependencies between them are extremely important (BCBS, 2010; Grundke, 2013). However, the dependence structure between bank risks is rather difficult to capture because it is characterized by four sophisticated features, i.e., nonlinearity, tail dependence, tail asymmetry and structure asymmetry.

Nonlinearity is one typical feature of bank risk dependence. The opposite of nonlinearity is linearity, which is one of the most ubiquitous concepts in modern finance (Embrechts et al., 1999). While being computationally convenient and straightforward to understand, linear correlations usually first come to mind when modeling dependence. However, the hasty assumption of linearity is quite problematic because the dependent real-world risks are always far from elliptical distributions (Embrechts et al., 1999; Weiß and Scheffer, 2015). As banks get involved with an increasing number of complex businesses and products, there is mounting evidence that bank risk dependencies are outside the linear category (Brockmann and Kalkbrener, 2010).

Tail dependence is another typical feature of bank risk dependence, which is a very important feature. Tail dependence measures the probability that all risk types are in their extremes. There is strong evidence of tail dependence in finance (Weiß and Scheffer, 2015; Koliai, 2016). The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision has observed that large losses, either from different risk types or within the same risk type, tend to strike simultaneously during stress situations (BCBS, 2010). Hu (2006) also expects significantly stronger dependence when risks are at their extremes. Moreover, tail dependence has now attracted broad attention partly because Value at Risk (VaR) has become one of the most popular tools for risk measurement (Jorion, 2007). As a quantile-based measure, VaR is tightly related to the tail structure of risk distribution. Consequently, a clear trend observed among practitioners is the desire to capture tail dependence more accurately in risk integration models (BCBS, 2010).

Tail asymmetry is the third typical feature of bank risk dependence. Tail dependence can be subdivided into upper tail dependence and lower tail dependence, and these two do not always exist concurrently. Even if that occurs, they are often not identical to each other. It is commonly observed that financial markets are much more likely to crash together than to boom together (Bartram and Wang, 2015). In bad times, there are more adverse risk outcomes observed than there are beneficial outcomes observed during good times (BCBS, 2010). Chen and Tu (2013) also note that bank risks always exhibit more joint negative extremes than joint positive extremes. These indicate that for bank risks, the upper tail dependence is usually stronger than the lower tail dependence. Tail dependence and its asymmetry pertain to the tail behavior of bank risks, which plays an important role in risk measurement.

Structure asymmetry is the fourth typical feature of bank risk dependence. It refers to interactions between different pairs of bank risks being distinctly different and asymmetric. Banks face many types of risks, such as credit, market and operational risks, and any two of them are relevant to each other (Baker and Filbeck, 2015). However, due to their diverse sources and interaction mechanisms, the dependencies between them are usually disparate (Brockmann and Kalkbrener, 2010). For example, it is widely recognized that the correlation between credit risk and market risk is quite different from correlations between operational risk and the two risk types (Rosenberg and Schuermann, 2006; International Financial Risk Institute Foundation and Chief Risk Officers Forum, IFRI/CRO, 2007). Generally, structure symmetry often exists only among risks that are uniform and interact in the same manner, such as credit portfolios of homogeneous risks (BCBS, 2010).

This chapter proposes a mixture copula framework to describe the dependencies between various types of bank risks, which is able to comprehensively capture the four typical features of the dependence structure. To be specific, by reasonably combining individual copulas with different feature capturing capabilities, a mixture copula that is able to capture all features can be constructed. Then, the mixture copula can be used to describe the dependence structure between different risk types and integrate them into the total risk of banks. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that constructs mixture copula to model bank risk dependence. In the empirical study, the proposed approach is applied to describe the dependencies between credit, market and operational risks of Chinese banks. The comparisons with seven other major approaches provide strong evidence of the effectiveness of the constructed mixture copulas and help to uncover some important pitfalls and misunderstandings in risk dependence modeling.

## 38.2 Literature Review

Many risk integration approaches have emerged to model the dependence structure between different types of risks to date (Böcker and Hillebrand, 2009; Kretzschmar et al., 2010; Grundke, 2010; Bellini, 2013). Among these approaches, simple summation approach, variance–covariance approach and copula approach are recognized as three major approaches (BCBS, 2010; Baker and Filbeck, 2015). With distinct advantages, these approaches model the risk dependencies in different ways. Table 38.1 summarizes these major risk integration approaches and their capabilities of capturing the typical features of risk dependence.

The simple summation approach appears the earliest and is a simple and highly tractable way of integrating different risk types (Li et al., 2015). This approach assumes that all risks are perfectly linearly correlated with each other and integrates the risks by simply adding them up. In other words, it considers that all inter-risk correlation coefficients are equal to one and each risk component receives equal weight. Therefore, it cannot capture nonlinear dependence, tail dependence and tail asymmetry. Moreover, because all correlation coefficients are equal, it also cannot capture structure asymmetry. Due to its oversimplified assumption, it performs the worst and has no ability to capture all the typical features, and so all symbols in the column of simple summation approach are “×”.

The variance–covariance approach takes it a step further by introducing linear correlation to consider the interactions between different risk types (BCBS, 2010). In this approach, linear correlation coefficients are calculated and used to integrate individual risk components into the total risk. In regard to modeling dependence, the linear correlation usually first comes to mind, and therefore, this approach has been considered the most basic and traditional risk integration approach, favored by over 75% of the banks surveyed by IFRI/CRO (2007). By allowing different correlation coefficients between different risk types, the variance–covariance approach can capture the asymmetries in the correlation structure, so the last row of this approach is symbol “√”. Nevertheless, it is apparent that linear correlation coefficients cannot capture nonlinear interactions between risks, let alone tail dependence and tail asymmetry. This significant deficiency restricts its further usage in many situations.
