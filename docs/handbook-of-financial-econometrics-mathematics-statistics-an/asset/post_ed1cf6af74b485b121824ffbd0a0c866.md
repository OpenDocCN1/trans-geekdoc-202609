Various types of marginal risk distributions can be joined together to produce a multivariate distribution. In contrast to linear correlation, a copula captures the complete dependence structure inherent in a random vector. Different copulas exhibit different tail dependencies, ranging from tail independence to tail dependence, and different types of asymmetries (Chen and Tu, 2013), which makes it possible to integrate risks involved in a wide variety of dependence structures. Among all types of copulas, frequently used copulas include Gaussian copula and t copula from the elliptical copula family and Gumbel copula, Clayton copula and Frank copula from the Archimedean copula family (Cherubini et al., 2004; Koliai, 2016). The specific mathematical expressions of these five copulas are shown in Appendix 38A. For more detailed descriptions of these copulas, please see Cherubini et al. (2004).

The Gaussian copula is very popular in practice, at first due to the common assumption of normality in many financial modeling applications and its ease of understanding. However, it does not allow for tail dependence, marked as “×” in the rows of tail behavior, which is a major drawback that makes it a less favorable candidate (Kole et al., 2007; Tang and Valdez, 2006; Hua, 2017). A growing mass of literature on the usefulness of the student-t copula as an alternative to the Gaussian copula for modeling financial risk is associated with its ability to incorporate tail dependence (Tang and Valdez, 2006). It is often chosen in order to incorporate an explicit degree of tail dependence into the integrated risk distribution. Generally, elliptical copulas do not have symmetric structure unless all off-diagonal elements of the correlation matrix are the same (BCBS, 2010). However, a drawback is the strong symmetry in tails (Weiß and Scheffer, 2015), which brings about the only “×” in the row of tail asymmetry for the t copula.

Some Archimedean copulas are actively used because they allow for asymmetric tail dependence. For example, the Gumbel copula shows strong association in the right tail, while the Clayton copula shows strong association in the left tail. However, a major flaw of these two copulas is that they cannot capture negative dependence (Jaworski et al., 2009; Viebig and Poddig, 2010), so the rows of negative nonlinear dependence of the two copulas are marked as symbol “×”. The Frank copula can capture both positive and negative nonlinear dependencies; however, no clear association in the tails can be observed. Another notable disadvantage of the copulas in the Archimedean class is that they are highly symmetric in structure (Tang and Valdez, 2006; Koliai, 2016). This symmetry limits the use of these copulas to aggregating risks that are uniform and interact in the same manner, such as credit portfolios of homogeneous risks. They cannot be used to model asymmetric
behaviors, which is quite commonly observed within different risks (BCBS, 2010), so the last rows of all Archimedean copulas are marked as “×” in Table 38.1.

The above analyses show that none of the major risk integration approaches is able to capture all the typical features of risk dependence at the same time. Among these approaches, the copula approach, in particular the t copula, comes closest to the goal. We notice that although no single copula is flexible enough to capture all features, they have their own characteristics, so their mixture might be applicable to all situations. Nelsen (1999) demonstrates that mixtures of copulas are also copulas. By carefully choosing the component copulas in the mixture, a model that is flexible enough to generate most dependence patterns can be constructed (Lai and Tseng, 2010; Chen and Tu, 2013). Therefore, this chapter proposes a mixture copula framework to comprehensively capture the typical features to accurately model the dependence structure between risks.

## 38.3 Mixture Copula Framework

In this section, the proposed mixture copula framework is introduced in detail. First, the rationale of the mixture copula is introduced. Then, the mixture copula that is able to capture all typical features of bank risk dependence is constructed. Finally, detailed steps of the mixture copula approach for bank risk integration are presented.

### 38.3.1 Illustration of the mixture copula

The idea of a copula was mooted as early as the 19th century in statistics, and modern theories about copulas date back to about 50 years ago when Sklar (1959) defined and provided some basic properties of a copula. Despite the long history in statistics, copulas have been applied in financial markets only very recently. Embrechts et al. (1999) and Li (2000) were among the first to popularize the use of copulas in the field of risk management. After that, research in this area has grown very fast. The mixture copula has emerged much later than did the copula and has attracted much less attention so far. Nelsen (1999) demonstrates that mixtures of copulas are also copulas. Hu’s (2006) research is the earliest research we know about applying mixture copulas, following which Hong et al. (2007), Rodriguez (2007), Chen and Tu (2013), Weiß and Scheffer (2015) and some other studies using mixture copulas have gradually come into existence. To the best of our knowledge,
our work uses the mixture copula to integrate different types of bank risks for the first time.

The mixture copula is the convex combination of several individual copulas and is also called mixed copula or M-copula in some studies (Turgutlu and Ucer, 2010). The function of mixture copula can be formulated as in equation (38.1).

```
$$C_{mix}(u_1, u_2, ..., u_n | \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{s} w_k C_k(u_1, u_2, ..., u_n | \theta_k), \qquad (38.1)$$
```

where {C_k(u_1, u_2, ..., u_n | θ_k)} is a sequence of known copulas with unknown parameters {θ_k} and {w_k}_{k=1}^s are the weights satisfying 0 ≤ w_k ≤ 1 and Σ_{k=1}^s w_k = 1 for k = 1, ..., s. Denote the entire parameters in the mixed copula as θ, that is, θ = (θ_1, ..., θ_s, w_1, ..., w_s)^T. The mixture copula facilitates separation of the concepts of degree of dependence and structure of dependence, embodied in two different groups of parameters, i.e., association parameters and weight parameters (Hu, 2006). The association parameters, denoted as (θ_1, ..., θ_s)^T, are the parameters that control the degree of dependence in each copula function, while weight parameters, denoted as (w_1, ..., w_s)^T, are the shape parameters reflecting the credence we should assign the corresponding copula. For example, if the mth copula is the most appropriate structure for the data, then we would expect that w_m is close to 1 (Cai et al., 2008).

The biggest advantage of using a mixture copula model is that it can nest different copula shapes, i.e., mixed copulas can capture different patterns of dependence structure. Therefore, it is more flexible to model the dependence and can deliver better descriptions of dependence structure than an individual copula (Hu, 2006; Cai et al., 2008). In other words, a mixture copula can integrate the dependence capturing abilities of its component copulas. For example, a mixture copula that includes Gaussian and Gumbel copulas can improve a single Gaussian dependence structure by allowing for possible right tail dependence (Cai et al., 2008). In general, a mixed copula model is able to generate dependence structure that does not belong to one particular existing copula family. By carefully choosing component copulas in the mixture, a model that is simple yet flexible enough to generate most dependence patterns can be constructed (Chen and Tu, 2013).
