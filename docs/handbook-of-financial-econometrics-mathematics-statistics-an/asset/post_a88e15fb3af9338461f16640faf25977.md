$$d\log S_t = \mu dt + \sqrt{V_t^{SV}}\, dW_{s,t}^P,$$

$$dV_t^{SV} = (a + b V_t^{SV})dt + \eta^c \sqrt{V_t^{SV}}\, dW_{v,t}^P,$$

$$dW_{s,t}^P dW_{v,t}^P = \rho^c dt, \tag{33.4}$$

where $W_{i,t}^P$ ($i=s,v$) are instantaneously correlated Brownian motions and $\mu$, $a$, $b$ are drift parameters of the processes, each under the actual probability measure. Due to the change of measure operations, the focus is on $\eta^c$ and $\rho^c$, while the drift is largely irrelevant to the current study. Unless the calibrated variances, $\{V_t^{SV}\}_{t=0}^T$, satisfy (33.4), the model is internally inconsistent.

Consider the following Euler-discretized time series stochastic volatility model specification:

$$\log S_{t+1} - \log S_t = \mu \Delta t + \sigma \sqrt{V_t} \Delta t \epsilon_{s,t}^P,$$

$$V_{t+1} - V_t = (a + b V_t^{SV}) \Delta t + \eta \sqrt{V_t} \Delta t \epsilon_{v,t}^P,$$

$$\mathrm{Cor}(\epsilon_{s,t}^P, \epsilon_{v,t}^P) = \rho, \tag{33.5}$$

where $\epsilon_{i,t}^P$ ($i=s,v$) follow a standard Normal distribution, $N(0,1)$. To test the internal consistency of the model while recognizing the discrete nature of the time series, we suggest the following hypothesis:

**H1. Internal Consistency.** *The series of calibrated variances, $\{V_t^{SV}\}_{t=0}^T$, satisfies the Euler-discretization of (1) under the actual probability measure, $\mathbf{P}$, with $\sigma=1$ and $\{\eta,\rho\}=\{\eta^c,\rho^c\}$ that solve the calibration problem (33.3).*

The interpretation of $V_t$ from (1) as the true variance of the underlying asset requires that the calibrated time series, $\{V_t^{SV}\}_{t=0}^T$, possess some of the same properties as the econometric estimates of variance based on high-frequency data. Recall that while the drift need not be preserved under the change of measure, by the Girsanov theorem (and the related diffusion invariance principle) the volatility of variance, $\eta$, and the instantaneous correlation between the innovations, $\rho$, are unaffected by the change of measure. Consequently, once again, the focus is on $\eta$ and $\rho$, and not the drift. Let $\{V_t^{RV}\}_{t=0}^T$ denote an econometric estimate of the variance process based on high-frequency return data.

**H2. External Consistency: Instantaneous Correlation and Volatility of Variance.** Both the time series of econometric estimates of variation and the time series of calibrated variances, $\{V_t^{RV}\}_{t=0}^T$ and $\{V_t^{SV}\}_{t=0}^T$, satisfy discretized stochastic volatility models (following the specification in (33.5)) with the same instantaneous correlation and volatility of variance parameters.

Since calibrated variance is based on closing prices, $V_t^{SV}$ is applied to explain the return over the following trading day $t+1$. In contrast, econometric realized variation measures characterize the return over the same trading day, which naturally follows from the algorithm for their construction. Therefore, the discretized stochastic volatility model for realized measures of variance is implemented with the corresponding shifts in time indexes. While Hypothesis 1 addresses the internal consistency of the Heston model, Hypothesis 2 relates to the external consistency between the dynamics of the calibrated and statistically estimated time series of variances.

While the value of a parameter may be influenced by the change of measure, the value of a variable must be identical under both probability measures. Research (see Andersen et al., 2007) suggests that the presence of jumps in the underlying process cannot be rejected, but jump activity is missing from a pure stochastic volatility model. However, for option contracts with longer maturities, jumps effectively increase the level of volatility, but may not significantly impact the implied volatility skew (see Rebonato, 2004). If $V_t$ is a device for generating an implied volatility surface matching the market prices of options, then calibrated variance series would generally exceed the continuous component of variation and closely follow measures of total variation.

**H3. External Consistency: Level of variance.** The mean calibrated variance exceeds the mean continuous component of variation and is close to the mean realized variation.

Hypothesis 3 should be distinguished from the results concerning the volatility risk premium, which is the difference between expectations of the future accumulated volatility under different probability measures.

## 33.3 Empirical Tests of Calibrated Variance

In this section, we proceed to test empirically the formulated hypotheses and seek to elucidate the role of variance in stochastic volatility models for option prices.

### 33.3.1 Dataset

**Option prices.** The first component of the dataset consists of option contracts on the S&P 500 index (ticker: SPX), traded on the CBOE. All data on option prices, levels of the S&P 500 index, interest rates, and dividend yields are obtained from the OptionMetrics database via Wharton Research Data Services. The option data spans the time period from April 29, 2011 until April 29, 2016, corresponding to five years of observations.² The first stage of the empirical study consists of calibrating the Heston stochastic volatility model to market prices of option contracts on the S&P 500 index. As has been widely documented (see Gatheral, 2006), the stochastic volatility model is capable of recovering the market prices of option contracts with sufficient length of time to maturity. Thus, the Heston model is calibrated only to market prices of options with at least 0.10 years to maturity. Extremely long-dated options with maturities of 0.75 years are also excluded from the sample. The sample contains only options traded on the day at which their closing prices are reported and with bid prices exceeding zero. To reduce the effect of illiquid options, we restrict the sample to options with volume and open interest in the top three quartiles. Furthermore, deep out-of-the-money and in-the-money options (with spot moneyness either in excess of 1.20 or below 0.80) are removed from the sample.³ Finally, we winsorized the data at the 99% level to mitigate the effect of jumps in the underlying S&P 500 index.

The Heston model is re-calibrated daily, as is the standard industry practice. The calibration errors are represented by the minimized IVRMSE objective function (advocated, among others, by Christoffersen et al., 2009)

$$\text{IVRMSE}(\Psi_t, V_t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}\frac{\left(P_{K_i,\tau_i}(t) - SV_{K_i,\tau_i}(\Psi_t, S_t, V_t)\right)^2}{\left(\partial C^{BS}/\partial \sigma^{BS}\right)^2}}, \tag{33.6}$$

where $\Psi_t = \{\lambda_t, \bar{v}_t, \eta_t, \rho_t\}$, $N_t$ is the number of option contracts in the calibration dataset corresponding to a given trading day, $t$, $P_{K_i,\tau_i}(t)$ is the market price of option $i$, $SV_{K_i,\tau_i}(\Psi_t, S_t, V_t)$ is the theoretical price obtained from the Heston model with parameters $\Psi_t$.⁴

### Econometric measures of variance

The second component of the dataset consists of high-frequency econometric estimates of the S&P 500 index variance. In particular, we consider realized variation, $RV$, realized kernel, $RK$, and bipower variation, $BPV$. These measures are obtained from the Oxford-Man Institute realized library (version 0.3), Heber et al. (2009). Days when the econometric measures of variation are not available are, by necessity, excluded from consideration.

With the advent of high-frequency trading and the consequent availability of ample intraday data on returns, it became possible to estimate the variation of a financial return series without resorting to a latent variable model (like the original ARCH of Engle (1982) or its stochastic volatility counterparts, see Kim et al., 1998). In particular, based on the path properties of stochastic processes, Andersen et al. (2001) introduced the realized variation as a model-free measure of variance over a given period of time (e.g., a trading day):

$$RV_t = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \left[\log \left( \frac{S_{t+(i+1)\delta}}{S_{t+i\delta}} \right) \right]^2, \tag{33.7}$$

where $t$ is the beginning of the trading day $t$ and $\delta > 0$ reflects the intra-day sampling frequency with $t+N\delta$ corresponding to the end of the trading day $t$. In a frictionless market without bid-ask spreads, one would prefer to sample the data as often as possible. However, the presence of market microstructure noise makes it optimal to select a lower sampling frequency. Following common practice, we use the realized variation measure constructed based on 5-minute returns. As a robustness check, we also consider the realized kernel of Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2008) (with Parzen weight function). Finally, to isolate the effect of jumps, we use the bipower variation measure of Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2004) to quantify the continuous component of variation. In particular, the mean and median instantaneous correlation are both close to $-0.77$, which generates a negative implied volatility skew. Moreover, the calibrated instantaneous correlation is never positive.
