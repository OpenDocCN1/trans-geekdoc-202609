In modeling volatilities, first Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) present martingale situation for option prices. Thus, they allow for no-arbitrage environment. The objective is to meet a world represented by a probability space. In defining no-arbitrage environment, they state that it implies law of one price and positive kernels exist. The latter phenomenon leads to a log-normal process. The log-normal process (ξ_T) has a mean of zero and variance equal to μ². Then, kernel-replicating portfolio

⎧ ln(m_{t,t+Δt}) = 1/2 μ/(rσ²) (μ - σ²) ln( β_{t+Δt}/β_t ) + (-μ/σ²) ln( S_{t+Δt}/S_t ) ⎫ = β' R*_{t+Δt}

is made up of underlying asset and cash account B_t, with B_{t+Δt} = e^{rΔt} B_t. R*_{t+Δt} is being the two-dimensional vector of log returns that constitutes the basis. Another advantage of the kernel-replicating portfolio is that it has a conditional expectation. The advantage of a conditional expectation on one hand, it is an expected value and on the other hand, it is probabilistic nature (see Sebehela, 2015a). Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) argue that when a jump is priced in the kernel-replicating portfolio, that jump would be an additional risk factor. This chapter shows that besides the fact that the jump is an additional risk factor, the jump is a risk premium which increases the overall return of an option.

The data used in Buraschi and Jackwerth (2001) is Berkeley Options Database containing all minute by minute European option quotes and trades on the S&P 500 index traded on CBOE from April 2, 1986 to December 29, 1995. The goal of the authors was to identify (i) index, (ii) risk-free interest rate, (iii) several options with different strike price/index level ratios (moneyness) and (iv) constant maturity. The index is typically used by traders as a benchmark, interest rate is one key input for option pricing, and options with different maturities are used for extrapolating deterministic and stochastic volatilities.
