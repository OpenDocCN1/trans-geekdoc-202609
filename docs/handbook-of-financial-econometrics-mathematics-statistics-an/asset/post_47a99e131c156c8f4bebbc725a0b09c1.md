Kim and In (2010) analyze the impacts of investment horizon on asset allocation between stocks and bonds. Likewise, In, Kim, and Gençay (2011) examine the portfolio allocation between value and growth stocks over various investment horizons. Their findings both confirm that investment horizons play an important role on the asset allocation decisions. ## 4.2 Methodology

The risk characteristics and correlation structure among various asset classes are the important features from the point of view of investors and portfolio managers. Here, wavelet analysis is applied to capture these characteristics among various assets over different time-frequency scales. As indicated above, investors may have different investment horizons due to their different patterns of consumption. Different investors have different portfolio management needs. Here, the impact of investment horizon on the optimal compositions of portfolio is also investigated by comparing the optimal portfolio weights in a minimum variance portfolio over different time-frequency scales.

### 4.2.1 Introduction of wavelet method

Unlike the traditional correlation method, wavelet analysis allows us to decompose the data into time-frequency domain to capture the different cyclical features of the data. It involves the projection of a signal onto an orthogonal set of components like conventional Fourier time-series analysis. The main difference is that wavelet analysis is localized in both time and frequency whereas the standard Fourier transform is only localized in frequency. That is, wavelet analysis presents the characteristics of the local behavior of the function whereas Fourier transform presents the characteristics of the global behavior of the function. The wavelet analysis has been successfully used for many studies in social science during the last decade. Scholars are usually familiar with the use of the discrete wavelet transform and the maximal overlap discrete wavelet transform such as In, Kim, and Gençay (2011) and Baruník, Kočenda, and Vácha (2016). More recently, the continuous wavelet transform is becoming more widely used in financial issues (Graham, Kiviaho, and Nikkinen, 2013; Lin, Chen, and Yang, 2016; Lin et al., 2018). One major benefit of continuous wavelet analysis has over discrete wavelet analysis, as Aguiar-Conraria et al. (2008) and Rua and Nunes (2009) pointed out, is that we need not define the number of wavelets (time-scales) in continuous wavelet analysis which generates itself according to the length of data. This allows us to easily identify and interpret patterns or hidden information. Therefore, continuous wavelet analysis is applied here to evaluate the time and frequency-varying features of asset returns relations.

Typically, the continuous wavelet transform decomposes a time-series in terms of some elementary functions which are derived from a time-localized mother wavelet by translation and dilation. These wavelets result from a mother wavelet, Ψ(t) that can be expressed as a function of the time position τ (translation parameter) and the scale λ (dilation parameter), which is related with the frequency. The wavelet can be defined as

$$\Psi_{\tau,\lambda}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\psi\left(\frac{t-\tau}{\lambda}\right),\tag{4.1}$$

where mother wavelet Ψ(t) is a continuous function in both the time domain and the frequency domain. τ acts to translate the function across the data and λ acts to vary the time scale of the mother wavelet function Ψ. Here, 1/λ is a normalization factor to ensure energy normalization that wavelet transform is comparable across time-frequency scale. Consequently, we have defined in the time domain a sequence of functions that are doubly indexed, once by location in the time domain, once by the scale. To be a mother wavelet, Ψ(t) must satisfy following conditions: it must have zero mean, i.e., ∫ ψ(t)dt = 0, its square integrates to unity, i.e., ∫ ψ²(t)dt = 1 and the so-called admissibility condition.

The most commonly used continuous mother wavelet is the Morlet wavelet. Morlet wavelet is composed of a complex exponential multiplied by a Gaussian envelope and defined as follows:

```
Ψ(t) = π^{-1/4} e^{i ω t} e^{-t^2/2}, (4.2)
```

where ω is the parameter which controls the number of oscillations within the Gaussian envelope. The Gaussian envelope gives less weight to pixels further from the center and makes this wavelet localized in time. In practice, ω is set between 5 and 6 for providing a good balance between time and frequency localization as suggested by Aguiar-Conraria and Soares (2011). Moreover, Morlet wavelet consists of a complex structure, given by the term e^{i ω t}, within a Gaussian envelope captured by the term e^{-t^2/2}. Therefore, we can compute the phase of the wavelet transform of each series from complex part, which gives us information about the position in the cycle of a time-series.

Given a time-series x(t), its continuous wavelet transform with respect to the mother wavelet Ψ can be represented as

```
W_X(τ, λ) = 1/√λ ∫_{-∞}^{+∞} x(t) ψ* ((t - τ)/λ) dt, (4.3)
```

where τ is the time position and λ is the scale which is related with the frequency and the (*) denotes complex conjugation. For a discrete time-series, x(t), t = 1, …, T, we have

```
W_X(τ, λ) = 1/√λ Σ_{t=1}^{T} x(t) ψ* ((t - τ)/λ). (4.4)
```

Here, a Morlet wavelet is chosen as the mother wavelet. Since the mother wavelet Ψ(t) is complex-valued, the corresponding wavelet transform W_X(τ, λ) is also complex-valued. With a complex-valued wavelet, we can separate the amplitude and phase information of a time-series from real and imaginary parts, respectively.

As we are interested in extracting the localized variance or volatility of time-series x(t) at a time-frequency scale. The amplitude of the wavelet transform can provide a useful tool for measuring the variance of x(t) at a time-frequency scale. Similar to the terminology used in Fourier transform, the wavelet power spectrum (WPS) can be defined as the square of the wavelet transform's amplitude. That is, one can define the wavelet power spectrum of time-series x(t) as

```
WPS_X(τ, λ) = |W_X(τ, λ)|^2. (4.5)
```

### 4.2.2 Portfolio optimization

In this chapter, we investigate the role played by gold in a diversified portfolio comprised of bonds and stocks. The optimal portfolio weights are solved by minimizing the variance of portfolio. Traditional static mean-variance portfolio choice model relies on the sample mean and variance-covariance to determine the optimal portfolio weights. Here, we consider the problem that the investor seeks to minimize the variance of the real returns at different investment horizons. The optimal portfolio weights w(λ_j) are obtained from minimizing the global variance of portfolio at a particular frequency λ and time j. We thus solve the following problem:

min_w w(λ_j)' Σ(λ_j) w(λ_j),

∑_{i=1}^k w_i(λ_j) = 1   and   w_i ≥ 0,    (4.8)

here w_i(λ_j) = (w_1(λ_j), w_2(λ_j), …, w_k(λ_j)) is the vector of weights invested in each asset at a particular frequency λ and time j, Σ(λ_j) is the variance-covariance matrix of portfolio simulated through wavelet method at a particular time-frequency scale λ_j. For each investment horizon, we present the optimal composition of minimum risk target portfolios. By comparing the vector of portfolio weights at different time scales, we can analyze the holding-period effect on portfolio allocation. As a whole, this will help investors to deepen the understanding of the true nature of these relationships over different time scales. The results therefore should be of interest to both individual investors with either short or long investment horizons, and to long-term investors such as pension plans and insurance companies.

The purpose of this chapter is to evaluate gold as a diversifier in the traditional stocks and bonds portfolio.
