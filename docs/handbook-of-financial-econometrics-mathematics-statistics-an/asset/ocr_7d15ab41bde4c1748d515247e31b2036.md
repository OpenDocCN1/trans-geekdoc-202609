# Abstract

This study uses recurrent survival analysis technique to show that higher spread of conversion-stock prices and higher buy-back ratio of stock repurchase provide the CBs' debt-like signals of Constantindides and Grundy (1989); while lower risk-free rate, higher capital expenditures, higher non-management institutional ownership and higher total asset value provide the CBs' equity-like signals of Stein (1992). While the equity-like signals might accelerate the rate of sequential conversions and weaken the CBs' risk-mitigating effect in the presence of risk-shifting potential, this study shows that this can happen only in a financially healthy firm with higher free cash flow. For financially distressed firms, the CBs' risk-mitigating effect is maintained.

Convertible bonds (CBs) are often adopted as a financing vehicle for firms with higher levels of agency and adverse selection costs associated with information asymmetry (see, e.g., Loncarski et al., 2006; Dutordoir et al., 2014). By reducing the agency and adverse selection costs, the CBs can serve as a risk-mitigating instrument (see, e.g., Green, 1984; Brennan and Kraus, 1987; Brennan and Schwartz, 1988; Constantindides and Grundy, 1989; see, e.g., Frierman and Viswanath, 1994; Mayers, 1998, 2000; Isagawa, 2002; Cornelli and Yosha, 2003). In Green (1984), when the investment opportunity and the CB issue are contemporaneous, it was shown the issue of CBs can resolve the agency cost problem raised by Jensen and Meckling (1976) by curbing shareholders' incentives for risk-shifting in a one-period setting. More evidences can be found by Lewis et al. (1998, 1999, 2003), Krishnaswami and Yaman (2008), Dorion et al. (2014), and King and Mauer (2014). Likewise, it was argued that CBs can serve as a sequential-financing tool to reduce the agency cost problem when the firm has a sequence of investment opportunities (see, e.g., Mayers, 1998, 2000; Cornelli and Yosha, 2003; Wang, 2009), as evidenced by Chang et al. (2004) and Korkeamaki and Moore (2004).
