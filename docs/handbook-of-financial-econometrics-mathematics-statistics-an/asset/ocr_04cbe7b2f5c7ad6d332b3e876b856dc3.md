Moreover, Gao and Huang (2016) determined that information disclosed as a result to reduce hedge fund managers’ ability to profit off of political inside information reduced managers’ ability to generate alpha. Agarwal et al. (2013), Shi (2017), and Gao et al. (2016) all illustrate that the relative opacity of the hedge fund structure affords hedge fund managers certain benefits over more traditional asset classes and if this advantage is taken away, hedge funds will lose their ability to generate a differentiated return series. There are two main points that are important to consider when attempting to evaluate and model hedge fund performance as Baker et al. (2017) illustrate in their examination of the first four moments of hedge fund returns (i.e., mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis) incentive-related factors and the hedge fund’s specific strategy have the greatest impact on the distribution of hedge fund returns. Incentives drive behavior and given the relative opacity that comes along with the structure of hedge funds, it is understandable that some are skeptical about what drives hedge fund performance and whether the performance that is reported by hedge fund managers is an accurate reflection of the underlying fund.

Given the relative opacity of the hedge fund product, managers have an incentive to conceal positive and negative performance results (Bollen and Pool 2008, 2009), but Smith and Mumtaz (2017) illustrate that researchers and practitioners should not just assume that this perceived performance manipulation is meant to harm investors. It seems as though, based on the results presented in Smith et al. (2017), that managers are more likely to delay reporting positive performance results and more fully integrate negative performance results into the hedge fund’s return series. Cassar and Gerakos (2001) and Jorion and Schwarz (2014) both make reference to the fact that hedge fund managers have some discretion over how they report their results but conclude that it is difficult to control this smoothing and it is also challenging to determine if the smoothing is intentional. Cao et al. (2018) illustrate that hedge fund managers play a market role as arbitragers and invest in stocks that are relatively inefficiently priced, which illustrates why there may be some inconsistencies in pricing and performance.
