## 24.2.3 Linear programming

The development of linear programming is driven by its applications in economics and management. The standard form of a linear programming problem can be written as

Maximize c^T x subject to A x ≤ b and x ≥ 0. (24.11)

In other words, linear programming is a technique for optimization of a linear objective function, subject to linear equality and linear inequality constraints. Any vector x satisfying the constraints of the linear programming problem is called a feasible solution of the problem. Linear programming is an important branch of applied mathematics that solves a wide variety of optimization problems. The biggest advantage of linear programming is that it always achieves the optimal solution if one exists.

Ritchken (1985) proposes using linear programming that places constraints on the time preference, risk aversion, and state probabilities. Under some specific set of assumptions, the price of a European call option can be viewed as the objective function to be optimized and formulated by a linear program. Then the lower and upper bounds of a European call option can be obtained by minimization and maximization of the objective function in linear programming problem.

In an incomplete market, the prices of the state securities are not unique. To replicate Merton (1973)'s result, Ritchken (1985) assumes investors always prefer more to less, which implies the positive value of the current price of the specific pure state security that pays out $1 at maturity day if this specific state occurs. The option bounds are, therefore, obtained by solving the following linear programming problem

Minimize (or Maximize) C0 = Σ_{i=1}^n c_i e_i, (24.12)
where $e_i$ is the current price of a pure state $i$ security that pays out $1 at maturity date if state $i$ occurs; $s_i$ is the value of the underlying security in state $i$; $c_i$ is the value of a European call option in state $i$; $d_i$ is the state discount factor that is non-increase with respect to wealth; $\pi_i$ is ex ante consensus probabilities that state $i$ occurs; $S_0$ and $B_0$ are current values of the underlying security and a portfolio containing all state securities, respectively.

Ritchken (1985), Ritchken and Kuo (1988) propose the application of the single-period linear-programming option model to handle multiple periods. Ritchken and Kuo (1988) obtain identical upper bounds and tighter lower bounds than Ritchken (1985). Ritchken and Kuo (1989) further consider the additional preference restrictions on the investors with third order or above stochastic dominance criteria. They also apply a decreasing absolute risk aversion (DARA) dominance rule in a state preference context. With the two restrictions, the closed form analytical solutions for the option bounds are not obtained, but a simple numerical example are presented.

The behavior of the DARA lower bound for the option price is not wholly explored by Ritchken and Kuo (1989). Basso and Pianca (1997) analyze the general rule of DARA dominance. They solve the optimization DARA problem in the case of three states of nature, proving that the optimal solution satisfies the nonlinear constraint as equality. This solution is obtained through an exponential-like utility function having constant absolute risk aversion (CARA).

Basso and Pianca (1997) reconsider the following maximization and minimization problem:

Minimize (or Maximize) $C_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i e_i$,
subject to $\sum_{i=1}^n s_i e_i = S_0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = B_0$
with $(e_1, e_2, e_3, \dots, e_n) \in D$, (24.17)

where the set $D$ depends on the stochastic dominance rule adopted and is determined by the assumptions made on the utility function. For example, given utility function with decreasing absolute risk-aversion (DARA) character, the set $D$ is constrained by:
- $e_i \geq 0$, $i = 1,2,\dots,n$
- $e_i \geq e_{i+1}$, $i = 1,2,\dots,n-1$
- $e_i e_{i+2} \geq e_{i+1}^2$, $i = 1,2,\dots,n-2$

## Alternative Methods for Determining Option Bounds: A Review and Comparison 929

Recently, Peña et al. (2009) show that the problem of computing sharp upper and lower static-arbitrage bounds on the price of a European basket option, given the prices of other similar options, can also be cast as a linear program. The linear program formulations readily yield super-replicating (sub-replicating) strategies for the upper (lower) bound problem. The dual counterparts of the linear program formulations in turn yield underlying asset price distributions that replicate the given option prices, and the bound on the new basket option's price. In the special case when the given option prices are those of vanilla options on the underlying assets, they show that the linear program formulations admit further simplifications. In particular, for the upper bound problem they derive closed-form formulas for the basket's price bound and for the corresponding super replicating strategy. In addition, their linear program approach admits efficient modeling of additional features such as basket options with negative weights, bid-ask spreads, transaction costs, and diversification constraints.

The option bounds discussed so far are derived by “parametric” methods. Although Perrakis and Ryan (1984) obtain bounds for all stock price distributions with a non-negative beta property, the actual values of the bounds depend on the specific stock price distribution. Similar to Perrakis and Ryan (1984), the bounds of Levy (1985) obtained by using SSDR are also parametric method. In addition, Ritchken (1985)'s linear-programming approach also requires parametric assumption of the state probabilities to calculate values for the bounds. In contrast to the previously discussed method, semi-parametric and non-parametric methods do not require strong assumptions in models and just need the historical data to derive option bounds.

Different from parametric methods, Lo (1987) initiated a literature on “semi-parametric” bounds on option prices. The option bounds derived by Lo (1987) depend only on the mean and variance of the terminal stock price and not on its entire distribution, i.e., they are “semi-parametric”.

Relying on the mathematical inequality provided by Scarf (1958), Lo (1987) derives the boundary of option prices. For the underlying asset price, S_t, with constant mean μ and constant standard deviation σ, the upper bound of an European call option, $C_{\max}^E$, can be written as

$C_{\max}^E = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\mu^2(\mu-K)+\mu\sigma^2}{\mu^2+\sigma^2} & \text{if } K \le \dfrac{\mu^2+\sigma^2}{2\mu} \\ \dfrac12\left[\mu - K + \sqrt{(\mu - K)^2 + \sigma^2}\right] & \text{if } K > \dfrac{\mu^2+\sigma^2}{2\mu}. \end{cases}$ (24.19)

The important feature of this equation is that the upper bound of the call option depends only on the first two moments of the price distribution.

Zhang (1994) sharpens Lo (1987)'s bounds by using the assumption that the underlying stock price is continuously distributed. Assume the terminal stock price $S_T$ with cumulative distribution $F(S_T)$ satisfying the moment conditions

$\int_0^{\infty} dF(S_T)=1,\ \int_0^{\infty} S_T dF(S_T)=\mu,\ \int_0^{\infty} S_T^2 dF(S_T)=\mu^2+\sigma^2.$ (24.20)

Based on these constraints, the upper bound, $C_{\max}^E$, and lower bound, $C_{\min}^E$, of a European call option can be obtained as

$C_{\max}^E = \dfrac12\left[\mu - K + \sqrt{(\mu - K)^2 + \sigma^2}\right],$ 
$C_{\min}^E = \begin{cases} \mu - \dfrac{K\mu^2}{\mu^2+\sigma^2} & \text{if } K \le \mu + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\mu} \\ 0 & \text{if } K > \mu + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\mu}. \end{cases}$ (24.21)
