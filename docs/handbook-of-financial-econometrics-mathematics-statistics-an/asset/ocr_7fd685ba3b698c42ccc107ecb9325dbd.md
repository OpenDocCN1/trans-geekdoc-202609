Shefrin and Statman (1985) suggest that investors frame winning stocks (risk-aversion) and losing stocks (risk-seeking) as different accounts and employ mental accounting to explain the disposition effect. Thaler (1999) indicates that a prediction of mental accounting is that investors will persistently hold losing stocks because closing an account at a loss is painful. In addition, although many studies adopt prospect theory to illustrate the disposition effect, they do not employ explicitly cognitive/emotional biases to account for this behavioral bias (e.g., Weber and Camerer, 1998; Dhar and Zhu, 2006; Da Costa et al., 2008; Dacey and Zielonka, 2009; Kohsaka et al., 2017). Since mental accounting is closely related with prospect theory, it is more likely to be a cognitive bias influencing investor’s decision-making. # 16.2.2 Regret avoidance

Regret, an emotional bias, consists of omission and commission. Omission means not performing an act that is usually expected to be performed, and commission means performing an act that results in some losses. According to traditional theory, the feeling of regret from omission and that from commission do not differ when investment decision led to a bad outcome. However, behavioral finance argues that omission is associated with decreased gain, whereas commission is related with increased loss. Based on the S-shaped value function, commission has a higher feeling of regret than omission (Fogel and Berry, 2006; Wong and Kwong, 2007; Huang and Zeelenberg, 2012).

Investors regret holding winning stock now if the stock price will decline tomorrow, and selling losing stock now if the stock price will increase tomorrow (Shefrin and Statman, 1985; Lin et al., 2006; Barber et al., 2007; Chong, 2009). Therefore, the feeling of regret might help account for why investors defer the selling of losing stocks and accelerate the selling of winning stocks (Shiller, 1999; Summers and Duxbury, 2012).
