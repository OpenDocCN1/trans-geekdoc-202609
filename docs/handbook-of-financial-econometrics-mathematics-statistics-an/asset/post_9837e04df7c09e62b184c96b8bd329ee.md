## 58.4.3 Estimates of the adjusted downside risk and return dynamics by a VAR model

One econometric issue that we face in testing the downside risk–return relation is that downside risk entails a long memory property. As a result, the test regression appears to be unbalanced with a stationary return process on the left-hand side and a fractionally integrated process in downside risk on the right-hand side of the test equation. To address this issue, we employ the fractional filter, $(1-L)^d$, and define $\Delta^d=(1-L)^d$, to deal with the long memory inherent in the VaR series.

The VAR system, expressing the joint dynamics of excess returns and the VaR, with a long memory adjustment is expressed as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{i,t+1}\\
\Delta^d V_{i,t+1}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\gamma^*_{ii}\lambda^*_i & \gamma^*_{ii}\phi^*_i\\
\lambda^*_i & \phi^*_i
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
R_{i,t}\\
\Delta^d V_{i,t}
\end{bmatrix}
+ \mathbf{u}_{i,t+1},
\tag{58.12}
$$

where γ*_{ii} is the estimated downside risk aversion parameter of country i using the long memory adjusted downside risk; Δ^d V_{i,t+1}; φ*_i is the coefficient of the first-order serial correlation in the adjusted downside risk process based on the semi-parametric method; and λ*_i measures the financial leverage effect of local market i. Referring to the system in equation (58.1), we have A*_i = [γ*_{ii}λ*_i γ*_{ii}φ*_i; λ*_i φ*_i] and the parameters in the A*_i matrix are estimated by the maximum likelihood method. Note that γ*_{ii} is calculated by γ*_{ii} = A*_{i,12}/A*_{i,22} = γ*_{ii}φ*_i/φ*_i. Equation (58.12) is a more general representation of the VAR process as it constitutes the leverage effect, the VaR feedback effect, the long memory effect, and the autocorrelations in stock returns and downside risk. Thus, the parameters in the VAR system not only contain information about modeling a long memory process, but they also capture the market dynamics associated with the risk-return interactions. In particular, risk-return relations can be tested by examining the significance of γ*_{ii}=0, whereas the leverage effect can be tested by examining the restriction on λ*_i=0. If λ*_i=0 is true, then equation (58.12) becomes

⎡ R_{i,t+1} ⎤   ⎡ 0      γ*_{ii}φ*_i ⎤ ⎡ R_{i,t} ⎤       
⎣ Δ^d V_{i,t+1} ⎦ = ⎣ 0      φ*_i      ⎦ ⎣ Δ^d V_{i,t} ⎦ + u_{i,t+1}.  (58.12)'

Equations (58.12) and (58.12)' allow us to examine the difference with and without the leverage effect, and its impact on the parametric relations in the system.

However, in a highly globalized financial system, equity prices in Asian markets often covary with those in a dominant market. Similar to the specification of equation (58.9), we introduce the cross-market risk effect into the VAR model while at the same time considering the long memory factor as

⎡ R_{i,t+1} ⎤   ⎡ γ*_{ij}λ*_jρ_{ij}   γ*_{ij}φ*_j ⎤ ⎡ R_{i,t} ⎤       
⎣ Δ^d V_{j,t+1} ⎦ = ⎣ λ*_jρ_{ij}        φ*_j       ⎦ ⎣ Δ^d V_{j,t} ⎦ + u_{ij,t+1}.  (58.13)

where γ*_{ij} represents the cross-country downside risk aversion. This coefficient measures the compensation for taking on the downside risk from the dominating country j, Δ^d V_{j,t+1}. The coefficient of φ*_j indicates a first-order serial correlation in the dominating downside risk process after taking the fractional difference. The cross-market leverage effect, λ*_j can be calculated by λ*_j = λ*_j ρ_{ij} / ρ_{ij} and its significance can be examined by testing λ*_j = 0. If we impose the restriction λ*_j = 0, equation (58.13) is reduced to

⎡ R_{i,t+1} ⎤   ⎡ 0      γ*_{ij}φ*_j ⎤ ⎡ R_{i,t} ⎤       
⎣ Δ^d V_{j,t+1} ⎦ = ⎣ 0      φ*_j      ⎦ ⎣ Δ^d V_{j,t} ⎦ + u_{ij,t+1}.  (58.13)'

If we further assume d = 0, equation (58.13)' becomes equation (58.6) in that γ*_ij = γ_ij and φ*_j = φ_j; the VaR then is characterized by a conventional downside risk series.

Having applied the fractional filtering by applying the d_GPH derived from Table 58.4 to the downside risk series for each market, we estimate equations (58.12) and (58.13) twice. Panel A in Table 58.5 reports the estimates of the unrestricted model, while Panel B reports the results of equations (58.12)' and (58.13)' by imposing the restriction of λ*_i = 0 and λ*_j = 0, respectively. Columns 4 and 7 contain the parameters (γ*_ii and λ*_i) for equation (58.12) and columns 5 and 6 present the parameters (γ*_ij) for equation (58.13). The test results are summarized as follows. First, in Panel A, we find that the leverage effect (λ*_i) is negative, and only the coefficients on Hong Kong, Indonesia and Singapore are significant.21 The cross-market leverage effects (λ*_j) of most of the Asian countries on the US (column 8) and China (column 9) are negatively significant, implying that the lagged return shocks to Asian countries will subsequently increase the downside risk both in the local market and in other markets. This finding is consistent with the market integration hypothesis: the cross-market effect is not restricted to a big event; rather it occurs on a weekly basis. More important, estimated values for γ*_ii are highly significant, indicating a positive downside risk–return relation across various markets (except Taiwan),22 and confirming that higher excess stock returns compensate for a rise in downside risk, supporting the tradeoff hypothesis.

Second, a rather striking phenomenon is the evidence of cross-market downside risk, γ*_ij, regardless of whether it is from the US (column 5) or China (column 6). The estimated coefficients are consistently positive and statistically significant.23 Note that the negative sign between the US downside risk and the expected returns on Asian stock markets in Tables 58.2 (column 5) and 58.3 (column 2) now turns positive (column 5 in Table 58.5), as the long memory component in downside risk has been modeled. Consequently, without giving an appropriate treatment to the long memory, the test result may be misleading about the downside-risk and return relation.
