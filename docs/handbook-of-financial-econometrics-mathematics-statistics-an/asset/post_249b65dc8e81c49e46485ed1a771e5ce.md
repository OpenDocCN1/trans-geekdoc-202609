
Thus, given the above information, our objective is to determine the values for \(y_{1,i}\), \(y_{2,i}\), \(y_{3,i}\) and \(x_{j,i}\) for each time interval that minimize the total discounted costs over the entire planning horizon. This problem can be solved in two ways. One can draw a tree diagram that delineates each possible path from one time period to the next until the end of the planning horizon, and then calculate the discounted total cost associated with each path. In this example, however, more than 5,000 paths are possible, which makes the tree diagram analysis impractical.¹ Another possibility to solving such an optimization problem is to use a method such as the method of conjugate gradients by Fletcher and Reeves (1964).² Briefly stated, the algorithm is as follows:

- 1. Arbitrarily choose a vector \(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_0\) which serves as an initial guess for the vector \(\overline{\mathbf{X}}\) that optimizes the function.
- 2. Find \(\overline{g}_0 = g(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_0)\), the gradient vector of the objective function at \(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_0\). Let \(\overline{p}_0 = -\overline{g}_0\) be the initial search direction.
- 3. Let \(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{i+1} =\) the position of the minimum of the objective function \(f(x)\) on the line through \(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_i\) in the direction \(\overline{p}_i\).
- 4. If \(\|\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{i+1} - \overline{\mathbf{X}}_i\|\) is less than \(\varepsilon\), a small number, terminate the process. The optimum is \(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{i+1}\). If \(\|\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{i+1} - \overline{\mathbf{X}}_i\| > \varepsilon\), go to the next step.
- 5. Find \(\overline{g}_{i+1} = \overline{g}(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{i+1})\), the gradient vector at \(\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{i+1}\). Calculate \(\beta_i = \frac{\overline{g}_{i+1}^{\,T}\overline{g}_{i+1}}{\overline{g}_i^{\,T}\overline{g}_i}\), where \(T\) represents the transpose of the vector.
- 6. Now \(\overline{p}_{i+1} = -\overline{g}_{i+1} + \beta_i \overline{p}_i\) is the new search direction. Go to step (3) and repeat to step (6).

Note that step (3) is a simple one-dimensional optimization problem that can be solved by such methods as the Golden section method or quadratic interpolation, etc. (Belegundu and Chandrupatla, 2011; Fox, 1971; Pozrikidis, 1999; Wilde and Beightler, 1967).

### 49.5.3 Model revision

The decision vector for each time interval derived here is optimal. However, economic conditions change and the costs associated with various sources of funds may also change (John, 1993; Panno, 2003). In addition, a firm is an ongoing entity and its funding requirements may change over time. Management may change or modify some or all of the constraints with respect to the debt/equity mix, the desired level of dividends payable to shareholders, or the most desired mix of short-term to long-term financing (Baskin, 1987; Lang et al., 1996).

The decision vectors derived here should minimize the total discounted cost of providing the necessary funds to the firm. These decisions simultaneously take into account all the information available and their interrelationships over successive periods of time. As the information available changes, the model could be rerun and new decision vectors derived. These decision vectors again would be optimal until new information becomes available.

## 49.6 Conclusions

This chapter has presented a model that deals with the problem of financing the current and projected activities of a firm at the lowest present value cost. The projected activities are influenced by the growth rate the firm wants to maintain and the types of projects the firm wants to undertake. The model considers all the dimensions of the funding problem and all the sources available to the firm, such as new equity issues, bond issues, bond refunding, short-term borrowings, surplus funds investment, desired dividend payments, stock repurchases, and earnings retention. Simultaneously, the model takes into account all their inter- and intra-period relationships.
