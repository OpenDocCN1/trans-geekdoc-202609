The major findings of this paper are as follows. The benefits of international diversification vary over time but still prevail despite an increasing integration of the global financial market. Both regression analyses and explanatory power tests show that market integration (adjusted R² and correlation) are negatively associated with the benefits of diversification; however, R² is more statistically and economically significant. We find that variables associated with the business cycle, such as inflation, country risk, liquidity, size of equity market, and the relative performance of domestic portfolio in the world market, are significantly associated with the potential values from overseas investments. P_{SS+DV(U)} = {w_p ∈ S : 0 ≤ w_i ≤ U_w(Cap)_i ≤ 1, i = 1,2,...,N}, U > 1, (13.2)

where w(Cap)_i is the proportion of the market value of each country i in the world, and U is any real number greater than 1. Investors may set U low if they wish the portfolio is close to the value-weighted portfolio, or a naïve diversification. Setting a high U means a great tolerance to low diversity and a possible illiquidity. The optimal weights can be solved by applying the Kuhn–Tucker conditions when the complementary slackness conditions, primal constraints, and gradient equations are fulfilled.

On the efficient frontier, the maximum Sharpe ratio (MSR) represents the highest mean-variance efficiency that can be achieved by the diversification. Specifically,

MSR_{SS+DV(U)} = max_{w_p} {(w_p^T μ)/(w_p^T V w_p)^{1/2} | w_p ∈ P_{SS+DV(U)}}. (13.3)

For an investor in any domestic market i, the greatest increase in unit-risk return brought by global diversification is

δ_{i,SS+DV(U)} = MSR_{SS+DV(U)} - SR_i, (13.4)

where SR_i is the Sharpe ratio of country i domestic portfolio.

The second measure of the benefits of international diversification is the maximum reduction in volatility. Elton et al. (2007) suggest that investors may seek to minimize the variance of a portfolio due to the lack of predictability of expected returns. In this case, an investor may want to construct a minimum-variance portfolio (MVP).

The third measure is to evaluate the out-of-sample performance of the optimal portfolio. Due to poor estimation in return (e.g., DeMiguel et al., 2009), it benefits academia and the Wall Street to evaluate the realized performance of the optimal portfolio. For a naïve investor without knowledge of the optimization technique, the value-weighted asset allocation, potentially, is a doable strategy for global diversification. Using the monthly returns in the previous 5 years, we construct internationally diversified portfolios that yield the realized maximum Sharpe ratio (RMSR). We calculate the Sharpe ratio for the RMSR portfolio in each month as

SR_{RMSR} = μ_{RMSR} / σ_{RMSR},  (13.7)

where μ_{RMSR} and σ_{RMSR} are the realized excess return and volatility, respectively, that are achieved by implementing the MSR portfolio weights. For a naïve investor who wishes to diversify her portfolio globally, she determines the distribution of her wealth according to the weights of market capitalization at the end of the previous year. The Sharpe ratio for the realized market value-weighted (RMVW) portfolio is

SR_{RMVW} = μ_{RMVW} / σ_{RMVW},  (13.8)

where μ_{RMVW} and σ_{RMVW} are the excess return and volatility of the value-weighted portfolio, respectively. For a holder of an international market portfolio, the increase in mean-variance brought by the RMSR portfolio is

ψ = SR_{RMSR} − SR_{RMVW}.  (13.9)

We construct global efficient frontiers using monthly returns over previous 60 months and measure the global diversification benefits every month by rebalancing the optimal portfolios. ## 13.3 Data

### 13.3.1 National indices

The dollar-denominated monthly returns of the Morgan Stanley Capital International (MSCI) indices for 21 developed countries and 13 developing countries available during the period of 1988 and 2011 are used in this study. The US 90-Day T-Bill yield is a proxy for the risk-free interest rate. The market capitalizations are obtained from the World Federation of Exchanges.

Table 13.1 shows the sample countries in this study. The countries with the largest growth in market value are Finland, Portugal, Indonesia, and Turkey. There is a considerable variation in market value growth among countries within each region. The stock markets in developed countries consistently represent more than 90% of the world equity market value. In addition, there are considerable cross-regional differences among the equity returns of these markets. Consistent with Bekaert and Harvey (2003), the risk-adjusted returns of developed countries, on average, are higher than those in emerging markets due to a difference in their volatility.

Table 13.1 also demonstrates the time-variation in risk-adjusted performance in each market. The non-synchronizing movements of mean-variance efficiency across countries imply potential gains for domestic investors who diversify their portfolios globally. For instance, in 1998, there are five markets (all of them are developed markets) of the maximum mean-variance efficiency and six markets (all of them are in East Asia) of the minimum. This suggests that local investors can avoid losses in their home markets by allocating their funds optimally in other countries due to cross-market variation in business cycle.
