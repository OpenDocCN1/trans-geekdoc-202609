## 23.2 History of the ASEAN

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations, or ASEAN, was established on 8 August 1967 in Bangkok, Thailand, with the signing of the ASEAN Declaration (Bangkok Declaration) by the Founding Fathers of ASEAN, namely Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand. Brunei Darussalam then joined on 7 January 1984, Vietnam on 28 July 1995, Lao PDR and Myanmar on 23 July 1997, and Cambodia on 30 April 1999, forming, what is today, a union of 10 countries. The main forces driving a greater economic integration between these East Asian countries included their economic expansion, their geographical proximity, an attractive possible web of business networks, and lower transportation and transaction costs between them. Japan’s emergence as a world economic power together with the reduction of the American presence in the region following the Vietnam War also played a considerable role.

During the 1960s, several unsuccessful attempts to create an association among Southeast Asian nations took place. The Association of Southeast Asia (ASA), composed of Malaya (which is now part of Malaysia), the Philippines, and Thailand was established in Bangkok on 31 July 1961. Originally, the Philippines and Malaya had sought an organization similar to the European Economic Community; however they assented to Thailand who insisted on an association with a looser structure. Indonesia, as a supporter of non-alignment, did not wish to join an organization that could be politically too strong for its own interests or in which it could have practically no role. It became clear that a new organization was therefore required.

Maphilindo, a combined name of Malaya, the Philippines, and Indonesia, was formally established in July 1963. It did not work well due to the different interests of the participating nations, and finally failed due to military disputes. The South East Asia Treaty Organization (SEATO), established in 1954, was an international organization for defensive collaboration, which had also failed due to the lack of unanimity among its member countries in pursuing common policies. There were several other organizations such as the Asian Pacific Council (ASPAC), or the Southeast Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SEAARC), none of which were successful.

The ASEAN Community did not base its foundation on dispute-resolution mechanisms and, therefore, was not a collective security agreement, since the founders did not want ASEAN to be seen as a military grouping among political allies as it had been the case of its unsuccessful predecessors. Some of the motivations for the establishment of ASEAN were the reduced faith in or mistrust of external powers in the 1960s, and a desire for economic development. The block grew when Brunei Darussalam became the sixth member on 8 January 1984, barely a week after gaining independence. On 28 July 1995, Vietnam became the seventh member. Laos and Myanmar (Burma) joined 2 years later on 23 July 1997. Cambodia should have joined together with Laos and Burma, but its entrance was deferred due to the country’s internal political conflict. It later joined on 30 April 1999, following the stabilization of its government.

During the 1990s the block experienced an increase in both membership and drive for further integration. In 1990, Malaysia proposed the creation of an East Asia Economic Caucus comprising the then members of ASEAN as well as the People’s Republic of China, Japan, and South Korea, with the intention of counterbalancing the growing influence of the United States in the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) and in the Asian region as a whole. This proposal failed, however, because of heavy opposition from the United States and Japan. Despite this failure, member states continued to work for further integration and ASEAN Plus Three (China, Japan and South Korea) was created in 1997. In 1992, the Common Effective Preferential Tariff (CEPT) scheme was signed as a schedule for phasing out tariffs and as a goal to increase the region’s competitive advantage as a production base geared for the world market. This law would act as the framework for the ASEAN Free Trade Area. After the East Asian Financial Crisis of 1997, a revival of the Malaysian proposal was established in Chiang Mai (Thailand) calling for better integration between the economies of ASEAN as well as the ASEAN Plus Three countries.

Trading has been an important element of growth for the members of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) since its creation in 1967. Today the 10 member states of ASEAN span more than 1.7 million square miles — over half the size of the continental United States — and include a population of over 626 million people with an economy valued at about US $2.4 trillion in 2012. Trade between its country members is an essential driver of the integration efforts that will soon culminate in the creation of the ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) in the coming years. This is the reason why we have decided to carry out an econometric analysis based on fractional integration and cointegration methods with data corresponding to trading values of the 10 country members of ASEAN.

## 23.3 Literature Review

There are several econometric studies that have been performed to analyze the macroeconomic structure of the member countries of the ASEAN. In the following lines we provide a concise summary of these.

Ibrahim (2009) conducted a Gregory and Hansen’s (1996) cointegration test-taking into account structural breaks of several financial time-series of the ASEAN+3 countries and finding as a result no significant improvement in the intraregional financial market integration after the South East Asian Market Crisis, thus concluding that measures introduced and implemented in the aftermath of the 1997 crisis may not have effectively affected the region’s financial market integration.

Ali and Janor (2007) examined the impact of structural breaks, showing a significant impact of a break generated by the Asian financial crisis, and highlighting the importance of applying multivariate cointegration techniques in testing for integration on these markets.

Ismail, Sanusi and Kusairi (2012) investigated the new linkage pattern of financial markets among ASEAN countries, applying cointegration and a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM), finding that there are some linkages in the financial market activities between the countries in both the short run and in the long run.
