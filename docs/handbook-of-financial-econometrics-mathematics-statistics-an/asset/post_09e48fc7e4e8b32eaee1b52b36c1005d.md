
Emerging literature examines the connection between social media and overall firm value or performance. For instance, Luo et al. (2013) examined if social media investment is predictive of market value. Chung et al. (2014) also utilized a market value framework to show that user-generated social media content, rather than firm-generated social media content, is associated with increased market value.

Social media may enable investors to effectively monitor customer sentiments and brand performance prospects and predict future equity value. Social media metrics can have a substantial predictive relationship with firm equity value (Luo et al., 2013). Therefore, investors’ valuation of a firm’s stock is associated with the quality and quantity of the firm’s social media activities (Chung et al., 2014) because investors’ emotions and, in turn, their investment decisions, can be influenced by public sentiments. Therefore, the public’s social media activities, rather than those of the investors, can also be used to predict stock prices (Li et al., 2014).

Through interaction with social media users (e.g., current or potential customers and investors), firms can obtain data about user interests, the business environment, product feedback and employee satisfaction in real time. Thus, to benefit from social media, firms should have the ability to process data efficiently, streamline the associated analysis and utilize the information obtained to improve various firm strategies. Customer satisfaction, brand recognition and even product sales can be good indicators for predicting firm performance (Hitt et al., 2014).

## 66.2.2 Bank relationships and firm value

A firm’s bank relationship influences firm value by improving its reputation, increasing its access to loans, reducing the negative effects of asymmetric information and decreasing interest costs (Thanh and Ha, 2013). The effects of asymmetric information between investors and business managers represent a long-term problem. Traditionally, firms provide information to investors through financial statements and other disclosures, such as annual reports and banks’ credit appraisal. Accordingly, the relationship between a firm and a bank can help overcome information asymmetries and agency problems.

Boot (2000) defines bank relationship as the proximity between the bank and the borrower that facilitates monitoring and screening and can overcome problems of information asymmetry. Bank relationships involve funding and include other financial services, such as letters of credit, deposits, check clearing and cash management services. These services can expand the information available to intermediaries. Thus, the scope of the relationship may affect a bank’s comparative advantage in lending. For example, the use of checking and deposit accounts may help the bank in assessing a firm’s loan repayment capability. That is, bank relationships might benefit business borrowers (i.e., company or firm) in terms of cash flow and credit and thereby improve firm value.

With bank relationships, borrowers might be inclined to reveal more information than that provided in transaction-oriented interactions, and lenders might have strong incentives to produce information. Therefore, when a bank is the financier, the borrower can disclose information to the bank without worrying about the information spilling over to its competitors. Thus, banks are indispensable in overcoming the problems of asymmetric information. A bank might also have incentives to produce information about the borrower because of its role as an enduring and dominant lender (Boot, 2000). Therefore, bank relationships increase total firm value.

A bank–borrower relationship is a mutual commitment based on trust and respect. The bank relationship has two principal costs, the soft budget constraint problem and the information capture problem (hold-up). Soft budget constraint suggests that banks serve as rescuers for enterprises that are on the verge of bankruptcy (Dewatripont and Maskin, 1995; Berglof and Roland, 1998). If a bank refuses to subsidize a fragile enterprise, then the enterprise will fail. Consequently, the borrower may reduce its total funding cost by accessing the bank’s credit and financial markets.

A bank’s assessment of the borrowing firm is of value because the bank has an insider position and thus holds detailed special (inside) information about the firm’s decision process and concurrent projects (Elyasiani and Goldberg, 2004). Banks have access to some proprietary information about borrowers that is unavailable to outside lenders that purchase publicly traded debt. The latter includes instruments such as bonds traded on the basis of publicly available information, as well as certificates of deposit, commercial papers and banker’s acceptances (Fama, 1985). The proprietary information may include historical data about the loan and deposit patterns of the borrowing firm, the firm’s balance sheet and income statements and familiarity with the firm’s plans and projects (Elyasiani and Goldberg, 2004). Access to such information provides the bank with a comparative cost advantage for collecting information about the borrowing firm. This advantage is useful in screening loans making risk assessments before approving a loan and monitoring the loan from then on.

Bank loans are close to last in terms of priority of claim on a borrower’s assets in cases of borrower bankruptcy. The approval of a bank loan sends a signal to the market that all claims of high priority are safe. This signal, in turn, creates a certification effect that reduces the cost of collecting information about the borrower for other suppliers of funds, that is, the cost of funds to the borrower (Fama, 1985). Financial institutions have an advantage over outside lenders because of their in-depth knowledge of the borrowers’ prospects and the cost complementarities they enjoy from multiple output production. The insider position allows banks to overcome the information asymmetry prevailing between firms and outside lenders and grants them a comparative advantage over outside lenders (Diamond, 1991).

Therefore, if banks have some special (unique) information about borrowing firms, their loan announcements should increase the market value of the borrower firms. James (1987) explained that the announcement of bank loans generates a positive and significant effect on the stock prices of borrowing firms, whereas announcements of private placement and public debt offerings produce considerable negative effects. These findings indicate that the supply of funds through bank loans and that through private placements and public offerings are different, with bank loans containing additional information value (Elyasiani and Goldberg, 2004).

The hold-up problem is associated with information monopoly that banks generate in the course of lending. This monopoly may allow banks to grant loans to borrowers at non-competitive terms in the future. In this way, banks could charge high interest rates on loans (Sharpe, 1990; Rajan, 1992). The information captured by banks may make borrowers reluctant to borrow from them. Potentially valuable investment opportunities may then be lost. A superior solution to the hold-up problem may be a long-term line of credit with a termination clause that can balance the costs and benefits of the problem and the effects of ex post facto competition (Von Thadden, 1995). Multiple bank relationships indeed reduce the hold-up problem, but they worsen the availability of credit (Ongena and Smith, 2000).
