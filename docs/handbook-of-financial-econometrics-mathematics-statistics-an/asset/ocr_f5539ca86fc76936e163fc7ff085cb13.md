# 34.2.2.2 *Dynamics of non-financial events or paramaters*

Kim and Mei (2001) show how volatilities, a major parameter of option pricing, behave when unexpected news gets disseminated into the stock market, specifically Hong Kong. The main driver of stock jumps is political news. The political parameters that they used to price political risk on Hang Seng are; (i) the question of Hong Kong’s democracy after 1997, (ii) China’s most favored-nation trade status, and (iii) China’s human rights developments and political reforms. In order to understand the chosen parameters, Hong Kong used to be British colony since 1842 and from 1898; a “lease” agreement of 99 years was signed and gave British government rights over Hong Kong. The turning point was in 1982, when Margaret Thatcher signed a new agreement that will allow China to take control on Hong Kong in 1997. That agreement, allow Kong Hong to have a dual government system was based on British and Chinese laws; i.e., “one country two system”. Despite signed agreements, there were notable political events, Tienanmen Democracy Movement in 1989 and introduction of legislative reforms in October 1992 by Chris Pattern.

In modeling stock jumps, Kim and Mei (2001) adopted GARCH (1;1) model, further, they introduced component filter on the model. The advantage of GARCH (1;1) is that it has a component that captures time-varying long-term volatility and a Poisson process, which accounts for spikes. The Margrabe model used in this chapter incorporates Poisson process for reasons stated in this paragraph. The filter component is for time-varying low frequency. In addition, the filter assists in capturing the forecasting error in an innovative way.

The empirical results of Kim and Mei (2001) illustrate that stock jumps direct volatilities to certain directions of the stock market. The volatility persistence is 0.937, this supports the notion that the model is appropriate in this study. When the filter component is taken into account, GARCH (1;1) is much stronger than GARCH (1;1) without the filter. The jumps are estimated to probability of 3.3% (approx.). All the political events had casual effects on stocks and they were all statistically significant. Moreover, the results illustrated that conventional option pricing models are inappropriate as they do not incorporate stock jumps effects and dynamic stop-loss/hedging strategy based on continuous price movements might be ineffective and could lead to unforeseen loss in the presence of stock jumps.
