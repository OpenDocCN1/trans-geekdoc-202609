## Abstract

The financial ratio-based credit-scoring model for bond rating system requires the maximization of two conflicting objectives simultaneously, namely, the explanatory and discriminatory power, which had not been directly addressed in literature. The main purpose of this study is to develop a credit-scoring model that combines the principal component analysis and Fisher’s discriminant analysis using the MINIMAX goal programming technique so the maximization of the two conflicting objectives can be compromised. The performance of alternative credit-scoring models including the stepwise discriminant analysis by Pinches and Mingo, Fisher’s discriminant analysis, and the principal component analysis is analyzed and compared using dataset from previous studies. We find that the proposed hybrid credit-scoring model outperforms other alternative models in both explanatory and discriminatory powers.

## 57.1 Introduction

There is an abundance of credit-scoring models based on statistical or data mining techniques in literature (e.g., see Hanić et al., 2013). Well-known statistical credit-scoring models include Fisher’s multivariate discriminant analysis (e.g., see Altman, 1968, 1983; Altman and Saunders, 1998; Kumar and Anand, 2013; Altman, 2013), multidimensional scaling (MDS) (e.g., see Molinero et al., 1996), and logistic regression-based credit-scoring model (e.g., see Smith and Lawrence, 1995). Credit scoring models that use data mining techniques include the multiple-criteria linear/quadratic programming technique to classify the cardholders’ credit ratings (e.g., Shi et al., 2002, 2005); the support vector machines (SVM) technique (e.g., see Van Gestel et al., 2003), neural networks used for bankruptcy prediction (e.g., see Brédart, 2014; Youn and Gu, 2010).

This study is focusing on the credit-scoring models for bond rating evaluation based on financial ratios. Though dominating the bond rating literature (e.g., see Altman, 1968, 1983, 2013; Altman and Saunders, 1998; Pinches and Mingo, 1973; Molinero et al., 1996; Pinches and Mingo, 1975; Pogue and Soldofsky, 1969; Ohlson, 1980; Van Gestel et al., 2003; Wang and Campbell, 2010; Li, 2012), few financial ratios-based credit-scoring models for bond ratings had addressed the two construction principles of a bond rating system mentioned in the revised Framework (e.g., see Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, 1999, 2005). The first construction principle is the selection of meaningful financial ratios that are key inputs to the credit-scoring model among hundreds of correlated financial ratios (e.g., see Chen and Shimerda, 1981; Horrigan, 1966). The second construction principle, i.e., the so-called rating quantification principle (e.g., see Crouhy et al., 2001; Falkenstein, 2000; Sobehart et al., 2000), under which the ratings produced by the underlying credit-scoring model are mapped successfully to an external and independent benchmark ratings assigned by rating agency that is of special credibility, e.g., Moody’s or S&P. To meet the two construction principles, efforts must be made for the selection of financial ratios, as well as the improvement in classification methodology.

Pinches and Mingo (1973, 1975) are the pioneers to develop a credit-scoring model that addressed the two construction principles using “stepwise discriminant analysis”, in which a subset of key financial ratios with significant explanatory power is chosen first using factor analysis. After the selection process, Fisher’s discriminant analysis (e.g., see Fisher, 1936) is implemented to combine and weigh the key financial variables to produce credit scores with maximal discriminatory power that discriminate obligors among different benchmark ratings ex ante as much as possible.

Nevertheless, the two objectives, namely, the maximization of the explanatory power and the maximization of the discriminant power are conflicting that variables with significant explanatory power might have very low discriminatory power, or variables with insignificant explanatory power might have very high discriminatory power (e.g., see Johnson and Wichern, 2002). Therefore, the stepwise approach does not guarantee the chosen discriminatory function is optimal. Instead of the stepwise approach, this study considers a compromising solution for the two conflicting objectives based on a hybrid of two multivariate techniques, namely, the principal component analysis and Fisher’s discriminant analysis (e.g., see Johnson and Wichern, 2002), which are often used together in a sequential way to classify objects with high-dimensional datasets (Jombart, Devillard, & Balloux, 2010; Park, Ku, Seo, Kim, Yeon, Lee, Jeong, Yoon, Harn, & Kim, 2006). A multi-objective optimization technique, namely, the Chebyshev goal programming technique (e.g., see Flavell, 1976), is used to find a compromise solution between the maximization of the explanatory power and the maximization of the discriminant power. In order to compare the proposed approach to Pinches and Mingo’s stepwise discriminant analysis, an empirical analysis is performed using Pinches and Mingo’s dataset with industrial corporate bonds rated B or listed above in Moody’s Bond Survey from January, 1967 to December, 1968. In addition, comparisons are made with the other two credit-scoring models based on the principal component analysis and Fisher’s discriminant analysis, respectively. The performance of the proposed model versus the three existing credit-scoring models is demonstrated.

The outline of this chapter is as follows. Brief reviews of the principal component analysis and Fisher’s discriminant analysis are given in Section 57.2. In Section 57.3, a hybrid of principal component analysis and Fisher’s discriminant analysis credit-scoring model using the goal attainment programming technique is developed. An empirical analysis is performed in Section 57.4 and Section 57.5 concludes the chapter.

## 57.2  Maximization of Explanatory Versus Discriminant Powers: Two Conflicting Objectives

Principal component analysis (PCA) and Fisher’s discriminant analysis (FDA) are two often used multivariate analysis techniques. PCA performs dimensionality reduction in high-dimensional space while preserving as much of the data variabilities, i.e., the explanatory power, as possible. FDA performs dimensionality reduction while preserving much of the class discriminatory information, i.e., discriminant power, as possible. In this section, we show the two objectives, namely, the maximization of explanatory and the discriminant power can be achieved simultaneously only under very restrictive conditions.
