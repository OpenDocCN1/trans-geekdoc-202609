It is widely accepted that jumps illustrate inverse relationship between options and some of their parameters (Bates, 1996). The contribution of this chapter is precisely to take on those challenges. At the heart of the contribution, the Margrabe formula is extended such that it captures price jumps. The advantages of accounting for jumps when you price options are as follows; (1) reduces model risk (see Cont, 2006), (2) introduce invectives to limit excessive risk-taking (see Ronn and Verna, 1986), and (3) proper accounting of benefits and losses (see Lint and Pennings, 1998). Generally, jumps occur in heterogeneous environment. Factors that cause stock jumps include earnings, price changes, returns, dividends, time, random disturbance and stocks risks. Some of those mentioned factors are synonymous with parameters of the Margrabe formula. Hence, it is interesting to extend the Margrabe formula such that it captures stock jumps. The Margrabe formula includes incorporating a Poisson process. Finally, the jumps that cause stock jumps can be induced by either political or financial events. Unlike Merton (1976) who assumed that jumps are diversifiable,² in this chapter jumps are assumed to be undiversifiable as those jumps will be “inherent” in the merged entity. Further, these jumps are discrete unlike Merton’s. Scott (1997: 419) stated that “to handle the extreme observations associated with the crash, one must use a model with discrete jumps”. On the other hand, Yan (2011) argues that jumps occur rarely and estimating them requires long-time series samples, which are difficult to find and large samples have their own error problems. In principle, the model is at-the-money time-type model. The reason for the latter statement is numerous models have been used to price options at different moneyness levels although, suitability at those moneyness levels has not been determined.

The study that is close to this chapter is one by Cheang and Chiarella (2011). They extend the Margrabe formula such that it is suitable when assets are driven by jump-diffusion (JD) process — introduced by Robert Merton in 1976. That is, Cheang and Chiarella (2011) use exchange option under JD process while this chapter explores exchange option under any randomness including JD process. Unlike Cheang and Chiarella (2011) who priced exchange option in a continuous environment, this chapter discretizes the environment so that the same Margrabe model can be used for hedging.
