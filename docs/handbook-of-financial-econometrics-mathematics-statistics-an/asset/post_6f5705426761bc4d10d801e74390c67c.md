
## 74.2 The Upward Bias of Sample Maximum Squared Sharpe Ratio

### 74.2.1 The sample squared maximum Sharpe ratio and its finite sample property

Under the assumption of multivariate normal distribution of excess returns of $N$ assets, the square root of maximum squared Sharpe ratio can be expressed as

$$S=\left[\psi^2+\frac{(\mu_g^+)^2}{\sigma_g^2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}, \tag{74.1}$$

where $\psi^2$ is the squared slope of the asymptote to the minimum variance frontier, $(\mu_g,\ \sigma_g^2)$ are the expected value and variance of excess return of the global minimum variance portfolio. $\psi^2=a-b^2/c$, $\mu_g=b/c$ and $\sigma_g^2=1/c$, where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are efficient set constants. Assume that the excess returns of $N$ assets have mean $\mu$ and covariance matrix $V$, we will have $a=\mu'V^{-1}\mu$, $b=1_N'V^{-1}\mu$ and $c=1_N'V^{-1}1_N$.

Because the square root of the right side in (1) adds difficulty to mathematical analysis, we will focus on the MSR $S^2$. The simplest way to estimating $S^2$ is to use the sample counterparts of $\psi^2$, $\mu_g$ and $\sigma_g^2$, which are, respectively, denoted as $\hat{\psi}^2$, $\hat{\mu}_g$, and $\hat{\sigma}_g^2$. The finite sample property of $\hat{S}^2$ is determined by $(\hat{\psi}^2,\hat{\mu}_g,\hat{\sigma}_g^2)$ (Bordnar and Schmidt, 2006; Kan and Smith, 2008): (i) $\hat{\psi}^2\sim\frac{(N-1)F_{N-1,T-N+1}(T\psi^2)}{(T-N+1)}$, (ii) conditional on $\hat{\psi}^2$, $\hat{\mu}_g\sim N\left(\mu_g,\frac{\sigma_g^2(1+\hat{\psi}^2)}{T}\right)$, (iii) $\hat{\sigma}_g^2$ is independent of $\hat{\psi}^2$ and $\hat{\mu}_g$, where $\frac{T\hat{\sigma}_g^2}{\sigma_g^2}\sim\chi^2_{T-N}$; $T$ is the time series length, and $N$ is the number of assets.

Table 74.1 shows the sample estimates for $S^2$ and its parameters using the 25 size and book-to-market portfolio returns for the US market from July 1926 to December 2017. It shows that using value-weighted portfolio returns, the sample estimate for $S^2$ is 0.095293 while it is 0.123009 when equal-weighted returns are used.

Let U = -\frac{\mu_g\sqrt{T}}{\sigma_g\sqrt{1+\psi^2}}, using integration by parts⁴

$$E\left[\int_0^{+\infty} x^2 f\left(x\mid\hat{\psi}^2\right) dx\right] = \mu_g^2\left\{\left[E[1 - N(U)]\right] + E\left[\frac{1 - N(U)}{U^2}\right] + E\left[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}U}e^{-\frac{U^2}{2}}\right]\right\}\tag{74.6}$$

then

$$E\left[\frac{(\hat{\mu}_g^+)^2}{\hat{\sigma}_g^2}\right] = \frac{T}{T - N - 2}\frac{\mu_g^2}{\sigma_g^2}\left\{E[1 - N(U)] + E\left[\frac{1 - N(U)}{U^2}\right] + E\left[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}U}e^{-\frac{U^2}{2}}\right]\right\}.\tag{74.7}$$

In addition to the scale factor $\frac{T - N -1}{T}$, the upward bias in $\hat{\psi}^2$ is a constant of $\frac{N-1}{T - N -1}$. In the case of $N = 25$ using the 25 size and book-to-market portfolios, this item is around 0.70 for five years of monthly returns ($T = 60$), 0.04 and 0.02 for 50 and 100 years, respectively. Using the longest US sample during July 1926 to December 2017 with $T = 1098$, the constant upward bias is 0.022388, which is significant for $\hat{\psi}^2 = 0.066469$, $\hat{S}^2 = 0.095293$ by using value-weighted monthly portfolio returns or $\hat{\psi}^2 = 0.087426$, $S^2 = 0.123009$ by using equal-weighted returns (Table 74.1).

To investigate bias in $\frac{(\hat{\mu}_g^+)^2}{\hat{\sigma}_g^2}$ in Table 74.2, we conduct simulations for different true values of $\psi$ and $\frac{\mu_g}{\sigma_g}$ and investigate the contribution of bias from different components in equation (74.7). Table 74.2 shows that the term $E\left[\frac{1 - N(U)}{U^2}\right]$ contributes to a significant bias in most cases when ignoring the scale factor $\frac{T}{T - N -2}$.⁵ For the terms $E[1 - N(U)]$ and $E\left[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}U}e^{-\frac{U^2}{2}}\right]$, Table 74.2 shows that they contribute to small bias for a wide range of $(\psi^2, \mu_g, \sigma_g^2)$.

Then we replace 1/\sigma_g^2 by 1/\sigma_g^2's unbiased estimator: 1/\hat{\sigma}_{gu}^2 = (T - N - 2)/T * 1/\hat{\sigma}_g^2.

As for the term E[ (1 - N(U))/U^2 ], we notice that

E[ (1 + \hat{\psi}^2) [1 - N(U)] ] = E[ (1 + \hat{\psi}^2) * Prob( \hat{\mu}_g > 0 | \hat{\psi}^2 ) ]
= E[ (1 + \hat{\psi}^2) * (1 + sign(\hat{\mu}_g))/2 ]. (74.9)

Let m_g = (1 + \hat{\psi}^2)/T * (1 + sign(\hat{\mu}_g))/2, we use (\hat{\mu}_g^+)^2 / \hat{\sigma}_{gu}^2 - m_g as an estimator for (\mu_g^+)^2 / \sigma_g^2.

This can remove most bias of E[ (1 - N(U))/U^2 ]. Table 74.2 shows that the bias contributed by other terms in (7) is very small in most cases and we leave them untreated. Equipped with this result and \hat{\psi}_u^2, we construct a new estimator, which is less biased than \hat{S}^2:

\hat{S}_{less_biased}^2 = \hat{\psi}_u^2 + (\hat{\mu}_g^+)^2 / \hat{\sigma}_{gu}^2 - m_g. (74.10)

Subtracting bias as in \hat{\psi}_u^2 will result in negative estimates whenever \hat{\psi}^2 is less than (N - 1)/(T - N - 1), which is not admissible in a statistical sense (Chow, 1987). With finite sample, say T = 60, the constant biases are 0.1836 for N = 10, and 0.7058 for N = 25. When MSR is very low, we will often end up in negative \hat{\psi}_u^2 with a limited sample period. To illustrate the frequent occurrence of negative estimates, in Table 74.3, we have 192 negative values out of 271 observations with value-weighted returns in Japanese market when five-year moving estimation windows are used. In addition, there are more than 30% instances of negative \hat{\psi}_u^2 for the US market under the same setting. Similarly, removing the constant bias in (\hat{\mu}_g^+)^2 / \hat{\sigma}_{gu}^2 will result in negative estimates which are statistically inadmissible.

To overcome the issue of negative estimates, we will propose three ways to adjust estimation of \psi^2 without increasing RMSE. The simplest way is to use the positive part of the unbiased estimator:

\hat{S}_{less_biased+}^2 = ( \hat{\psi}_u^2 )^+ + [ (\hat{\mu}_g^+)^2 / \hat{\sigma}_{gu}^2 - m_g ]^+. (74.11)
