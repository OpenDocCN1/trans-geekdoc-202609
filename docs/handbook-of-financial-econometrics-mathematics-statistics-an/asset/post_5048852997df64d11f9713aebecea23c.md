
## The Association Between Book-Tax Differences and CEO Compensation

## 32.2 Hypotheses Development

Prior studies (Blaylock et al., 2017; Clemons and Shevlin, 2016; Hanlon, 2003; Lev and Nissim, 2004) suggest that large positive book-tax differences (accounting income greater than taxable income) seem to signal low earnings quality and investors use information in book-tax differences to assess earnings persistence. Phillips et al. (2003) also show the incremental usefulness of deferred tax expense (which increases with book-tax differences) as a metric to detect earnings management.

Prior research documents that compensation committees do intervene in setting executive compensation. Dechow et al. (1994) and Gaver and Gaver (1998) find that compensation committees shield executive compensation from the adverse effect on current period earnings of potentially value-enhancing restructurings. Adut et al. (2003) extend this analysis by documenting that compensation committees evaluate the context of individual restructurings in determining the degree to which they will intervene to protect executive compensation from these charges. Specifically, compensation committees appear to:

- completely shield initial and subsequent restructuring charges for CEOs with long tenure, provided that the firm had not recorded a charge in the two immediately prior years,
- do not shield subsequent restructuring charges taken by short-tenured CEOs if the firm reported a prior restructuring charge within 2 years of the current charge,
- and partially shield the other categories of restructuring charges.

They interpret their evidence as suggesting that compensation committees assess a compensation penalty on CEO in contexts where the restructuring charge is more likely to be opportunistic. Duru et al. (2002) provide evidence that firms shield CEO compensation from income-decreasing effects of strategic expenditures such as research and development and advertising expenditures.

The board of directors is an important monitoring mechanism of managerial performance. Fama and Jensen (1983) argue that reputational considerations and managerial labor-market provide incentives to directors to properly monitor firm’s performance. As a result of difficulty in observing managerial effort, directors are likely to use observable signals of managerial effort. Prior studies typically model CEO compensation as a function of two performance signals: accounting earnings and stock price (Lambert and Larcker, 1987; Banker and Datar, 1989; Sloan, 1993). An important issue in the choice of performance signals is the precision of the signal in reflecting managerial performance. Given managerial discretion over generally accepted accounting principles and evidence of earnings management, earnings quality is an important factor affecting the precision of earnings in measuring managerial performance. We posit that directors are likely to use information in book-tax differences to assess earnings quality in determining CEO compensation. Thus, if CEOs opportunistically exercise their discretion over generally accepted accounting principles to manage accounting income upwards without affecting current taxable income, board of directors use the information in book-tax differences to undo such attempts that artificially inflate accounting income. Hence, the efficiency hypothesis predicts that CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences that result in accounting income exceeding taxable income.

**H1a:** *Under the efficiency hypothesis, the CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences.*

Bertrand and Mullainathan (2001) suggest an alternative rent-seeking view where CEOs gain effective control of the pay-setting process itself. Due to entrenchment problems such as lack of board independence and due to the complexity of the pay process, many CEOs may in effect set their own pay with little oversight from directors and shareholders. CEOs’ pay level then becomes constrained by their fear of drawing directors or shareholders’ attention. The results of Bertrand and Mullainathan’s study are consistent with the skimming hypothesis: CEO pay is as sensitive to a “lucky dollar” as to a “general dollar.”² Under the skimming view, CEOs can pay themselves through option grants without affecting current earnings of the firm and if shareholders are mainly fixated on reported earnings, option grants are an easy way to skim without drawing unwanted attention. Thus, the CEO who gives himself more options will not need to lower other components of pay at all. Bertrand and Mullainathan also find that skimming is less prevalent in well-governed firms as these firms limit the CEO's ability to capture the pay process.

> ²In contrast, under agency models, pay should not respond to luck since by definition, CEOs cannot influence luck. Tying pay to luck will not provide better incentives; it will only add risk to the contract (Holmstrom, 1979).

Other studies also provide evidence that CEOs opportunistically influence the terms of their compensation. Aboody and Kasznik (2000) find that CEOs manipulate the timing of corporate voluntary disclosure to maximize their stock-based compensation. Their results suggest that CEOs tend to accelerate the announcements of bad news and delay the announcements of good news around scheduled option awards. Such a strategy ensures that decreases in the firm’s stock price related to the arrival of bad news occur before the award date while stock price increases related to the arrival of good news occurring after the award date. Yermack (1997) documents that CEOs opportunistically schedule stock option award before favorable earnings announcements.³

> ³The primary difference between Aboody and Kasznik (2000) and Yermack (1997) is that Aboody and Kasznik focus on firms with scheduled option awards whereas Yermack examines firms that do not award options on a fixed schedule.

The rent-seeking hypothesis is based on similar arguments as the skimming hypothesis discussed in Bertrand and Mullainathan (2001). As such under the rent-seeking hypothesis, we posit that the CEOs gain effective control of the pay process and directors do not use the information in book-tax differences to undo the CEOs’ attempted earnings manipulation in determining CEO compensation. Consequently, CEOs derive private benefits as their compensation is positively associated with book-tax differences.

Core et al. (1999) find that after controlling for standard economic determinants of the level of CEO compensation, CEOs earn greater compensation when corporate governance structures are less effective. With respect to board of director variables, they find that CEO compensation is higher when CEO is also the chair of the board, the board is larger and there are greater “gray” directors on the board. With respect to ownership variables, they document that CEO compensation is negatively associated with CEO’s equity ownership and the existence of an external blockholder who owns at least 5% of the equity.

In our study, we predict that firms with strong corporate governance structures are more likely to use the information in book-tax differences to determine CEO compensation than do those with weak corporate governance structures. Under the efficiency hypothesis, we predict that strong corporate governance structures will strengthen the negative association between compensation and book-tax differences.

This model closely resembles the model used by Core et al. (1999) in examining the determinants of CEO compensation. TOTPAY is total compensation paid to the CEO divided by total assets at the start of the fiscal year. BTD is temporary book-tax differences. To compute the deferred tax expense, we use the sum of Federal and foreign deferred tax expense (Compustat item 269 and item 270) and where missing, we use total deferred tax expense (Compustat item 50). To derive an estimate of the firm's temporary book-tax differences, we divide the deferred tax expense by the statutory tax rate during the sample period (35%). We scale the temporary book-tax differences by total assets at the start of the fiscal year to mitigate potential heteroscedasticity problem. Under the efficiency hypothesis (H1a), we posit that CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences and accordingly, we predict the coefficient β1 to be negative. In contrast, under the rent-seeking hypothesis, we predict that CEO compensation is positively associated with book-tax differences and thus, we expect the coefficient β1 to be positive. Therefore, it is an empirical question as to which hypothesis dominates.

### 32.3.2.1 Control variables

Prior studies suggest that large firms and firms with complex operations will demand higher quality managers with higher equilibrium wages (Smith and Watts, 1992). We proxy for firm size and complexity with the natural logarithm of firm sales (SIZE). We proxy for the firm's growth opportunities with the ratio of market value of equity to book equity (MKTBK). Standard agency models suggest that compensation level is positively associated with firm performance. Firm performance is measured using return on assets (ROA) and the annual market return on the common stock (RETURN). Theoretical models (Lambert and Larcker, 1987; Banker and Datar, 1989) suggest that firm risk is also an important determinant of compensation levels. We use the standard deviation of return on assets for the past 5 years (STDROA) and the standard deviation of common stock returns over the prior 5 years (STDRET) to control for firm risk.
