# 67.2 Identifying Cycles

As mentioned in the introduction, Ibbotson et al. (1975) introduced the idea of the “hot” IPO market, determined that there is serial correlation in IPO performance, and that the performance between IPOs performance and volume is somewhat related (i.e., the two variables are correlated); since then, academics and practitioners have spent a great deal of time attempting to model IPO volume and performance in an attempt to understand the underlying forces that lead to increases in issuance and performance, but also to profit off of that understanding. Research on the cyclical nature of IPO performance and issuance that is relevant for this section include Ibbotson and Jaffe (1975), Ritter (1984), Ibbotson et al. (1994), Loughran et al. (1994), Pagano et al. (1998), Stoughton et al. (2001), Beveniste et al. (2002), Lowry and Schwert (2002), Lowry (2003), Helwege and Liang (2004), Purnanandam and Swaminathan (2004), Alti (2005), Benninga et al. (2005), Derrien (2005), Pastor and Veronesi (2005), Yung et al. (2008), Gao et al. (2013), Hu and Wang (2013) and Smith and Mumtaz (2018). Our goal, in this section, is to explore the cyclical nature of IPO issuance.

When we think about cyclicality or hot and cold markets, an interesting next step in the literature would be to evaluate what Ritter (1984) found in his follow-up study to Ibbotson et al. (1975). Ritter (1984) focused on the average performance of IPOs issued during the 15-month period starting in January 1980, a performance of 48.4%, which was far greater than the aggregate performance of the IPO market during the Ibbotson et al. (1975) 1977–1982 time period (i.e., that performance was 16.3%). In the end, Ritter (1984) concluded that there was a “market” factor that led to this outperformance. This factor, according to Ritter (1984), was a boom in the performance of natural resources stocks issued during this time and the “hot” market was contained mostly in this particular industry in which underwriters would substantially discount the shares of the firms that had small market capitalizations and were issuing their shares at this time. Namely, according to Ritter (1984), if the firms that were issuing their shares during this 15-month period had less than $500,000 in sales, their average initial return was 140.4%.
