
The results of Merton (1976) illustrate that the jump component of the stock’s return represents non-systematic risk. And the main formula derived by Merton (1976) is BS solution but it had a larger interest rate component. According to Merton (1976), option prices increase with an increase in interest rates. Further, the probability in this scenario is conditional on Poisson jumps occurring during the life of an option. Given that his article is theoretical, he mapped out a possible empirical scenario. First, he finds that ceteris paribus, an option becomes more valuable with the presence of stock price jumps.

Scott (1997) derived a closed option formula for a European option under jump diffusion process. According to Scott (1997), in prior studies, jumps were due to ordinary jumps and stochastic volatility, and his study incorporates all those elements. In order to make his pricing “elegant”, he used Fourier Transform. Scott (1997) states that jumps increase risk premium in options, phenomenon that will be illustrated in the formulation section. For modeling section, he adopts an exponential model for pricing a European option that is driven by two Wiener processes. The derived Margrabe model adopts similar approaches.

Based on the relationship between interest rates and stock prices; results show (i) significant positive relationship on squared stock price changes, (ii) significant negative correlation between interest rates and stock changes, and (iii) significant negative correlation between volatility and stock price changes. Further, sample kurtosis increases dramatically after stock market crashes. He argues that models that do not incorporate jumps would not be able to illustrate the latter phenomenon. Moreover, Scott (1997) calibrated his model to second, third and fourth moments (variance, skewness and kurtosis). At the heart of the calibration was the inclusion of risk premium. The results improved with calibration of the model. On the other hand, those options were valued using Monte-Carlo simulation (MCS). From MCS results, the following could be observed based on Pearson correlation (ρ). Firstly, a negative ρ produces higher values for out-of-the-money (in-the-money) puts (calls) and lower values for in-the-money (out-of-the-money) puts (calls). Secondly, value of options increases with an increase in stock jumps.

Pan (2002) addressed factors that affect risk premia on the S&P 500 index options. She argues that the understanding of risk premia in options is limited. The three distinctive factors that affect the risk premium are (i) diffusive price shocks, (ii) price jumps, and (iii) diffusive volatility shocks. She allowed the jump-risk premium to be dependent on the market volatility. Pan (2002) states that when jumps are not taken into account when pricing options, it leads to mispricing of options. In modeling, she adopted Bates' characteristic function post-crash distributions which were inferred from S&P500 future option prices in 2000 by David Bates. The model is driven by two stochastic processes and it had two important features, stochastic volatility and price jumps. Further, the model incorporated a premium for jump-risk uncertainty and the model focused mainly on risk premium for jump-size uncertainty. For estimation purposes, Pan (2002) used implied state-generalized method of moments (GMM) estimators and optimal moment selection. The models used represent jump-risk premia (SVJ0), volatility-risk premia (SV) and no risk premia (SV0). On all three models, Pan (2002) performed joint actual estimations and risk-neutral dynamics using the time series.

The data used in Pan (2002) is jointly spot and option data from the Berkeley Options Data Base (BODB) on complete trading activity on the floor of CBOE. The data is from January 1989 to December 1996, on weekly trading days. The results of Pan (2002) show that volatility-risk premium is positive and significant for the SV model. And there are tensions between the spot and option prices in SV0 model. When the volatility-risk premium doubles to 20% from 10%, jump-risk premium almost doubles, and volatility risk increases by small amounts. She went further and simultaneously estimated jump and volatility risk premiums. Both types of risk premia are positive. Further, risk premia is present in underlying stock prices. Broadly, equity risk premium compensates for risk aversion to jumps. The skewness of the curves tends to have fat tails (i.e., high kurtosis). The jumps were more diverse with no specific patterns.

Eraker (2004) examines how different volatility models react to stock price dynamics and from joint options and stock market data. Although joint options are dissimilar, but the options phenomenon are one, it brings a bit of similarity in between them. According to Eraker (2004), prior studies on time-varying volatility models and jump models have not proven their suitability in practice. And the jumps had no impact on stock prices. The data used in Eraker (2004) is on sample of S&P500 options contracts. In this sample, daily closing prices from CBOE. The full sample is made up of 22,500 options and he narrowed it down to 3,270 contracts recorded over 1,006 trading days covering the period of January 1, 1987 to December 31, 1990. The reason why the sample was randomly narrowed down is because large datasets require excessive computing times. The pricing models varied from the basic stochastic volatility (SV) model, the stochastic volatility with jumps (SVJ) model, the stochastic volatility model that allows volatility to jump (SVCJ) to the stochastic volatility with state-dependent, correlated jumps (SVSCJ). The SVSCJ is a general case.

## 34.2.2.2 *Dynamics of non-financial events or parameters*

Kim and Mei (2001) show how volatilities, a major parameter of option pricing, behave when unexpected news gets disseminated into the stock market, specifically Hong Kong. The main driver of stock jumps is political news. The political parameters that they used to price political risk on Hang Seng are; (i) the question of Hong Kong’s democracy after 1997, (ii) China’s most favored-nation trade status, and (iii) China’s human rights developments and political reforms. In order to understand the chosen parameters, Hong Kong used to be British colony since 1842 and from 1898; a “lease” agreement of 99 years was signed and gave British government rights over Hong Kong. The turning point was in 1982, when Margaret Thatcher signed a new agreement that will allow China to take control on Hong Kong in 1997. That agreement, allow Hong Kong to have a dual government system was based on British and Chinese laws; i.e., “one country two system”. Despite signed agreements, there were notable political events, Tiananmen Democracy Movement in 1989 and introduction of legislative reforms in October 1992 by Chris Patten.

In modeling stock jumps, Kim and Mei (2001) adopted GARCH (1,1) model, further, they introduced component filter on the model. The advantage of GARCH (1,1) is that it has a component that captures time-varying long-term volatility and a Poisson process, which accounts for spikes. The Margrabe model used in this chapter incorporates Poisson process for reasons stated in this paragraph. The filter component is for time-varying low frequency. In addition, the filter assists in capturing the forecasting error in an innovative way.

The empirical results of Kim and Mei (2001) illustrate that stock jumps direct volatilities to certain directions of the stock market. The volatility persistence is 0.937, this supports the notion that the model is appropriate in this study. When the filter component is taken into account, GARCH (1,1) is much stronger than GARCH (1,1) without the filter. The jumps are estimated to probability of 3.3% (approx.). All the political events had causal effects on stocks and they were all statistically significant. Moreover, the results illustrated that conventional option pricing models are inappropriate as they do not incorporate stock jumps effects and dynamic stop-loss/hedging strategy based on continuous price movements might be ineffective and could lead to unforeseen loss in the presence of stock jumps.
