
The previous studies investigated the assumption of bankruptcy criteria during the period of banks’ auditing time. Under the prompt corrective action (PCA) and closure rules, that is, bankruptcy occurring when banks fail to meet minimum levels of capital adequacy, the FDIC can take charge of the banks and be responsible for their liabilities. According to the forbearance policy, the defaulted bank can be allowed to operate under supervision. With regard to closure policy, forbearance is granting the bank time to return to solvency before it is closed. Ronn and Verma (1986) consider that the insurer would infuse funds to the bank whose asset is below the deposit liability under the forbearing range and then the bank would not be liquidated immediately. Taking account of the forbearing policy into the framework of option pricing model, Ronn and Verma (1986) state that the deposit insurance premiums are underestimated. Allen and Saunders (1993) indicate that the value of the deposit insurance can be viewed as the value of a callable perpetual American put option under the consideration of forbearance policy. They show that the deposit insurance premium increases with loose closure policy. Lee et al. (2005) propose the formula for fair deposit insurance and state the fair value of deposit insurance decreases with the strict policy, which is consistent with the results of Allen and Saunders (1993).

Since the FDIC has the power to set closure policy which makes the deposit insurance contract expire when the bank asset knocks the regulatory closure point, the deposit insurance could be evaluated as a down-and-out barrier option that the barrier can be viewed as the regulatory closure rule. Brockman and Turtle (2003) state that the valuation of the corporate security can be viewed as a path-dependent barrier option model (BT model). Episcopos (2008) utilizes the BT model to price the deposit insurance premium. In bank regulation, the value of equity is given by a down-and-out call option with the barrier as a critical point of failure. In contrast with equity, the FDIC's contingent asset value can be viewed as a down-and-in call option and the FDIC's obligation to pay liabilities, generally deposits, can be viewed as a European put option on the asset of bank. Hwang et al. (2009) modify the barrier option model to price the deposit insurance by adding the bankruptcy cost as a function of the bank asset return volatility. They consider the bankruptcy cost explicitly incorporated into the pricing framework of deposit insurance and make the barrier option approach close to realistic closure policies and regulatory forbearance.

In the basic FDIC deposit insurance contract, the FDIC collects a deposit insurance premium from each surviving insured financial institution at the beginning of each quarter. Therefore, it is possible for the FDIC to receive the premium at this quarter and to take over the failed banks during the next quarter with no deposit insurance premium. On the other hand, the assumption is not realistic if structural models assume continuous monitoring because the accounting information of financial institution only can be observed quarterly. Although in a practical provision the FDIC would cover insurance funds in many kinds of deposit accounts, including checking and savings accounts, money market deposit accounts, and certificates of deposit (CDs). The FDIC does not cover other financial products such as stocks, bonds or securities and only protects each depositor with limitation.²

The purpose of this chapter is to combine all of the above mentioned real provisions of the FDIC contract and to take into account the cover limitation of insurance for depositors. When a bank asset fails to closure barrier, the FDIC would take over the bank; therefore, the value of the bank's shareholders transfers to the FDIC. At this moment, the indirect bankruptcy cost occurs and the bank asset suddenly drops. At the maturity time, the FDIC would gain the profit if the bank asset was enough to pay deposits; otherwise, the FDIC would lose the insurance fund because of the proportion of promised payment to deposits insurance due to limited coverage regulation.

Furthermore, future research is being conducted to find the implied barrier as the optimal barrier that does not make the FDIC gain or lose on average, that is

$P(X) = DIC(H, X).$ (15.4)

If we take into account bankruptcy and insurance premiums, the determination of implied barrier follows the equation:
