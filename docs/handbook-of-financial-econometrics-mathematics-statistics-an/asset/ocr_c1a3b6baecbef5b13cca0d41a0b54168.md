Davidson *et al.* (1995) used the expected time for the CBs to become attractive to be converted as the measure of the signal provided by the CBs. Following Davidson *et al.* (1995), a higher difference between the conversion price and the current stock price signals the shorter expected time for the CBs to become attractive to be converted. This is due to Kim (1990), that higher conversion price signals higher expected future earnings. When this is the case, the issue of the CBs is an equity-like issuance since investors would want to convert sooner. For this reason, the spread between the conversion price and the current stock is modelled as an equity-like signal.

In Lee *et al.* (2009), it was argued firms with higher shareholder rights, characterized with a greater extent of non-management institutional ownership, tend to issue more equity-like CBs with higher conversion probability at maturity (see, e.g., Lewis *et al.*, 1999, 2003), which is given by $N(d_2)$, where

$d_2 = (\ln(S/H) + (r - div - \sigma^2/2)T)/\sigma\sqrt{T}. \quad (59.1)$

$N(\bullet)$ is the cumulative probability of a standard normal distribution, $S$ is the stock price, $H$ is the conversion price, $r$ is the risk-free rate at the time of bond’s issue, $div$ is the firm’s dividend yield the year before the bond’s issue date, $T$ is the time to maturity, and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the equity return. It can be expected that a greater extent of non-management institutional ownership has a larger sequential conversion rate.

In addition to the aforementioned explanatory variables, this study includes the firm size as an explanatory variable for the equity-like signaling effect of CBs, as information asymmetries would not be the same with firms of different sizes, so does the use of CBs as a signal to acquire equity. In summary, eight explanatory variables are considered: the risk-free rate, the dividend, the capital and/or investment expenditures, the buy-back ratio of stock repurchase, the percentage spread between the conversion price and the current stock price, the non-management institutional ownership, and the total asset. Seven propositions are considered.
