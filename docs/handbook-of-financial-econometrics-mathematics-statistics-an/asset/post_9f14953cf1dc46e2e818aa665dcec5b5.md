## 28.8 Panel Data Estimators

For simplicity, consider the linear (in parameters) model

$y_{it} = \mathbf{X}_{it}\beta + e_{it}, \quad i = 1,\dots,N,\ t = 1,\dots,T, \qquad (28.22)$

where $\mathbf{X}_{it}$ is a $1 \times k$ vector of independent variables, $\beta$ is a $k \times 1$ vector of parameters, and $e_{it}$ is the error. Given that the data is ordered so that all observations of the 1st cross-section appear first, etc., then the row corresponding to observation $it$ is row $T(i-1)+t$.

If $E(e_{it}|\mathbf{X}_{it}) = 0$ then (28.22) can be estimated by OLS, which will be efficient if the $e_{it}$ are iid. However, the iid assumption is likely to be violated with panel data:

- Shocks affecting cross-section $i$ $\forall$ $t = 1,\dots,T$ imply correlation between $e_{it}$ and $e_{is}$ $\forall$ $t \neq s$.
- Shocks affecting all cross-sections i = 1, …, N at a particular t imply correlation between e_it and e_jt ∀ i ≠ j.
- ⇒ LS inefficient, inconsistent estimate of the covariance matrix.

If E(e_it|X_it) ≠ 0 then LS is inconsistent (e.g., if X_it contains lagged dependent variables and e_it are autocorrelated).

## 28.9 Error Component Models

The two most popular modeling approaches for panel data specify the error term in (28.22) to be composed of separate shocks, e.g.,

e_it = u_t + v_i + ε_it, (28.23)

where u_t are unobserved effects for time t, v_i affects all cross-sectional observations i, and ε_it is the common error that affects observation it.

It is generally assumed that the u_t, v_i and ε_it are independent across all t, i, and it, respectively.

## 28.10 Fixed Effects (FE) Models

In estimating the model, the u_t and v_i are specified as either FE or RE.

- **Fixed Effects (FE)**: parameters to be estimated, can be estimated using dummy variables (the so-called LSDV estimator) or by group mean differencing and LS.
- **Random Effects (RE)**: the e_it covariance matrix is specified as functions of the variances of the u_t, v_i, and ε_it. FGLS, ML or GMM estimators can be used for estimation.

Joint hypothesis tests of the effects can be carried out with an F-test (for the FE model) or Wald, LR, or LM test (RE model). A Durbin, Wu, Hausman test can be used to choose between the FE and RE specifications of the model.

For ease of exposition in what follows, let u_t = 0 in the error components specification (28.23). This implies that we are considering shocks specific to the cross-section but no time-specific shocks. The model is then

**y** = **X**β + **D**γ + ε, E(εε') = σ_ε²**I**_NT, (28.24)

where **y** and ε are NT vectors and **D** is a NT × N matrix of dummy variables such that the element in the row corresponding to observation it, for $i = 1,\ldots,N$ and $t = 1,\ldots,T$, and column $j$, for $j = 1,\ldots,N$, is equal to 1 if $i = j$ and 0 otherwise. The $N$ vector $\gamma$ has typical element $v_i$ so that the $NT$ vector $D\gamma$ has $v_i$ in the row corresponding to observation $it$. Therefore there is exactly one element of $D$ equal to 1 in each row, which implies that the $NT$ vector $\mathbf{ι}$ with each element equal to 1 is a linear combination of the columns of $D$. The parameter vector $\gamma$ measures the impact of the *fixed effects*, $v_i$. They must be independent of the errors $\varepsilon_{it}$ but may be correlated with the $\mathbf{X}$ variables. Regardless, the fixed effects model (28.24), interpreted conditionally on $\gamma$, implies the following moment conditions are satisfied:

$$E\left(\mathbf{X}_{it}'\left(y_{it}-\mathbf{X}_{it}\beta-v_i\right)\right)=0 \quad\text{and}\quad E\left(y_{it}-\mathbf{X}_{it}\beta-v_i\right)=0. \tag{28.25}$$

The FE estimator (28.10) is based on these moment conditions. From Frisch and Waugh (1933) and Lovell (1963), we have what is known as the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell Theorem. The OLS estimator of $\beta$ in (28.24) can be obtained by regressing $\mathbf{M}_{D}\mathbf{y}$, the errors from a regression of $\mathbf{y}$ on $\mathbf{D}$, on $\mathbf{M}_{D}\mathbf{X}$, the matrix of errors from regressing each column of $\mathbf{X}$ on $\mathbf{D}$. This leads to the fixed effects estimator presented in (28.10). For any $NT$ vector $\mathbf{x}$, the group mean is $\bar{x}_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{it}$. It is straightforward to check that element $it$ of the vector $\mathbf{M}_{D}\mathbf{X}$ is equal to the deviation from the group mean, $x_{it}-\bar{x}_i$.

In the linear (in parameters) FE model, the sufficient statistic is the group mean (a statistic is called sufficient for a parameter if the distribution of the sample given $i$ does not depend on the parameter). Since all the variables in (28.10) are premultiplied by $\mathbf{M}_{D}$, it follows that the estimator uses only information on the variation around the mean of each of the $N$ groups, which is why this is called the within-groups estimator. This estimator is unbiased if $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{D}$ are exogenous (if there is no contemporaneous correlation between explanatory variables and the error term). If the LS assumptions hold, the FE estimator is BLUE (the Gauss–Markov Theorem holds).

An advantage of the FE estimator is that it is easy to compute (all that is needed is to compute the $N$ group means for each variable if parameters associated with $\mathbf{D}$ are not of interest). Specification of the dummy variables is required only if estimates of $\hat{\gamma}$ are of interest. Multicollinearity may be an issue. If the $\mathbf{X}$ variables are well explained by the dummy variables in $\mathbf{D}$ then $\beta$ will not be precisely estimated with FE, due to the correlation between $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{D}$. Exact multicollinearity prohibits some specifications. The FE estimator cannot be used for explanatory variables that are constant within each cross-section because such a variable would be perfectly collinear with the columns of dummy variables, $\mathbf{D}$.

## 28.11 Random Effects (RE) Models

The efficiency of the FE estimator can be improved upon by applying the RE estimator if the **X** and $v_i$ are independent (recall what is required with the FE model is that the $\varepsilon_{it}$ are independent of **X** and $v_i$). RE also allows the possibility of estimating parameters associated with variables that are fixed across the cross-section (these variables are perfectly correlated with the fixed effects so their parameters can’t be estimated with FE).

RE is not consistent if the random effect is correlated with the **X** variables. For example, consider a panel of observations of individual workers, an observed variable like compensation may be correlated with an unobserved (to the econometrician) variable such as effort or ability, which implicitly enters the individual specific error $v_i$.

If **X** and $v_i$ are independent, then

$$E(e_{it} | \mathbf{X}_{it}) = E(v_i + \varepsilon_{it} | \mathbf{X}_{it}) = 0, \tag{28.26}$$

since both $v_i$ and $\varepsilon_{it}$ are independent of **X**. Note that (28.26) is the condition that implies that the pooled OLS model, (28.22), provides unbiased parameter estimates.

However, OLS estimation of (28.22), the pooled regression model, is not efficient because the $e_{it}$ are not iid. We can compute the covariance matrix of the $e_{it}$ if the $v_i$ are iid $(0, \sigma_v^2)$, hence the term “random effects.” From the error components, $e_{it} = u_t + v_i + \varepsilon_{it}$, assuming shocks are independent for simplicity and $u_t = 0$, it follows that

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Var}(e_{it}) &= \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \\
\text{Cov}(e_{it}e_{jt}) &= \sigma_v^2 \forall t \text{ and } i \neq j, \\
\text{Cov}(e_{it}e_{js}) &= 0 \forall i,j \text{ and } t \neq s,
\end{aligned}\tag{28.27}$$

which define the elements of the $NT \times NT$ covariance matrix, $\Omega$, necessary for GLS estimation. This assumption allows heteroskedasticity and contemporaneous correlation between the errors across the cross-sections, but rules out lagged correlations between the errors (no autocorrelation). If the data are ordered by cross-section units in $N$ blocks of $T$ observations each, this matrix has the form

$$\Omega = \begin{bmatrix}
\Sigma_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & \Sigma_2 & \ddots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 0 \\
0 & \cdots & 0 & \Sigma_N
\end{bmatrix}, \tag{28.28}$$
