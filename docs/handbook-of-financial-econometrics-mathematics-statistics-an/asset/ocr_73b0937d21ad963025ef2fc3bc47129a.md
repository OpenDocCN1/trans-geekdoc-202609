For example, the use of checking and deposit accounts may help the bank in assessing a firm’s loan repayment capability. That is, bank relationships might benefit business borrowers (i.e., company or firm) in terms of cash flow and credit and thereby improve firm value.

With bank relationships, borrowers might be inclined to reveal more information than that provided in transaction-oriented interactions, and lenders might have strong incentives to produce information. Therefore, when a bank is the financier, the borrower can disclose information to the bank without worrying about the information spilling over to its competitors. Thus, banks are indispensable in overcoming the problems of asymmetric information. A bank might also have incentives to produce information about the borrower because of its role as an enduring and dominant lender (Boot, 2000). Therefore, bank relationships increase total firm value.

A bank–borrower relationship is a mutual commitment based on trust and respect. The bank relationship has two principal costs, the soft budget constraint problem and the information capture problem (hold-up). Soft budget constraint suggests that banks serve as rescuers for enterprises that are on the verge of bankruptcy (Dewatripont and Maskin, 1995; Berglof and Roland, 1998). If a bank refuses to subsidize a fragile enterprise, then the enterprise will fail. Consequently, the borrower may reduce its total funding cost by accessing the bank’s credit and financial markets.

A bank’s assessment of the borrowing firm is of value because the bank has an insider position and thus holds detailed special (inside) information about the firm’s decision process and concurrent projects (Elyasiani and Goldberg, 2004). Banks have access to some proprietary information about borrowers that is unavailable to outside lenders that purchase publicly traded debt. The latter includes instruments such as bonds traded on the basis of publicly available information, as well as certificates of deposit, commercial papers and banker’s acceptances (Fama, 1985). The proprietary information may include historical data about the loan and deposit patterns of the borrowing firm, the firm’s balance sheet and income statements and familiarity with the firm’s plans and projects (Elyasiani and Goldberg, 2004). Access to such information provides the bank with a comparative cost advantage for collecting information about the borrowing firm. This advantage is useful in screening loans making risk assessments before approving a loan and monitoring the loan from then on.
