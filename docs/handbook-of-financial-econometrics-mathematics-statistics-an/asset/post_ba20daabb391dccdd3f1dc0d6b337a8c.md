### 35.2.1.1.2 Non-debt tax shield

DeAngelo and Masulis (1980) extend Miller’s (1977) model to analyze the effect of non-debt tax shields increasing the costs of debt for firms. Bowen et al. (1982) find their empirical work on the influence of non-debt tax shields on the capital structure consistent with DeAngelo and Masulis’s (1980) optimal-debt model. Graham (2000) investigates the effect of debt issuance on tax shield benefits and finds a significant magnitude of the tax-reducing value of the interest payments. However, the firms with large size, more profitability, and high liquidity use less debt as financing sources although the reducible tax from interests of debt can increase the earnings of firms with a lower bankruptcy possibility. Lin and Flannery (2013) investigate whether personal taxes affect the cost of debt and equity financing and find that personal tax is an important determinant of the capital structure. Lin and Flannery (2013) also show that the tax reduction policy in 2003 has a negative influence on firms’ leverage ratio.

In accordance with Titman and Wessels (1988) and Fama and French (2002), the indicators of non-debt tax shields are investment tax credits over total asset (ITC_TA), depreciation over total asset (D_TA), and non-debt tax shields over total asset (NDT_TA) where non-debt tax is defined as the corporate tax rate 34%.²

### 35.2.1.1.3 Growth

According to Titman and Wessels (1988), we use capital expenditures over total asset (CE_TA), the growth of total asset (GTA), and research and development over sales (RD_S) as the indicators of the growth attribute. The research and development over total asset (RD_TA) is added into these attributes to test the construct reliability in CFA.³

Titman and Wessels (1988) argue that growth opportunities can only add to a firm’s value but cannot collateralize or generate taxable income; therefore, the negative relationship between growth opportunities and debt can be observed.

### 35.2.1.1.4 Uniqueness and industry classification

Furthermore, the indicators of uniqueness include research and development over sales (RD_S) and selling expense over sales (SE_S). Titman (1984) indicates that uniqueness negatively correlates to debt because the firms with high-level uniqueness will cause customers, suppliers and workers to suffer the relatively high costs of finding alternative products, buyers and jobs when firms liquidate. In addition, Dhaliwal et al. (2016) show that firms with more concentrated customer base have higher cost of equity due to the increase of supplier’s risk.

Titman (1984) and Titman and Wessels (1988) suggest that firms manufacturing machines and equipment have high liquidation costs and are thus more likely to issue less debt. Therefore, SIC code (IDUM) can be a proxy of the industry classification attribute. Graham (2000) uses sales- and assets-Herfindahl indices to measure the industry concentration (Phillips, 1995; Chevalier, 1995) and utilize the dummy of SIC codes to measure product uniqueness. Graham (2000) demonstrates that a firm will use less debt if its product is more unique. Here, we introduce a dummy variable and assign one to firms in the manufacturing industry (SIC codes 3400 to 4000) and zero to other firms.

Quit rates (QRs) are used in both uniqueness and industry classification to represent the cost of human capital. The Bureau of Labor Statistics uses quit rates to track how many people are quitting their jobs within industries. Therefore, quit rates can be used to measure the uniqueness of industries. Specifically, low quit rates implicitly symbolize a high level of job-specific costs in which workers find it costly to find alternative jobs in the same industry. Thus, we expect quit rates to be negatively related to the debt ratio.

### 35.2.1.1.5 Size

The indicator of the size attribute is measured by the natural logarithm of sales (LnS). The financing cost of firms may relate to firm size since small firms have a higher cost of non-bank debt financing (see Bevan and Danbolt, 2002). Therefore, size should be positively associated with debt level.

### 35.2.1.1.6 Volatility and financial rating

The previous literature on the dynamic capital structure model focuses on the trade-off between the benefits of debt tax shields and the costs of financial distress (e.g., Fisher et al., 1989; Leland, 1994; Leland and Toft, 1996).

The tax benefits by issuing debts can be offset by the costs of financial distress. Therefore, Graham (2000) uses Altman’s (1968) z-score as modified by MacKie-Mason (1990) to measure bankruptcy and shows that the policy of debt conservatism is positively related to the z-score. This finding implies that firms using less debt can avoid financial distress. Here, we use Altman’s (1986) z-score (Z_Score) as an indicator of financial rating.

Furthermore, the volatility attribute is estimated by the standard deviation of the percentage change in operating income (SIGOI), coefficient of variation of ROA (CV_ROA), coefficient of variation of ROE (CV_ROE), and coefficient of variation of operating income (CV_OI). The large variance in earnings means a higher possibility of financial distress; therefore, to avoid bankruptcy, firms with larger volatility of earnings will have less debt. In addition, we can indirectly confirm Lambrecht and Myers’ (2012) non-coexistence of target adjustment models for the dividend payout and the capital structure. Lambrecht and Myers (2012) theoretically show that a non-smoothed payout will increase the volatility of the net income when a target debt ratio exists. Therefore, if the volatility attribute is significant in our empirical work, it may imply that the debt ratio will be adjusted to the target level, and the dividend payout will not be smoothed.

In addition, we consider the cost of issuing debt measured by Standard & Poor’s (S&P) long-term credit rating (SP_Rate) and the S&P investment credit rating (SP_INV).⁴ High levels of financial ratings can decrease the cost of issuing debt. Therefore, according to the pecking order theory, the level of financial ratings should be positively related to the leverage ratio.

### 35.2.1.1.7 Profitability

The pecking order theory developed in Myers’ (1977) paper indicates that firms prefer to use internal finance rather than external finance when raising capital. Profitable firms have relatively more retained earnings and therefore are less likely to issue debt. Hence, the profitability is negatively related to debt level. In contrast, to avoid the free cash flow problem, a profitable firm may increase its borrowing. The interest and principal payments will reduce the free cash flow and the potential agency problem of the profitable firm. Therefore, based on Jensen’s (1986) agency costs theory, the association between the capital structure and the profitability is positive. In accordance with Titman and Wessels (1988), the indicators of profitability are operating income over sales (OI_S) and operating income over total assets (OI_TA).

## 35.2.1.2 Macroeconomic factors

McDonald (1983) extends Miller’s (1977) theory and investigates the impact of government financial decisions on the capital structure. The equilibrium of McDonald’s (1983) model shows that the corporate debt-to-wealth ratio is negatively related to the government debt-to-wealth ratio. This finding implies that the decrease in federal borrowing would lead to an increase in the firm’s debt–equity ratio.

The previous studies confirm the negative relationship between government leverage and private sector debt (see Greenwood et al., 2010; Greenwood and Vayanos, 2010, 2014; Bansal et al., 2011; Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen, 2012; Graham et al., 2015). Bansal et al. (2011) provide an equilibrium model to illustrate the endogenous supply relationship between short-term public debt and private debt. The researchers employ the vector auto-regression (VAR) to perform empirical work and confirm the prediction of their model that an increase in government leverage leads to the decrease in private debt. Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen (2012) show the negative correlation between the government leverage and the corporate bond spread, which is the difference of yields on AAA corporate bonds and long maturity Treasury bonds. When the supply of public debt decreases, the wide corporate bond spread implies an increase in the supply of corporate debt. This evidence is consistent with the finding in Greenwood et al. (2010) that the issues of private debts appear to shift in the supply of government debt. Graham et al. (2015) use both macroeconomic factors and firm characteristics to investigate the determinants of capital structure and find that government leverage (debt-to-GDP ratio), which is defined as the ratio of federal debt held by the public to GDP, is an important determinant of the variation in the aggregate leverage, which is defined as the ratio of aggregate total debt to aggregate book value.
