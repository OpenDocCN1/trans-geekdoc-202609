By comparing portfolio A and portfolio B to derive the lower bound and comparing portfolio A and portfolio C to derive upper bound, the bounds of an European call option for a single-period can be shown as

$C_{\min}^E = \text{Max}\left(0, S_0 + \frac{1}{1+r}\left[-K + \int_0^K F_Y(w - S_0)dw\right]\right), \quad (7.6)$

$C_{\max}^E = S_0 + \frac{S_0}{S_0 + E[Y]}\left[-K + \int_0^K F_Y(w - S_0)dw\right], \quad (7.7)$

where $F_Y(w - S_0)$ is the cumulative probability distribution of price change $Y$ for $Y \in [-S_0, \infty)$. The option bounds are functions of the initial stock price $S_0$, the exercise price $K$, the riskless rate of interest, $r$, and the distribution of stock price change, $F_Y(Y)$.

In the following, Perrakis (1986) extends the results of Perrakis and Ryan (1984) by tightening the bounds with the restrictions that the one-period return (of per dollar investing in the underlying stock) is finite and has a strictly positive lower limit. In addition, the bounds for American put option are also developed within the framework of Perrakis and Ryan (1984).

# 7.2.2.2 Second degree stochastic dominance with riskless asset (SSDR)

Distinct with Perrakis and Ryan's (1984) option bounds, which are obtainable by applying second degree stochastic dominance method, Levy (1985) applies the concept of second degree stochastic dominance with the riskless asset (SSDR) to derive the upper and lower bounds for European options. Levy (1985) assumes that the investors' preferences belong to the set of all non-decreasing concave utility function and the underlying assets are those with nonnegative consumption beta. Let $F_c$ and $F_s$ be the cumulative distributions of the terminal wealth investing in an European call option and its underlying asset. If borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate are allowed, which is crucial assumption to derive relative narrower bounds, then an investor may find that any combination of the underlying asset with the riskless asset is dominated by some combination of European call option with the riskless asset even though neither $F_c$ nor $F_s$ dominates each other without the riskless asset. That is, in the presence of a riskless asset, if every risk-averse investor prefers to hold the investment $F_c$ rather than the investment $F_s$, then $F_c$ is said to dominate $F_s$ by SSDR.
