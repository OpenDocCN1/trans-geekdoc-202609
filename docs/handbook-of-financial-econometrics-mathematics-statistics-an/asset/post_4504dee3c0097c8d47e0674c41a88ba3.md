### 24.5.2 Index futures

Constantinides et al. (2011) utilize the stochastic dominance bounds derived by Constantinides and Perrakis (2009), which consider incomplete and imperfect markets involving bid-ask spread and transaction costs in the derivation of the bounds for American options, as the benchmarks for trading strategy in the market of American options on the S&P 500 index futures. Constantinides et al. (2011) test whether the options on S&P 500 index futures from 1983 to 2006 are profitable by only using the violations of option bounds as signals for trading. Their major findings are as follows: (1) there are significant violations of upper bounds in the bid price of American call options rather than American put options. (2) The trading strategy of selling (buying) options when they violate the upper (lower) bounds is profitable in the market of American options on S&P 500 index futures. Without a given probability distribution of S&P 500 index futures, the empirical results in Constantinides et al. (2011) are not affected by estimation error and model misspecification problem of the underlying asset’s return.

### 24.5.3 Foreign exchange rates

Chung and Wang (2008) derive the bounds of a currency cross-rate option under a risk-neutral condition. By using a no-arbitrage pricing approach, the bounds of a currency cross-rate option can be presented as the combination of two other currency options on related dollar rates. Chung and Wang (2008) use daily OTC currency option prices from 11 March 1999 to 11 January 2001 and test the bounds of a currency cross-rate option on euros/British pounds (€/£) by using two other currency options on US dollars/British pounds ($/£) and US dollars/euros ($/€), respectively. The results show that the bounds of a currency cross-rate option have powerful explanatory power for its market prices. Therefore, the persistent relationships between the market prices of a cross-rate option and its bounds can be applied to hedging strategies.

### 24.5.4 Real options

Since real options are involved in capital budgeting decisions, which have distinct characteristics from other options, the risk preference of the decision maker is an important factor in pricing real option bounds. Mello and Pyo (2006) and Pyo (2008) employ a minimax deviation approach incorporated with risk-averse utility functions to obtain real option bounds in incomplete markets. Their numerical results show that their minimax bounds are more efficient and narrower than the other two approaches, gain–loss ratio bounds by Bernardo and Ledoit (2000) and good-deal bounds by Cochrane and Saa-Requejo (2000).

## 24.6 Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a comprehensive literature review of option bounds. Option bounds can be generally obtained with fewer assumptions on the investor’s preferences and/or the underlying asset’s price processes. The studies on option bounds are categorized either by their mathematical techniques to derive the bounds or by their different approaches to tighten the bounds. The most important mathematical methods for deriving option bounds include searching for arbitrage conditions, stochastic dominance, linear programming, and semi-parametric and non-parametric methods. The bounds on option prices can be further tightened using a variety of approaches including: imposing restrictions on investor preferences or pricing kernels, the selection of a risk-neutral measure, and consideration of market frictions. We also survey the papers that have applied option bounds to different empirical applications. Although the empirical studies concentrate on the options with underlying assets in the equity market, their results conclude with a summary that the violations of no-arbitrage and stochastic dominance approaches are a concern in empirical work. These option bounds have also been shown to be useful in many areas, such as providing qualitative properties of option prices and screening market data for mispricing.

To our best knowledge, this is the most complete review paper that discusses the evolution of option bounds. We hope that it will serve as a foundation for researchers to continue developing tighter option bounds and motivate future applications of option bounds in different empirical settings.
