## Appendix 60A: Estimation of Panel Vector Autoregressive Models

### Appendix 60A.1: The model

Time-series vector autoregression (VAR) models are originated in the macro-econometrics literature as an alternative to multivariate simultaneous equation models (Sims, 1980). All variables in a VAR system are typically treated as endogenous, although identifying restrictions based on theoretical models or on statistical procedures can be allowed as well. We give a brief overview of the panel VAR model and the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) estimating framework. We consider a k-variate homogeneous panel VAR of order *p* with *N* unit-specific fixed effects represented by the following system of linear equations

$$Y_{it}=Y_{it-1}A_1+Y_{it-2}A_2+\cdots+Y_{it-p+1}A_{p-1}+Y_{it-p}A_p+X_{it}B+u_i+e_{it}$$

$$i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\},\ t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\},\qquad (60A.1)$$

where $Y_{it}$ is a $(1\times k)$ vector of dependent variables; $X_{it}$ is a $(1\times l)$ vector of exogenous covariates; $u_i$ and $e_{it}$ are $(1\times k)$ vectors of dependent variable, unit specific fixed-effects and idiosyncratic errors, respectively. The $(k\times k)$ matrices $A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_{p-1},A_p$ and the $(l\times k)$ matrix $B$ are parameters to be estimated and are restricted to be the same for all units. Therefore, we do not allow for cross-sectional heterogeneities or for dynamic interdependencies. We assume that the innovations have the following characteristics: $E[e_{it}]=0$, $E[e_{it}e'_{it}]=\Sigma$ and $E[e'_{it}e_{is}]=0$ for all $t>s$ which implies that we do not allow for static interdependencies among units as well. All of the parameters are suggested to be estimated jointly with fixed effects.

### Appendix 60A.2: GMM estimation

Many researchers suggest that estimators based on the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) framework are appropriate in order to get consistent estimates of equation (60A.1), especially in settings where T is fixed and N is large. The first-difference transformation may be consistently estimated equation-by-equation by introducing as instruments the lagged first-differences and levels of Yit as proposed by Anderson and Hsiao (1982). However, this procedure causes some problems in case of unbalanced panels, in the sense that if some Yit−1 are missing, then the first differences at time t and t−1 will be missing as well. Also, it has to be added that the necessary time periods that are needed for the proper use of the model get larger with the lag order of the panel VAR.

Arellano and Bond (1991) were the first to investigate the GMM estimation of dynamic panel data. Alonso-Borrego and Arellano (1999) suggest that the standard GMM estimator obtained after first differencing suffers from substantial finite sample biases. The choice of transformation is of great importance, since it is considered to have an impact on the finite sample behavior of the GMM estimator. The evidence suggests that the GMM estimator of the forward orthogonalized deviation model performs better than the estimators of first difference model.

Arellano and Bover (1995) proposed the forward orthogonal deviation as an alternative transformation. So, in order to remove fixed effects we use forward mean-differencing, also referred to as the “Helmert procedure”. In Helmert transformation (forward orthogonal transformation), each of the original variables, except the last, is transformed by subtracting the mean of those variables that follow it. This transformation preserves the orthogonality between transformed variables and lagged regressors, which allows us to use lagged regressors as instruments and estimate efficiently the coefficients by system Generalized Method of Moments (GMM). Finally, the Helmert approach removes the panel-specific fixed effects as the first-difference approach does.

$$Y^*_{it} = \bar{Y}^*_{it} A + e^*_{it}$$

$$Y^*_{it} = [y^*_{1it} y^*_{2it} \cdots y^*_{kit}]$$

$$\bar{Y}^*_{it} = [Y^*_{it-1} Y^*_{it-2} \cdots Y^*_{it-p+1} Y^*_{it-p} X_{it}]$$

$$e_{it}^* = [e_{it}^{1*} e_{it}^{2*} \cdots e_{it}^{k-1*} e_{it}^{k*}]$$
$$A' = [A_1' A_2' \cdots A_{p-1}' A_p' B'],$$ (60A.2)

where the asterisk denotes some transformation of the original variable. If the original variable is presented as $m_{it}$, then the first-difference transformation implies that $m_{it}^* = m_{it} - m_{it-1}$, while for the forward orthogonal deviation $m_{it}^* = (m_{it} - \bar{m}_{it}) \sqrt{T_{it}/(T_{it}+1)}$, where $T_{it}$ is the number of available future observations for panel $i$ at time $t$, and $\bar{m}_{it}$ is the average of those future observations. Notice that this transformation gives larger weight to observations closer to the beginning of the sample, so these observations have equal variance.

Suppose these observations are stacked both over panels and over time, then the GMM estimator is given by

$$A = (\overline{Y^{*'}} Z \hat{W} Z' \overline{Y^*})^{-1} \overline{Y^{*'}} Z \hat{W} Z' Y^*,$$ (60A.3)

where $\hat{W}$ is a $(L\times L)$ weighting matrix assumed to be non-singular, symmetric and positive semi-definite. Assuming that $E[Z'e] = 0$ and rank $E[\overline{Y^{*'}} Z] = kp + l$, the GMM estimator is consistent. According to Hansen (2002), the weighting matrix $\hat{W}$ may be selected to maximize efficiency. Joint estimation of the system of equations makes cross-equation hypothesis testing straightforward. Wald tests about the parameters may be implemented based on the GMM estimate of $A$ and its covariance matrix. Granger causality tests, with the hypothesis that all coefficients on the lags of the variable of interest are jointly zero, may likewise be carried out using this test.
