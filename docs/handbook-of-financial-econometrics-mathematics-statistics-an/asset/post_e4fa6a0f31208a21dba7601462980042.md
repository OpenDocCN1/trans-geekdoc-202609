## Abstract

This study examines how a firm’s usage of social media and banking relationships influence its value. Using a sample of 6,636 year-firm observations from 2008 to 2015, the results show that social media (Facebook, Google+, and LinkedIn) positively influence firm value, whereas bank relationships affect firm value differently: the high number of banks a firm borrows from reduces value, whereas the high bank debt a firm uses creates value. The impacts of YouTube and Twitter on firm value are insignificant. Although social media have a similar function as banks in mitigating the information asymmetry between firms and outsiders, the information types vary. Banks create more soft and private information, while social media deliver more public and hard information. The accuracy of information is more than the quantity; hence, whether more information sharing via social media creates value is uncertain. We also find the substitution and complementary effects between various types of social media and banking relationships on firm value. Our results remain robust after conducting a difference-in-differences (DID) analysis using the exogenous shock of the Facebook IPO in 2012.

## 66.1 Introduction

Knowledge of corporate fundamentals influences security prices. The transmission of such information is one of the central topics in economics. A pipeline of research initiated by Grossman and Stiglitz (1980) and Verrecchia (1982) investigates the incentives to acquire costly information and its impact on asset markets. Based on the theory of rational expectation, the investors’ diversified information is reflected in asset prices and the public also incorporate the information content into their trading decisions.

Prior research related to information would focus on banking relationships as bank contributions benefit from reduced asymmetric information and help to price corporate value (Boot, 2000). The potential benefits of relationship banking on mitigating asymmetric information have been well addressed in the literature. However, the issue of whether social communication delivers effective information and helps value an enterprise is a topic that is still under study.

Social media have grown rapidly and remarkably influenced the valuation of a firm. Its participation has increased from 8% of adults in 2005 to 65% in 2015 (Perrin, 2015). The population of monthly active users on Facebook and Twitter in the world exceeded 2.45 billion as of 2019.¹
¹See https://www.statista.com/statistics/264810/number-of-monthly-active-facebook-users-worldwide/.
Social media platforms allow users to communicate and interact on the Internet instantly. People can easily send messages through a messenger or a tweet and obtain a fast response (Correa et al., 2010; Edosomwan et al., 2011). Similarly, companies can create firm value by engaging with customers and other stakeholders through social media. A firm can potentially reach new customers, increase interaction with the existing customers and generate word of mouth (WOM) for new and existing product offerings (Aral et al., 2013; Hitt et al., 2014). Besides, companies can use social media to reduce information asymmetry which affects the pricing of financial assets and securities (Blankespoor et al., 2013).

The effects of social media on economic activity are extensive and pervasive, with social networks playing a role in the transmission of information on jobs, products and technologies. Some research shows that regularly sharing information with peers via social networks plays a vital role in investment decision-making, including stock market participation and portfolio choices. Organizations can improve their competitive advantages through social media by learning about the customer’s tastes, sentiments and competitors’ tactics (Tajudeen et al., 2017). While customer satisfaction, brand recognition and even product sales can be good indicators for predicting firm performance, very little evidence exists detailing how the usage of social media affects firm value (Hitt et al., 2014).

In this chapter, we examine the triangular relationships between social media, bank relationships and firm value. Specifically, we aim to answer the following questions: How do social media influence corporate valuation? How do bank relationships affect firm value? Does a firm’s social media usage affect its banking relationships, or does the interaction of social media and bank relationships affect a firm’s value? Moreover, what are the implications for consumers and investors? To address these questions, we design research pathways using big data from social media platforms and the associated financial and lending databases from the Taiwan Economic Journal (TEJ).

The social media platforms include Facebook, YouTube, Twitter and Google+. Data are collected from the official online forums that companies use for communication and interaction. The sample exclusively comprises non-financial firms listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange (TSE) over 2008-2015. We also exclude firms with missing data regarding short-term and long-term bank debt or other required banking relationship data obtained during our sample period. The final effective sample was a 6,636 year–firm observations from 849 listed firms.

In Taiwan, banks still play a vital role in providing the funds and sustainable growth to firms. The robust banking relationships help overcome the frictions caused by credit and help maintain certain advantages. Currently, the e-commerce market in Taiwan has been growing for the past decade. The advantage of using social media is more pronounced in enhancing efficiency.

Using ordinary least squares (OLS), quantile regression and difference-in-differences (DID) analysis, we find that social media (Facebook, Google+ and LinkedIn) positively influence firm value, particularly for firms that have a value that ranges in the low to median quantiles. However, while the number of bank relationships negatively affects firm value, the bank debt ratio raises firm value. A complementary relationship exists between YouTube and bank relationship measured by the bank debt ratio. Social media play a similar role as banks in mitigating information asymmetry and affect firm value significantly.

The rest of this chapter is organized as follows. Section 66.2 presents a brief literature review. Section 66.3 describes the sample, data and methodology. Section 66.4 analyzes the empirical results. Section 66.5 concludes.

## 66.2 Literature Review

In this section, we aim to understand the impact of social media and bank relationships on firm value. The argument is that social media represents a factor that can beneficially influence firm value and bank relationships. This idea has attracted considerable attention in the literature.

### 66.2.1 Social media and firm value

Social media were developed during the 1970s and are defined as online technologies and practices that people use to share knowledge and opinions (Laroche et al., 2012). Through social media, people can easily send messages through an instant messenger or a tweet and obtain a fast response (Correa et al., 2010; Edosomwan et al., 2011). In addition, recent reports suggest that social media may positively affect the bottom line and brand image of businesses (Hardy, 2012). Through social media, a firm’s effort to communicate affects its market value because the information it disseminates can influence its stock price by providing signals about its marketing strategy, customer service quality and growth potential to potential and current investors (Chung et al., 2014).

Numerous researchers have explored the use of enterprise social media as a method for transforming businesses and improving firm value. As firms look to forge strong connections with their customers in a competitive marketplace, the use of social media can dramatically influence their performance through customer engagement and the value created from customer interactions (Trainor, 2012). For example, businesses use social media as a tool to develop and maintain strong relationships with customers for WOM marketing, i.e., the intentional influence of customer-to-customer communication (Kozinets et al., 2010); for community-based customer support (Greenberg, 2010) and for innovation co-creation (Sawhney et al., 2005). Firms create community support for their brands by sharing information, perpetuating the history and culture of the brand and assisting consumers. In addition, the creation and maintenance of long-term customer relationships, instead of emphasizing individual transactions, has been introduced as a competitive advantage and strategic resource for firms. Thus, social media-based online communities enhance and build brand loyalty and trust (Ba, 2001). That is, these communities decrease uncertainty and information asymmetry and make customers feel comfortable with their brands (Chiu et al., 2010; Pavlou et al., 2007; Doney and Cannon, 1997). Hence, when firms use social media to engage with customers, they enhance their relationship with them and create additional marketing value.

In addition, social media can assist companies to create online resources containing the accumulated wisdom of the organizations by allowing knowledge to be codified, searched and shared (Cairncross, 2001; Tajudeen et al., 2017). Moreover, companies can collect general information about the market and information regarding new trends and industry through social media. Companies can also easily deliver organizational information to the public in a timely manner (Parveen et al., 2015). However, social media platforms have shifted the direction of information flow such that the roles of users have transformed from content consumers to producers. This situation creates the potential for asymmetric information conditions between conversation partners. Information asymmetry is a condition in which one participant in an exchange possesses more or better information than the other (Akerlof, 1970).

Numerous studies have examined the effects of the specific types of social media (e.g., recommender systems) and marketing outcomes (e.g., brand perception, customer satisfaction and even product sales) (Chevalier and Mayzlin, 2006; Dellarocas et al., 2007; Duan et al., 2008; Forman et al., 2008).
