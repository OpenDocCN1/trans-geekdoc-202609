On the other hand, the combination of these results suggests systemic risk: as more loans are concentrated into economic sectors and commercial products where increased loss expense is anticipated.

Loss provisions, as a share of loans, increased after DFA was implemented and increasing delinquent loan volumes tend to increase loss provision expenses. This suggests a moral hazard argument for motivating the management response to the DFA. DFA implementation induced bank managers to expand their assets, equity and efforts to make profitable loans. This finding also appears to have provided operational constraints that allowed expansion of efforts to make riskier loans.

The effect of SOX on loan loss provision budgeting has been to make this process a more complex management activity. The negative coefficient associated with the SOX_t variable indicates a relationship between loss provision and governance quality exists. A marginal decrease in loan loss provision-to-total loans, in particular, occurs when the bank grows (in assets) underscores the importance of establishing effective governance as the firm expands since this would be offset by substantial increases in non-performing loan volume. This implies that operational policies are generated, and activities monitored that make more effective use of existing loss provision expense. The result suggests an adverse selection interpretation of the effect of SOX on loan activities. An increased governance quality allows bank managers to reduce operational expenses per dollar loaned. The impact of this finding is to reduce these expenses, even after controlling for DFA.

## 131.4 Regulation and Financial Institution Product Selection

Section 131.3 illustrates that a consequence of SOX and DFA has been to affect risk management expenses. Regulatory effects are also measured in financial institutions by changes in a variety of economic efficiency measures (Du, 2013; Zelenyuk and Zheka, 2006). Community bank financial performance has been studied by a variety of researchers using various measures of economic efficiency (Acharya et al., 2008; Degl'Innocenti et al., 2017; Diallo, 2018; Matousek and Tzeremes, 2016). In this section, the effect of regulation on financial institution financial performance is measured by observing its effect on non-interest expenses.

Non-interest expense is an important feature in the analysis of bank failure (Samad, 2014). Changes in non-interest expenses are interpreted as early warning signals of future failure. Failed financial institutions have a statistically greater non-interest expense than non-failed firms five quarters before their failure. Kovner et al. (2014) study whether policies limiting the growth of big banks should be considered based on their effect on the non-interest expense ratio, concluding that any such policy should consider the economies of scale associated with the bank holding company size. The authors find a relationship between size and the non-interest expense-to-income as well as to-assets ratio. These researchers find that controlling for asset size and loan concentration reveals a negative relationship exists between the non-interest expense ratio and bank size. Capital structure was also statistically relevant.

A consistent finding in the literature is that managing expenses offers opportunity for increased performance. Efficient use of labor contributes to performance since staff expenses should be expected to be inversely proportional to measures of performance. Naceur and Omran (2011) study the importance of non-interest expenses and conclude that lower operating costs and policies accommodating market entry intensify competition and improve bank transparency. Apergis (2014) finds expense management is an important component of profitability as banks diversify their operations into non-traditional types of financial products.

The importance of capital structure in financial institutions is well documented. Capital mitigates financial distress within financial institutions (Hyun and Rhee, 2011). The importance of adequate capital has been discussed in many settings including community banks (Marsh, 2014). Samad (2014) observes capital ratio deteriorates in the quarters preceding bank failure when non-interest expenses increase, an indicator that capital adequacy is a component of financial institution response to regulation, even in the presence of changes in non-interest expenses. Capital is likely to play a unique role in community banks since these are typically not publicly listed and have limited equity capital formation ability. Nevertheless, capital requirements are imposed by the Office of Comptroller of the Currency upon community banks.

The effect of regulation on financial institution performance is also affected by the diversity of a firm’s credit risk. Shen and Hartarska (2013) conclude that community banks avoid elements of the financial crisis by managing their credit risk rather than participate in derivative markets for income purposes. Naceur and Omran (2011) observe tradeoffs exist between credit risk and loan interest income, which suggests accompanying levels of personnel expense to underwrite and service loans. Community banks are created to serve a relatively homogeneous group, such as agricultural producers in a region or residents of a particular area. Community bank executives are likely to develop loan policies to protect equity and will benefit from the increased information available by serving a geographically homogeneous set of customers, thereby better evaluating credit risk. Klomp and Haan (2012) account for loan quality, measured as non-performing to total loans, in their study of bank behavior. Nevertheless, increases in non-interest costs associated with generating loans may offset any desire to increase income through any perceived risk of offering loans affected by the Dodd–Frank Act, in particular the perception of increased costs due to regulatory modifications. Alternatively, Naceur and Omran (2011) find increased credit risk has a positive and significant effect on cost efficiency.

Prior research indicates regulation compliance has a heterogeneous effect on financial institution performance. Zywicki (2013) considers compliance costs of DFA “invariant to the size or output of the firm,” thereby affecting small institutions disproportionately. Since most community banks have assets smaller than traditional FDIC measures that denote large banks, this is of particular concern. Klomp and Haan (2012) depart from traditional observations of regulatory compliance costs by asset size when analyzing the effect of regulation based on financial institution risk quantiles and conclude regulations disproportionately affect riskier financial institutions. To the extent that asset risk depends on financial institution capitalization, DFA compliance costs may disproportionately affect community banks because of their relatively small size as observed in the heterogeneity in compliance costs across bank size bands for compliance with SOX (Garneau and Shahid, 2009) or the Patriot Act (Dolar and Shughart, 2012).
