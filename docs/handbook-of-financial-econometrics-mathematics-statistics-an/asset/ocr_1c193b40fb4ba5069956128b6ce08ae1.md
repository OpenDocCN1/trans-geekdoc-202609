somewhat underappreciated in professional practice; therefore, it deserves some attention. According to Asness et al. (2001) a good way to think about the systematic risk associated with hedge fund performance is to aggregate both the current systemic risk and lagged components associated with systemic risk, which is illustrated in the following formula:

$$R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{0i}R_{m,t} + \beta_{1i}R_{m,t-1} + \cdots + \beta_{3i}R_{m,t-3} + \varepsilon_{i,t},\qquad (91.1)$$

where $R_{i,t}$ is the return on an asset in time period $t$, $R_{m,t}$ is the return on the market proxy during time period $t$, $R_{m,t-1\,to\,t-3}$ are the lagged returns for the market proxy in time periods $t-1$, $t-2$, and $t-3$, $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ is an error term, and $\beta$ are the regression coefficients (i.e., $\beta_{0i}$, $\beta_{1i}$, and $\beta_{3i}$).

For instance, Asness et al. (2001) use this regression framework to illustrate how stale pricing could be used by managers to reduce the volatility experienced when investing in a particular type of hedge fund. Although the sample used to generate the regression results are from the 1994–2000 period, the illustration provides some meaningful insight, which is that hedge funds take on more systemic market risk than standard regression estimates would indicate.

In a more recent study, Smith and Mumtaz (2017) extended this analysis focusing specifically on the relationship between an aggregate hedge fund index with one modification which was that the hedge fund returns were compared against a proxy for the global stock market (i.e., the MSCI World Index). In Smith et al. (2017), the results indicate that the ‘effect’ of the smoothing seems to last for two periods instead of three as identified in Asness et al. (2001) and the ‘summed beta,’ which is used for a proxy of total systemic risk, is 0.49, which is a fraction of the 0.84 estimate provided in Asness et al. (2001). However, this is still a significant increase in the level of systemic risk that the hedge fund is shouldering as compared against the traditional estimate of systemic risk (i.e., proxied for through $\beta_0$ in Table 91.1).

Bollen and Pool (2008) provided researchers with a slightly different way to think about performance smoothing in that they examined the conditional smoothing of hedge fund managers. Smith et al. (2017) made a simple adjustment to the Asness et al. (2001) formula presented in equation (91.1) and estimated regressions based on the following two equations to determine if there were any ‘conditional effects’ associated with positive or negative performance events:

$$R_{i,t}^{-} = \alpha_i + \beta_{0i}R_{m,t} + \beta_{1i}R_{m,t-1} + \cdots + \beta_{3i}R_{m,t-3} + \varepsilon_{i,t},\qquad (91.2)$$
