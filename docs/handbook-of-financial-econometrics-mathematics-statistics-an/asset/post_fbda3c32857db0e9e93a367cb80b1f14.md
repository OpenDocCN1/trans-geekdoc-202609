### 121.2.2.2 Bali, Peng, Shen, and Tang (2014)’s liquidity shocks

To study the effect of liquidity shocks on future stock returns, Bali et al. (2014) argue that market frictions such as investor inattention and illiquidity can lead to underreaction to liquidity shocks. Following Amihud (2002), Bali et al. (2014) measure the illiquidity of individual stocks as the average daily ratio of absolute return over dollar trading volume in a month, i.e.,

$$ILLIQ_{i,t} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{d=1}^{N} \frac{|r_{i,d}|}{VOL_{i,d}}, \tag{121.4}$$

where $r_{i,d}$ denotes the daily return of stock $i$ on day $d$, $VOL_{i,d}$ denotes the daily dollar trading volume of stock $i$ on day $d$, and $N$ denotes the number of days with non-missing data within month $t$. Their first measure of liquidity shock is simply the negative difference between $ILLIQ_{i,t}$ and its previous 12-month average. The second measure assumes $ILLIQ_{i,t}$ to follow an ARMA(1,1) process so that the liquidity shock is defined as the negative difference between $ILLIQ_{i,t}$ and its estimated value from the ARMA(1,1) model. The third measure of liquidity shock is the negative difference between volume-weighted effective relative spread (SPRD) and its previous 12-month average. In their univariate portfolio-level analysis, stocks are ranked on liquidity shocks and assigned into ten portfolios. Next, one-month-ahead portfolio returns are calculated. Contrary to the findings that are based on the assumption of perfect informational efficiency, Bali et al. (2014) show that the 10th portfolio (stocks with positive liquidity shocks) tend to produce higher subsequent returns, up to six months in the future, than the 1st portfolio (stocks with negative liquidity shocks).

### 121.2.2.3 Money market liquidity premium

Rounds of the Fed’s Quantitative Easing after the 2007–2009 Great Recession helped money flow in the economy and improved stock market liquidity. The President of the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, Eric Rosengren (2010), argued that the seriousness of the recent financial crisis was underestimated by economic forecasters because financial channels, like the provision of liquidity to the real economy, were only crudely incorporated into most macroeconomic modeling (p. 221). Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) find that asset market liquidity and funding liquidity (of financial intermediaries) are codependent. Particularly, during the periods of financial distress when margins are destabilizing, deterioration of market liquidity is intensified interactively with that of funding liquidity, leading to “liquidity spirals”. As a result, institutional investors shift their holdings of assets toward the ones with lower margins. They also show that the impact of monetary policy on market liquidity is asymmetric, i.e., expansionary (contractionary) monetary policy causes a smaller (larger) increase (decrease) in liquidity. Similarly, Chordia, Sarkar, and Subrahmanyam (2005) find that during crisis periods, increases in both stock and bond market liquidity are associated with monetary expansions. They find that innovations to liquidity in both stock and bond markets are significantly correlated, and “macro” liquidity (fund flows between sectors in the economy) is linked to “micro” (or trading activities) liquidity.

Examining bank deposits market and monetary policy, Drechsler et al. (2017) find that as federal funds rate increases, so do deposit spreads. As a result, deposits flow out of the banking system, which can lead to a reduction of financial market liquidity and an increase of liquidity premium. Their study implies that the liquidity premium of near-money assets (Federal funds loans and T-Bills) is likely to affect asset prices and firms’ cost of capital. Following Drechsler et al. (2017), we measure the money market liquidity as the difference between Federal funds rate and three-month T-Bill rate.

## 121.3 Methodology and Data

### 121.3.1 Methodology

To examine whether aggregate liquidity has marginal power to explain cross-section of stock returns, we focus on the comparison between the performance of standard asset-pricing models and that of their enhanced versions with aggregate liquidity as an additional factor. The first standard model is the Fama and French (1996) three-factor model (FF3), the most commonly used model in empirical asset pricing:

$$r_{i,t}= \beta_i^0 + \beta_i^M MKT_t + \beta_i^S SMB_t + \beta_i^H HML_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \tag{121.5}$$

where $r_{i,t}$ is the excess return on asset $i$ in period $t$, $MKT_t$ is the excess market return, $SMB_t$ is the difference between the returns on diversified portfolios of small stocks and big stocks, and $HML_t$ is the difference between the returns on diversified portfolios of high book-to-market (value) stocks and low book-to-market (growth) stocks. If aggregate liquidity is a priced factor, it should reduce pricing errors of the FF3. Thus, we consider an enhanced version of the FF3, a four-factor model, which augments the FF3 with the Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity factor (FF3-PS):

$$r_{i,t}= \beta_i^0 + \beta_i^L PS_t + \beta_i^M MKT_t + \beta_i^S SMB_t + \beta_i^H HML_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \tag{121.6}$$

where $PS_t$ is the Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity factor. The FF3-PS is used by Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) as their benchmark model. We explore the pricing power of alternative liquidity measures (i.e., DSS and BPST) in a similar four-factor model framework. That is, we also consider the following two models, namely FF3-DSS and FF3-BPST:

$$r_{i,t}= \beta_i^0 + \beta_i^L DSS_t + \beta_i^M MKT_t + \beta_i^S SMB_t + \beta_i^H HML_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \tag{121.7}$$

$$r_{i,t}= \beta_i^0 + \beta_i^L BPST_t + \beta_i^M MKT_t + \beta_i^S SMB_t + \beta_i^H HML_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \tag{121.8}$$

where $DSS_t$ and $BPST_t$ are the liquidity measures of Drechsler et al. (2017) and Bali et al. (2014).

More recently, Fama and French (2015) propose a five-factor model (FF5):

$$r_{i,t}= \beta_i^0 + \beta_i^M MKT_t + \beta_i^S SMB_t + \beta_i^H HML_t + \beta_i^{OP} RMW_t + \beta_i^{INV} CMA_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \tag{121.9}$$

where $RMW_t$ (Robust Minus Weak) is the average return on the two robust operating profitability portfolios minus the average return on the two weak operating profitability portfolios, and $CMA_t$ (Conservative Minus Aggressive) is the average return on the two conservative investment portfolios minus the average return on the two aggressive investment portfolios. We therefore also compare the FF5 to its enhanced version, a six-factor model:

$$r_{i,t}= \beta_i^0 + \beta_i^L PS_t + \beta_i^M MKT_t + \beta_i^S SMB_t + \beta_i^H HML_t + \beta_i^{OP} RMW_t + \beta_i^{INV} CMA_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \tag{121.10}$$

To evaluate the performance of these asset-pricing models, we employ the time-series regression approach of Fama and French (1993, 1996, and 2015).¹⁰ Essentially, alternative asset-pricing models are estimated for each test asset with monthly data. Following relevant literature (e.g., Fama and French, 2015), we will evaluate each model based on the magnitude of pricing errors (the average absolute value of the intercepts and the average of the standard errors of the intercepts), the explanatory power (the average adjusted R²), and the Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken (1989) GRS F-test statistic for the hypothesis that the intercepts are jointly equal to zero for all test assets.

The monthly Fama–French factor data are from Professor French’s website. The Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity measure as well as its traded version, which we refer to as PS and PSTraded, are from Professor Pástor’s website. To construct the Drechsler et al. (2017) measure, we retrieve the federal funds rate and the three-month Treasury yield from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED). We also use the CRSP data to construct the Bali, Peng, Shen, and Tang (2014) measure. Our sample period is from July 1963 to December 2017, which is dictated by the availability of the Fama–French factors.

Figure 121.1 plots the 12-month moving averages of the Fama–French as well as alternative liquidity factors, with the shaded vertical bars denoting the NBER-dated recessions. Evidently, all the factors exhibit business-cycle frequency fluctuations, suggesting that they could potentially be state variables underlying the cross-section of expected stock returns. Table 121.1 shows the summary statistics of these factors as well as their correlations. Interestingly, alternative liquidity measures are not highly correlated. For instance, the correlation between PS and DSS is −0.14, and that between PS and BPST is 0.14. Therefore, which liquidity measure is more informative may be largely an empirical question, and warrants a comprehensive examination.
