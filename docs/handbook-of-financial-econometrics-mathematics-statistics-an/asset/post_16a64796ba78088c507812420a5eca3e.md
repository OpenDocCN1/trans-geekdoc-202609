### 97.4.4 The likelihood of dividend policy decision

In the previous section, we have confirmed that a firm’s payout ratio is negatively correlated to the covariance between its profitability and its growth rate. If a firm’s covariance increases, it may lower its dividend or even stop paying a dividend. Therefore, we can use a probit regression model to examine whether the interaction of profitability risk and growth risk can affect a firm’s dividend policy decision.

We extend our sample from only dividend paying firm-years to both dividend paying firms and non-dividend paying firm-years. We also include firm-years in which the firm initiates or stops dividend payouts in our sample. Five-year averages of variables are obtained in the same way described in Section 97.4.1. We then have a sample with total observations of 34,918 firm-years. Table 97.5 presents the results of the probit regression model for the dividend payout policy. In models (1) and (2), the dependent variable is 1 if the firm pays a dividend in a certain year and 0 if the firm does not pay a dividend in a certain year.

The significantly negative coefficients for the covariance between the rate of return on equity and the total asset growth (-0.1076 and -0.1304) confirm Hypothesis 3 that firms with a lower covariance term have higher probability to pay dividend. That is, the interaction between the profitability risk and the growth risk does affect a firm’s decision of paying or not paying a dividend.

Results from models (3)–(6), to some extent, can explain the disappearing dividend paying firms as shown in Table 97.2. We find the magnitude of the positive covariance term is increasing over time from 0.59 in 1969 to 1.03 in 2011. From the results of models (3) and (4), the higher covariance terms force a certain amount of dividend paying firms to either decrease or stop their dividend payouts. In contrast, from models (5) and (6), a decreasing covariance term of a non-dividend paying firm cannot make the firm decide to initiate paying dividend. Such an irreversible phenomenon may cause the fact of disappearing dividend paying firms over the last four decades.

## 97.5 Summary and Conclusion Remarks

Our concern in this chapter has been to sharpen the focus on corporate decisions concerning the optimal growth and the dividend payout policy. The interdependence of corporate decisions on these two important policy variables is explicitly recognized in deriving the simultaneous solution to the problem of maximizing the present value of the firm. We find that the optimal growth rate follows a mean-reverting process. In more detail, we examine the time path of the optimal growth rate, starting from the stochastic initial conditions, and approaching its steady state value. Important insights into the effects of the degree of market perfection, rate of return on equity, and initial growth rate on the optimal growth rate are also developed. Furthermore, we derive the optimal payout policy under most general conditions with regard to market imperfection, non-stationarity, and the stochasticity of the growth rate and the profitability rate. In particular, the error introduced in the optimal payout ratio by misspecification of the growth rate as deterministic is identified and shown to relate to its initial stochastic conditions. The model developed in this chapter brings out explicitly the influence of covariance between risks of non-stationary profitability and growth rate on the optimal dividend policy.

Empirical work on examining the mean-reverting process of the optimal growth rate and the importance of the interaction between the profitability risk and the growth risk are confirmed. The partial adjustment regressions show that, on average, firms will partially adjust their growth rate and take five years to make the growth rate reach the optimal target rate. The fixed-effects model with a structural change not only confirms the flexibility and the free cash flow hypotheses proposed by Lee et al. (2011), but also shows the covariance between the profitability and the growth rate is one of the determinants of dividend payout. In addition, the probit model shows that the interaction between the profitability risk and the growth rate can affect a firm’s decisions on not only stopping dividend payments but also in not initiating dividend payments. Such an irreversible effect along with the intertemporal behavior of the covariance term may shed some light on the puzzle of disappearing dividends over the last 40 years.

Important work, as mentioned earlier, in determining the optimal equilibrium values of corporate decision variables has been done by Gordon (1962), Lintner (1964), Modigliani and Miller (1961), Miller and Modigliani (1966), DeAngelo et al. (1996), Fama and French (2001), DeAngelo and DeAngelo (2006), and Lee et al. (2011). The need, however, for further research in tracing the optimal time path under uncertainty leading to these equilibrium values and the relevant stability conditions remains, and this chapter is a modest step forward in that direction.
