# 38.2 Literature Review

Many risk integration approaches have emerged to model the dependence structure between different types of risks to date (Böcker and Hillebrand, 2009; Kretzschmar et al., 2010; Grundke, 2010; Bellini, 2013). Among these approaches, simple summation approach, variance–covariance approach and copula approach are recognized as three major approaches (BCBS, 2010; Baker and Filbeck, 2015). With distinct advantages, these approaches model the risk dependencies in different ways. Table 38.1 summarizes these major risk integration approaches and their capabilities of capturing the typical features of risk dependence.

The simple summation approach appears the earliest and is a simple and highly tractable way of integrating different risk types (Li et al., 2015). This approach assumes that all risks are perfectly linearly correlated with each other and integrates the risks by simply adding them up. In other words, it considers that all inter-risk correlation coefficients are equal to one and each risk component receives equal weight. Therefore, it cannot capture nonlinear dependence, tail dependence and tail asymmetry. Moreover, because all correlation coefficients are equal, it also cannot capture structure asymmetry. Due to its oversimplified assumption, it performs the worst and has no ability to capture all the typical features, and so all symbols in the column of simple summation approach are “×”.

The variance–covariance approach takes it a step further by introducing linear correlation to consider the interactions between different risk types (BCBS, 2010). In this approach, linear correlation coefficients are calculated and used to integrate individual risk components into the total risk. In regard to modeling dependence, the linear correlation usually first comes to mind, and therefore, this approach has been considered the most basic and traditional risk integration approach, favored by over 75% of the banks surveyed by IFRI/CRO (2007). By allowing different correlation coefficients between different risk types, the variance–covariance approach can capture the asymmetries in the correlation structure, so the last row of this approach is symbol “√”. Nevertheless, it is apparent that linear correlation coefficients cannot capture nonlinear interactions between risks, let alone tail dependence and tail asymmetry. This significant deficiency restricts its further usage in many situations.
