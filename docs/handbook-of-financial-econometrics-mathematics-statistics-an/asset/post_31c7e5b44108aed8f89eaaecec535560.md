### Abstract

This chapter investigates the characteristics of a subset of the infinite number of Security Market Lines (SMLs) that ensure the market portfolio is mean–variance efficient both at a point in time and over time. The analysis employs raw rather than excess returns. With some specifications of the SML, the riskfree rate exceeds the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean, which is inconsistent with CAPM theory. At a point in time, a Hotelling’s T² test may reject most of the SMLs or none of them, although other mean–variance criteria may indicate some are economically reasonable and others are not.

### Keywords

Mean–variance capital asset pricing • Econometric and statistical methods.

### 47.1 Introduction

The debate as to whether the Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) mean–variance (MV) capital asset pricing model (CAPM) holds is both lengthy and contentious — with strong evidence both for and against the model. Tests of the model are legion. The clear majority concentrates on whether securities plot on the Security Market Line (SML) in mean–beta space. A minority concentrates on whether the market portfolio plots on the efficient frontier in mean–standard deviation space. However, I take a different approach focusing on the weights in the market portfolio. I examine characteristics of a subset of the SMLs that insure the changing-weights market portfolio is MV efficient both at a point in time and over time.¹ I extend the analysis in Best and Grauer (1985), who employed a reverse-engineering approach to show that there are an infinite number of SMLs from a two-parameter family that ensure the market portfolio is MV efficient at a point in time. I specify the two parameters — the intercepts and slopes of the SMLs — in two ways. First, like Best and Grauer, I hold either the market expected return, market risk premium, market mean excess return to variance ratio, or market mean excess return to standard deviation ratio constant over time. Second, I extend the analysis minimizing a variety of distances between the sample means and different sets of SML means at a point in time. The constrained regressions restrict the slope of an SML to be positive and the intercept to be greater than or equal to the riskfree rate as required by MV theory.

> ¹ Ferson et al. (1987) also developed tests of asset pricing models that allow expected risk premiums and market betas to change over time.

I employ raw rather than excess returns in the analysis. With some specifications of the SML, the riskfree rate exceeds the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean, which is inconsistent with CAPM theory. Finally, I extend the Best and Grauer analysis employing a Hotelling’s T² test to examine the statistical significance of the alphas at a point in time. For an SML, where intercept is the average risk-free rate and the slope is the average excess return on the market portfolio, the Hotelling’s T² test is more directly related to the efficient set mathematics than the Gibbons et al. (1989) (GRS, hereafter) test is. Some results provide strong support for this model. Others provide strong evidence against it. Moreover, the statistical results may or may not agree with other MV criteria.

The contradictory results (though perhaps disappointing) should not be surprising as they are consistent with strong evidence for and against the CAPM that has characterized the literature almost since its inception. Although the methods employed to study the model are overwhelmingly statistical in nature, the most fundamental doubts about it arise from logical rather than statistical considerations. Roll (1977) was the first to appreciate the importance of the statement: Securities plot on the SML if and only if the market portfolio is MV efficient. This theory asserts that a particular portfolio, the market portfolio, is MV efficient. Therefore, this theory is not testable (at least in terms of the SML) unless the market portfolio is observable and used in the tests. Even so, the model itself may preclude testing with ex post data. Rosenberg and Ohlson (1976) and Cheng and Grauer (1982) showed that if return distributions and the riskfree rate do not change over time, then at any date all risky securities realize the same rate of return. Moreover, Cheng and Grauer showed that if the riskless rate changes over time, securities realize different rates of return. But ex post returns are not drawn from ex ante return distributions. And, the lack of correspondence between ex post and ex ante returns cannot be corrected by assuming nonstationary return distributions.

The market portfolio is positively weighted — no securities have negative weights in the market portfolio. Best and Grauer (1992) showed that if there are any positively weighted MV efficient portfolios, they lie in a single segment of the minimum-variance frontier. Analytic and computational results indicate that the segment of the frontier containing positively weighted portfolios decreases as the number of assets in the universe increases.
