## Abstract

The purpose of this chapter is to critically evaluate the methods used to examine hedge fund performance, review and synthesize studies that attempt to explain the inconsistencies associated with the performance of hedge funds and to attempt to compare the returns of hedge funds against more liquid investments. In fact, research related to hedge fund performance seems to have been focused on whether hedge fund managers manipulate their performance and what investors should think about this performance manipulation; however, recent studies have questioned whether this perceived performance manipulation is manipulation per se or something else. In general, researchers have used a number of different techniques to attempt to model hedge fund performance and the relative opacity and latency that is evident in the reporting of hedge fund returns. Nevertheless, the very nature of the structure of a hedge fund makes it difficult to mark the returns to market on a frequent basis and even if managers wanted their performance marked to market, which would unveil their positioning through time, the relative illiquidity and stale pricing associated with some of the investments that are held by hedge funds make pricing the hedge fund a difficult and somewhat pointless exercise. To this end, studies that attempt to analyze and evaluate aggregate performance for hedge fund returns have focused on identifying the true determinants of hedge fund performance, attempted to account for and explain the relative staleness of pricing in hedge fund returns, and to relate the performance of hedge funds to more liquid and transparent investments. This chapter offers key suggestions for financial market participants such as hedge funds managers, portfolio managers, risk managers, regulatory bodies, financial analysts, and investors about their evaluation and interpretation of hedge fund performance. In addition, this critical review chapter can benefit investors, portfolio managers, and researchers in the establishment of a yardstick for the assessment of hedge fund performance and the performance of assets that have stale pricing and are relatively opaque.

### 91.1 Introduction

The purpose of this chapter is to critically evaluate the methods used to examine hedge fund performance, review and synthesize studies that attempt to explain the inconsistencies associated with the performance of hedge funds and to attempt to compare the returns of hedge funds against more liquid investments. With that stated, the reader should understand that the opaque nature of the hedge fund structure is what provides hedge fund managers with opportunities to outperform the market on a risk-adjusted basis; according to Agarwal et al. (2013), the fact that they are able to take on certain positions and not disclose their positioning provide hedge funds with an informational advantage which they show is valuable and can lead to outperformance. Similarly, Shi (2017) illustrated that disclosure of relatively illiquid holdings decreases the performance of hedge fund managers in general but causes a more significant decrease in the performance of hedge funds that have the larger proprietary costs associated with disclosing their positions. Moreover, Gao and Huang (2016) determined that information disclosed as a result to reduce hedge fund managers’ ability to profit off of political inside information reduced managers’ ability to generate alpha. Agarwal et al. (2013), Shi (2017), and Gao et al. (2016) all illustrate that the relative opacity of the hedge fund structure affords hedge fund managers certain benefits over more traditional asset classes and if this advantage is taken away, hedge funds will lose their ability to generate a differentiated return series. There are two main points that are important to consider when attempting to evaluate and model hedge fund performance as Baker et al. (2017) illustrate in their examination of the first four moments of hedge fund returns (i.e., mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis) incentive-related factors and the hedge fund’s specific strategy have the greatest impact on the distribution of hedge fund returns. Incentives drive behavior and given the relative opacity that comes along with the structure of hedge funds, it is understandable that some are skeptical about what drives hedge fund performance and whether the performance that is reported by hedge fund managers is an accurate reflection of the underlying fund.

Given the relative opacity of the hedge fund product, managers have an incentive to conceal positive and negative performance results (Bollen and Pool 2008, 2009), but Smith and Mumtaz (2017) illustrate that researchers and practitioners should not just assume that this perceived performance manipulation is meant to harm investors. It seems as though, based on the results presented in Smith et al. (2017), that managers are more likely to delay reporting positive performance results and more fully integrate negative performance results into the hedge fund’s return series. Cassar and Gerakos (2001) and Jorion and Schwarz (2014) both make reference to the fact that hedge fund managers have some discretion over how they report their results but conclude that it is difficult to control this smoothing and it is also challenging to determine if the smoothing is intentional. Cao et al. (2018) illustrate that hedge fund managers play a market role as arbitragers and invest in stocks that are relatively inefficiently priced, which illustrates why there may be some inconsistencies in pricing and performance. Aragon and Nanda (2017) provide some interesting insight into the question about whether performance smoothing is intentional by looking at the length of delay in the reported hedge fund performance, whether the actual performance is positive and negative, and this seems to be an interesting area of inquiry going forward. Lim et al. (2016) illustrate that hedge fund managers have significant incentives to manipulate their performance because on average they share in a substantial portion of the wealth increases of their investors. Patton et al. (2015) illustrate that over 50% of hedge funds in their sample restated performance and during the restatements, the performance that was initially reported typically presented an overly optimistic view of the hedge fund’s performance and indicated that after managers revise, they can predict with greater certainty which hedge funds will underperform based upon their previous revisions. Rudin (2018) argues that one of the determinants of hedge fund performance that is truly persistent is hedge fund managers’ fees in his study of equity long–short and macro/managed futures hedge funds.

This chapter moves from an exploration of performance smoothing and the relative opacity of hedge funds to examining how researchers and practitioners should model hedge fund performance. Lo (2010) provides one of the most comprehensive reviews on hedge fund performance, but Chen and Tindall (2014), Grummitt and Satchell (2011), O’Doherty et al. (2017), and Hasanhodzic and Lo (2007) focus specifically on attempts to replicate hedge fund returns using various cloning techniques. Stafylas et al. (2018) review the literature on hedge fund performance and the estimation of hedge fund performance and illustrate that Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Common Factor Analysis (CFA) are common techniques that researchers use to model hedge fund performance and that different factors should be considered when examining different return series; this understanding has led some researchers to pursue using nonlinear estimation techniques to help account for the optionality involved with hedge fund performance results. Agarwal et al. (2018) make an important point in their segmentation between traditional factor exposures and more exotic factor exposures and indicate that there is evidence that investors have a preference for exotic alpha when allocating to hedge funds but find no evidence that hedge fund managers can persistently generate exotic alpha; so, investors should not base their evaluations of hedge fund managers based on their ability to generate alpha from exotic risk exposure. Panopoulou and Vrontos (2015) attempt to model hedge fund performance using models to integrate discounting factor errors, cluster combining, and principal components combing and the kitchen sink, bootstrap aggregating, lasso, ridge, and elastic net specifications and find that a simple combination of forecasting techniques works better than more sophisticated models.
