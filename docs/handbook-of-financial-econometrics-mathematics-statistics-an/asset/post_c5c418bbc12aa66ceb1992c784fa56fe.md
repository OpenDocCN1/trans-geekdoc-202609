As inflation increases, a domestic currency value should depreciate and thus exporters benefit. Exporting economy should do well from an increase in inflation. When a domestic market is doing well, there is less benefit from diversification. Nonetheless, inflation has a negative impact on importing economy and purchasing power in that consumers have to pay more for the same amount of goods. The higher the inflation, the higher the diversification benefit in this aspect.

The second group is nominal sector growth represented by a natural logarithm difference of money supply. Applying the same concept of a change in price level, money supply can have a negative or positive impact on diversification benefit. The third group is real sector growth. That is, we further investigate how economic development, such as unemployment rate and growth in GDP, affect diversification benefits. Lower GDP growth and higher unemployment rate decrease local equity value and, thereby, increase diversification benefits. We also include secondary school enrollment, log life expectancy, and population growth. The economy benefits from an increase in these variables, thereby decreasing diversification benefits.

The fourth group captures risk premium and debt yield. To understand the impact of business cycle, we include default and maturity risk premiums, past local equity market returns, TED spread, T-bill yield, and T-bond yield. The default risk premium is calculated as the yield from BAA corporate bonds minus the yield to AAA corporate bonds and maturity risk premium is calculated as the yield difference between 30-year Treasury bonds and the 3-month Treasury bill. Higher default risk premium and maturity risk premium; higher diversification benefits. To proxy for momentum investing by international investors, we include past local equity market return defined as the lagged annual return of country level market portfolio and to capture global credit risk, we include TED spread, which is the difference in interest rate for the 90-day Eurodollar contracts and the 90-day T-bill secondary market contracts. An increase in local equity market return and global credit risk decrease diversification benefits. To capture debt yields, we include T-bond and T-bill yields. Although higher debt yields may imply good condition of a domestic economy and thus lower diversification benefits, higher benchmark interest rates also lead to a decrease in local security values.⁹

Next, we study the association with domestic financial market development. To measure domestic equity market development, we consider dividend yield, market turnover, price/earnings per share (PE) ratio, market capitalization per GDP, and number of listed stocks in national exchange(s). To capture the development of the banking sector, we include the amount of private credit per GDP. Higher domestic financial market quality implies lower diversification benefits. Further, we include the domestic economic conditions relative to international markets on the diversification benefits. The variables include exchange rate risk, correlation coefficient between a domestic market and other countries, integration measure R² from Pukthuanthong and Roll (2009), and relative Sharpe ratio (RSR) of the domestic portfolio compared to international markets. We construct a RSR of country X’s portfolio relative to the world market as follows:

RSR_Country X = [SR_Country X − SR_min)/(SR_Max − SR_Min)], (13.10)

where SR_Max and SR_Min represent the maximum and minimum of the national Sharpe ratios in the world market, respectively. As shown in Figure 13.1, the cross-country comparative gauge specifies the risk-adjusted performance of a domestic portfolio relative to the rest of the world and can vary over time. Exchange rate risk should increase diversification benefits whereas high RSR should decrease potential diversification benefits.
