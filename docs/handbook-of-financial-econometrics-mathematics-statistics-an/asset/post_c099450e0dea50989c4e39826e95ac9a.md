## 31.6 Conclusion

A selected group of analysts, often referred to as lead or star analysts, are highly regarded in the investment community and well-compensated in the profession. Release and update of opinions by these analysts can therefore
significantly move stock prices. Prior studies propose to identify these high-ranked analysts by the accuracy, timeliness, and consistency of their earnings forecasts. Our paper departs from the prior literature on top-ranked analyst identification by utilizing and uncovering information embedded in the network constructed by peer analysts with overlapping firm coverage.

We construct a network based on analyst coverage data by linking together analysts (nodes) who cover the same firm(s) and use structural hole to measure analyst’s ability to obtain information. Our study focuses on the fact that analysts do not work in isolation, but rather with peer analysts producing information and competing for clients. We hypothesize that analysts can produce higher quality equity research if they are better able to access peer analysts’ wealth of knowledge. When an analyst elects to cover a firm, she exchanges information with not only the firm’s manager in the process, but also can get access to the market information, industry trends and the method used to process information from peer analysts. These peer analysts can form a valuable network of knowledge and information that may improve an analyst’s performance and her ranking in the profession. In such a network, an analyst stands to benefit from non-redundant, more comprehensive information if she occupies the position of structural holes.

We report empirical evidence that high-ranked analysts identified by the network structural holes have more significant impact on stock prices. The high-ranked analysts identified in our study tend to issue more timely research opinions, but not necessarily more accurate or consistent earnings forecasts. Analysts occupying structural holes are more experienced, and impact stock prices more when they work for large brokerages. Finally, our results indicate that analysts who span more structural holes are more likely to have better career development by moving to jobs at larger brokerages.

Our paper adds to the literature by pointing to an alternative approach of ranking analysts based on analyst coverage data. Our results suggest that the information sharing among peer analysts is empirically significant, and that analysts could strategically choose certain firms to provide coverage for. The potential impact of information sharing in the analyst coverage network on firm performance and corporate policy awaits more research in the future.
