
For constant μ, σ > 0 and W is a standard Wiener process. Ilhan and Sircar (2006) adopted the same principle when markets are complete. Given that this chapter deals with incomplete markets, the Wiener process changes into

$$ dS_t = e^{\tau(\pm y_1 \pm y_2)} \left( \underbrace{\mu S_t dt}_{\text{part 1}} + \underbrace{\sigma S_t dW_t}_{\text{part 2}} \right), \quad S_0 = S, \tag{34.16} $$

where S is the spot price. And S0 = S implies that S can be at any point in time. Equation (34.16) is Wiener process when discrete jumps are taken into account. Options are forward looking and an option writer would want to hedge that risk premium in “future”. Therefore, this chapter incorporates a standard forward contract for hedging reasons. Similar phenomenon is adopted in Ilhan and Sircar (2006), and Carr and Wu (2014). Equation (34.16) has some traits of the JD model. A typical JD model is given by the formula:

$$ dS_t = \underbrace{\mu S_{t-} dt}_{\text{similar to part 1 of eq. (34.16)}} + \underbrace{\sigma S_{t-} dZ_t}_{\text{similar to part 2 of eq. (34.16)}} + S_t dJ_t, \; i \in \{1,2\} $$

where Z_t is the Brownian motion and J_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} Y_i is a compound Poisson process where the jump price sizes Y_i are independent and identically distributed with distribution F and the number of jumps N_t is a Poisson process with jump intensity λ. The difference between equation (34.16) and JD model is that S follows Brownian motion — any type of randomness while in JD model S follows geometric Brownian motion. That is, equation (34.16) is more encompassing in terms of movements that JD model. The J_t part JD model is compound parameter (which implies jumps double every time); however, not every jump doubles the preceding jumps. In certain cases, jumps might increase by decimal places (see Wurgler and Zhuravskaya, 2002). That is, the brilliance of equation (34.16) is that it can account for any stock jump of any form and nature, under any environment. The JD model is incapable of the latter phenomenon. On hedging, the strategy applicable to this study postulates a subset of hedging strategies that broadly appropriate for hedging any asset class under a Poisson process.

## 34.4.2 Synthetic hedging

In illustrating the effectiveness of equation (34.30), this chapter uses a synthetic example. Synthetic examples in pricing and hedging have been used before as shown by Merton (1976). And synthetic examples in certain cases have proven to be the best in illustrating applications on pricing and hedging of investment products. This chapter focuses only on options (both call and put) that are in-the-money as only traded options are hedged. Table 34.2 illustrates total hedge values when time to expiration approaches maturity and risk premium changes from positive to negative when unit risk premium is zero.

Although, the analysis in Table 34.2 is based on arbitrary numbers the results reveal interesting findings; (i) all hedges increase as the option approaches its maturity. This is probably because a combination of basis risk that rolled all the way to maturity and risk premium inherent in a jumpy exchange option. Interestingly, when the net risk premium is positive, the total hedge has the lowest amount and when the risk premium is negative, the total hedge is at its highest level. Thus, positive premium has minimization effect and negative premium has maximization effect. The latter statement is counterintuitive as one would have expected the opposite to happen. It seems more like “the winner curse” case. One of the possible reasons that explains the winner curse is that some of the positive premium goes to compensate the option investor. On the other hand, negative premium is advantageous in capturing reverse skew or volatility smirk (Pan, 2002). Secondly, very few investors invest when the risk premium is negative. It seems that negative risk premium is similar to options out-of-the-money in the sense that risk in options increase due to the fact that those options are rarely traded in practice.
