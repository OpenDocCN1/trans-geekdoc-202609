The ex post relationship examined in the present study is based on the sampling distributions of the estimators of performance measures and their risk proxies while Levy (1972) and Levhari and Levy (1977) investigate the relationship in terms of ex ante parameters without specifically considering the sample variation in the estimators. Moreover, their results determine the relationship between n-period Treynor (or Sharpe) measure and its one-period risk measure as the length of the investment horizon varies. In contrast, we examine the m-period correlation between the estimated m-period performance measure and its m-period estimated risk proxy. This m-period correlation can be explained by means of one-period market conditions (R_f and μ) and varying investment horizons. Thus, their studies and our analysis examine two different relationships.12 Nevertheless, the result of our analysis has strengthened the understanding of this important relationship in ex post terms.

In sum, a shorter observation horizon and a large sample size should be used in empirical research to test the relationship between estimated Treynor measure and estimated systematic risk.

## 68.3 The Empirical Relationship Between the Estimated Jensen’s Measure and its Estimated Risk Proxy

Following Heinen (1969) and the definitions defined in this paper, the covariance between the estimated Jensen’s measure and its estimated risk proxy can be defined as

Cov( α̂_i , β̂_i ) = − X̄_M σ²_i / Σ_{t=1}^n ( X_Mt − X̄_M )²,  (68.14)

X_Mt = R_Mt − R_f,   X̄_M = R̄_M − R_f.  (68.15)

where “*” indicates m-period parameters or estimates. The m-period Cov*(α̂_i, β̂_i) in (68.16) has an expression similar to Cov[(R̂*_i − R*_f)/β*, β̂*] in equation (68.7). Therefore, the analysis and conclusions for the relationships associated with the estimated Treynor’s and Sharpe’s measures can be generalized to include the association between the estimated Jensen’s measure and its estimated risk proxy.

If the estimated Jensen’s measure, α̂_i, and the estimated systematic risk, β̂_i, are significantly correlated, the estimate of the residual return (ε̂_it) used in testing the efficient market hypothesis will be biased. The residual return is estimated by

```
ε̂_it = (R_it − R_f) − α̂_i − β̂_i (R_Mt − R_f). (68.17)
```

Following the above conclusions, α̂_i is negatively correlated with β̂_i during the time period over which R_f is smaller than the expected market return (μ). Then, a negative correlation between α̂_i and β̂_i means that securities having low beta coefficients are favorably evaluated to have high non-market returns (or intercept terms), α̂_i. As a result, the use of equation (68.17) leads to underestimating the residual returns. This result can be easily shown as follows: let α̂'_i be the estimate of non-market return when β̂_i and α̂_i are negatively correlated, then α_i being favorably evaluated implies that α̂'_i is greater than the true estimate of α_i, say α̂*_i. One may then write

```
α̂'_i = α̂*_i + δ_i,   δ_i > 0. (68.18)
```

Using equations (68.17) and (68.18) leads to the equation

```
ε̂'_it = ε̂*_it − δ_i,   δ_i > 0, (68.19)
```

where ε̂'_it = (R_it − R_f) − α̂'_i − β̂_i(R_Mt − R_f) and ε̂*_it = (R_it − R_f) − α̂*_i − β̂_i(R_Mt − R_f). Equation (68.19) indicates that ε̂'_it is less than ε̂*_it. In other words, the residual return is underestimated if the non-market return is favorably assessed. This implies that the estimated average residual return and the estimated cumulative average residual return will be downward biased. On the other hand, high beta securities are unfavorably evaluated to have low non-market returns.
