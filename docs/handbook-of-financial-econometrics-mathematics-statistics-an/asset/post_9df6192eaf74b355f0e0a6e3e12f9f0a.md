(2015) provide a summary of estimates of the systemic impact of equity (and lagged equity), size spread (i.e., the difference in the returns of small-cap stocks and large-cap stocks), and a bond market factor and generate a good model that estimates hedge fund performance, in general, and that cautions that there may be interpretation issues pertaining to whether these factors actually caused the hedge fund’s performance or is just a good description of that performance, that they only provide an approximate fit of hedge fund returns, that returns are often non-stationary, meaning that the factors driving hedge fund returns will likely change over time, and that some of the factors that researchers use to approximate hedge fund returns are not investable.

Grummitt and Satchell (2011), in their efforts to replicate hedge fund performance, explain that their estimation of hedge fund returns does a good job of estimating hedge fund returns using an OLS regression. The particular method that Grummitt et al. (2011) used was based on Hasanhodzic and Lo (2007) in which the hedge fund return series was regressed on various systemic factors over the preceding 24 months to allow their model to adjust to changes in the weights to the holdings in the aggregate hedge fund managers’ portfolio. Grummitt et al. (2011) contend that at the extremes (i.e., the peaks and troughs), values seemed to be smoothed away in the hedge fund’s return series and reference Bollen and Pool (2009) and Agarwal et al. (2011) to explain the disconnect between the estimate and the actual return series. Di Cesare et al. (2014) illustrate that standard unadjusted risk measures may underestimate the true extent of individual and systemic risk factors that hedge fund managers take on.

Smith and Mumtaz (2017) use standard OLS and an in-sample estimation procedure to replicate hedge fund returns as illustrated in Figure 91.2. The returns, standard deviation, and return per unit of risk of the actual return series (i.e., average return: 0.72%; standard deviation: 2.13%; return per unit of risk: 33.69%) and the replication of that series (i.e., average return: 0.72%; standard deviation: 1.99%; return per unit of risk: 36.08%) were almost identical. For our purpose, which is to illustrate what systemic factors might be driving hedge fund performance, the Smith and Mumtaz (2017) replication does a relatively good job of estimating the hedge fund return series.

Bali, Brown, and Caglayan (2014) claim that macroeconomic risk factors also have a significant relationship with hedge fund returns and should be considered when analyzing hedge fund return performance. Although Bali et al. (2014) make a good argument, an attempt to illustrate the drivers of hedge fund performance, which may lead to efforts to model hedge fund performance, should begin with factors that are proxies for investable assets. This analysis is interesting because in Model I, without the use of the lagged variables, the explanatory power of the model is significant. In Model II, Smith et al. (2017) incorporate the lag of the Global Stock Market Index and in Model III, they incorporated the lag of the Hedge Fund return series. Finally, in Models IV–VI, Smith et al. (2017) use the backward elimination technique to reduce the model. In the final model, the explanatory power of the model is not reduced, it is actually maximized, and the number of variables used to explain hedge fund performance is minimized. Interestingly, none of the lagged variables was included in the final model as it seems as though other variables provide better descriptions of hedge fund performance when compared to the lagged variables. As outlined in Smith et al. (2017), a factor model of hedge fund performance is developed as follows:

$$R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,1} X_{1,t} + \cdots + \beta_{i,K} X_{K,t} + \epsilon_{i,t}, \quad (91.7)$$

where $R_{i,t}$ is the return for the individual hedge fund return series (i.e., $i$) at time $t$, $\alpha_i$ is the slope coefficient associated with the hedge fund return series, $\beta_{i,1}$ and $\beta_{i,K}$ signify the regression coefficient associated with factor 1 and factor $K$, respectively, and $X_{1,t}$ and $X_{K,t}$ are risk exposures 1 and $K$ that researchers believe have an influence over the hedge fund return series $i$. The formula in equation (91.7) is then modified to allow us to discuss the expectation associated with the model of hedge fund performance.

$$E[R_{i,t}] = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,1} E[X_{1,t}] + \cdots + \beta_{i,K} E[X_{K,t}] + \epsilon_{i,t}. \quad (91.8)$$

Finally, the full model of hedge fund performance is developed as follows:

$$E[R_{i,t}] = \alpha_i + \beta_1 R_{Micro} + \beta_2 R_{Global Stocks} + \beta_3 R_{NASDAQ} + \beta_4 R_{SmallCap} + \beta_5 R_{Gold} + \beta_6 R_{Global HY Bonds} + \beta_7 R_{CrudeOil} + \beta_8 R_{Commodities} + \beta_9 R_{GlobalCorpBonds} + \beta_{10} R_{USAggBond} + \beta_{11} R_{GlobalAggBond} + \epsilon_i, \quad (91.9)$$
