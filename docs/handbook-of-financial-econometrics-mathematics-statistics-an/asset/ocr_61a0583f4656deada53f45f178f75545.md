# 24.5.1 Equities and equity indices

The options with underlying assets in equity market are the most investigated and priced in both academic and industry. The option bounds in hence can be used to price the options which the underlying assets are traded in equity market. Chung et al. (1997) use option data with either stock price or index as underlying asset from 1987 to 1989 to test the law of one price proposed by Chen and Welch (1991) that options are redundant if they can be replicated by its underlying stock. According to Chung et al. (1997) major results, they found that the distributions of trading volumes for options on both stock and index are bound in a narrow range when underlying assets remain constant. The narrow width of option trade price bands not only confirms Chen and Welch (1991)’s single price law, but also support the senses of using option bounds to price option price without complex option models.

The option pricing bounds also can be transferred to the bounds of Black–Scholes implied volatilities to compare the implied volatilities of stock options and test market efficiency. Cochrane and Saa-Requejo (2000) use S&P 500 index options which expire in the end of June, September, and December, 1996 to compare its implied volatilities with the implied volatilities of their option bounds. The results show that Cochrane and Saa-Requejo’s bounds are only valid for at-the-money options. Masson and Perrakis (2000) use the implied volatilities of observed ask and bid call options on S&P 500 during 1993–1995 and obtain tight option bounds which only less 0.2% observations fail within these bounds. Constantindides, Jackwerth and Perrakis (2009) utilize implied volatility criteria based on Constantindides and Perrakis (2002) options bound model to test whether S&P 500 index options market is rational or not. It is the first large-scale S&P 500 empirical investigation of whether the observed cross sections of 1-month S&P 500 index option prices over 1986-2006 are consistent with various economic models that explicitly allow for a dynamically incomplete market and an imperfect market that recognizes trading costs and bid-ask spreads. They used both the unconditional distribution and the conditional distribution of the stock returns to avoid that the problem of mismatching S&P 500 index options in Black–Scholes–Merton (BSM) model is contributed to the assumption of lognormal distribution of the index returns. The unconditional distribution of the stock returns is based on the historical date; on the other hand, the conditional distribution of the stock returns can be calculated by three different ways: GARCH(1, 1) process, Black-Scholes implied volatility method, or Chicago Board Options Exchange Volatility Index(VIX) data.
