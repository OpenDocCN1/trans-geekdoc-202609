## 58.6 Conclusions

This chapter presents empirical evidence of the risk–return dynamics for a group of emerging Asian stock markets. This chapter differs from previous studies in three respects. First, we emphasize the role of downside risk in explaining the risk–return relation. Second, we explore the significant impact of long memory on the parametric relations between excess returns and downside risk. Third, we introduce the cross-market risk effect and cross-market leverage effect into the system.

Bivariate VAR models are used to capture feedbacks between stock returns and downside risk. The VAR processes combine the risk–return dynamics, the leverage effect, and cross-market spillover risk into a unified framework. The preliminary findings of the VAR estimations cannot produce a consistent sign on the relation between downside risk and excess returns, either from domestic market or cross-market risk. The main source of mixed signs on the risk coefficient is rooted in the regression estimation: the excess stock return series, the left-hand side variable, is stationary in short memory and it is inconsistent with the right-hand side variable, downside risk, since downside risk has an inherent long memory property. It appears that the presence of a long memory component in downside risk may distort its behavioral relationship with excess stock returns, as demonstrated by an extensive empirical literature on the uncertain risk–return relation.

After adjusting for the long memory component of downside risk by applying a fractional difference process, the evidence consistently supports a positive and statistically significant risk–return relation. This holds true for downside risk not only in domestic markets but also foreign markets, including the US and China. The evidence suggests that excess returns are required even if the risk originates from a foreign country because of the contagion effect in an integrated global market. The magnitude and level of significance are even stronger if the risk originates in a dominant country, such as the US or China, compared with risk from the domestic market. The evidence suggests that the leverage effect is not quite significant in process of testing the risk–return relation, in part because of the price limits imposed by regulators in most Asian markets.

Having taken an appropriate fractional difference on downside risk, we also investigate the risk–return relation using a FIEGARCH (the fractionally integrated exponential generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity model developed by Bollerslev and Mikkelsen, 1996) process.
