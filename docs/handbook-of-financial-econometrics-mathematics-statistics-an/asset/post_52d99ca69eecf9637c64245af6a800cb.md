## Abstract

Filtering methods such as the Kalman Filter (KF) and its extended algorithms have been widely used in estimating asset pricing models in many topics such as rational stock bubble, interest rate term structure and derivative pricing. The basic idea of filtering is to cast the discrete or continuous time series model of asset prices into a discrete state-space model where the state variables are the latent factors driving the system and the observable variables are usually asset prices. Based on a state-space model, we can choose a specific filtering method to compute its likelihood and estimate unknown parameters using maximum likelihood method. The classical KF can be used to estimate the linear state-space model with Gaussian measurement error. If the model becomes nonlinear, we can rely on Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) or Particle Filter (PF), for estimation. For a piecewise linear state-space model with regime switching, the Mixture Kalman Filter (MKF), which inherits merits of both KF and PF, can be employed. However, if the measurement error is non-Gaussian, only PF is the applicable method. For each filtering method, we review its algorithm, application scope, computational efficiency and asset pricing applications. This chapter provides a brief summary of applications of filtering methods in estimating asset pricing models.

### 64.1 Introduction

One important strand of asset pricing studies is to extract insightful information from prices of different asset classes such as stocks, bonds and derivatives. This information may be used to understand the fundamental situation of the macro economy and infer the beliefs of the market participants including the expectation for the future price movement of financial assets and the premium of different sources of risks. In addition, this information can sometimes be used to compute the fair prices of other related assets and find arbitrage investment opportunities. One important strategy to obtain such useful information is to design and estimate asset pricing models to fit the historical time series of asset prices. Given the estimated parameters, one can filter the unobservable information that people are interested in. In many cases, these pricing models can be expressed as state-space models, of which the observable variables are asset prices and the state variables are latent factors driving the price dynamics. As the main methodology for estimating state-space models, filtering methods such as Kalman filter (KF) (Kalman, 1960) and its different extended versions are widely used in asset pricing research. The basic idea of the filtering methods is to sequentially apply the Bayesian rule in the end of each period to infer the updated (posterior) distribution of state variables given their prior distribution implied by the transition equation, and the latest observable information along with its measurement equation. A side-product of this filtering process is the likelihood function of the model. Hence, given a set of unknown parameters, we can perform the above filtering procedure to compute the likelihood of models and then obtain the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) of unknown parameters by doing optimization. Finally, given the estimated parameters, we can get the filtered state variables, which are the updated mean of state variables, for further inference. No matter whether a model is structural (general equilibrium model stemming from economic theory) or non-structural (latent factor model based on naive econometric specification) and whether it is continuous or discrete, as long as it can be written as a state-space model (for continuous models, some discrete approximations such as Euler approximation can be performed), we can always use the filtering methods to estimate parameters.

Based on the type of state-space model, we can choose a specific filtering method to compute its likelihood and estimate parameters using maximum likelihood method. The classical KF can be used to estimate the linear state-space model with Gaussian measurement error. In this case, both transition equation and measurement equation are linear and the innovation of each equation follows a Gaussian distribution with zero mean. The KF can be quickly computed, however, the limitation that it can only be applied in linear systems hinders its application in many nonlinear problems.
