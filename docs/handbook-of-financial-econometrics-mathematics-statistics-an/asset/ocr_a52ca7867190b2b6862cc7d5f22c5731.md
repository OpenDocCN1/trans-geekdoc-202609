c_{t+1} - E_t c_{t+1} = r_{m,t+1} - E_t r_{m,t+1} + (1 - sigma)(E_{t+1} - E_t) sum_{i=1}^{infinity} rho^i r_{m,t+1+i}. (122.37)

Thus, the covariance of any asset return with consumption growth must satisfy

Cov(r_{i,t+1}, Delta c_{t+1}) = V_{ic} = V_{im} + (1 - sigma)V_{ih}. (122.38)

where V_{ih} denotes the covariance of asset return i with revisions in expected future returns on wealth:

V_{ih} equiv Cov( r_{i,t+1} - E_t r_{i,t+1}, (E_{t+1} - E_t) sum_{i=1}^{infinity} rho^i r_{m,t+1+i} ). (122.39)

The letter h here is used as a mnemonic for hedging demand (Merton, 1973), a term commonly used in the finance literature to describe the component of asset demand that is determined by investors’ responses to changing investment opportunities. Substituting equation (122.38) into equation (122.35) and using the definition theta = (1 - gamma)/[1 - (1/sigma)],

E_t r_{i,t+1} - r_{f,t+1} = - V_{ii}/2 + gamma V_{im} + (gamma - 1)V_{ih}. (122.40)

The only parameter of the utility function that enters equation (122.40) is the coefficient of relative risk aversion gamma. The elasticity of intertemporal substitution sigma does not appear once consumption has been substituted out of the model. Intuitively, this result comes from the fact that sigma plays two roles in the theory. A low value of sigma reduces anticipated fluctuations in consumption, but it also increases the risk premium required to compensate for any contribution to the fluctuations (equation (122.35)). These offsetting effects lead sigma to cancel out of the pricing equation (122.40).
