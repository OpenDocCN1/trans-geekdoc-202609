
Compared to the GARJI model, the VG NGARCH model is more informative parsimoniously. In addition, an *ex ante* probability for the occurrences of large price movements can be obtained. As the GARJI model can provide only an *ex post* filter for the probability of large price movements, one can expect the VG NGARCH model is more informative than the GARJI model.

The performance of the proposed VG NGARCH model with the benchmark GARJI model is compared using the equity returns of five financial companies in S&P 500. By introducing a random business time to the GARCH framework, an extra complication in model calibration and parameter estimation is added on. A Monte Carlo method, namely, the Monte Carlo EM algorithm, is used together with the Metropolis algorithm for parameter estimation in the VG NGARCH models. This method provides maximum likelihood estimates of the model parameters via the iterative sampling of the unknown random business times. The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows. Section 70.2 specifies the two GARCH models under consideration, namely, the VG NGARCH model and the GARJI model. Section 70.3 outlines the Monte Carlo EM algorithm and the Metropolis algorithm. Empirical analysis and the performance of the two types of GARCH models are then presented in Section 70.4. Finally, Section 70.5 presents conclusions.

## 70.2 GARCH-Jump Versus Variance-Gamma Model

The most significant influence on financial markets may be the news arrival (e.g., see Clark, 1973; Rose, 1989; Andersen, 1996; Jones *et al.*, 1994). According to Andersen (1996), two or more types of news arrival processes that have different implications for asset returns exist. In Maheu and McCurdy (2004), it is postulated that there are two types of news events, namely, normal and extreme news events. Usually, normal news events cause smooth changes in price movements; while extreme news events, such as earnings surprises, crashes, terrorist attacks, etc., cause infrequent, unanticipated and large price movements. To account for both types of price movements, a GARCH-jump model that combines a GARCH-type model with a Poisson jump process for the dynamics of log-returns was proposed (Jorion, 1988). More complicated GARCH-jump models consider jumps in both log-returns and volatilities (e.g., see Duffie *et al.*, 2000; Pan, 2002; Eraker *et al.*, 2003, 2004).

In the literature, smooth price movements are often described by a diffusion process (e.g., see Black and Scholes, 1973; Merton, 1973), while large price movements are described by a jump process, such as, a Poisson process (e.g., see Merton, 1976). Nevertheless, it is unrealistic to describe the asset price dynamics driven by a diffusion or jump-diffusion process with a continuous or piece-wise continuous sample path where the sum of absolute price movements during a finite time period is infinite. For this reason, a purely jump process in which the sum of absolute price movements during a finite time period is finite is considered in the literature.

### 70.2.1 GARCH-jump with autoregressive conditional jump intensity: GARJI model

Chan and Maheu (2002) proposed the GARCH-jump model with autoregressive conditional jump intensity (GARJI), in which the conditional variance of log-return innovations is divided into two separate components that are described by two conditionally independent autoregressive processes in a discrete-time setting. Let the trading period be partitioned into T subintervals (0, 1], (1, 2], ..., (T − 1, T], and ℱ_{t−1} be the information set at time t − 1. Consider the log-return Y_t = ln[S(t)/S(t − 1)], t = 1, ..., T, which is modeled as

Y_t = μ + ε_t. (70.1)

The innovation ε_t = ε_{1,t} + ε_{2,t} is decomposed to two independent components, ε_{1,t} and ε_{2,t} corresponding to the normal and the unusual price movements, respectively. Suppose the first innovation component is

ε_{1,t} | ℱ_{t−1} ~ N(0, σ_t), (70.2)

where the conditional variance σ_t^2 follows a GARCH process

σ_t^2 = a_0 + a (ε_{t−1} − c)^2 + b σ_{t−1}^2, (70.3)

where ε_{t−1} is the time t − 1 innovation. The parameterization in (70.3) is a simplification of that of Maheu (2002, 2004), which allows not only for the asymmetric responses to good versus bad news, but also to the ex post expected number of jumps during (t − 2, t − 1] given the information set ℱ_{t−1}. The second component ε_{2,t}, termed as the jump innovation, is the discrepancy between the total size of the n_t jumps and the expected total jump size θ λ_t during (t − 1, t]

ε_{2,t} = Σ_{j=1}^{n_t} U_{t,j} − θ λ_t,

where $U_{t,j}$ denotes the $j$th jump size and is assumed to be normally distributed with mean $\theta$ and standard deviation $\delta$. The number of jumps, $n_t$ is assumed to be Poisson distributed with an autoregressive conditional jump intensity (ARJI)

$\lambda_t = \lambda_0 + \rho\lambda_{t-1} + \gamma\xi_{t-1}, \quad (70.4)$

where the intensity residual $\xi_{t-1}$ is defined as the difference between the expected number of jumps given the log-return $Y_{t-1}$ and the previous intensity $\lambda_{t-1}$. The specification of the conditional intensity allows the probabilities of jumps to vary over time and cluster with a persistence parameter $0 < \rho < 1$. The conditional density of the log-return $Y_t$ given the information set $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ at time $t-1$ is

$f(Y_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \sum_{n_t=0}^{\infty} f(Y_t \mid n_t, \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) \frac{e^{-\lambda_t} \lambda_t^{n_t}}{n_t!}, \quad (70.5)$

where the conditional probability density $f(Y_t \mid n_t, \mathcal{F}_{t-1})$ is

$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma_t^2 + n_t\delta^2)}} \exp\left(-\frac{(Y_t - \mu + \theta\lambda_t - \theta n_t)^2}{2(\sigma_t^2 + n_t\delta^2)}\right).$

The GARJI model (70.1)-(70.5) allows for different components of the return innovation, $\varepsilon_{t-1}$ to affect future expected volatility differently. However, the cut-off point between normal and extreme price movements cannot be identified based on the observed log-returns. Thus, the number of jumps $n_t$ is unobservable and only an ex post probability distribution for $n_t$ given the information set $\mathcal{F}_t$ can be derived as follows:

$P(n_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t) = \frac{f(Y_t \mid n_t, \mathcal{F}_{t-1})}{f(Y_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})} \times \frac{e^{-\lambda_t}\lambda_t^{n_t}}{n_t!}. \quad (70.6)$

Differing from the GARJI model, the VG NGARCH model is constructed based on a purely jump VG process, therefore no cut-off point is required between normal and extreme price movements. Furthermore, an ex ante probability for large price movements can be derived. An introduction of a variance-gamma process is given in Section 70.2.2.

### 70.2.2 Variance-gamma process: Purely jump Levy process

Madan et al. (1990, 1991, 1998), Carr et al. (1998, 2002, 2003), and Geman et al. (2001) considered the use of a VG process, which is a purely jump Levy process that has independent increments over non-overlapping intervals.

More specifically, a VG process X is a Brownian motion evaluated at a random business time g = γ(t;ν,ξ), where {γ(t;ν,ξ) : t > 0} is a stochastic gamma process with shape parameter ν (mean rate) and the scale parameter ξ (variance rate), respectively. To control for the dimensionality of parameters, set the scale parameter ξ = 1. At time s, the variance-gamma process X is given by

X(s) = θΔg + σW(Δg), (70.7)

where W represents a standard Brownian motion, and θ and σ are the drift and volatility parameters, respectively, and Δg = γ(s;ν,ξ) − γ(0;ν,ξ) is the extent of random time-change during the interval (0, s]. Note Δg is gamma-distributed with shape and scale parameters νs and ξ = 1, respectively.

Note the Levy density for the gamma process γ(t;ν,ξ) is given by π_γ(x) = (ν²/x) exp(−νx), x > 0, where the Levy measure π_γ(x)dx gives the arrival rate for jumps of size x. As the Levy density π_γ(x) has infinite integral and approximates 0 as x → ∞, this implies that a gamma process, being a purely jump process, has an infinite arrival rate of jumps of sizes near zeros and declining arrival rate for jumps with increasing sizes. Note that as the shape parameter ν increases, the arrival rate of jumps of large sizes increases. Our proposed VG NGARCH model for an asset’s log-return will be based on the VG process in (70.7), which is given in Section 70.2.3.

### 70.2.3 Variance-gamma NGARCH model for log-return

Let the trading period be partitioned into T sub-intervals (0, 1], (1, 2], ..., (T − 1, T]. For t = 1, ..., T, we consider the dynamics of the log-return Y_t = ln[S(t)/S(t − 1)], where S(t) is the time t price of an asset, which is modeled as

Y_t = m + φ_t + θ g_t + ε_t, (70.8)

where m denotes the instantaneous mean return rate, g_t is the random business time-change during the interval (t − 1, t] modulated by a gamma process {γ(s;ν_t,κ): t − 1 < s ≤ t} with shape parameter ν_t and scale parameter κ = 1. The innovation ε_t corresponds to the term σW(Δg) in (70.7), thus is Gaussian distributed as

ε_t | ℱ_{t−1} ~ N(0, σ² g_t). (70.9)

Note the variance of the innovation ε_t depends on the random business time-change g_t, which is gamma-distributed with a time-varying shape parameter ν_t, namely.

g_t | ℱ_{t−1} ~ gamma(ν_t, κ = 1). (70.10)
