This simple example shows how to use the Black–Scholes model to price a call option under the assumptions of the model. The example is presented for illustrative purposes only, and it relies heavily on the implicit estimate of the variance, its constancy over time, and all the remaining assumptions of the model. The appropriateness of estimating the implicit instantaneous variance is ultimately an empirical question, as is the entire Black-Scholes pricing formula. Boyler and Ananthanarayanan (1977) studied the implications of using an estimate of the variance in option-valuation models and showed that this procedure produces biased option values. However, the magnitude of this bias is not large. Chiras and Manaster (1978) compared implied volatilities from option prices with volatilities estimated from historical data and found the latter much better forecasts.

One additional remark must be made. The closeness in this example of the calculated call option price to the actual call price is not necessarily evidence of the validity of the Black–Scholes model. Earlier extensive empirical work that took place to investigate how market prices of call options compare with price predicted by Black-Scholes, showed positive evidence of the model’s robustness in the market (see MacBeth and Merville (1979) and Bhattacharya (1980)). For example, Chiras and Manaster (1978) also studied whether a strategy of buying low-volatility options and sell high volatility options produced excess returns. Their evidence was supportive of the Black-Scholes model.

However, the market crash of 1987 revealed its limitations in predicting abnormal market conditions. Indeed, the appearance of phenomena like the pronounced “volatility smile”,³ or daily price jumps that defied the assumption of a log-normal distribution for the asset returns, called for alternative models that explain in a more parsimonious and general way all market prices.

The search for these new models moved in the direction of the local volatility models (see for example Derman-Kani and Zhou (1996)), asset jump models (see Merton (1992)) or stochastic volatility models (Dupire (1994), Heston (1991)).
