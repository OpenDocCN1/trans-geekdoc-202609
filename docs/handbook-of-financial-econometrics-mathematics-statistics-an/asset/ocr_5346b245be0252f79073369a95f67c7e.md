A network position with access to a set of diverse and rich information from peers will be helpful and instrumental for an analyst to make better and timely investment recommendations (Leone and Wu, 2007).¹ We follow Burt (2003) and use the measure of “structural hole” in measuring an analyst’s position to access complementary resources or information in the coverage network. As pointed out by Burt (2003), economic agents spanning structural holes in a network are in key positions of controlling flow of information and resources. Thus, if, as we hypothesize, there is an information sharing and learning among peer analysts, earnings forecasts released by those analysts occupying more structural holes should have greater impact on stock prices.

We report empirical results in line with our predictions. Analysts occupying more structural holes have more impact on stock prices when they release earnings forecast than other analysts. The impact is both economically and statistically significant. An increase in the structural hole (measured by network constraint) by one standard deviation will translate to an increase in the stock price reaction to an earnings forecast announcement by 21.6%. Further, we examine the relationship between the structural hole measure and the analyst performance measures of earnings forecast accuracy, timeliness, and consistency. While the structural hole measure has no obvious relation with forecast accuracy and consistency, it has a positive and significant correlation with forecast timeliness.

We proceed to examine some important attributes of high-ranked analysts by structure hole measure. Our results indicate that analysts who occupy more structural holes are more experienced. Moreover, analyst’s position in structural hole plays a more important role when analysts work at larger brokerages. As analysts working at small brokerages have to cover more firm out of necessity and have less supporting resources available, they will benefit less from their network positions. Ke and Yu (2006) report that corporate managers prefer to cooperate with analysts who work for larger brokerage firms, which make it much advantageous for those analysts working at large brokerages to access more information in the structural hole positions. Finally, our results indicate that analysts spanning more structural holes have better career outcome and are more likely to move to larger brokerages later in their career in the analyst profession.

¹ Alternatively, an analyst occupying the position of structure hole can simply benefit from the information she produces for her portfolio of covered firms. To account for such an effect, we control for the number of firms an analyst is providing coverage for in our regression analysis.
