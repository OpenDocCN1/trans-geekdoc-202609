It also better accounts for potential changes in the data generating process. Second, developments in high-frequency econometrics and greater data availability allow for the use of accurate non-option-derived measures of variance, which offer another test of model consistency. The obtained insights can be used to explain the relative performance of stochastic volatility models and their alternatives.

On the one hand, the mathematical formulation of stochastic volatility models strongly suggests that variance is a variable. On the other hand, the application of stochastic volatility models requires calibration to market prices of plain vanilla options, during which the variance is routinely included in the set of optimized parameters. It is imperative to know whether variance within the stochastic volatility framework represents true unobserved variance or a parameter of the model, potentially separated from the features of the underlying process. Implications of this distinction for the theory and practice of stochastic volatility models are substantial. If variance is a variable, one would favor models inspired by the historic evolution of variance. Alternatively, if variance is a parameter, a device to obtain flexible theoretical implied volatility surfaces, then there is no imperative to tie it in any way to the historic evolution of true variance. Thus, stochastic volatility models become effective, ‘as if’ models, akin to the local volatility surface models of Dupire (1994) and Derman and Kani (1994). In the latter case, the research effort should be focused on the calibration to market prices of options without regard to the historic evolution of the relevant statistical moments of underlying assets.

To establish whether the variance in stochastic volatility models represents a variable or a parameter, the chapter focuses on the prototypical Heston (1993) stochastic volatility model. The empirical study tests a series of hypotheses designed to distinguish between the two alternative views on the variance process. The methodology of the empirical study is as follows. First, the Heston model is calibrated to the market prices of option contracts on the S&P 500 index for a series of trading days, and the corresponding calibrated variance process emerges as a result of solving this optimization problem. Next, the features of calibrated variance are contrasted with those of the measures of variance obtained from high-frequency returns. The realized variation measure of Andersen et al. (2001) is the standard benchmark in the literature for assessing the variance of a process over a period of time. Therefore, we use it as the main econometric estimate of variance. Since the contribution of jumps to the stochastic process for the underlying asset may be significant (see Huang and Tauchen, 2005; Andersen et al., 2007, among others), we winsorize the sample. In addition, we include robustness checks based on bipower variation, a measure of continuous variation introduced in Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2004).

The main results of the paper indicate that the calibrated variance process lacks internal consistency. That is, it cannot be adequately described by the postulated stochastic differential equation of the calibrated stochastic volatility model. Moreover, there is strong evidence that the characteristics of the calibrated and statistically estimated measures of variance are substantially different in ways that cannot be attributed to probability measure changes. These results lead to the conclusion that variance in stochastic volatility models is an artificial device used to confer desired properties to the theoretical implied volatility surface generated by the model. This conclusion has implications for the gradient of innovations within the framework of stochastic volatility models.

## 33.2 Variance in the Stochastic Volatility Model

This paper uses the Heston (1993) stochastic volatility model, which is the de facto industry standard. Formulated under the risk neutral probability measure, Q, driven by calibration to market prices, the model comprises two stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

dS_t = r_{RN} S_t dt + sqrt(V_t) S_t dW_{s,t}^Q,

dV_t = λ(V_t - \bar{v}) dt + η sqrt(V_t) dW_{v,t}^Q,  (33.1)

dW_{s,t}^Q dW_{v,t}^Q = ρ dt.

The Heston model has two variables — the underlying asset value, S_t, and its variance, V_t. Each variable evolves in accordance with a stochastic differential equation, which is described by a series of parameters: r_{RN} for the evolution of S_t; λ, \bar{v}, η for the evolution of V_t; with ρ further linking the Brownian motions, W_{i,t}^Q (i = s,v), in the two SDEs. The popularity of the Heston model is in large part due to the computational ease of obtaining European call and put options as inverse transforms. To illustrate the transform pricing methodology, following Carr and Madan (1999) we reproduce

the price of a call option, $SV_{K,\tau}^{\text{call}}$, struck at $K$ and $\tau$ years to expiration:

$SV_{K,\tau}^{\text{call}} = \frac{\exp(-\alpha k)}{\pi} \int_{0}^{+\infty} e^{-i v k} \psi_{\tau}(v) \, dv,$ (33.2)

with $k = \log K$, $\alpha > 0$ chosen to ensure integrability and

$\psi_{\tau}(v) = \frac{e^{-r\tau} \phi_{\tau}(v - (\alpha + 1)i)}{\alpha^{2} + \alpha - v^{2} + (2\alpha + 1) v i},$

where $\phi_{\tau}(u) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{i u s} q_{\tau}(s) \, ds$ is the characteristic function for $\log S_t$ under the risk-neutral probability density $q_{\tau}$. While different from the original formulation in Heston (1993), the expression in (33.2) allows for a more stable and numerically accurate solution.

When the model is calibrated to market data, the distinction between variables and parameters may become somewhat blurred. The current value of the underlying asset, $S_t$, is observed, while its variance, $V_t$, is routinely obtained as a product of calibration. Calibration consists of minimizing a pricing error function:

$\min_{\Psi, V_t} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \text{err}\{P_{K_i,\tau_i} - SV_{K_i,\tau_i}(\Psi,S_t,V_t)\},$ (33.3)

where $\Psi = \{\lambda, \bar{v}, \eta, \rho\}$, N is the number of option contracts in the calibration dataset, $w_i > 0$ is the weight corresponding to option $i$, $\text{err}\{\}$ is an error function for an individual option, $P_{K_i,\tau_i}$ is the market price of option $i$, $SV_{K_i,\tau_i}(\Psi, S_t, V_t)$ is the theoretical price in accordance with the Heston model, $w_i$ is the weight assigned to the calibration error due to option $i$. Thus, in the process of calibration, the variance, $V_t$, receives the same treatment as a parameter. Denote by $\{\lambda^c, \bar{v}^c, \eta^c, \rho^c\}$ the parameter values that solve the calibration problem (33.3).

Consider calibration of the Heston model to market prices of option contracts on a series of trading days, $t = 0, \ldots, T$. The calibration optimization problem (33.3) is solved independently each day, and, thus, does not force the calibrated variance process, $\{V_t^{SV}\}_{t=0}^T$, to follow the Heston-implied dynamics of (33.1). The calibrated variance originates from the specification (33.1) under the risk-neutral probability measure; however, if variance is a variable, its observed dynamics must agree with the corresponding equation under the actual probability measure, $\mathbf{P}$. Naturally, the change of probability measure leads to implications for the dynamics of calibrated variance, $\{V_t^{SV}\}_{t=0}^T$, under the actual probability measure.
