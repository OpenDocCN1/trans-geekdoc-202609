An increase in NIEI suggests this increase in firm level revenues is more rapid than income expansion, and may indicate that revenue generated from loans have low margins associated with these products. A decline in NIEI can be explained by revenues of community banks increasing faster than their income in the period after DFA was enacted.

A statistically significant increase is observed in the ratio of non-interest expenses-to-income when the real estate loans-to-total loans ratio increases, and a statistically significant decrease is observed in the ration of non-interest expense to assets when the real estate loans-to-total loans ratio increases. In the model where NIEI is the dependent variable, an increase in the ratio of real estate loans-to-total loans increases the ratio of non-interest expenses to total income. When NIEA is the dependent variable, an increase in the real estate-to-total loan ratio decreases the non-interest expense ratio. Regardless of model, prior to the Dodd–Frank Act financial institutions with relatively large shares of real estate loans were more cost efficient than other firms, all else equal, but became less cost efficient after the passage of the Act. When NIEI is the dependent variable, this indicates an increase in the ratio of real estate loans-to-all loans tended to increase expenses faster than income. This suggests an inclination by lenders seeking to provide loans to local residents, thereby growing the bank’s assets, with regard to maintaining relationships.

Results also imply economies of scale for any size lender when increasing the level of assets within the institution is present. Deliberate attention to exploiting economies of scale may be a strategic objective since the NIEI ratio is elastic with respect to organizational assets. If, on the other hand, service provision is the objective, the inelasticity of the NIEA response to assets suggests little interest to expanding product offerings requiring additional compliance expense.

When the capital ratio coefficient is evaluated it appears to be positive and significant in the NIEA model and negative in the NIEI model. This indicates a combination of adequate capital is an important consideration when businesses begin to incur compliance expenses. Capital must be combined with financial institutions of a minimum size. Capital can withstand the increased compliance expenses but the institution must be large enough to afford the expense. Non-interest expenses, however, decline as capital increases. This suggests that cost efficiency improves, all else equal, when capital increases since institutions obtain this by reducing their compliance or other operations cost.
