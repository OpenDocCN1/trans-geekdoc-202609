## Appendix 38A: Individual Copulas

Assume $u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N$ follow univariate standard uniform distribution, and then, the five popular copulas mentioned in this chapter are introduced as follows.

- (1) The Gaussian copula is presented as

  $C_{\text{Gaussian}}(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N | R) = \Phi_R(\Phi^{-1}(u_1), \Phi^{-1}(u_2), \dots, \Phi^{-1}(u_N)), \quad (38A.1)$

  where $\Phi_R$ denotes the standard multivariate Gaussian distribution function with correlation coefficient matrix $R$ and $\Phi^{-1}$ denotes the inverse function of the standard univariate Gaussian distribution. The Gaussian copula has zero tail dependence and is generally viewed as a benchmark for comparison.

- (2) The $t$ copula is presented as

  $C_t(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N | \nu, R) = t_{\nu,R}(t_{\nu}^{-1}(u_1), t_{\nu}^{-1}(u_2), \dots, t_{\nu}^{-1}(u_N)), \quad (38A.2)$

  where $t_{\nu,R}$ denotes the $t$ distribution function with a degree of freedom $\nu$ and correlation coefficient matrix $R$, and $t_{\nu}^{-1}$ denotes the inverse function of $t$ distribution function. The $t$ copula exhibits the same dependence in both right and left tails. The larger is the degree of freedom, the weaker is the tail dependence for a $t$ copula. The Gaussian copula is, in fact, a limiting case of the $t$ copula as the degree of freedom approaches $\infty$.

- (3) The Gumbel copula is presented as

  $C_{\text{Gumbel}}(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N | \alpha) = \exp\left\{ -\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-\ln u_n)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right\}, \quad \alpha \in [1, \infty), \quad (38A.3)$

  where the larger $\alpha$ is, the heavier the dependence is. Specifically, $\alpha = 1$ implies the independence copula, and as $\alpha$ approaches $\infty$, we obtain the perfect dependence copula. The Gumbel copula exhibits greater dependence in the right tail than in the left.

- (4) The Clayton copula is presented as

  $C_{\text{Clayton}}(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N|\beta) = \left(\sum_{n=1}^{N} u_n^{-\beta} - N + 1\right)^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}, \quad \beta \in (0,\infty),$ (38A.4)

  where the larger $\beta$ is, the heavier the dependence is. Specifically, as $\beta$ approaches 0, we obtain the independence copula, whereas as $\beta$ approaches $\infty$, we obtain the perfect dependence copula. The Clayton copula exhibits greater dependence in the left tail than in the right.

- (5) The Frank copula is presented as

  $C_{\text{Frank}}(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N|\lambda) = -\frac{1}{\lambda} \ln\left(1 + \frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N} (e^{-\lambda u_n} - 1)}{(e^{-\lambda} - 1)^{N-1}}\right), \quad \lambda \neq 0,$ when $N \ge 3, \lambda \in (0,\infty),$ (38A.5)

  where the Frank copula displays perfect negative dependence for $\lambda$ equal to $-\infty$, perfect positive dependence for $\lambda$ equal to $\infty$, and independence for $\lambda$ equal to 0. The Frank copula can only capture symmetric dependence and is insensitive to either left or right tail dependence.

## Appendix 38B: Marginal Distributions

The three distributions fit for the marginal risks in this chapter are introduced as follows.

- (1) The Skewed Generalized Error Distribution (SGED) is presented as

  $f(x|\mu,\sigma,k,\lambda)=\frac{C}{\sigma}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{[1+\operatorname{sgn}(x-\mu+\delta\sigma)\lambda]^{k}\theta^{k}\sigma^{k}}|x-\mu+\delta\sigma|^{k}\right\},$ (38B.1)

  where $C = \frac{k}{2\theta}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{-1}, \quad \theta = \Gamma\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{k}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}} S(\lambda)^{-1}, \quad \delta = 2\lambda A S(\lambda)^{-1},$ $S(\lambda)=\sqrt{1+3\lambda^{2}-4A^{2}\lambda^{2}}, \quad A = \Gamma\left(\frac{2}{k}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{k}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},$ (38B.2)

  $\mu = E(x)$ and $\sigma$ are the expected value and standard deviation of the random variable $x$, respectively, sgn is the sign function, and Γ is Gamma function. The shape parameter $k$ governs the height and tails of the density function with constraint $k > 0$, while $\lambda$ is a skewness parameter of the density with constraint $-1 < \lambda < 1$. Particularly, SGED distribution turns out to be the standard normal distribution when $k = 2$ and $\lambda = 0$.

- (2) The student's t distribution is presented as

  $$f(x) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)} \left(1 + \frac{x^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}, \qquad (38B.3)$$

  where $\nu$ is degree of freedom and $\Gamma$ is the Gamma function. The student's t distribution is a common distribution in statistics whose shape is decided by parameter $\nu$.

- (3) Lognormal distribution is presented as

  $$f(x|\mu,\sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma x} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(\ln x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right\}, \; x > 0, \mu > 0, \sigma > 0, \qquad (38B.4)$$

  where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of logarithmic $x$, respectively. The lognormal distribution is one of the standard distributions used in insurance and operational risk to model larger claims and operational risk loss.
