more realistic — case of incomplete markets (see Pliska (1997) Britten-Jones and Neuberger (1996)) adds significantly to their complexity and thus obscure their intuitive content: instead of a single non-observable parameter — the volatility — on which the Black–Scholes model depends, these models require the estimation (“calibration”) of more parameters that relate to the price in usually untractable ways.

This fact alone, and considering the conceptual transparency and robustness of the Black–Scholes model, kept it to this day still very popular among practitioners in many option markets, especially in the more liquid ones that trade the simpler options in very large volumes. Traders that are aware of its limitations can be very often much more effective in dealing with their “out of model” unpredictable risks than the ones who can be lulled in false security behind a more sophisticated but much more obscure model. Taleb (1997) relates many cases that provide evidence of this fact.

Several textbooks such as Hull (2006), Chance (2006) and McDonald (2005) extend the discussion above of the Black–Scholes model and give numerous applications and examples.

# 27.7 Extending the Risk-Neutral Argument:
The Martingale Approach

Following the works of Harrison–Kreps (1979), Harrison–Pliska (1981) and Cox–Ross (1976), a more general approach in pricing derivatives was devised than setting up each time a riskless replicating portfolio which we described in Section 27.6. Although the mathematics for the development of the Martingale method demand some basic knowledge of abstract measure theory that is outside the scope of this chapter, in this section we will attempt to give a simple and intuitive explanation of this approach.

We have already mentioned the concept of a Martingale process. Let’s assume that at every time t, we can collect the entire “flow of information” about the asset in question, that is, everything that is known up to time t. This flow of information is formally symbolized by the so-called filtration {F_t}_{t≥0}. A stochastic process X(t) is called a Martingale if it satisfies the relation

E[X(t)|F_s] = X(s). (27.63)

In other words, a process is a Martingale, if after collecting all the available information up to time s, the best forecast of the value of the process at any future time is no other than its current value.
