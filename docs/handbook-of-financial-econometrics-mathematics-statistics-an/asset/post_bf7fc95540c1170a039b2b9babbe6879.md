
The observed time of a particular subject i and jth event is denoted by

$$Y_{ij} = \min(T_{ij}, C_i),$$

and the jth event indicator by

$$\delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } T_{ij} \le C_i, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Given a set of $T_{ij}$s, only the smallest one can be observed. Accordingly, the type-specific hazard function is $\lambda_{ij} = \lambda_i \delta_{ij}$. Because all event types are mutually exclusive, we have

$$\lambda_i(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \lambda_{ij}(t)$$

and

$$S_i(t) = \exp\left\{ -\int_0^t \lambda_i(u) du \right\}.$$

The covariate estimates can be incorporated into both AFT models and the Cox PH models. It is often convenient to assume the same hazard functions for each type of events, but it is not necessary. For example, for a specific event type j, its competing risk Cox PH model is

$$\lambda_{ij}(t) = \lambda_{0j}(t) \exp(\beta_j^T \mathbf{x}_i).$$

Under sub-hypotheses of $\beta_j = 0$ or $\beta_j = \beta_k, j \neq k$, the competing risk Cox PH models can be used to test the covariate effects on a specific event or the equivalence of the covariate effects among different event types.

Use the data for firms with persistent low stock prices for an example. Researchers might be interested in the risk a firm being delisted other than being merged. Mergers could be treated as a disturbance event, because once a firm has been merged, no delisting events could be observed. In order to conduct the competing risk analysis, we first decompose the indicator variable status into two separate indicator variables, delist and merge, as follows:

- *status* 1 if firm is delisted or merged, and 0 otherwise
- *delist* 1 if firm is delisted, and 0 otherwise

### 120.6.3 Bayesian estimation and testing

Bayesian analysis can be implemented in both parametric methods and semi-parametric methods for survival time data. In a Bayesian analysis, the parameters of the model are treated as random variables rather than as fixed characteristics of the population. ## Abstract

In this study, we aim to test the pricing power of market liquidity in the cross-section of US stock returns. We examine three liquidity measures: Pástor and Stambaugh (2003)'s liquidity factor, Bali et al. (2014)’s liquidity shocks, and Drechsler, Savov, and Schnabl (2017)’s money market liquidity premium. With a large set of test assets and the time-series regression approach of Fama and French (2015), we find that aggregate liquidity is not priced in the cross-sections of stock returns. That is, adding the liquidity factor to common asset-pricing models does not improve the performance of models significantly. Therefore, our results call for more research on the impact of aggregate liquidity on the stock market.

### 121.1 Introduction

Stock market liquidity has long been drawing attention from scholars as well as practitioners. Generally speaking, liquidity in financial markets indicates the ease with which one trades a security or an asset: how much trading cost is incurred? And how impactful is trading on price? Chordia, Roll, and Subrahmanyam (2000) find empirical evidence of commonality and time variation in their stock market liquidity measures. Studies (e.g., Levine and Zervos, 1998; Næs et al., 2011) have also found that stock market liquidity is a leading economic indicator and a significant predictor of economic growth. In an influential seminal work, Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) forcefully argue that aggregate liquidity in theory should be a priced state variable in the equity market.¹

If he holds assets with higher sensitivities to liquidity, then such liquidations are more likely to occur when liquidity is low, since drops in his overall wealth are then more likely to accompany drops in liquidity.
