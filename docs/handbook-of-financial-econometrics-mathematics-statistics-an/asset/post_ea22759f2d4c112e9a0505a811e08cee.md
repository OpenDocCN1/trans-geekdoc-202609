In addition, the empirical evidence from Conover *et al.* (2009) shows that among the precious metals, gold provides the greatest portfolio performance improvement of the US equity portfolios. As such, the role of gold in a diversified portfolio has been an important issue in financial literature.

Gold in asset management is currently very topical. Gold is commonly known as a safe haven commodity to avoid risk in financial market, especially to hedge the currency value in the short run. Reboredo (2013b) adopts copulas approach to examine the role of gold as a hedge or safe haven for the US dollar. However, from a long-term perspective, a fairly wide consensus exists that gold retains inflation hedging properties despite considerable fluctuations in the shorter term. Thus, gold functions as the risk management tool in hedging and diversifying asset portfolios (Sherman, 1986; Aggarwal and Soenen, 1988; Jaffe, 1989; Chua *et al.*, 1990; Dooley *et al.*, 1995; Johnson and Soenen, 1997; Capie *et al.*, 2005; Baur and Lucey, 2010). Obviously, the lack of correlation with other financial assets made gold an ideal tool for portfolio diversification. However, the financialization of gold and other commodities since the early 2000s has integrated the commodity markets with other financial markets, such as the bonds’ and stocks’ markets (Tang and Xiong, 2012). On the other side, the relative volatility of stocks and bonds has changed over time (Reilly, Wright, and Chan, 2000; Jones and Wilson, 2004). Therefore, it raises our interests to investigate how the changing nature of stocks’ and bonds’ volatility alters the role of gold in a diversified portfolio.

If the purpose of investment is to accumulate funds to finance future expenditures, investors then need to meet different investment planning horizons due to the different life cycle of investment goals. In literature, Marshall (1994) confirms that the length of the investors’ investment horizon reflects their willingness to bear risk, the investors’ perception of riskiness changes as their horizon grows shorter. In other words, as time passes and the investors’ investment horizon grows shorter, the investors will find it desirable to move from a more risky portfolio to a less risky one. In addition, Gunthorpe and Levy (1994) state that the difference in investment horizons mainly reflect the difference in investors’ transaction cost. Therefore, the role played by the investment horizon in optimal portfolio construction has been an important issue of portfolio management. Academic research has documented the influence of various investment horizons on portfolio compositions (Gunthorpe and Levy, 1994; Levy and Spector, 1996; Bennyhoff, 2009). Given that investors have different time horizons, information on the variation in the time domain is of questionable value. Because the optimal portfolio weights are determined by the means, variances, and covariances of assets returns, investors with different horizons should concern the different return characteristics in the return intervals. However, the return characteristics are varying with different time intervals (Chua et al., 1990; Gunthorpe and Levy, 1994; Jones and Wilson, 2004). Thus, investors with different investment horizon will have different optimal portfolio compositions.

As discussed above, the returns characteristics of financial assets are often not time-invariant, the characteristics follow different patterns at various investment horizons. Empirically, researchers could apply rolling-window method or multivariate GARCH family models to capture the time-varying relation structures at a predetermined frequency (Jones and Wilson, 2004; Chiang, Li, and Yang, 2015). The short-run and long-run interdependences can be investigated by using data with different frequencies. In this setting, however, the number of observations used to calculate the optimal weights will vary with specified frequency. Moreover, no matter whether low frequency or high frequency data is used, the realizations of observation at a predetermined frequency simultaneously contain both long-term and short-term information. In a departure from traditional approaches using data with different frequencies (Chua et al., 1990; Gunthorpe and Levy, 1994), wavelet analysis decomposes data into different time scale components to capture different cyclical features of the data in this study. From this perspective, wavelet analysis can provide the investors necessary data to investigate the long term and short term dynamics simultaneously, and help to deepen the understanding of the true nature of these dynamics across different frequencies. By this setting, this study helps us in identifying the portfolio diversification opportunities for different type of investors with different investment horizons.

As a whole, the purpose of this chapter is to explore the role of gold in a diversified portfolio comprised of stocks and bonds. Moreover, we further apply wavelet analysis to investigate portfolio allocation over different time-frequency scales. That is, this chapter examines how the investment horizon affects portfolio allocation by comparing the vector of optimal portfolio weights at different time-frequency scales. The results therefore should be of interest to both individual investors with either long or short term investment horizons, and to long-term investors such as pension plans and insurance companies.
