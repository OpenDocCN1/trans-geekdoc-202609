Chinn and Coibion (2014) examine whether futures prices are unbiased and/or accurate predictors of subsequent prices, by analyzing four commodities including gold. The results show that gold is a poor predictor of subsequent prices changes.

In practice, such deviations may be due to asymmetric information as the price may reflect the information earlier in the futures market than the spot market. Evidence from hedging practices shows that firms would hedge only when an expected unfavorable event may occur. In markets with severe information asymmetry in which hedgers are having superior knowledge on supply and demand, such as futures prices, traders would selectively hedge price movements, causing sharp price adjustments upon resolution of information asymmetry (Knill, et al., 2006).

Prior literature related to the gold spot and futures prices are analyzed in fewer studies. Ruan et al. (2016) employ the detrended cross-correlation analysis (DCCA) to examine the correlations between gold spot and futures returns and find that the cross-correlations are significant for all lagged orders and are weakly persistent. Other extant literature mainly focuses on the forecast of gold prices, including the reports of Koutsoyiannis (1983), Baker and Van Tassel (1985), and Pindyck (1993). Other research examining gold related issues is tremendous, including studies on both the linear and nonlinear relationships of gold and oil prices, exchange rates, or the macroeconomic effects (Sherman 1982, 1983; Ariovich, 1983; Fortune, 1987; Dooley et al., 2004; Wang and Lee, 2011; Lee and Zeng, 2011). However, few of them provide a more complete view of possible causal relationships once variables are outside the mean, non-normally distributed or have extreme values. Similarly, Michaud et al. (2006) examined the correlations between gold and other major commodities and reported the low correlations between gold and other commodities. Worthington and Pahlavani (2007) employ a co-integration approach and confirm the effectiveness of gold as an inflation hedge in the short and long run. Wang et al. (2011) apply the threshold models to examine similar issues and conclude that gold acts as an effective inflation hedge instrument as well.

Most recent, Adrangi et al. (2000) employed the quantile co-integration to estimate the relationship of gold and silver, and suggested that silver prices respond more strongly to gold prices if silver prices are high.
