When a company repurchases its stock, there is a fixed cost, η, involved. Also, the company generally repurchases its stock at a premium, ω, to the market value of the equity repurchased. Thus, the repurchase cost of equity is

# (3) The Equity and Interest Costs.

Let $s_i$ be the ($/$) cost of equity for the firm for the time interval $i$. Then the total equity cost for time interval $i$ will be $I_i$, where

$I_i = \sum_{k=0}^{i} (Y_{1,k} + Y_{3,k}) s_i,$ where $i = 1, \dots, N.$ (49.8)

A coupon rate $c_j$ has to be paid on the $j$th bond issue if it is outstanding at the time interval $i$. Thus the total interest cost on long-term bonds payable at the time interval $i$, $C_i$, becomes

$C_i = \sum_{j=1}^{N'} c_j X_{j,i}.$ (49.9)

If any bond $j$ is called in the interval $i$, then the corresponding $X_{j,i}$ becomes zero and no more interest is payable on this bond. The call premium $H_i$ becomes payable in the time interval as the $j$th bond is called. Thus,

$H_i = \sum_{j=1}^{N'} (X_{j,i-1}) e_{j,i}$ for all $X_{j,i}=0$ and $m_{j,i} \neq 0,$ (49.10)

where $e_{j,i}$ is the ($/bond) call premium rate for the $j$th bond issue. The call premium $e_{j,i}$ is known to be a declining function of the time to maturity and the coupon rate of the bond (Weingartner, 1967). A precise analytical expression for $e_{j,i}$ is needed, but for the purposes of this model a call premium schedule over time would suffice.
