
$\hat{\beta}_j''$ and $\hat{\alpha}_j''$ are the sample estimates of $\beta_j''$ and $\alpha_j''$ by employing the observable values of $R_{jt}$, $R_{Tt}$ and $R'_{mt}$.[^5] Note that the sign of $\psi_j$ depends again on whether $\beta_j''$ is greater or less than unity.

## 62.3 Derivation of Plim b̂ Assuming $R_m$ and $R_b$ are Measured with Error

Friend and Blume (1970) have regressed the estimate of Jensen’s measure of performance $\hat{\alpha}_j''$ on the estimate of systematic risk $\hat{\beta}_j''$ to test whether the one-factor CAPM is a useful model. If the measurement errors of $R_{mt}$ and $R_{bt}$ do exist as we have postulated, FB’s regression can be regarded as having the following form:

$$
\hat{\alpha}_j'' = a + b \hat{\beta}_j'' + V_j, \tag{62.15}
$$

where $V_j \sim N(0, \sigma_V^2)$. After considerable arrangement, and letting the true value of $b$ equal zero, it can be shown that[^6]

$$
\operatorname{plim}_{j\to\infty} \hat{b} = \frac{-B + \tau + \frac{\theta k}{1-\theta} - \frac{\sigma_s^2(B+k-\tau)}{\sigma_{\beta_j}^2(1-\theta)^2}}{1 + \frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_{\beta_j}^2(1-\theta)^2}}, \tag{62.16}
$$

where $k = \bar{R}_m - \bar{R}_b$, $\sigma_{\beta_j}^2 = \text{variance of } \beta_j$, $\sigma_s^2 = \text{variance of } S_j$, and $\theta = (\sigma_e^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)/(\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_e^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)$.

If there are no measurement errors on both $R_m$ and $R_b$, then (62.16) reduced to

$$
\operatorname{plim}_{j\to\infty} b = -\frac{\sigma_s^2 k}{\sigma_{\beta_j}^2 + \sigma_s^2}. \tag{62.17}
$$

Equation (62.17) was actually used by FB to explain the fact that the effect of sampling error on $\beta_j$ cannot cause $\hat{b}$ to be negative. However, when only constant measurement errors in both $R_m$ and $R_b$ are also considered, (62.16) is reduced to

$$
\operatorname{plim}_{j\to\infty} \hat{b} = \frac{-B + \tau - \left[\sigma_s^2 (B + k - \tau)/\sigma_{\beta_j}^2\right]}{1 + \sigma_s^2/\sigma_{\beta_j}^2}. \tag{62.18}
$$

Equation (62.18) can be used to explain FB's principal finding that $b < 0$ since (62.18) can be rewritten as

$$
\operatorname{plim}_{j\to\infty} \hat{b} = -\frac{\sigma_s^2 k}{\sigma_{\beta_j}^2 + \sigma_s^2} - B + \tau. \tag{62.19}
$$

where the first term of equation (62.19) represents the bias caused by the sample variation of systematic risk in the first-round regression, $-B$ represents the bias caused by the difference between the borrowing and lending rate, and $\tau$ represents the bias caused by the error in the measurement of $R_m$. Hence, if $-B + \tau$ is significantly negative (positive), $\hat{b}$ asymptotically will also be negative (positive) when the bias caused by the sampling variation in $\beta_j$ in the first-round regression is very small. Since $-B = R_f - R_b < 0$, FB's specification that the reason $\hat{b} < 0$ is due to the divergence of borrowing and lending rate appears as a reasonable proposition.

Finally, when the sampling period is divided into two subperiods, FB found that $\hat{b}$ for 1960–1964 is negative while $\hat{b}$ for 1965–1968 is positive. FB attributed the latter phenomenon to “unanticipated appreciation” in the market return, though FB did not define the term rigorously. If, following many economists, FB defined unanticipated appreciation as the difference between the realized return to the market and its expected value for the period, the latter cannot cause any bias in $b$ since the one-factor model takes into account the stochasticity in $R'_m$. However, from our equation (62.19), $b$ can be positive (negative) if $-B + \tau$ is positive (negative). Further, if the return to the segment of capital market not representing stocks listed in the New York Stock Exchange has a $\beta$ greater than unity, $\tau$ for a subperiod can be positive (negative) when the realized $R_m$ is positive (negative). Hence, even if $-B < 0$, $b$ for the prosperity period can be positive while $b$ for the recession period will remain negative.

## 62.4 An Alternative Hypothesis Testing Procedure for the CAPM

BJS and MS have also used an alternative hypothesis testing procedure. After the measurement errors are considered, the null used to test the predictive ability of the systematic risk is

$$
H_0: c = \tau - B, \quad d = \bar{R}_m - \bar{R}_b = (\bar{R}'_m - \bar{R}_T) + (\tau - B), \tag{62.20}
$$

where $c$ and $d$ are the intercept and the slope of a linear relationship

$$
\bar{R}_j = c + d \hat{\beta}_j. \tag{62.21}
$$

The effects of $\psi_j$ and $s_j$ on the estimates of both $c$ and $d$ can be analyzed using (62.13a). If we assume that $\psi_j$ is normally distributed with mean $(1 - \bar{\beta})(\sigma_e^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)/(\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_e^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)$ and finite variance $\sigma_{\beta_j}^2 \left[\sigma_e^2 + \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_e^2 + \sigma_\eta^2}\right]^2$, then the probability limit of $\hat{c}$ and $\hat{d}$ is

$$
\operatorname{plim}_{j\to\infty} \hat{d} = \frac{d\left(\frac{\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_e^2 + \sigma_\eta^2}{\sigma_M^2}\right)}{1 + \sigma_s^2/\sigma_{\beta_j}^2}, \tag{62.22a}
$$

$$
\operatorname{plim}_{j\to\infty} \hat{c} = -B + \frac{d(1-\bar{\beta}_j)(\sigma_e^2+\sigma_\eta^2)}{\sigma_M^2+\sigma_e^2+\sigma_\eta^2} + \lambda \bar{\beta}_j + \frac{\lambda(1-\bar{\beta}_j)(\sigma_e^2+\sigma_\eta^2)}{\sigma_M^2+\sigma_e^2+\sigma_\eta^2}, \tag{62.22b}
$$

where $\lambda$ is the asymptotic bias of $\hat{d}$, i.e., $\operatorname{plim}_{j\to\infty} \hat{d} - d$.

Comparing (62.16) with (62.22a), it appears that regressing $\alpha$ on $\beta$ will produce a higher bias on estimated parameters than the method described in this section. The reason is that the estimate of $b$ is also affected by constant measurement errors of $R_b$ and $R_m$ in addition to the sample variation of $\beta_j$ and the random measurement error of $R_b$ and $R_m$. However, both $\bar{R}_m$ and $\bar{R}_b$ are not observable and $(\bar{R}'_m - \bar{R}_T)$ is employed to substitute for $(\bar{R}_m - \bar{R}_b)$ in the null hypothesis. Therefore, the hypothesis to be tested is not precisely stated. Hence, even if the bias in the estimated parameters is smaller, it does not mean that the test is statistically more powerful. Incidentally, (62.22a) is the general case of the result derived by Black, Jensen and Scholes (1972) who considered only the effect of sampling variation.

The relationship between $b$ and $d$, and the relative magnitude between the coefficient of determination for (62.15) and (62.21) will be investigated as follows:

- (i) From the definition of the regression slope, we have

$$
\hat{b}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\alpha}_j,\hat{\beta}_j)}{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)}
=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\{(\bar{R}_j-\bar{R}_T)-\hat{\beta}_j(\bar{R}'_m-\bar{R}_T),\hat{\beta}_j\}}{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)}
=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}[(\bar{R}_j-\bar{R}_T),\hat{\beta}_j]}{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)}-\frac{(\bar{R}'_m-\bar{R}_T)\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)}{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)}
=\hat{d}-(\bar{R}'_m-\bar{R}_T). \tag{62.23}
$$

- (ii) The coefficient of determination for (62.15) can be derived as[^8]:

$$
R_1=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)-k\operatorname{Var}(y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(y)\left(\operatorname{Var}(x)+k^2\operatorname{Var}(y)-2k\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)\right)}}
=\left[\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(y)\operatorname{Var}(x)}}-k\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}}\right]
\left[\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(x)}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(x)+k^2\operatorname{Var}(y)-2k\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}}\right], \tag{62.24}
$$

where $x=\bar{R}_j-\bar{R}_T$, $k=\bar{R}'_m-\bar{R}_T$, $y=\hat{\beta}_j$.

Similarly, the coefficient of determination for (62.21) can be defined as

$$
R_2=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(y)\operatorname{Var}(x)}}. \tag{62.25}
$$

By comparing $R_1$ and $R_2$, it is found that the coefficient of determination of (62.15) is determined by three different components, i.e., (1) the coefficient of determination (62.21), (2) $k\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(y)/\operatorname{Var}(x)}$, and (3) $\left[\frac{\operatorname{Var}(x)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)+k^2\operatorname{Var}(y)-2k\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}\right]^{1/2}$. If $\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)$ approaches zero, then $R_1^2$ will approach $\frac{k^2\operatorname{Var}(y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)+k^2\operatorname{Var}(y)}$. Therefore, the magnitude of $R_1^2$ is generally different from that of $R_2^2$.

Jensen (1968) has employed the sample distribution of $\hat{\alpha}'_j$ ($j=1,\dots,m$) to show the usefulness of Jensen performance measures. Black, Jensen and Scholes employed the similar concept to do the time series test of CAPM. In addition, they claimed that the time series test is free from the measurement error of the systematic risk. From (62.13b), however, we have demonstrated that the sample distribution of $\hat{\alpha}_j''$ is affected by: (1) the sample variation of $\beta_j$, and (2) the measurement error of both $R_b$ and $R_m$.[^10]

## 62.5 Conclusion

To sum up, our analysis indicated that both the cross-sectional tests and the time series test of the CAPM are affected by the measurement error of $R_b$ and $R_m$ and the sample variation of the systematic risk. Hence, interpretation of the empirical results of CAPM should be done with extreme care. Further, the use of the ordinary least squares method should also be reconsidered and the error-in-variable approach used wherever possible.

As to areas for further research, it is clear that measurement error will also affect estimates derived from the two-factor models since the one-factor errors-in-variable model presented above is conceptually quite similar to the two-factor model.[^11] Finally, there are problems associated with nonstationarity of $\beta$'s which may mean that the current CAPM is not correctly specified in the first place (see Galai and Masulis (1976)).
