
Out-of-the-money (OTM) if $S/K \leq 0.97$; and in-the-money (ITM) if $S/K \geq 1.03$. A finer partition resulted in six moneyness categories. By the term to expiration, an option contract can be classified as (i) short-term (<60 days); (ii) medium-term (60–180 days); and (iii) long-term (>180 days). The sample properties of the S&P 500 call prices are reported in the paper of Bakshi *et al.* (1997) and not repeated here.

## §1.4.1.2 *Option pricing models*

We follow the framework of Bakshi *et al.* (1997) and conduct the empirical tests in the CEV model. The testing results will then be compared with those of Bakshi *et al.* (1997): (i) the Black–Scholes (BS) model, (ii) the square root stochastic-volatility (SV) model, (iii) the stochastic-volatility and stochastic-interest-rate (SVSI) model, and (iv) the stochastic-volatility random-jump (SVJ) model. The empirical results will focus on the CEV and four models as described above.

## §1.4.1.3 *Structural parameter estimation and in-sample performance*

### §1.4.1.3.1 Estimation procedure

**Step 1.** Collect $N$ option prices on the same stock and taken from the same point in time (or same day), for any $N$ greater than or equal to one plus the number of parameters to be estimated. For each $n = 1, \ldots, N$, let $\tau_n$ and $K_n$ be respectively the time-to-expiration and the strike price of the $n$-th option. Let $\hat{C}_n(t, \tau_n, K_n)$ be its observed price, and $C_n(t, \tau_n, K_n)$ its model price as determined by, for example, (equation (51.9)) with $S(t)$ and $R(t)$ taken from the market. The difference between $\hat{C}_n$ and $C_n$ is a function of the values taken by $V(t)$ and by $\Phi = \{k_R, \theta_R, \sigma_R, \kappa_V, \theta_V, \sigma_V, \lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J\}$. For each $n$, define

$$\varepsilon_n[V(t),\Phi] \equiv \hat{C}_n(t,\tau_n,K_n)-C_n(t,\tau_n,K_n). \tag{51.16}$$

**Step 2.** Find $V(t)$ and parameter vector $\Phi$, to solve

$$\operatorname{SSE}(t) \equiv \min_{V(t),\Phi} \sum_{n=1}^{N} [\varepsilon_n[V(t),\Phi]]^2. \tag{51.17}$$

This step results in an estimate of the implied spot variance and the structural parameter values, for date $t$. Go back to Step 1 until the two steps have been repeated for each day in the sample.

## §1.4.1.4 Assessment of the relative model misspecification

As Rubinstein (1985) had done, the most popular diagnostic of relative model misspecification is to compare the implied-volatility patterns of each model across both moneyness and maturity.^12^ The procedure is as follows: First, substitute the spot index and interest rates of date $t$ as well as the structural parameter values implied by all date ($t$ − 1) option prices, into the option pricing formula, which leaves only the spot volatility undetermined. Next, for each given call option of date $t$, find a spot volatility value that equates the model-determined price with the observed price of the call. Then, after repeating these steps for all options in the sample, obtain for each moneyness-maturity category an average implied-volatility value.

Using the subsample data from July 1990 to December 1990 as in Bakshi *et al.* (1997), the average implied volatilities of the CEV model are computed in Table 51.2. In Figure 51.1, the implied volatility graph is presented. We then compare it with the results by Bakshi *et al.* (1997).^13^ For short-term calls, the CEV model still shows large U-shaped moneyness-related biases. However, the magnitude of the biases, 6.5%, is only slightly larger than that of the SV model, around 6%. For medium-term and long-term calls, the moneyness-related smiles of implied volatility are greatly reduced, and the corresponding magnitudes are only 1.68% and 1.36%, respectively. We can also find that the implied volatility of the CEV model in long-term options (maturity ≥180 days) is the most stable case compared with other maturity-based options. For those options with longer than 180 days to expiration, the implied volatility of the CEV model is more stable than all of the other models including the SVJ model.

In sum, the CEV model is still subject to the model misspecification problems as all of the option pricing models tested in Bakshi *et al.* (1997).
