## 76.3 Sample Selection

The sample of M&As is collected from the SDC database. While the SDC database is widely used to collect information about mergers and acquisitions, one of the advantages to this study is that the SDC database provides the information to identify the nation of the financial advisor. The term “the parent nation of financial advisors” is used to determine the nation of financial advisors. This allows the current study to classify whether financial advisors are domestic or foreign firms. This study focuses on the Australian market and the investigation period covers 1995 to 2011. To be included in the final sample, the sample is required to meet the following criteria. The sample includes the deals classified as merger, acquisition, acquisition of majority interest or exchange offer. Either the target or bidder is listed on the stock exchange. It is expected to obtain unmatched sample for targets and bidders. In addition, the transactions are complete and bidders own more than 50% of target shares after the transactions in order to focus on the change of control.

Share price and financial data are collected from the Datastream database. If the share price is missing, the deal will be removed from the sample. Financial characteristics are collected from the calendar year end prior to the announcement date. Imposing these criteria, the final sample includes 333 targets and 949 bidders.

### 76.3.1 Determination of different types of financial advisors

This study investigates whether firms hired by more reputable financial advisors can create higher value to those with low-reputable financial advisors. More importantly, the current study examines the performance of domestic and foreign advisors in M&As. While focusing on the Australian market, domestic (financial) advisors are defined as those whose parent nation is Australia. Otherwise, financial advisors with parent nations outside Australia are classified as foreign advisors.¹

To determine the quality of financial advisors, prior studies used league tables from the SDC database to measure the level of financial advisors (e.g., Walter et al., 2008; Wang and Whyte, 2010; Golubov et al., 2012). Several studies use a static ranking system to measure the quality of financial advisors (McLaughlin, 1992; Rau, 2000; Hunter and Jagtiani, 2003). However, Da Silva Rosa et al. (2004) and Walter et al. (2008) argue that the static ranking system does not take into account the dynamics of the M&A advisory market. As Walter et al. (2008) argue that the reputation of financial advisors depends on its past performance, this study uses market share for each financial advisor in the previous year prior to the announcement date to measure the rank of financial advisors. The market share is measured as a fraction of the total value of transactions advised by investment banks from the previous year.

To determine the quality of financial advisors, this study collects the top 30 investment banks from the league table in the SDC database. Financial advisors are divided into two groups in terms of high- and low-reputable financial advisors to determine the quality of financial advisors. If the market share of financial advisors ranks the top eight banks in the previous year prior to the announcement date, these financial advisors are classified as high-reputable financial advisors; otherwise, as low-reputable financial advisors. If firms hire multiple financial advisors, this study uses the highest rank of financial advisors to measure the quality of financial advisors.²

### 76.3.2 Control variables

Prior studies have documented the importance of deal and firm specific characteristics on firm performance in M&As. This study also controls for these characteristics in the regression analysis in order to better reveal the importance of financial advisors on deal outcomes in M&As. Song et al. (2013) argue that bidders in hostile deals are more likely to choose boutique advisors. In addition, bidders are more likely to hire financial advisors in cross industry M&As. Prior studies also indicate several factors that influence the completion time in M&As. For example, Hunter and Jagtiani (2003) and Walter et al. (2008) report that hostile transactions would take longer time to complete. Walter et al. (2008) and Song et al. (2013) indicate that transactions will spend more time in completion in association with stock payment. When acquiring listed targets, bidders similarly need to take longer time to complete. When targets and bidders are in the same industry, completion time for the deals is longer (Walter et al., 2008).

Furthermore, prior studies have documented the importance of deal and firm specific characteristics on firm performance in M&As. Morck et al. (1990) report that the market reacts negatively to diversifying acquisitions. Similarly, Lang and Stulz (1994), Servaes (1996) and Doukas and Kan (2004) find that diversifying acquisitions may reduce the gains to bidders in M&As. However, Jensen and Ruback (1983), Bradley et al. (1988) and Hadlock et al. (2001) report that diversifying acquisitions are in general related to small positive abnormal returns.

Hostile deals can be more complex to complete relative to friendly deals. Servaes (1991) finds that bidders obtain lower returns when the transactions are hostile deals. Hunter and Jagtiani (2003) find that bidders in hostile deals obtain higher post-announcement gains. In addition, bidders may be relatively easy to negotiate the deals when acquiring private targets. Kohers (2004) reports that bidders obtain higher gains when acquiring private targets. Conn et al. (2005) report similar results, showing that bidders in private acquisitions result in positive announcement returns. In addition, Moeller et al. (2004) report that bidders paid by cash obtain positive announcement returns. Hagendorff et al. (2008) similarly find that bidder announcement returns are positively associated with cash payment. However, Loughran and Vijh (1997) find that stock payment results in lower bidder returns and bidders with cash payment break-even on the announcements of takeover deals.

Several studies have reported that firm characteristics can be important determinants to influence firm performance in M&As. Morck et al. (1990) report that firms with superior prior performance make better acquisitions. Rau and Vermaelen (1998) document that acquirers with low book-to-market ratio (glamour acquirers) outperform those with high book-to-market ratio (value acquirers). Dong et al. (2006) report positive relationship between targets’ book-to-market ratio and target abnormal returns. In addition, Moeller and Schlingemann (2005) find that bidder announcement returns are positively associated with their market-to-book ratio. Hunter and Jagtiani (2003) find that bidders obtain higher post-merger gains when the bidder size is large.

Following Brown and Warner’s (1985) study, the standard event study methodology with the market model is used to calculate abnormal returns. The market model parameters are estimated from day −270 to day −61, where day 0 is the announcement date. Datastream total return index for the Australian market is employed as a benchmark for the market. The abnormal returns are calculated by subtracting expected returns from actual returns.

ARᵢₜ = Rᵢₜ − (α + βRₘₜ). (76.3)

The cumulative abnormal returns are calculated by aggregating the abnormal returns over a certain period in order to capture the drift of market reaction within the selected event window. This study focuses on short-term announcement returns for targets and bidders as share price generally has a significant impact surrounding the announcement date. In addition, this study also examines long-run post-announcement returns for bidders, which allows the study to capture long-run post-announcement effects on firm performance. Cross-sectional t-statistic is employed to test the significance level for the hypothesis, H₀: mean abnormal returns are equal to 0. The model specification for statistic test is provided in Appendix 76A.

In addition, the current study also conducts cross-sectional regression analysis to explore the relationship between announcement returns and the quality and type of financial advisors. Similarly, the regression analysis also controls for deal and firm specific characteristics. The variables include cash payment, friendly deals, relatedness, market-to-book ratio, ROA and firm size. In addition, prior studies have reported that bidder announcement returns can differ when acquiring private targets. Thus, this study further controls for private targets in the regression analysis for bidding firms.
