Student’s t copulas have relatively strong tail dependence compared with Gaussian copula. The dependence coefficient is given by

$$\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 2t_{v+1}\left(-\sqrt{v+1}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}}\right), \qquad (73.24)$$

where $t_{v+1}$ is the univariate Student’s t cumulative distribution function with $v+1$ degrees of freedom. Nevertheless, not all of the Student’s t copulas are symmetric. Demarta and McNeil (2005) have developed a number of Student’s t copulas with asymmetric tail dependence.

Contrariwise, Archimedean copulas have asymmetric tail dependence, $\lambda_{ij}^l \neq \lambda_{ij}^u$ or $\lambda_L \neq \lambda_U$. Thus, they are of higher relevance for examining the tail dependence between financial assets, such as stock returns. Clayton copulas have zero upper tail but a positive lower tail dependence coefficient. The tail dependence coefficient is given by

$$\lambda_L = \begin{cases} 2^{-(\frac{1}{\alpha})}, & \text{if } \alpha > 0 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad (73.25a)$$

and

$$\lambda_U = 0. \qquad (73.25b)$$

If $\alpha = 1$, the lower tail dependence coefficient $\lambda_L$ between two series is 0.5. If $\alpha > 1$, the coefficient $\lambda_L$ will be larger than 0.5. If a series has a value that is in the lower tail of the distribution, a coefficient of more than 0.5 suggests that the conditional probability that the other series will have a value that is in the lower tail of its distribution is more than 50%. This means that there is a fairly strong lower tail dependence between the two series if $\alpha > 1$. Gumbel copulas have zero lower tail but a positive upper tail dependence coefficient. The tail dependence coefficient is given by

$$\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{(\frac{1}{\delta})} \qquad (73.26a)$$

and

$$\lambda_L = 0. \qquad (73.26b)$$

The parameters in the copula densities can be easily estimated using MLE. Appendix 73C provides the details of the procedure using Clayton copula as an example. The same process is applicable to other copulas.
