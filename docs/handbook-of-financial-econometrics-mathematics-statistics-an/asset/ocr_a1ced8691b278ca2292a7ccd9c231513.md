Empirically, Jensen (1968) specified a time series model as

R_{jt} - R_{Tt} = α_j + β_j(R_{mt} - R_{Tt}) + ε_{jt}, (62.2)

where α_j and β_j are assumed by Jensen to be time invariant, R_{Tt} is the rate of return for short-time Treasury Bills, and ε_{jt} is assumed to be N(0, σ_j^2) and to be identically and independently distributed over time (i.i.d.). Excess returns for the jth asset are also assumed to be proportional to excess return for the market portfolio at all times. These assumptions of intertemporal independence and the use of treasury bill rate as a proxy for R_f enables Jensen to use time series observations of R_{jt}, R_{mt}, and R_{Tt} to estimate α_j and β_j for different mutual funds. In addition, Jensen interpreted α_j as a measure of performance of the jth fund.

> “... if the model is valid, the particular nature of general economic conditions or the particular market conditions over the sample or evaluation time period has no effect whatsoever on the measure of performance...”

Jensen’s results have been criticized on the ground that the assumptions used to derive CAPM are not realistic and that errors of measurement in R_m and R_f can affect the results. Recently, Friend and Blume (1970) estimated α_j and β_j for many random portfolios. They then regressed the estimated α_j’s of different portfolios on the corresponding estimated β_j’s and found the regression coefficient b to be negative for the entire period 1960–1968 and also for the subperiod 1960–1964. They attributed these phenomena to the fact that one of the assumption used in deriving CAPM — that investors can borrow at the riskless rate for infinite amount — is violated in practice thus causing the estimated β_j’s to have a negative b. They further found that in the subperiod 1964–1968, b is positive. They attributed the latter phenomenon primarily to the unanticipated appreciation in price and not to the violation of the assumption on borrowing. Black, Jensen and Scholes (1972) and Miller and Scholes (1972) regressed average excess return on the estimated β_j’s and concluded that the second round regression coefficient is a downward biased estimator of the average excess market rate of return. BJS further attributed these phenomena to the fact that there exists no risk-free rate and advocated the use of zero beta factor.
