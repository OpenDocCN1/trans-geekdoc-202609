It is interesting to go one step further to see whether the covariance term can affect a firm to change its dividend payout decision (e.g., stop a dividend payment or initiate a dividend payment). For models (3) and (4), the dependent variable is 1 if the firm stops a dividend payout in the firm-year and 0 if the firm keeps paying a dividend in the firm-year. We find the significantly positive coefficients for the covariance between the rate of return on assets and the total asset growth are 12.9885 and 12.9136 with t-statistics 7.34 and 7.53, respectively. This indicates that firms with higher interaction between the profitability risk and the growth risk have a higher probability to cancel their dividend payments (Hypothesis 3a). For models (5) and (6), the dependent variable is 1 if the firm initiates a dividend payout in the firm-year and 0 if the firm continues not paying a dividend in the firm-year. However, the insignificant coefficients for the covariance term do not support Hypothesis 3, in which the covariance term should be negative.

Results from models (3)–(6), to some extent, can explain the disappearing dividend paying firms as shown in Table 97.2. We find the magnitude of the positive covariance term is increasing over time from 0.59 in 1969 to 1.03 in 2011. From the results of models (3) and (4), the higher covariance terms force a certain amount of dividend paying firms to either decrease or stop their dividend payouts. In contrast, from models (5) and (6), a decreasing covariance term of a non-dividend paying firm cannot make the firm decide to initiate paying dividend. Such an irreversible phenomenon may cause the fact of disappearing dividend paying firms over the last four decades.

# 97.5 Summary and Conclusion Remarks

Our concern in this chapter has been to sharpen the focus on corporate decisions concerning the optimal growth and the dividend payout policy. The interdependence of corporate decisions on these two important policy variables is explicitly recognized in deriving the simultaneous solution to the problem of maximizing the present value of the firm. We find that the optimal growth rate follows a mean-reverting process. In more detail, we examine the time path of the optimal growth rate, starting from the stochastic initial conditions, and approaching its steady state value.
