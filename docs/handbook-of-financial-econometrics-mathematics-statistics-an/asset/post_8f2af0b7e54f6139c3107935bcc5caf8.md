
The second challenge is the problem of switching. A user will have formed banking habits. If the process of using mobile banking hurts the usual routine, the customer will avoid using it. It is also important how it correlates with consumer’s lifestyle and cultural values. Since mobile banking is not a face-to-face service, it does not require meeting bank’s staff, affecting the relationship between the customer and the bank. It means that the customer may feel that mobile banking services are impersonal. Some customers even evaluate face-to-face interactions as valuable (Hanafizadeh et al., 2014; Laukkanen and Kiviniemi, 2010). Thus, we assume the following hypotheses:

- H7: Self-efficacy negatively affects Perceived Efforts.
- H8: Compatibility with lifestyle negatively affects Perceived Efforts.

Social values have a great impact on consumer decision making. In other words, the social influence may encourage, or otherwise, the use of new technologies. The popularity of mobile banking will increase the motivation to try it. When a user gets recommendations to use mobile banking from colleagues, friends and family members, they will be more likely to do so. Mobile banking can be perceived as an attribute of successful social groups. It may also be considered as a good way to improve consumer’s image. Some researchers found this factor significant (Riquelme and Rios, 2010; Gu et al., 2009). Thus, we assume the following hypothesis:

H9: Social Influence positively affects Intention to Use mobile banking.

Thus, Figure 71.1 illustrates the empirical model developed in this study. To test it, seven factors are used. They are: Perceived Usefulness, Perceived Financial Costs, Perceived Efforts, Self-efficacy, Compatibility with lifestyle, Social Influence and Perceived Risk. All lines reflect the respective hypothesis and the expected directions of the effect are in brackets, where (+) means positive relationship while (−) means negative relationship.

## 71.4 Methodology and Data Collection

Our empirical model contains eight constructs and for each construct, a set of multiple items was developed. To ensure the content validity, all the items were developed from recent empirical studies. The data from Russia were used to test the model, so all the items were translated into Russian and modified in order to make them more understandable for Russian respondents. We also checked the clarity of the questionnaire on a small sample of seven respondents, including experts in sociology and economic sociology. According to their recommendations, we made some corrections to the questionnaire. Each construct was measured by three items and each item was measured with a five-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). However, three items were measured conversely from strongly agree (1) to strongly disagree (5). The list of final items and their sources are presented in Appendix 71A.

The survey also includes two other parts. The first part identifies respondents from the focus group. The focus group includes users of mobile Internet through smartphones and tablets. Applications for smartphones and tablets are the most advanced technologies for mobile banking. The second part of the survey collects demographic characteristics, such as gender, age, education, income and employment.

To test the model, we collected data via an online survey in March 2015. We used a snowball approach (Koenig-Lewis et al., 2010) and sent invitations with links to an online survey through social networks. We also sent emails with invitations to students of campuses of the National Research University Higher School of Economics (HSE University)¹ located in four cities (Moscow, Saint-Petersburg, Perm and Nizhny Novgorod).
