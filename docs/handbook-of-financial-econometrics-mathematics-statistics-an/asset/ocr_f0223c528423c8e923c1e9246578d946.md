of random variables.¹⁴ In our case, u as well as v are (multivariate) normally distributed with E[u] = E[v] = 0. The measurement errors u_i in the betas and v_j in the mean returns are uncorrelated, i.e., E[u_i v_j] = 0.¹⁵

We expand f(β̂, r̄) in a Taylor series around the expected values E(β̂) = β and E(r̄) = E(r) up to quadratic terms in the errors u and v and get

```
f(β̂, r̄) = f(β, E(r)) + (∇_β f) · u + (∇_r f) · v
+ ½ { u' H_ββ u + u' H_βr v + v' H_rβ u + v' H_rr v } + ⋯
```

with the Hessian matrix H_ββ containing the second-order derivatives with respect to β̂, H_βr and H_rβ containing the mixed derivatives and H_rr containing the second-order derivatives with respect to r̄. However, since f(β̂, r̄) is linear in r̄, this last term vanishes, H_rr = 0. Taking the expected value E[f(β̂, r̄)] not only the first-order terms vanish as well, but due to the independence of the errors u and v also both terms with mixed derivatives vanish. Since the errors u are (multivariate) normally distributed the Taylor series converges if the errors are sufficiently small, i.e., if the variance of the errors u is small compared to the variance of the true parameters β. We cut off the series after the second-order term and get

```
E[f(β̂, r̄)] ≈ f(β, E(r)) + ½ E[u' H_ββ u].
```

Using the pricing model (98.1), we can write

```
E(r) = \begin{pmatrix} (E(r_1) - \overline{E(r)}) \vdots (E(r_N) - \overline{E(r)}) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_1 (\beta_1 - \bar{\beta}) \vdots \gamma_1 (\beta_N - \bar{\beta}) \end{pmatrix} = \gamma_1 \beta
```

> The above-defined vectors are centered, i.e., their elements measure the deviation of the values of individual assets from the corresponding cross-sectional average.
