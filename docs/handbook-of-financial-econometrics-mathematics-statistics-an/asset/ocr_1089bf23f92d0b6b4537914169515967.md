Foreign banks, as outsiders, initially do not possess this soft information, and, instead, primarily use universally available ‘hard’ information when making their lending decisions (Berger and Udell, 2002). Because of this information disadvantage, foreign banks may be more reluctant than domestic banks to lend to opaque borrowers (Dell’Ariccia and Marquez, 2004), and consequently their lending behavior might produce unique information that may be of value to stakeholders. Bank debt is such an important part of emerging market firms’ capital structures, and since we have reason to suspect that the use of foreign bank debt might provide a useful signal to the market of a firm’s quality, an investigation into the potential differential impact of domestic and foreign banking relationships on borrower performance may be of interest.²

No analysis of bank lending in an emerging market would be complete without some consideration given to the use of collateral in the lending agreement. Collateral provisions are used ubiquitously in emerging markets and are common in developed markets too. Avery et al. (1998) document that, in the US, ~90% of small business loans (in dollar value) are secured by collateral (see 1998). Berger and Udell (1998) report that 92% of bank loans to US small businesses are backed by collateral, and they suggest that collateral is used to help reduce credit risk and increase the efficiency of the loan. As large as these ratios are in the developed world, these ratios approach one hundred percent in emerging markets. Credit market researchers explain the use of collateral as either a means to avoid potential adverse selection problems (Bester, 1985; Chan and Kanatas, 1985; Besanko and Thakor, 1987a, 1987b) or to avoid potential moral hazard problems (Boot et al., 1991) that might otherwise exist between borrowers and lenders. Some researches view collateral as a function of borrower characteristics (Jimenez et al., 2006) and document a negative relationship between collateral and borrower’s risk. Others address many exogenous factors may influence the use of collateral, such as market competition (Besanko and Thakor, 1987a), relationship lending (Sharpe, 1990; Rajan, 1992), and as a screening cost for borrowers (Manove and Padilla, 1999, 2001).
