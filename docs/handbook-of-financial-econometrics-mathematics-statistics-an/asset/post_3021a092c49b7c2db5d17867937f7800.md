
where $C_{\min}^A$ and $C_{\max}^A$ represent the lower and upper bounds for an American call option, respectively, $P_{\min}^A$ and $P_{\max}^A$ are the lower and upper bounds for an American put option.

The derivations of upper bounds for European and American call options are trivial. If the current underlying asset is not the value of these upper bounds, then arbitrageur can easily earn riskless profit by purchasing the current underlying asset of call options and selling call options. The upper bounds for European and American put options can also be proven by the same arbitrage trading strategy.

To derive the lower bounds of European options, we consider the following investments:

**Portfolio A.** *Purchase a European call option at price $C_0$ with strike price $K$ and purchase $K e^{-rT}$ value of bond*

**Portfolio B.** *Purchase the underlying stock at price $S_0$*

**Portfolio C.** *Purchase a European put option at price $P_0$ with strike price $K$ and purchase the underlying stock at price $S_0$*

**Portfolio D.** *Purchase $K e^{-rT}$ value of bond*

At maturity, portfolio B and portfolio D are worth $S_T$ and $K$ respectively. The value of portfolio A and portfolio C will be $\max(S_T, K)$ where portfolios A and C are worth $S_T$ if $S_T > K$ and worth $K$ if $S_T \le K$. Therefore, unless the current value of portfolio A is at least as large as portfolio B, there will be arbitrage opportunity. By the same way, portfolio C should be always worth as much as portfolio D. Hence, we can obtain the relations as follows:

$C_0 + K e^{-rT} \ge S_0 \quad \text{and} \quad P_0 + S_0 \ge K e^{-rT},$ 
 or 
 $C_0 \ge S_0 - K e^{-rT} \quad \text{and} \quad P_0 \ge K e^{-rT} - S_0. \quad (7.5)$

Then, the lower bounds of European options are derived. Since American call options are never early exercised if the underlying asset without dividend payment, therefore the upper and lower bounds for American call options are same as the bounds of European call options. However, an American put option should be larger than $K - S_0$ since it can be exercised at any time. Handley (2005) investigates the extension of Merton’s (1973) approach to the options with dividends. The tightened upper bound for European and American call options with dividends under arbitrage opportunity framework are determined by the comparison between dividend payment and strike price.

## 7.2.2 Stochastic dominance

There are two ways to derive option bounds in stochastic dominance: one is to utilize second degree stochastic dominance (SSD), and the other one is to use SSD with the consideration of a riskless asset (called second degree stochastic dominance with riskless asset, SSDR). These two methods are introduced in detail in the following two sections.

### 7.2.2.1 Second degree stochastic dominance (SSD)

The concept to obtain the option bounds of Perrakis and Ryan (1984) is looking for the second degree stochastic dominance relationship between different portfolios. Second degree stochastic dominance (SSD) allows the universal ranking of distributions among risk-averse individuals. The criterion is stated as follows:

> *Let X and Y be two random variables whose cumulative distributions are F_X and F_Y, respectively. Then F_X dominates F_Y by second degree stochastic dominance if and only if ∫_{-∞}^{z}[F_Y(t) − F_X(t)]dt ≥ 0 for all z.*

Any risk-averse expected-utility investor prefers a second degree stochastic dominant investment to a dominated alternative.

Based on Rubinstein's (1976) equation of the valuation of uncertain income streams, Perrakis and Ryan (1984) assume that the normalized conditional expected marginal utility of the consumptions is a non-increasing function of price change (Y). The option bounds can be obtained by comparing the values of three portfolios. Assume an investor has wealth endowment S_0 that can only be invested in one of these portfolios:

- **Portfolio A.** One share of stock at price S₀
- **Portfolio B.** One call option on the stock at price C₀ with strike price K, and S₀ − C₀ invested in the riskless asset with risk free rate, r
- **Portfolio C.** S₀/C₀ call options on the stock at price C₀ with strike price K

The values of these three different portfolios are represented as function of Y as:

Value of Portfolio A = S₀ + Y
Value of Portfolio B = (S₀ − C₀)(1 + r) + Max(S₀ + Y − K, 0)
Value of Portfolio C = S₀/C₀ (Max(S₀ + Y − K, 0)).

By comparing portfolio A and portfolio B to derive the lower bound and comparing portfolio A and portfolio C to derive upper bound, the bounds of an European call option for a single-period can be shown as

$C_{\min}^E = \text{Max}\left(0, S_0 + \frac{1}{1+r}\left[-K + \int_0^K F_Y(w - S_0)dw\right]\right), \quad (7.6)$

$C_{\max}^E = S_0 + \frac{S_0}{S_0 + E[Y]}\left[-K + \int_0^K F_Y(w - S_0)dw\right], \quad (7.7)$

where $F_Y(w - S_0)$ is the cumulative probability distribution of price change $Y$ for $Y \in [-S_0, \infty)$. The option bounds are functions of the initial stock price $S_0$, the exercise price $K$, the riskless rate of interest, $r$, and the distribution of stock price change, $F_Y(Y)$.

In the following, Perrakis (1986) extends the results of Perrakis and Ryan (1984) by tightening the bounds with the restrictions that the one-period return (of per dollar investing in the underlying stock) is finite and has a strictly positive lower limit. In addition, the bounds for American put option are also developed within the framework of Perrakis and Ryan (1984).

### 7.2.2.2 Second degree stochastic dominance with riskless asset (SSDR)

Distinct from Perrakis and Ryan's (1984) option bounds, which are obtainable by applying second degree stochastic dominance method, Levy (1985) applies the concept of second degree stochastic dominance with the riskless asset (SSDR) to derive the upper and lower bounds for European options. Levy (1985) assumes that the investors' preferences belong to the set of all non-decreasing concave utility function and the underlying assets are those with nonnegative consumption beta. Let $F_c$ and $F_s$ be the cumulative distributions of the terminal wealth investing in an European call option and its underlying asset. If borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate are allowed, which is crucial assumption to derive relatively narrower bounds, then an investor may find that any combination of the underlying asset with the riskless asset is dominated by some combination of European call option with the riskless asset even though neither $F_c$ nor $F_s$ dominates each other without the riskless asset. That is, in the presence of a riskless asset, if every risk-averse investor prefers to hold the investment $F_c$ rather than the investment $F_s$, then $F_c$ is said to dominate $F_s$ by SSDR.

Therefore, the European call option bounds can be obtained by using the intuition that the lower (upper) bound of a European call option is derived
in such a way that $F_c$ ($F_s$) exactly does not dominate $F_s$ ($F_c$). The criterion of SSDR is stated as follows:

*Let $F_c$ and $F_s$ be the two cumulative distributions with the pth quantile, $Q_{F_C}(p)$ and $Q_{F_S}(p)$, respectively. Then $F_c$ dominates $F_s$ in the SSDR if and only if*

$$\inf_{0\le p < p_C} \frac{\int_0^p [Q_{F_S}(w)-r]dw}{\int_0^p [Q_{F_C}(w)-r]dw} \ge \sup_{p_C < p \le 1} \frac{\int_0^p [Q_{F_S}(w)-r]dw}{\int_0^p [Q_{F_C}(w)-r]dw},$$

*where $p_C$ is the value which solves $\int_0^{p_C} [Q_{F_C}(w)-r]dw = 0$.*

Levy (1985) proves that the cumulative distribution of the return of a European call option, $F_c$, dominates the cumulative distribution of the return of its underlying asset, $F_s$, in the SSDR if and only if $p_c \le p_S$, where $p_c$ and $p_s$ are the values that solve

$$\int_0^{p_c} [Q_{F_C}(w)-r]dw = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \int_0^{p_S} [Q_{F_S}(w)-r]dw = 0. \tag{7.8}$$

The lower bounds of a European call option, $C_{\min}^E$, then can be solved by using Levy (1985)'s dominance theory. In order to avoid such dominance, we must set $p_c > p_S$. Therefore, one can solve for a European call option price, $C_0$, that equates this condition, and then conclude that the market price of a European call option must be greater than such a value. Using this line of reasoning, it can be shown that:

$$C_0 \ge C_{\min}^E = S_0 - \frac{K}{r} + \frac{1}{r p_C} \int_0^K (K - S_T) f(S_T) dS_T, \tag{7.9}$$

where $C_0$ denotes current price of a European call option; $S_0$ denotes current price of the underlying asset, $S_T$ is the future price of the underlying asset at expiration day, $T$; $K$ and $r$ represent the strike price and risk-free rate, respectively; $p_c$ is the value that solves $\int_0^{p_c}[Q_{F_C}(w)-r]dw = 0$ and $F_c$ is the cumulative distribution of the return of a European call option.

Similarly, the upper bound of a European call option, $C_{\max}^E$, can be obtained by considering the dominance of the price of the underlying asset over the price of a European call option in SSDR. By using Levy (1985)'s dominance theory and setting $p_S > p_c$, the upper bound of a European call option, $C_{\max}^E$ can be shown as:

$$C_0 \le C_{\max}^E = \frac{S_0}{E(S_T)} \int_K^{\infty} (S_T - K) f(S_T) dS_T, \tag{7.10}$$
