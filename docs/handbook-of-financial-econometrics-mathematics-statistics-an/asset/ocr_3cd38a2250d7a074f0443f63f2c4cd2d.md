# 36.1 Introduction

Towards the end of the Second World War, world markets were characterized by many barriers to the free movements of goods, services and capital. Deregulation policies, which began in the developed countries in 1970 and in developing countries in the second half of 1980, removed the obstacles to trade and investment flows across the regions. Since the formation of the General Agreement on Trade and Tariffs (GATT), which was replaced on January 1, 1995 by the World Trade Organization (WTO), many governments have upheld the elimination of the traditional trade barriers. The economic and financial market liberalization has resulted in growing international stock market integration (Bekaert and Harvey, 2002; Forbes and Chinn, 2004; Chambet and Gibson, 2008; Phuan et al., 2009; Beine and Candelon, 2011; Paramati et al., 2015, 2016; Chuang and Karamatov, 2018). Furthermore, technological innovations have reduced transaction costs related to foreign assets and, consequently, have led to an increase in cross-border financial flows, which have contributed to an increase in the international stock market integration (Zimmermann et al., 2003; Sharma and Seth, 2012; Theodorou, 2017). The formation of regional trading blocs such as the European Union (EU), the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) and the Asia Pacific Economic Council (APEC) have also increased the international stock market integration (Kim et al., 2005; Bartram et al., 2007; Hooy and Goh, 2014; Kaur et al., 2017). The forces cited above (market liberalization, advanced technology and regional economic bloc), coupled with multinational corporations and Global Social Networks, have rapidly contributed to the globalization of markets.
