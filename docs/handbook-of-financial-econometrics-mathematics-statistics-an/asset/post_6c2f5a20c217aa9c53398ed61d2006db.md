
Then the values of x, y and z can be found as follows:

$x = \frac{\begin{bmatrix} d_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ d_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ d_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}},\quad y = \frac{\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & d_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & d_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & d_3 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}},\quad \text{and}\quad z = \frac{\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & d_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & d_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & d_3 \end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}}.\quad (77A.9)$

And then you need to use determinant calculation, which will be discussed in the next section.

## Determinant calculation

### 3 × 3 matrices

The determinant of a 3 × 3 matrix is defined by

$\begin{vmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{vmatrix}=a\begin{vmatrix} e & f \\ h & i \end{vmatrix}-b\begin{vmatrix} d & f \\ g & i \end{vmatrix}+c\begin{vmatrix} d & e \\ g & h \end{vmatrix}$
$=a(ei-fh)-b(di-fg)+c(dh-eg)$
$=aei+bfg+cdh-ceg-bdi-afh.\quad (77A.10)$

We use the same example as we did in the first method:

$x = \frac{\begin{bmatrix}5 & 3 & -2 \\ 7 & 5 & 6 \\ 8 & 4 & 3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -2 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 4 & 3\end{bmatrix}},\quad y = \frac{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 & -2 \\ 3 & 7 & 6 \\ 2 & 8 & 3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -2 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 4 & 3\end{bmatrix}},\quad z = \frac{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 7 \\ 2 & 4 & 8\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -2 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 4 & 3\end{bmatrix}}\quad (77A.11)$

$y = \frac{1\times7\times3+5\times6\times2+(-2)\times3\times8-(-2)\times7\times2-5\times3\times3-1\times6\times8}{1\times5\times3+3\times6\times2+(-2)\times3\times4-(-2)\times5\times2-3\times3\times3-1\times6\times4}$
$=\frac{21+60-48+28-45-48}{15+36-24+20-27-24}=\frac{-32}{-4}=8\quad (77A.12)$

z = (1 × 5 × 8 + 3 × 7 × 2 + 5 × 3 × 4 − 5 × 5 × 2 − 3 × 3 × 8 − 1 × 7 × 4) / (1 × 5 × 3 + 3 × 6 × 2 + (−2) × 3 × 4 − (−2) × 5 × 2 − 3 × 3 × 3 − 1 × 6 × 4) = (40 + 42 + 60 − 50 − 72 − 28) / (15 + 36 − 24 + 20 − 27 − 24) = −8 / −4 = 2 (77A.13)

## Method 3: Matrix method

Using the example above, we can derive the following matrix equation:

[x y z]^T = [[1 3 -2],[3 5 6],[2 4 3]]^{-1} × [5 7 8]^T . (77A.14)

The inversion of matrix A is, by definition,

A^{-1} = 1 / det A * (Adj A). (77A.15)

Adjoint A is defined by the transpose of the cofactor matrix. First, we need to calculate the cofactor matrix of A. Suppose the cofactor matrix is

cofactor matrix = [[A11 A12 A13],[A21 A22 A23],[A31 A32 A33]],

A11 = |5 6;4 3| = −9,   A12 = −|3 6;2 3| = 3,   A13 = −|3 5;2 4| = 2,
A21 = −|3 -2;4 3| = −17,   A22 = −|1 -2;2 3| = 7,   A23 = −|1 3;2 4| = 2,
A31 = |3 -2;5 6| = 28,   A32 = −|1 -2;3 6| = 12,   A33 = |1 3;3 5| = −4,

therefore,

cofactor matrix = [[−9 3 2],[−17 7 2],[28 −12 −4]]. (77A.16)

Then, we can get Adjoint $A$ as follows:

Adj $A$ = $\begin{bmatrix} -9 & -17 & 28 \\ 3 & 7 & -12 \\ 2 & 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$, (77A.17)

The determinant of $A$ we have calculated in Cramer's rule is as follows:

Det $A$ = $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & -2 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ = $-4$,

$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{(-4)} \times \begin{bmatrix} -9 & -17 & 28 \\ 3 & 7 & -12 \\ 2 & 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{9}{4} & \frac{17}{4} & -\frac{28}{4} \\ -\frac{3}{4} & -\frac{7}{4} & 3 \\ -\frac{1}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, (77A.18)

Therefore,

$\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{9}{4} & \frac{17}{4} & -\frac{28}{4} \\ -\frac{3}{4} & -\frac{7}{4} & 3 \\ -\frac{1}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{9}{4}\times5+\frac{17}{4}\times7+\left(-\frac{28}{4}\right)\times8 \\ \left(-\frac{3}{4}\right)\times5+\left(-\frac{7}{4}\right)\times7+3\times8 \\ \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\times5+\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\times7+1\times8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -15 \\ 8 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$. (77A.19)

## Appendix 77B: Relationship between Discriminant Analysis and Dummy Regression Analysis

### 77B.1 Derivation of the Discriminant Function

Data composed of two samples of size N₁ and N₂ for two-group discriminant analysis must meet the following assumptions: (1) that the groups being investigated are discrete and identifiable; (2) that each observation in each group can be described by a set of measurements on m characteristics or variables; and (3) that these m variables have a multivariate normal distribution in each population. In vector notation, the nth observation can be represented as an m × 1 column vector of the form

X′ₙ = (X₁ₙ, X₂ₙ, …, Xₘₙ),

where n = 1, …, N₁, or n = 1, …, N₂.

Under these assumptions, the linear discriminant function can be defined as equation (77.10) of the chapter text (repeated here for convenience)

Yᵢ = a₁X₁ᵢ + a₂X₂ᵢ + ⋯ + aₘXₘᵢ. (77B.1)

The aᵢ’s were then chosen to maximize the ratio of the weighted between-group variance to the pooled within-group variance. Ladd (1966) has proposed a discriminant criterion E, as defined as equation (77A.2) or (77.18)

of the text, to determine the coefficients $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m$:

$E = \frac{A' D D' A}{A' C A} = \frac{A' B A}{A' C A}\qquad(77B.2)$

where

- $A' = [a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m]$;
- $D' = [\bar{X}_{1,1} - \bar{X}_{1,2}, \bar{X}_{2,1} - \bar{X}_{2,2}, \bar{X}_{m,1} - \bar{X}_{m,2}]$;
- $C = \text{Within-group variance matrix}$;
- $DD' = \text{Between-group variance matrix}$.

There are two alternative methods that can be used to derive the basic equation of discriminant analysis as defined in equation (77B.3) later in this appendix.

### (a) Subsequent Vector Derivation Method

Symbolically, we may find the derivative of $E$ with the respect to the column vector of $A$ and equate the result to the $(m \times 1)$ vector [see Tatsuoka (1971), 160–161]. The vector equation thus obtained is

$\frac{\partial E}{\partial A} = \frac{2[(BA)(A'CA) - (A'BA)(CA)]}{(A'CA)^2} = 0$

Dividing both numerator and denominator of the middle member by $A'CA$ and using the definition of $E$ in equation (77B.2), this equation reduces to

$\frac{2[BA - ECA]}{A'CA} = 0,$

which is equivalent to

$(B - EC)A = 0.\qquad(77B.3)$

### (b) Long-hand Method

If $m = 2$, then equation (77B.2) can be rewritten as

$E = \frac{b_{11}a_1^2 + b_{22}a_2^2 + 2b_{12}a_1a_2}{c_{11}a_1^2 + c_{22}a_2^2 + 2c_{12}a_1a_2},\qquad(77B.2a)$

where $b_{11}, b_{22},$ and $b_{12}$ are elements of $B$, and $c_{11}, c_{22},$ and $c_{12}$ are elements of $C$.

Taking the partial derivative of E with respect to a1, we obtain

∂E/∂a1 = [(2b11a1 + 2b12a2)(C11a1² + C22a2² + 2C12a1a2) − (b11a1² + b22a2² + 2b12a1a2)(2C11a1 + 2C12a2)] × (C11a1² + C22a2² + 2C12a1a2)−2 = 2[b11a1 + b12a2) − E(C11a1 + C12a2)] × C11a1² + C22a2² + 2C12a1a2)−1.

Setting this equation equal to zero and simplifying, we get

b11a1 + b12a2 = E(C11a1 + C12a2).

Using vector notation, we have

[b11, b12]A = E[C11, C12]A. (77B.4)

Similarly, it can be shown that, upon equating ∂E/∂a2 to zero and simplifying, we get

[b21, b22]A = E[C21, C22]A. (77B.5)

It is evident that equations (77B.4) and (77B.5) can be written as a single matrix equation:

[ b11  b12 ]       [ C11  C12 ]
[ b21  b22 ] A = E [ C21  C22 ] A

or

(B − EC)A = 0. (77B.3)

Equation (77B.3) can be used to formulate the characteristic equation for solving eigenvector A as indicated in the text.

If A* maximizes the function shown in equation (77B.1), then so does any A** = KA*, where K is a scalar. Substituting A** for A* simply multiplies both the numerator and denominator by K². Because the coefficients themselves are not unique, there are several methods of calculating the discriminant function. Johnston and Dinardo show (1996), for example, that the vector A* that maximizes the ratio E is proportional to the vector A (that is, A = KA*), which maximizes G = A′DD′A subject to the constraint that L = A′CA, where L is an arbitrary constant.
