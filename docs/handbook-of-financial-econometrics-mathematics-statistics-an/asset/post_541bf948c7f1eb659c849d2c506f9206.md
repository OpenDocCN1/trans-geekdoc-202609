More important than examining (and controlling for) the impact of the number of banking relationships on firm performance, we are really interested in examining whether there is any differential impact on performance due to the use of domestic bank debt relative to foreign bank debt. If the more sophisticated foreign banks possess superior credit screening and monitoring skills, and lend primarily on hard information rather than the soft information that is often relied upon by domestic banks, then the market might find some valuable information in the fact that a firm was able to secure foreign bank debt. Thus, we expect a positive relationship between the use of foreign bank debt and firm performance.

We are also interested in measuring the impact on firm performance of the concentration of a firm’s debt that comes from any given bank on average. Sharpe (1990), Rajan (1992), or Greenbaum et al. (1989) argue that there are “hold up” costs that can occur when too much of a firm’s debt comes from one lender; especially when the concentration comes from a lender having a long relationship with the firm. These hold up costs are value-destroying quasi-rents that the lender extracts from the borrower. We try to capture the concentration of bank lending with the variable CONCENT, the ratio of bank debt over total assets, divided by the number of banking relationships. Our expectation is that there will be a negative relationship between the concentration of a firm’s debt that comes from any given bank and the firm’s performance.

As reported in Table 25.1, 84% of domestic Taiwanese bank loans are collateralized. Collateral provisions are used extensively in emerging markets, and they are prevalent even in developed markets. Avery et al. (1998) document that, in the US, ~90% of small business loans (in dollar value) are secured by collateral (see 1998). Berger and Udell (1998) report that 92% of bank loans to US small businesses are backed by collateral, and they suggest that collateral is used to help reduce credit risk and increase the efficiency of the loan. As large as these ratios are in the developed world, these ratios approach 100% in emerging markets. Because of this, emerging market firms have very little power to differentiate themselves based upon their willingness to offer collateral to local banks.

Foreign banks, however, offer borrowers a choice of either providing more collateral in return for a lower interest rate, or putting up less collateral and
suffer a higher interest rate. High quality firms can signal their quality by their willingness to offer up their valuable assets as collateral for their foreign loans. A high quality firm might prefer to have a higher level of collateral — given that it has a lower likelihood of defaulting on its obligations — and in return receive a lower interest rate. Low quality firms may be concerned with losing their collateral, and may find it too expensive to mimic. Consequently, a separating equilibrium ensues. So, firms using foreign bank debt collateral may be signaling to the market that they are of high quality. For this reason, one might expect that stockholders may view collateral posted against foreign bank loans in a positive way, while viewing collateral posted against domestic bank loans as either conveying very little information. To examine this, we include dummy variables for local bank and foreign bank collateral to examine whether foreign bank collateral has a differential impact on performance than does collateral pledged to local banks.¹¹ COLLATDB and COLLATFB are dummy variables that equal one if domestic and foreign corporate borrowings are secured by collateral, respectively.

We begin our analysis with an Ordinary Least Squares regression analysis. Tobin’s Q is our measure of firm performance. Tobin’s Q has been a widely employed proxy for both a firm’s growth opportunities and value or performance. Lang et al. (1989), Howe et al. (1992), Doukas (1995), and Szewczyk et al. (1996) are among a host of papers that use Q for this purpose. Similar to these prior papers, we calculate Q by summing the market value of equity and the book value of debt and dividing this sum by the total of the book value of equity and the book value of debt.

Our regression model is specified in equation (25.1):

TOBINQ_{it} = γ₀ + γ₁LOGNB_{it} + γ₂CONCENT_{it} + γ₃PD/TD_{it}
+ γ₄NBD/TD_{it} + γ₅FBD/TD_{it} + γ₆COLLATDB_{it}
+ γ₇COLLATFB_{it} + γ₈AGE_{it} + γ₉LOGTA_{it}

Credit market researchers explain the use of collateral as either a means to avoid potential adverse selection problems (Bester, 1985; Chan and Kanatas, 1985; Besanko and Thakor, 1987a, 1987b) or to avoid potential moral hazard problems (Boot et al., 1991) that might otherwise exist between borrowers and lenders. Some researchers view collateral as a function of borrower characteristics (Jimenez et al., 2006) and document a negative relationship between collateral and borrower’s risk. Others address many exogenous factors that may influence the use of collateral, such as market competition (Besanko and Thakor, 1987a), relationship lending (Sharpe, 1990; Rajan, 1992), and as a screening cost for borrowers (Manove and Padilla, 1999, 2001).

In addition to the variables described above, (LOGNB and CONCENT) the regression employs a traditional set of control variables. Older firms are often more stable and may have smaller growth opportunities (and lower Q values), so we control for age with the variable AGE, which is the number of years since the firm's first incorporation. Firm size is included because large firms may be more successful in developing new technology than small firms (Chauvin and Hirshey, 1993) thus yielding different Q scores. In addition, Rajan and Zingales (1995) find that firm size is a proxy for the probability of default. Our proxy for size is LOGTA, the log of the firm's total assets. We control for overall leverage by including the traditional leverage ratio (LEVERAGE). More efficient firms may have superior performance, so we control for efficiency by including FA/TA, the ratio of fixed assets to total assets. We control for sales growth by including the variable GROWTH, the past year's growth in sales. Houston and James (1996) find that Tobin's Q depends on future growth opportunities, which are often affected by capital expenditures; consequently, we control for this by including the capital expenditure ratio, CAPEX/TA, which is capital expenditure divided by total assets. Lastly, Lang and Stulz (1994) and Chen and Steiner (2000) find that R&D expenditure is also positively related to Tobin's Q; to do this, we include the R&D expenditure ratio, RD/SALES, which is the ratio of research and development expenditure to total net sales. Additional control variables include ROA, which is the return to assets; earnings volatility, EBITDA, which is the standard deviation of EBIT during the previous 3 years; and the dividend payout ratio, DIV/TA, measured by total cash dividends divided by total assets. We also include the percentage of public debt to total debt (PD/TD), the percentage of non-bank debt to total debt (NBD/TD), and the percentage of foreign bank debt to total debt (FBD/TD). To control for variations in performance that might be attributable to managerial ownership, we include the percentage of equity that is owned by management and its square (MO and MO2, respectively).
