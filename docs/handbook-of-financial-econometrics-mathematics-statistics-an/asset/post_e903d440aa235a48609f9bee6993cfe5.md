## Abstract

We examine the effect of book-tax differences on CEO compensation. We posit that CEOs can opportunistically exercise the discretion in GAAP to increase accounting income without affecting taxable income and in so doing increase their compensation. We test the data to determine which competing hypothesis dominates — efficiency or rent-seeking.

Under the efficiency hypothesis, the board of directors uses the information in book-tax differences to undo CEOs’ attempts to artificially inflate accounting income and hence CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences. Under the rent-seeking hypothesis, CEOs gain effective control of the pay-setting process so that they set their own pay with little oversight from shareholders and directors. Directors do not use the information in book-tax differences to undo CEOs’ attempted earnings manipulation and this gives rise to a positive association between CEO compensation and book-tax differences. Consistent with the efficiency hypothesis, we find that CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences suggesting that directors use the information in book-tax differences to reduce excessive CEO compensation. We also find that strong corporate governance structure strengthens the negative association between CEO compensation and book-tax differences. Specifically, firms with high insider equity ownership and high proportion of independent directors on the board have lower CEO compensation when book-tax differences are large.

### 32.1 Introduction

Prior studies have examined whether investors use the information in book-tax differences to assess earnings quality (Clemons and Shevlin, 2016; Edwards et al., 2016; Mills and Newberry, 2001; Blaylock et al., 2017; Phillips et al., 2003; Hanlon, 2003; Shevlin et al., 2017). Specifically, these studies suggest that large positive book-tax differences (accounting income greater than taxable income) seem to signal low earnings quality and investors use information in book-tax differences to detect earnings management and assess earnings persistence. We examine the use of book-tax differences in the setting of determining CEO compensation. This setting is important because proponents of “excessive CEO compensation” argue that CEOs capture the pay process and personally enrich themselves (Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2001; Bebchuk and Fried, 2003). To date, there is no empirical evidence on whether the board of directors uses information in book-tax differences to determine CEO compensation. This paper investigates the association between CEO compensation and book-tax differences. The second research question examined in this study is the interaction between corporate governance structure and book-tax differences in affecting CEO compensation.

This paper makes four contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the stream of research on using book-tax differences to infer earnings quality (Clemons and Shevlin, 2016; Blaylock et al., 2017). While prior studies examine whether investors use the information in book-tax differences to assess earnings persistence, we focus on whether the firm in general, and the board of directors in particular, use the information in book-tax differences to determine CEO compensation. Understanding the linkage between CEO compensation and book-tax differences is important because there is increasing criticism that CEOs personally enrich themselves by opportunistically managing accounting income upwards to increase their own compensation at the expense of shareholders. Second, our study also complements prior research that find compensation committees do intervene in setting executive compensation (Dechow et al., 1994; Gaver and Gaver, 1998; Duru et al., 2002; Adut et al., 2003). This stream of research documents that compensation committees shield executive compensation from the adverse effect of current period earnings of potentially firm-value-enhancing activities such as corporate restructurings and research and development. Our finding that CEO compensation is positively associated with large negative book-tax differences suggests that compensation committees shield CEO compensation from the effect of income-decreasing book-tax differences. Moreover, our result that CEO compensation is negatively associated with large positive book-tax differences suggests that compensation committees are not narrowly fixated on income-increasing book-tax differences. Third, we extend Core et al. (1999) finding that firms with excessive CEO compensation have weak corporate governance structure. Our result that strong corporate governance structure strengthens the negative relation between CEO compensation and book-tax differences suggests that firms with strong corporate governance are more likely to use the information in book-tax differences to mitigate excessive CEO compensation. Finally, we also contribute to the body of research on the determinants of compensating CEOs using pre-versus after-tax earnings (Newman, 1989; Atwood et al., 1998; Dhaliwal et al., 2000; Phillips et al., 2003). These studies find that firms with greater tax planning opportunities are more likely to use after-tax performance measures. We extend this body of research by examining the association between CEO compensation and book-tax differences. We show that different compensation components (such as salary, cash compensation and stock-based compensation) are negatively associated with book-tax differences.
