Kimball (1990) deeply and stringently discussed the standard risk-aversion concept to explain that investors will reduce current expense and save more for the uncertainty of future income. Therefore, previous papers usually assume risk-averse investors in markets to obtain narrower option bounds. For example, non-decreasing concave utility functions used in Levy (1985), assumption of decreasing absolute risk aversion (DARA) used in Ritchken and Kuo (1989) and Basso and Pianca (1997), application of standard risk aversion (SRA) rules in Basso and Pianca (2001), and non-increasing absolute risk-aversion utility functions utilized in Mathur and Ritchken (1999).

If the investor preference (utility function) is given, the pricing kernel in each state can be constructed by the discounted marginal utility of wealth in that state divided by the expected marginal utility. Thus, we can use this pricing kernel to evaluate all securities including options as well. By incorporating with no-arbitrage assumption, the option bounds can be obtained by searching minimum and maximum option prices which are determined by the pricing kernel (Ritchken, 1985; Ritchken and Kuo, 1989; Basso and Pianca, 1997, 2001; Mello and Pyo, 2006). Mello and Pyo (2006) and Pyo (2008) employ minimax deviation approach to derive the bounds for real options in accordance with the risk preference of the decision maker.

Alternative way to apply pricing kernel technique to the derivations of option bounds is to investigate the characteristics of pricing kernel. Bernardo and Ledoit (2000) propose “gain–loss ratio bounds” for options by linking pricing kernel to gain–loss ratio of investment’s excess payoff, i.e., the ratio of positive expected value of excess payoff to its expected negative value. The narrow option bounds can be obtained by maximizing gain–loss ratio. Cochrane and Saa-Requejo (2000) link the volatility of pricing kernel to Sharpe ratio and propose “good-deal bounds” for options that lower (upper) option bounds are the minimization (maximization) of option prices under the constraints of the volatility of pricing kernel less than Sharpe ratio of the option. Bondarenko and Longarela (2004) further modify “good-deal bounds” for an option which its underlying asset follows stochastic volatility model. Huang (2004) generalizes option bounds by using the elasticity of the pricing kernel and shows that Cochrane and Saa-Requejo’s (2000) approach is a special case of Huang’s (2004) approach when underlying asset follows lognormal distribution. Christoffersen et al. (2010) show that using a quadratic equivalent martingale measure (EMM) and GARCH framework also can derive good-deal bounds on option prices.
