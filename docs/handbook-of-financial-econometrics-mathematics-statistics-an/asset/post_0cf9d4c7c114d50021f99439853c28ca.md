## (ii) The Effect of Loss Averse Coefficient on Equity Premium.

One of the primary features of prospect theory is to introduce the loss averse attitude of human beings. Because there is no role of λ in the degenerate model, we report the results for prospect utility model only. The solid line in Figure 48.5 presents the equity premium as a function of the level of loss aversion in the dividend-claim case, and the dash line portrays the results of our consumption-claim case. In both cases, when λ increases, the equity premium becomes higher. The result is consistent with our expectations — if one is more loss averse, he needs more gains to cover the pains of losses that have occurred, so the equity premium rises. This is also why we are tempted to apply loss aversion in prospect theory to resolve the equity premium puzzle.

In addition, marginal increasing level of equity premium decreases with respect to the loss aversion coefficient. From Table 48.5, when the value of λ is initially 1 (which means when it is in the bad state, the decreasing utility level of the representative agent is not amplified) and then becomes 2, the equity premium rises significantly. However, when λ is large to an extent, it becomes harder to increase equity premium by enlarging λ. In our opinion, the above results reflect the fact that human beings do have loss averse attitude, but the degree of the level of loss aversion is limited.

Despite the definitions of risk aversion and loss aversion being independent, the above results show that interactions exist between the ways risk aversion and loss aversion affect the equity premium. It is worth noting that in order to let the first-order derivative of prospect utility be continuous, when $c_t - v_t < 0$, we change the risk aversion coefficient from $\beta$ to $\beta/\lambda$. Interactions may arise from the modification.¹³

Note that when $c_t \ge v_t$, the absolute risk aversion coefficient (ARA), $-u''/u'$, equals the constant $\beta$. When $c_t < v_t$, the ARA is $-\beta/\lambda$. It is noted that $\partial ARA/\partial \lambda = 0$ when $c_t \ge v_t$, and $\partial ARA/\partial \lambda = \beta/\lambda^2$ when $c_t < v_t$. Therefore, in the bad state, the effect of loss aversion on ARA increases when $\beta$ increases. As a result, globally looking, when the value of $\beta$ becomes larger, the effect of $\lambda$ weakly increases.
