Acharya and Pedersen (2005) derive a liquidity-adjusted capital asset pricing model (CAPM), demonstrating that there are three forms of liquidity risks. They are linked to: (1) the covariance between a security’s illiquidity and the market illiquidity. Since investors require higher returns for holding securities that become more illiquid when the whole market is illiquid, the risk premium associated with such covariance should be positive; (2) the covariance between a security’s return and the market illiquidity. Because securities that generate higher returns in times of market illiquidity are more desirable, the implied risk premium should be negative. As described above, Pastor and Stambaugh (2003) find supportive evidence for this liquidity risk; (3) the covariance between a security’s illiquidity and the market return. In a down market, investors prefer less illiquid securities. Thus, the associated risk premium should be negative. Employing Amihud (2002) illiquidity measure, Acharya and Pedersen (2005) show that all three liquidity risks are priced in the cross-section of stock returns. The strongest effect is ascribed to the third form, i.e., the covariance between a security’s illiquidity and the market return, which contributes about 80 percent of the overall liquidity premium.

Liquidity is a broad and multifaceted concept. It encompasses transaction cost, price impact (the responsiveness of stock prices to changes in trading activity), market depth (trading volume), and its effect on real economic activities. The complex nature of liquidity makes it very difficult, if not impossible, to incorporate all its dimensions and sum it up with one single measure. There are, however, some commonly used proxies in the literature: bid-ask spread (Chodia et al., 2000), turnover ratio (Chodia et al., 2001), Amihud (2002) illiquidity measure, and Pastor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity index.

In our study, we investigate the pricing impact of various market-wide liquidity measures. We would like to find evidence to answer the question: conditional on common risk factors (market, size, value, and momentum), is it the stock market liquidity, or the money market liquidity, or both that are priced in the cross-section of stock returns?
