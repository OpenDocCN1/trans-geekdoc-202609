
We further develop three testable hypotheses and their implications based on our theoretical model. Hypothesis 1 investigates the nature of the mean-reverting process of the optimal growth rate of a firm. Hypothesis 2 examines whether the covariance between the profitability risk and the growth risk can be one of the key factors in determining a firm’s dividend payout ratio. Hypothesis 3 examines whether the covariance term affects a firm’s decision to stop or initiate a dividend payout. To tackle our research questions, we empirically test the hypotheses by using 31,255 firm-years of data during the period from 1969 to 2011. Acknowledging that the partial adjustment model has been passively used in testing the partial adjustment process of dividend policy (see Fama and Babiak, 1968; Lee et al., 1987; Andres et al., 2009) and capital structure (see Flannery and Rangan, 2006; Lemmon et al., 2008; Huang and Ritter, 2009), we use the partial adjustment model to test the mean-reverting process of the growth rate as indicated in our theoretical model. In addition, the structural change model and the probit model are used to examine determinants and decisions of the dividend policy. Empirical results support the mean-reverting process of the total asset growth and the importance of the interaction between the profitability risk and the growth risk in determining the dividend payout ratio. We also find that a positive covariance term can increase the probability of stopping a dividend payout, while a negative covariance term cannot increase the probability of initiating a dividend payout. Such irreversible phenomenon along with a decreasing magnitude of the covariance term may, to some extent, explain the puzzle of disappearing dividends over decades (see Fama and French, 2001).

The present study deviates from earlier studies in two major aspects. First, this study presents a fully dynamic model for determining the optimal growth rate and the optimal dividend policy under stochastic conditions. Second, the focus is on tracing the time optimal path of the relevant decision variables and exploring their inter-temporal dependencies. The entire analysis in this chapter is under the joint consideration of the investment and finance decisions, and is carried out in the stochastic control theory framework. In addition, implications induced from our theoretical model are confirmed by the empirical work. We believe that this study is the first of its kind to provide a theoretical basis for combining the investment decision and the finance decision under uncertainties.

This chapter is organized as follows. In Section 97.2, we develop a theoretical model jointly considering the investment decision and the finance decision. The optimal growth rate is obtained and analyzed. In Section 97.3, we present the optimal dividend payouts under conditions of a static growth rate and a dynamic growth rate. In Section 97.4, we develop three hypotheses and discuss the empirical results of optimal growth rate and dividend payout policy, and the conclusion is in Section 97.5.

## 97.2 The Model

### 97.2.1 Sustainable growth rate with new equity allowed

This section is to generalize Higgins’ (1977) sustainable growth rate with new equity issue allowed. To explore the relationship between the payout policy and the growth rate, we allow that a firm can finance growth by new debt, external equity, and internal equity through retained earnings, and thus leave the growth unconstrained by retained earnings. Our model operates under the usual assumptions of rational investor behavior, zero transactions costs, and the absence of tax differentials between dividends and capital gains and between distributed and undistributed profits. We further assume that the rate of return on equity is nonstationarily distributed and the growth rate varies over time. The model to maximize price is developed under stochastic growth rate assumptions, but first a simplified case under a deterministic time variant growth rate is presented. Under this specification, neither the growth rate nor the level of assets in any time interval are predetermined. Thus, the asset size at time t is

$A(t) = A(0)e^{\int_0^t g(s)ds}, \quad (97.1)$

where $A(0)$ is the initial total asset, $A(t)$ is the total assets at time $t$, $g(t)$ is the time variant growth rate, and $s$ is the proxy of time in the integration.

Assuming a constant leverage ratio exists for a firm, the earnings of this firm can also be defined as a stochastic variable that is the product of the rate of return on equity times the total equity.

$\tilde{Y}(t) = \tilde{ROA}(t)A(t)$
$= \frac{\tilde{r}(t)}{1-L}A(0)(1-L)e^{\int_0^t g(s)ds}$
$= \tilde{r}(t)A'(0)e^{\int_0^t g(s)ds}, \quad (97.2)$

where $\tilde{Y}(t)$ are earnings of the leveraged firm at time $t$, $\tilde{ROA}(t)$ is the rate of return on total asset for a leverage firm at time $t$, $\tilde{r}(t)=\frac{\tilde{ROA}(t)}{1-L}$ is the rate of return on total equity at time $t$, normally distributed with mean $r(t)$ and variance $\sigma^2(t)$, $A'(0)=(1-L)A(0)$ is the total equity at time 0, and $L$ is the debt to total assets ratio.

In the rest of this study, we use the firm’s earnings in terms of rate of return on equity and total equity.

In addition, we denote $\dot{n}(t)=dn(t)/dt$, where $n(t)$ is the number of shares outstanding of common stock at time $t$. We allow that a firm can finance its new investments through retained earnings, external equity, and new debt when it faces a growth opportunity. We also assume a target leverage ratio for a firm by allowing that a firm can issue a proportional debt only to meet its target leverage ratio; therefore, a firm can grow not only by issuing new debt, but also by retaining its earnings and issuing external equity. Then, we can define the change of investment as follows:

$\frac{dA(t)}{dt}=\dot{A}(t)=g(t)A(0)e^{\int_0^t g(s)ds}=\tilde{Y}(t)-\tilde{D}(t)+\lambda\dot{n}(t)p(t)+L\dot{A}(t),\quad(97.3)$

where $\tilde{D}(t)$ is the total dollar dividend at time $t$, $p(t)$ is the price per share at time $t$, $\lambda$ is the degree of market imperfections, $0<\lambda\le1$, $\lambda\dot{n}(t)p(t)$ is the proceeds of new equity issued at time $t$, and $L$ is the debt to total assets ratio.

Note that the value of $\lambda$ equal to one indicates that the new shares can be sold by the firm at current market prices.

Equation (97.3) is a generalized equation which Higgins (1977, 1981, 2008) uses to derive his sustainable growth rate. Higgins’ equation allows only internal source and external debt financing, while our model, equation (97.3) also allows external equity financing.

We can further modify equation (97.3) as follows: $\dot{A}(t)[1-\tilde{r}(t)(1-\tilde{d}(t))]=\tilde{r}(t)\cdot A(t)[1-\tilde{d}(t)]+\frac{\lambda\dot{n}(t)p(t)}{1-L},\quad(97.4)$ where $\tilde{d}(t)$ is the payout ratio at time $t$.

From equation (97.4), we can obtain the generalized sustainable growth rate as follows: $g(t)=\frac{\dot{A}(t)}{A(t)}=\frac{\tilde{r}(t)[1-d(t)]}{1-\tilde{r}(t)[1-d(t)]}+\frac{\lambda\dot{n}(t)p(t)/E(t)}{1-\tilde{r}(t)[1-d(t)]},\quad(97.5)$ where $E(t)$ is the total equity at time $t$.

Equation (97.5) will be reduced to Higgins’ (1977, 1981, 2008) sustainable growth rate if $\dot{n}(t) = 0$. Therefore, our model shows that Higgins’ (1977) sustainable growth rate is underestimated because of the omission of the source of the growth related to new equity issue which is the second term of our model. In Section 97.2.4, we then use the joint optimization approach to determine the optimal growth rate and the optimal payout ratio.

### 97.2.2 Joint optimization of growth rate and payout ratio

From equations (97.2) and (97.3), we can obtain the dividends per share as

$\tilde{d}(t) = \frac{\tilde{D}(t)}{n(t)} = \frac{[\tilde{r}(t) - g(t)] A'(0) e^{\int_0^t g(s) ds} + \lambda \dot{n}(t) p(t)}{n(t)}. \tag{97.6}$

and the mean and variance of the dividends per share can be expressed as

$E[\tilde{d}(t)] = \frac{(\tilde{r}(t) - g(t)) A'(0) e^{\int_0^t g(s) ds} + \lambda \dot{n}(t) p(t)}{n(t)}$
$\operatorname{Var}[\tilde{d}(t)] = \frac{A'(0)^2 \sigma^2(t) e^{2 \int_0^t g(s) ds}}{n^2(t)}. \tag{97.7}$

Also, we postulate a utility function of the following form²

$U[\tilde{d}(t)] = -e^{-a \tilde{d}(t)}, \quad \text{where } a > 0. \tag{97.8}$

From the certainty-equivalent principle and the moment-generating technique,³ the certainty-equivalent dividend stream can be written as

$\hat{d}(t) = \frac{([\tilde{r}(t) - g(t)] A'(0) e^{\int_0^t g(s) ds} + \lambda \dot{n}(t) p(t))}{n(t)} - \frac{a' A'(0)^2 \sigma^2(t) e^{2 \int_0^t g(s) ds}}{n(t)^2}, \tag{97.9}$

where $\hat{d}(t)$ is the certainty equivalent value of $\tilde{d}(t)$ and $a' = a/2$.
