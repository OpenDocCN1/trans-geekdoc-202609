Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002) specifically explored a perpetual American option while Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) focus on strategic option behavior. What is central to Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002) is how expected rate of return and expected time to exercise affect optimality of the equilibrium model. The perpetual illustrated is risk-neutral diffusion, had stopping threshold, density of risk-neutral stopping times and optimal stopping. Project and option returns were taken into account. The results of Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002) show that expected rate of return of an equilibrium model is constant over time, depending on risk-free rate, rate of return dividend yield and volatility of underlying asset. Interestingly, probabilities of real options vary over time; hence, models used to price real options should be flexible in handling the probability to exercise.

Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) argue that if one follows delay investment strategy, the optimal investment time is often later that the first date at which present value of discounted cash flow exceeds zero. They further argue that one cannot bargain without introducing frictions. In building their optimal equilibrium model, they assumed non-strategic firm. Other assumptions include threat of pre-emption, incomplete trigger levels and characteristics consistent with an equilibrium model in incomplete market. The results of Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) show that there are equilibrium mappings and isoelastic cases which are consistent with Pareto distribution. Threat to pre-emption has the same effect as the opportunity cost of waiting to invest. Xu (2006) extended complete option pricing to incomplete markets. In her, she does not eliminate perfect hedging but she allows for partial hedging and risk residual at expiration time. Xu (2006) incorporates two principles in her pricing; (i) martingale measures and (ii) utility pricing. The reason for the latter principle is so that the price should remain the same throughout the trading period.
