We then merge the analyst-firm data with CRSP to obtain stock price and return information, and with Compustat to gather financial records. We use the two-digit Global Industrial Classification Standard (GICS) codes from Compustat in our following analysis, as the GICS classification is commonly used among practitioners (Bhojraj et al., 2003).

# 31.4.2 Measures of network structural hole

We utilize techniques developed in the social science network research to analyze information flows within these annually constructed analyst coverage networks. Specifically, we measure the position of each analyst in the network and focus primarily on analysts who are positioned at “structural holes”. Structural hole is one of the most important concepts from social network research and widely used in psychology and informatics. A structural hole characterizes an absence of bridging ties between two groups. When agents occupy a structural hole, they are able to see early, see more broadly, and translate information across groups. Agents in the position(s) of structural hole are associated with a larger exposure to diverse knowledge base and hence “good ideas” (Burt, 2003). In our context, we expect that analysts occupying structural holes are favorably positioned to be exposed to a diverse and comprehensive set of information in the analyst community.

We choose constraint (Burt, 1992) as our main measure to capture the degree of aggregated information flow of each node in the analyst coverage network. A lower constraint points to analysts with more direct and indirect connections to peers with overlapping research coverage on firms. Peer analysts, referred to as analysts covering the same companies, could provide research coverage on different sets of industries and firms. In our setting, peer analysts can potentially share not only the firm-level information, but also the relevant potential macroeconomic and industry information. In addition, there is a network effect. For example, while analyst A interacts and competes more intensely with peer analysts at other brokerages (i.e., her direct connections), these peers may cover other firms and thus can learn from their own direct connections, so on and so forth. We use structure hole measures to proxy for the size or breadth of an analyst’s information set transmitted in the network, accounting for these higher-order dependencies and effects from an analyst’s indirect connections. In the modern interconnected economy, this type of information may be particularly valuable because seemingly unrelated developments across various parts of the economy may influence one another (Mansharamani, 2012).
