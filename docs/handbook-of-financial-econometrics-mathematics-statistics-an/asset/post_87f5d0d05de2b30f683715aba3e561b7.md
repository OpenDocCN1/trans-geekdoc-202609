Table 21.4, Panel A shows our MCLP model results using Altman's 5 variables. In our study, we tried the MCLP data mining approach using Altman's 5 variables with a larger number of control firms since Altman's approach of using the same number of control firms is criticized by Ohlson and others for upward bias of the prediction error rate. The overall prediction rate was 87.2%. Altman reported a 95% prediction rate in his original study, but this rate dropped to 78.4% in the 1980's holdout sample (Begley et al., 1996). From these results, our MCLP model shows that our approach is a lot more efficient and effective in bankruptcy prediction using only financial data even in a financial crisis. In particular, our model had a 13.8% Type-I error rate. Type-I errors are misclassifications of bankrupt firms as non-bankrupt, and Type-II errors are misclassifications of non-bankrupt firms as bankrupt. In the case of bankruptcy prediction, the costs of Type-I errors are more significant than the costs of Type-II errors. For example, investors and creditors suffer actual economic damage from the bankrupt firms because of Type-I errors, but there will be no actual damage from the Type-II errors (Takahashi et al., 1984).

From the results of Panel B of Table 21.4, we find that Ohlson's 9 variable model is less effective than Altman's model since the overall prediction rate is 83.6%. For Ohlson's model, the Type-I error is higher than Altman's model. Panel C of Table 21.4 reports a combination of both model and overall prediction rate of 88.7%. As expected, this is the best model and the Type-I error rate is only 12.3%. Overall, our MCLP data mining model is very efficient and prediction rates are reasonably good compared to other models.

### 21.2.5 Summary and conclusions

In this paper, we used the MCLP data mining approach to predict bankruptcy and compared our results with the results of Altman's and Ohlson's models using 1997--2003 financial data of Korean bankrupt firms after the 1997 financial crisis. The MCLP data mining approach has recently been applied to credit card portfolio management (Shi et al., 2002). This approach has proved to be practical and powerful even for a large sample size using a huge financial database. The results of the MCLP approach in our bankruptcy prediction study are promising as this approach performs better than the traditional multiple discriminant analysis of Altman's (1968) or Ohlson's (1980) logit analysis using financial data. Similar approaches can be applied to other accounting areas such as fraud detection, detection of tax evasion, and an audit-planning tool for financially distressed firms.

One of the limitations of our study is using only 1997--2003 bankruptcy data in Korea after a financial crisis. However, as previous bankruptcy studies have shown, there will be downward biases for using financial data in times of turmoil. Another limitation is bankrupt firm data are mostly from 1997 and 1998 and our results cannot be extended to other time periods or other countries. Therefore, caution should be taken in the interpretation of our results.

Our next research will compare our MCLP data mining approach with the decision tree approach to see which approach is more efficient and effective in bankruptcy prediction.
