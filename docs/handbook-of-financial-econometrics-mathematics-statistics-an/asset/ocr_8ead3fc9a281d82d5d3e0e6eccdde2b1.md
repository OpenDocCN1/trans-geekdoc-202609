Another strain of research focuses on how the actual company that takes the shares public examines the role that management plays in the issuance of the IPO shares and the role that founders play. For example, Boone et al. (2016) indicate that firms that redact information prior to their issuance experience greater underpricing at the issuance, but over the long-run, they earn greater abnormal returns when compared to non-redacting firms; the assumption is that the redaction is an indication that these firms are protecting proprietary information from their rivals. Wang and Song (2016) focus specifically on the relationship between the founding owners of the firm and their board structure and find that as the non-founder component of the firm increases initially the level of the IPO price premium increase, but only to an extent because too much non-founder board representation leads to a decrease in the initial IPO price premium. This seems to indicate that founders have to have sufficient representation on the board to help steer decisions, but there is an optimal level of non-founder board members that provide investors and underwriters with the belief that both fractions of the board will keep the other inline and make good decisions for the firm. Boulton and Campbell (2016) illustrate that overconfident managers tend to use IPO underpricing as a signal of firm quality, which indicates that there is a relationship between managerial confidence and IPO underpricing, and that these managers access the capital markets quicker and more often following an initial offering; hence, overconfident managers use underpricing as a signaling process to investors.
