Nonlinearity is one typical feature of bank risk dependence. The opposite of nonlinearity is linearity, which is one of the most ubiquitous concepts in modern finance (Embrechts et al., 1999). While being computationally convenient and straightforward to understand, linear correlations usually first come to mind when modeling dependence. However, the hasty assumption of linearity is quite problematic because the dependent real-world risks are always far from elliptical distributions (Embrechts et al., 1999; Weiß and Scheffer, 2015). As banks get involved with an increasing number of complex businesses and products, there is mounting evidence that bank risk dependencies are outside the linear category (Brockmann and Kalkbrener, 2010).

Tail dependence is another typical feature of bank risk dependence, which is a very important feature. Tail dependence measures the probability that all risk types are in their extremes. There is strong evidence of tail dependence in finance (Weiß and Scheffer, 2015; Koliai, 2016). The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision has observed that large losses, either from different risk types or within the same risk type, tend to strike simultaneously during stress situations (BCBS, 2010). Hu (2006) also expects significantly stronger dependence when risks are at their extremes. Moreover, tail dependence has now attracted broad attention partly because Value at Risk (VaR) has become one of the most popular tools for risk measurement (Jorion, 2007). As a quantile-based measure, VaR is tightly related to the tail structure of risk distribution. Consequently, a clear trend observed among practitioners is the desire to capture tail dependence more accurately in risk integration models (BCBS, 2010).

Tail asymmetry is the third typical feature of bank risk dependence. Tail dependence can be subdivided into upper tail dependence and lower tail dependence, and these two do not always exist concurrently. Even if that occurs, they are often not identical to each other. It is commonly observed that financial markets are much more likely to crash together than to boom together (Bartram and Wang, 2015). In bad times, there are more adverse risk outcomes observed than there are beneficial outcomes observed during good times (BCBS, 2010). Chen and Tu (2013) also note that bank risks always exhibit more joint negative extremes than joint positive extremes.
