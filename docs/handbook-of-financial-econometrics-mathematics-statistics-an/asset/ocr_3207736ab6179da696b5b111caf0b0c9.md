\hat{x}_{t+1} = s_t = \alpha x_t + (1 - \alpha) s_{t-1}, \tag{26.15}

where \hat{x}_{t+1} is the next period's forecast value. In other words, \hat{x}_{t+1} is expressed in terms of the smoothing constant times x_t plus (1 - \alpha) times s_{t-1}.

If the manager of a company in 1990 (t = 1) knows only that current sales of his or her company equal x_1 = 5,000 units and that current sales have been forecasted as s_0 = 5,100 units, then he or she can use equation (26.15) to forecast 1991 sales. If we choose \alpha = 0.30 as a smoothing constant, then the sales for 1991 are forecasted in terms of equation (26.15) as

\hat{x}_2 = s_1 = (0.30)(5,000) + (1 - 0.30)(5,100) = 5,070 units

Rewriting equation (26.15) as

\hat{x}_{t+1} = s_t = s_{t-1} + \alpha (x_t - s_{t-1}), \tag{26.16}

implies that simple exponential smoothing is the weighted average of s_{t-1} and the forecast error (x_t - s_{t-1}) with weights of 1 and \alpha, respectively. The term exponential smoothing refers to the fact that s_t can be expressed as a weighted average with exponentially decreasing weights, as we now illustrate.

We substitute the expressions for s_{t-1} and s_{t-2} into the expression for s_t as defined in equation 26.15 and obtain

s_{t-1} = \alpha x_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha) s_{t-2}

s_{t-2} = \alpha x_{t-2} + (1 - \alpha) s_{t-3}.

Repeatedly substituting s_{t-2} and s_{t-1} into equation (26.15) reveals that

s_t = \alpha x_t + (1 - \alpha) s_{t-1}
= \alpha x_t + \alpha (1 - \alpha) x_{t-1} + (1 + \alpha)^2 s_{t-2}
= \alpha x_t + \alpha (1 - \alpha) x_{t-1} + \alpha (1 - \alpha)^2 x_{t-2} + (1 - \alpha)^3 s_{t-3}.

Continuous substitution for s_{t-k}, where k = 2,3,...,t, yields

s_t = \left[ \alpha \sum_{k=0}^{t-1} (1 - \alpha)^k x_{t-k} \right] + (1 - \alpha)^t s_0 \quad (0 < \alpha < 1), \tag{26.17}

where s_0 is an initial estimate of the smoothed value.
