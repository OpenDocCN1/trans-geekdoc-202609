## Appendix 33A: Brief Review of Stochastic Volatility Models

Volatility was identified as the focal quantity for option pricing by Black and Scholes (1973). In the Black-Scholes framework volatility is assumed to be a deterministic process. Randomness entered the model via the Brownian motion driving the underlying asset. Later contributions, starting with Merton (1976) recognized the presence of jumps. Another strand of research, initiated by the constant elasticity of variance (CEV) model of Cox (1975), made stochastic volatility an adapted stochastic process and investigated different functional forms for volatility. A different approach to option pricing allows the variance process to be influenced by its own source of randomness. The general form of the stochastic volatility model (without jump components) is:

```
dS_t = r_{RN} S_t dt + f(S_t, V_t) dW_{s,t}^Q,

dV_t = μ_V(V_t) dt + g(V_t) dW_{v,t}^Q,

dW_{s,t}^Q dW_{v,t}^Q = ρ dt, (33A.1)
```

where r_{RN} is the risk-neutral drift parameter, f(S_t, V_t) is a diffusion function in the equation for the underlying asset, and g(V_t) is a diffusion function in the equation for the variance, W_{i,t}^Q (i = {s, v}) are Brownian motions under the risk-neutral measure Q and ρ is the instantaneous correlation coefficient.

Hull and White (1987), Scott (1987), and Wiggins (1987) are the original contributions that launched the research into stochastic volatility. While Hull and White (1987) take both the stock and variance processes to be lognormal, Scott (1987) postulates an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process for the variance with μ_V(V_t) = -λ(V_t - v̄) and g(V_t) = γ, where λ, v̄ and γ are positive constants.

Stochastic volatility models gained broad recognition among the academics and industry professionals with the Heston (1993) model, which became almost synonymous with the entire class of stochastic volatility models. The Heston model utilizes the Cox et al. (1985) process for variance, that is, μ_V(V_t) = −λ(V_t − v̄) and g(V_t) = η√V_t with a constant η > 0. Heston (1993) obtains a solution for European call and put options in terms of integrals in the complex plane. Guo and Hung (2007), Lord (2010), and Lord and Kahl (2010) investigate further refinements to the representation of the Heston model characteristic function with the goal of improving the numerical stability of the solutions for option prices. The approach of Carr and Madan (1999), based on the use of the fast Fourier transform further streamlines the computational algorithm for Heston model calibration to observed market prices.

Another prominent variant of the stochastic volatility model is SABR of Hagan et al. (2002), which has the form:

```
dF_t^T = a_t (F_t^T)^β dW_{s,t}^Q

da_t = η a_t dW_{a,t}^Q

 dW_{s,t}^Q dW_{a,t}^Q = ρ dt
(33A.2)
```

where F_t^T is a T forward price, a_t — a volatility process, W_{i,t}^Q (i = {s, a}) are Brownian motions under the risk-neutral measure Q, β and η are parameters. Note that the second equation of the SABR framework (33A.2) is for a volatility process, rather than for a variance process. Moreover, the term a_t (F_t^T)^β in the forward price equation effectively makes SABR a combination of the CEV and stochastic volatility models.

Christoffersen et al. (2009) further expanded the class of stochastic volatility models by including multiple factors driving the variance process, effectively yielding the following specification:

```
dS_t = r_{RN} S_t dt + √V_{1,t} S_t dW_{s,1,t}^Q + √V_{2,t} S_t dW_{s,2,t}^Q

dV_{1,t} = −λ_1 (V_{1,t} − v̄_1) dt + η_1 √V_{1,t} dW_{v,1,t}^Q

dV_{2,t} = −λ_2 (V_{2,t} − v̄_2) dt + η_2 √V_{2,t} dW_{v,2,t}^Q

dW_{s,1,t}^Q dW_{v,1,t}^Q = ρ_1 dt      dW_{s,2,t}^Q dW_{v,2,t}^Q = ρ_2 dt

dW_{s,1,t}^Q dW_{s,2,t}^Q = 0          dW_{s,1,t}^Q dW_{v,2,t}^Q = 0

dW_{s,2,t}^Q dW_{v,1,t}^Q = 0          dW_{v,1,t}^Q dW_{v,2,t}^Q = 0
(33A.3)
```

where λ_i, v̄_i, η_i, ρ_i (i = {1,2}) are parameters and W_{i,j,t}^Q (i = {s,v}, j = {1,2}) are Brownian motions under the risk-neutral measure Q. In specification (33A.3), variance process is the sum of two modeled processes, V_{1,t} and V_{2,t}.

Another approach is taken by Jones (2003) which generalizes the Heston model by making the instantaneous correlation, ρ, in (33A.1) an adapted stochastic process driven by variance, V_t. However, Lee and Sokolinskiy (2015) demonstrate that even a restricted version of the model may become tricky to calibrate.

Following the success of the Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) model in econometric applications, Heston and Nandi (2000) develop a GARCH-type option pricing model with a closed-form solution for European options. An interesting feature of the approach in Heston and Nandi (2000) is its formulation in a discrete trading setup.

Jump activity may be integrated into a stochastic volatility to achieve a better fit to the shorter maturity portion of the implied volatility surface. In particular, the SVJ model (see Gatheral, 2006) allows for jumps in the price process:

dS_t = r_{RN} S_t dt + √V_t S_t dW_{s,t}^Q + (e^{α+δ ε_t} − 1) S_t dN_t,

dV_t = −λ(V_t − v̄) dt + η√V_t dW_{v,t}^Q,

dW_{s,t}^Q dW_{v,t}^Q = ρ dt,  (33A.4)

where dN_t is a Poisson process, ε_t is standard normal random variable, α and δ are jump size parameters. Matytsin (1999) and Matytsin (2000) consider the SVJJ model that accommodates jumps in both the price and variance processes.

Finally, Lorig et al. (2015) develops an asymptotic expansion for the implied volatility surface, which allows a general stochastic volatility model to be fitted directly to an observed implied volatility surface.

## 34.1 Introduction

For intraday investors (i.e., traders) in option markets, the key issue is how to price, hedge and calibrate models in order to maximize investment opportunities. This chapter focuses on three issues on an entropic two-asset option (from here e2AO). Two-asset option-commonly known as exchange option, is one of the most complex exotic options partly because of the two underlying assets. Then, e2AO is even greater in complexity than exchange option because the former is entropic in nature. Entropic means the option is more risky because of risk(s) associated with stock jumps. Further, two-asset option is normally priced in a continuous manner but e2AO is in a discrete form. Discrete pricing works well hedging derivatives but see Hutchinson et al., 1994. Secondly, converting a continuous option into a discrete form might increase basis risk (Trigeorgis, 1996). The big question is what is the distribution of options and related parameters when there are stock jumps?

Numerous empirical studies have illustrated that stock returns including related products (i.e., options) are leptokurtic or fat-tailed (Yan, 2011). Further, she states that one of the causes of these fat-tails is stock jumps.

Normally, when there are more than one stocks put together, the jumps get even higher. One of the financial products or assets that are characterized by higher stocks jumps are two-asset options or exchange options. The phenomenon where volatility is high due to interaction of more than one asset can be illustrated earlier by Masset and Wallmeier (2010).

Exchange options can be traced back to William Margrabe in 1978. Despite the Margrabe (1978) formula, it falls short when pricing options in incomplete markets. One of the reasons for the latter statement is that the Margrabe formula is a continuous equation which is not necessarily true in reality especially when pricing real options.
