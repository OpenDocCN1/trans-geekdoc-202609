The model is driven by two stochastic processes and it had two important features, stochastic volatility and price jumps. Further, the model incorporated a premium for jump-risk uncertainty and the model focused mainly on risk premium for jump-size uncertainty. For estimation purposes, Pan (2002) used implied state-generalized method for moments (GMM) estimators and optimal moment selection. The models used represent jump-risk premia (SVJ0), volatility-risk premia (SV) and no risk premia (SV0). On all three models, Pan (2002) performed joint actual estimations and risk-neutral dynamics using the time series.

The data used in Pan (2002) is jointly spot and option data from the Berkeley Options Data Base (BODB) on complete trading activity on the floor of CBOE. The data is from January 1989 to December 1996, on weekly trading days. The results of Pan (2002) show that volatility-risk premium is positive and significant for the SV model. And there are tensions between the spot and option prices in SV0 model. When the volatility-risk premium doubles to 20% from 10%, jump-risk premium almost doubles, and volatility risk increases by small amounts. She went further and simultaneously estimated jump and volatility risk premiums. Both types of risk premia are positive. Further, risk premia is present in underlying stock prices. Broadly, equity risk premium compensates for risk aversion to jumps. The skewness of the curves tends to have fat tails (i.e., high kurtosis). The jumps were more diverse with no specific patterns.

Eraker (2004) examine how different volatility models react to stock price dynamics and from joint options and stock market data. Although joint options are dissimilar, but the options phenomenon are one, it brings a bit of similarity in between them. According to Eraker (2004), prior studies on time-varying volatility models and jump models have not proven their suitability in practice. And the jumps had no impact on stock prices. The data used in Eraker (2004) is on sample of S&P500 options contracts. In this sample, daily closing prices from CBOE. The full sample is made up of 22,500 options and he narrowed it down to 3,270 contracts recorded over 1,006 trading days covering the period of January 1, 1987 to December 31, 1990. The reason why the sample was randomly narrowed down is because large datasets require excessive computing times. The pricing models varied from the basic stochastic volatility (SV) model, the stochastic volatility with jumps (SVJ) model, the stochastic volatility model that allows volatility to jump (SVCJ) to the stochastic volatility with state-dependent, correlated jumps (SVSCJ). The SVSCJ is a general case.
