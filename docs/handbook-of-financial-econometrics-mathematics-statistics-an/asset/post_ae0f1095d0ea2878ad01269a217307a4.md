
Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002) specifically explored a perpetual American option while Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) focus on strategic option behavior. What is central to Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002) is how expected rate of return and expected time to exercise affect optimality of the equilibrium model. The perpetual option illustrated is risk-neutral diffusion, has a stopping threshold, density of risk-neutral stopping times and optimal stopping. Project and option returns were taken into account. The results of Shackleton and Wojakowski (2002) show that expected rate of return of an equilibrium model is constant over time, depending on risk-free rate, rate of return, dividend yield and volatility of underlying asset. Interestingly, probabilities of real options vary over time; hence, models used to price real options should be flexible in handling the probability to exercise.

Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) argue that if one follows a delayed investment strategy, the optimal investment time is often later than the first date at which present value of discounted cash flow exceeds zero. They further argue that one cannot bargain without introducing frictions. In building their optimal equilibrium model, they assumed a non-strategic firm. Other assumptions include threat of pre-emption, incomplete trigger levels and characteristics consistent with an equilibrium model in an incomplete market. The results of Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) show that there are equilibrium mappings and isoelastic cases which are consistent with Pareto distribution. Threat to pre-emption has the same effect as the opportunity cost of waiting to invest. After calibration, the value of ratio³ shows that when two firms have identical costs, complete information option values are destroyed indeed. According to Lambrecht and Perraudin (2003) the latter statement implies that option values are higher when markets are incomplete. Further, stock jumps in a firm illustrate that “profit risk” is positively correlated with the firm but negatively correlated with the competitor. Finally, on average negative jumps are smaller than positive ones.

Xu (2006) extended complete option pricing to incomplete markets. In her, she does not eliminate perfect hedging but she allows for partial hedging and risk residual at expiration time. Xu (2006) incorporates two principles in her pricing; (i) martingale measures and (ii) utility pricing. The reason for the latter principle is so that the price should remain the same throughout the trading period. For the modeling of the risk measure, she (i) set up the model, (ii) defined the risk measure pricing, and (iii) built the properties of risk measure. In setting up the model, it assumed that the whole trading process follows a stochastic process, i.e., the spot price follows a local martingale process, the probability of risk measure for liability and payoff lies in between positive and negative infinity. In defining the risk measure, selling and buying prices were outlined. And in building the risk measure, different lemmas were incorporated for the pricing. The prices were outlined such that they are bounded by super- and sub-hedging strategies. Further, she embedded utility theory in her pricing. The analysis explored how one can price with a shortfall risk measure where the shortfall is the smallest amount injected to minimize the liability in order for the trading position to be acceptable. The results illustrate that her approach is subjective and each firm can tailor its trading strategy, including risk control. Further, a trader who takes a high risk, she is not necessarily better off than traders who are risk averse. On the other hand, where there is buying and selling of securities, Xu's (2006) approach provides a better methodology. Broadly, her results are applicable to European options.

Gârleanu et al. (2009) modeled demand–pressure effects on option prices. The crux of their study is competitive pricing of options for risk-averse dealers when risk is imperfect hedgeable. In Gârleanu et al. (2009), dealers can arbitrarily trade option contracts at discrete time. The agents that are covered in this study are end-users. According to them, the demand–pressure should affect option prices and should lead to optimal hedges having positive payoff. They estimate that larger dealer positions should lead to profits and/or losses varying between $100 million and –$100 million with a cumulative amount approximately $800 million over a six-year sample. The model for demand pressure is discrete-time infinite-horizon economy. Prices of securities are endogenously determined. Moreover, Gârleanu et al. (2009) assumed that economy is populated by two agents: dealers and end-users. Firstly, they have constant absolute utility function and their “linear” wealth evolved. Further, they assumed that prices are underpinned by competitive Markov process.

The first lemma in Gârleanu et al. (2009) assumed that prices are determined at equilibrium level. Followed by two propositions; proposition one is on demand pressure for end-users and proposition two is when demand is zero, given a certain price. The theoretical results on effects of demand pressure illustrate that demand affects option prices. On a hedge position, based on theorem one, they found that hedge position has an optimal level with a certain expectation. The pricing kernel is “unhedgeable”. And the pricing kernel decreases the price of derivative which in turn entices the dealer to buy the derivative. They also looked at convexity, which shows that convexity of the option should be similar to the underlying asset. In order to see the total price that end-users pay for their demand at different times, they vectorize the derivatives. Vectorization shows that the price end-users pay is dependent on demand pressure.

Gârleanu et al. (2009) also looked at specific cases; (i) inability to hedge continuously, (ii) jumps in underlying price, and (iii) stochastic volatility risk. The latter illustrations are to assist with implementation. The first case, (i) shows that “unhedgeable” position changes, (ii) exemplifies imperfect hedging characterized by high jumps, and (iii) shows that hedging is more complicated and realism needs to be added to the model. On equilibrium numbers of dealers, they assumed that aggregate dealer is risk averse. For the empirical section, data is from three sources; (i) closing short and long open interest on all SPX options from the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) during the period of 1996 to 2001, (ii) Ivy DB dataset from OptionMetrics LLC for the same period as data on the CBOE, and (iii) daily returns obtained from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP).

The empirical results Gârleanu et al. (2009) illustrate that net demand is concentrated at moneyness when puts are out-of-the-money (OTM). The non-market demand is large negative. Secondly, the demand across the entire moneyness affects implied volatilities. Thus, demand affects prices, which translates into volatility. Thirdly, demand is positively correlated with kappa. Fourthly, the risk increases with demand. Finally, options Sharpe ratios were as high as 0.85. That is, there are alphas in these options. Note that Sharpe ratios higher than 0.50 occur rarely. Index options have high positive end-user demand while equity options on average seem to have negative end-user demand.

## 34.2.2 The effects of stock jumps on securities

### 34.2.2.1 Dynamics of financial events or parameters

The first article that explored the effect of stock price jumps on options is Merton (1976). According to Merton (1976), the BS solution is inappropriate when price dynamics are non-stochastic in nature. He argues that jumps in stock prices are due to (i) normal variations in price, and (ii) abnormal variations due to arrival of new information. Note that both the points put forward by Merton (1976) have nothing to do with incompleteness of stock markets, which this chapter takes into account. In modeling the stock price jumps, assume that prices follow geometric BM. Moreover, stock jumps are either specific to the firm or industry. He went further and illustrated probabilities in the presence of jumps and in the absence of jumps.
