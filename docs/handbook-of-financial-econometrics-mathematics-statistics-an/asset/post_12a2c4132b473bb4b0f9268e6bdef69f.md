Qu’s (2011) testing method is based on information from the periodogram, spectral density frequency and the local Whittle likelihood function given by (see Künsch, 1987)

$$Q(G, d) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \left\{ \log G \theta_j^{-2d} + \frac{I(\theta_j)}{G \lambda_j^{-2d}} \right\}, \qquad (58A.3)$$

where $I(\theta_j) = \frac{(\sum_{t=1}^{T} V_t \exp(i\theta_j t))^2}{2\pi T}$ is the periodogram of downside risk, $V_t$, evaluated at the frequencies $\theta_j = \frac{2\pi j}{T}$, $j \in (l, m)$ where $l$ is a trimming parameter discarding the lowest frequencies and $m$ is a bandwidth parameter, $T$ is the sample size. As suggested by Qu (2011), we set the bandwidth to $m = T^{0.7}$ and the trimming proportion is set to 0.02 and 0.05, respectively. $\hat{d}$ is estimated by maximizing the above likelihood function. The test statistic is then defined as

$$W = \sup \left( \sum_{j=1}^{m} v_j^2 \right)^{-1/2} \left| \sum_{j=1}^{m} v_j \left( \frac{I(\theta_j)}{G(\hat{d}) \theta_j^{-2\hat{d}}} - 1 \right) \right|, \qquad (58A.4)$$

where $v_j = \log \theta_j - m^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \log \theta_j$, and $G(\hat{d}) = m^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \log \theta_j^{2\hat{d}} I(\theta_j)$. From Table 58.1 of Qu (2011), the critical values of the $W$ test for the 2% trimming proportion are 1.118 and 1.252 at the 10% and 5% significance level, respectively. The critical values of the $W$ test for the 5% trimming proportion are 1.022 and 1.155 at the 10% and 5% significance level, respectively. The test statistics show that the true long memory hypothesis for Thailand and Malaysia is rejected because it entails a spurious long memory. This means that the downside risks of Thailand and Malaysia are essentially characterized by a short memory process.

## Abstract

This study uses recurrent survival analysis technique to show that higher spread of conversion-stock prices and higher buy-back ratio of stock repurchase provide the CBs' debt-like signals of Constantinides and Grundy (1989); while lower risk-free rate, higher capital expenditures, higher non-management institutional ownership and higher total asset value provide the CBs' equity-like signals of Stein (1992). While the equity-like signals might accelerate the rate of sequential conversions and weaken the CBs' risk-mitigating effect in the presence of risk-shifting potential, this study shows that this can happen only in a financially healthy firm with higher free cash flow. For financially distressed firms, the CBs' risk-mitigating effect is maintained.

Convertible bonds (CBs) are often adopted as a financing vehicle for firms with higher levels of agency and adverse selection costs associated with information asymmetry (see, e.g., Loncarski et al., 2006; Dutordoir et al., 2014). By reducing the agency and adverse selection costs, the CBs can serve as a risk-mitigating instrument (see, e.g., Green, 1984; Brennan and Kraus, 1987; Brennan and Schwartz, 1988; Constantinides and Grundy, 1989; see, e.g., Frierman and Viswanath, 1994; Mayers, 1998, 2000; Isagawa, 2002; Cornelli and Yosha, 2003). In Green (1984), when the investment opportunity and the CB issue are contemporaneous, it was shown the issue of CBs can resolve the agency cost problem raised by Jensen and Meckling (1976) by curbing shareholders' incentives for risk-shifting in a one-period setting. More evidences can be found by Lewis et al. (1998, 1999, 2003), Krishnaswami and Yaman (2008), Dorion et al. (2014), and King and Mauer (2014). Likewise, it was argued that CBs can serve as a sequential-financing tool to reduce the agency cost problem when the firm has a sequence of investment opportunities (see, e.g., Mayers, 1998, 2000; Cornelli and Yosha, 2003; Wang, 2009), as evidenced by Chang et al. (2004) and Korkeamaki and Moore (2004).

On the other hand, the CBs can help in reducing the adverse selection costs associated with information asymmetry on the perceived risks of the firms between managers and shareholders (see, e.g., Brennan and Kraus, 1987; Brennan and Schwartz, 1988; Cornelli and Yosha, 2003), as evidenced by an in-depth interview with convertible bond issuers by Dong et al. (2013). As a risk-mitigating instrument, the CBs can serve as an alternative to straight debt (see, e.g., Lewis et al., 1999). In a survey analysis of Billingsley and Smith (1996), most managers use CBs to obtain favorable coupon rates on bond financing. In Lee *et al.* (2009), firms with higher agency cost of expropriation minority shareholders’ wealth are more likely to issue debt-like CBs. In this respect, the CBs should have debt-like features. In Europe, CB issuers tend to be larger firms having the potentials to issue debt-like securities, and the CB market is perceived as an extension to the bond market by the CB investors (see, e.g., Dutordoir and Van de Gucht, 2009). In contrast, other survey studies found that the majority of managers issue CBs in the hope of eventually converting them into equity (see, e.g., Graham and Harvey, 2001; Brounen *et al.*, 2006). This result supports Stein (1992) that CBs serve as back-door equity issues to help in reducing the adverse selection costs associated with information asymmetry (see, e.g., Kim, 1990; Stein, 1992; Nyborg, 1995; Davidson *et al.*, 1995; Brown *et al.*, 2012; Lyandres and Zhdanov, 2014). In this respect, the CBs should have equity-like features. This is highlighted in an investment-based theoretical framework by Lyandres and Zhdanov (2014) that the issuance of CBs alleviates the underinvestment problem of Myers (1977).

Be it debt-like or equity-like, in response to the adverse selection cost associated with information asymmetry, both Constantinides and Grundy (1989) and Stein (1992) emphasize on the signaling role of CBs to the market so that efficient investments can be achieved. However, there is a significant difference between the models by Constantinides and Grundy (1989) and Stein (1992). In Constantinides and Grundy (1989), the issue of CBs must be accompanied with stock repurchase and investment financing. Spatt and Sterbenz (1988) further argued that if the firm uses the proceeds from the CB issues to repurchase common stocks and/or to increase the firm’s capital/investment expenditures, a gain from hoarding can arise and, as a result, the rate of conversions is slowed down. Further evidence can be found in an empirical study that the issue of CBs represents a positive signal if it enables the firm to make profitable investments and to optimize its financial structure (see, e.g., Gillet and de La Bruslerie, 2010).

In contrast to Constantinides and Grundy (1989), Stein (1992) argued that CBs are issued by firms as back-door equity with the expectation to have more equity to eventually attain a less-levered capital structure. It is less likely the proceeds of the CB issues are to be used for stock repurchase (see, e.g., Loncarski *et al.*, 2006), and a shorter time to conversion facilitates the firm to acquire the equity financing it needs. Under this rationale, the stronger the signaling effect of the CB issues, the shorter the time for the CBs to become attractive to be converted (Davidson *et al.*, 1995). This is in line with Nyborg (1995) that the benefits of CBs as back-door equity issues are preserved only if conversion is voluntary before their optimal conversion time. On the other hand, the CBs’ risk-mitigating effect can be weakened if the CBs are converted voluntarily as the risk-shifting opportunities arise before their optimal conversion time. This is due to the possibilities that the CB bondholders who convert their bonds before the optimal conversion time can rally the initial shareholders to restore incentives towards risk at the expense of the straight bondholders (Francois et al., 2011). It can be expected that the higher the firm’s risk-shifting potential, the weaker the risk-mitigating effect of the equity-like CB issues.

In literature, the optimal conversion time occurs at the maturity or at the call in a perfect market with no dividend payments and constant conversion terms (see, e.g., Ingersoll, 1977), or immediately prior to a dividend payment date if there are dividend payments (see, e.g., Brennan and Schwartz, 1977, 1980). Given the optimal conversion time, it is implicitly assumed that conversions should occur in a block, i.e., all CBs are converted completely or not at all. However, as CB issues are divisible and held by competitive bondholders, the conversions of CBs take place sequentially instead of in a block over the CB’s lifecycle (see, e.g., Buhler and Koziol, 2004). These observed sequential conversions might be an outcome of Nash equilibrium in a non-cooperative game played by competitive bondholders (see, e.g., Emanuel, 1983; Constantinides, 1984; Constantinides and Rosenthal, 1984; Francois et al., 2011). The potential advantage of sequential conversions over a block conversion had been addressed under the conditions: (a) the presence of straight debt vs. no straight debt, and (b) the distribution of CBs’ ownerships. When there is no straight debt in the capital structure, a sequential conversion strategy is optimal for a monopolist bondholder even without regular dividend payments (see, e.g., Emanuel, 1983; Constantinides, 1984; Constantinides and Rosenthal, 1984). When there are straight debts in the capital structure, Buhler and Koziol (2004) showed that the values of the CBs held by competitive bondholders adopting sequential conversions are greater than those under a block conversion. This is due to the fact that a conversion impacts the default probability of the straight bonds, which results in the wealth transfer between stockholders and straight debtholders.
