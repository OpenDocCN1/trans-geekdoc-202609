
## 60.2 Relevant Literature

Merton (1974) suggested a credit risk model that explains how the probability of company default can be inferred from the market valuation of companies under some assumptions on asset and loan values evolution. More specifically, the event of default is determined by the market value of the firm’s assets jointly with the firm’s liability structure. If the value of the assets falls below a threshold, then the firm is considered to be in default. Many authors such as Black and Cox (1976), Geske (1977), Longstaff and Schwartz (1995), and Collin-Dufresne and Goldstein (2001) have expanded Merton’s model. Also, there exist a number of studies which compare the performance of alternative models but fail to establish their superiority to Merton’s model (see Eom et al., 2002; Gemmill, 2002).

Merton’s model uses a formula for credit spread which is based on asset growth, asset volatility and leverage as the key economic drivers for bankruptcy. The best measure to capture a firm’s asset value is through the firm’s equity value. The firm issues a zero coupon bond and equity shares to finance its assets. The leverage shows the bounds of default. When the assets’ value falls below the default boundary then the firm defaults. According to Christie (1982), the changes in the degree of financial leverage are related to the bank’s stock return (also see Alexander and Kaeck, 2008). If stock returns are positive, leverage will decrease, leading to lower credit spreads and hence a negative relationship between stocks returns and credit risk is expected.

A firm’s probability of default may be influenced by the firm value volatility. Benkert (2004) tests different volatility measures on CDS spreads and concludes that option-implied volatility is the most appropriate one. Merton’s model suggests that the higher the asset volatility, the higher credit spreads are going to be because the probability of default is greater. Chen et al. (2007) use historical volatilities while Cao et al. (2010) show that the implied volatility of individual stock options has significant explanatory power for CDS spreads. Alexander and Kaeck (2008), have tested historical volatility against implied volatility indices and conclude that the latter performs better.

In Merton (1974), the risk-free interest rate constitutes the drift in the risk neutral world. His model suggests that the risk-free interest rate should decrease the probability of default as the higher the rate is, the higher the risk neutral drift will be. Interest rates are positively linked to economic growth and higher growth should, ceteris paribus, imply lower default risk (see Tang and Yan, 2006). Another way to rationalize the importance of risk-free interest rate in pricing of credit risk is through the excess bank risk-taking behavior in an environment of low interest rates. Many researchers have blamed the low interest rate environment for the GFC and the euro-area sovereign debt crisis. The combination of low interest rates and the abundant liquidity led financial institutions to take excessive risks and increase leverage. This theory touches upon the role of the loose monetary policy; observers argue that a more aggressive policy about interest rates would have prevented the development of the bubble that led to the GFC. Rajan (2006) investigates whether a low interest rate period may drive banks to search for higher yields by adopting a riskier behavior. Delis and Kouretas (2011) use various interest rates to investigate the relationship between interest rates and bank risk-taking and conclude that the short-term rates are negatively and strongly related to the bank risk-taking. Houweling and Vorst (2005), Hull et al. (2004) support that short-term rates have stronger impact on bank risk-taking than longer term interest rates since they refer to very liquid assets, they have no short sale constraints and they are not influenced by special tax regulations (see Clarida et al. 2001).

Although there have been numerous studies on the determinants of corporate CDS spreads, until recently, the number of cases where structural models had been applied for understanding the formation of credit risk in the banking sector has been small. The reason is often cited as being the high leverage ratios under which banks operate which would exaggerate the "credit-spread" puzzle. Also, capital ratios were considered as not being informational or useful since they were determined in compliance to the capital ratios regulatory demands and were susceptible to manipulations due to the regulatory capital arbitrage policies followed by many banks (e.g., Admati and Hellwig, 2013).

Recent studies on banks’ credit risk have attempted to expand the structural model in, at least, two directions; the first one introduces common risk factors extracted from financial markets data while the second one introduces balance sheet items expressing idiosyncratic risks. The importance of market variables in improving the explanatory power of the structural models can hardly be refuted. For instance, Eichengreen et al. (2012) applied a principal components analysis on the CDS prices of a number of international banks and showed that even in normal times four factors explain a high share of CDS spreads’ variation. Moreover, the CDS spreads were strongly associated with short-term global economic prospects which were best expressed by the US high yield spreads. Annaert et al. (2013) have also highlighted the importance of market wide factors such as the slope of the term structure, the general business climate, the market wide volatility as well as the liquidity at the aggregate and individual level, in the specification of structural credit risk models for banks. More importantly, they showed that the determinants of banks’ CDS spreads varied strongly across time and that the variables suggested by the structural model became important during the crisis period.

On the other hand, balance sheet variables have been introduced in a number of other studies where their importance has been established although there are differences on the type of financial ratios that are found to be relevant for the pricing of bank CDS contracts. Chiaramonte and Casu (2012) relied only on balance sheet ratios in order to reveal their relevance for the CDS spreads. Their main results were that first, CDS spreads reflected the risk captured by balance sheet ratios and that their importance varied across time. Among the financial ratios, leverage and the Tier I ratio appeared insignificant in all three time periods that were examined while the liquidity factor became relevant only during the crisis and the post-crisis periods. Jenkins et al. (2016) tested the CDS market for being semi-strong efficient. They relied on data from 692 firms and were able to establish that announced earnings announcements and accruals were associated with systematic patterns of reaction in subsequent CDS returns during crisis periods while under stable economic conditions the semi-strong hypothesis could not be rejected. Drago et al. (2017) made use of both balance sheet and market determinants of a sample made up from 54 European and US large banks. They also found that a great number of balance sheet ratios expressing capital adequacy, leverage, asset quality, bank size and funding stability were important determinants of banks’ CDS spreads.

Finally, the sovereign debt crisis in Euro-zone has given impetus to a great number of studies which tried to unveil the interaction between sovereign and bank credit risk. De Bruyckere et al. (2013) investigated the impact of bank-specific factors for the bank/sovereign spillover risk. They found that banks with a weak capital base, weak funding structures and less traditional activities were more in danger of risk spillovers. Angeloni and Wolff (2012) fail to find strong evidence of correlation between Euro-zone banks’ asset holdings on peripheral sovereign debt and their stock market returns. Rather, bank stock prices appear to be more associated to the risk of the country these banks are located in. In a similar vein, Drago et al. (2017) show that sovereign risk rating is an important determinant of banks’ CDS spread. Arezki et al. (2011) find that downgrades of sovereigns have implications not only for the country involved but that they spillover to other markets and countries as well.
