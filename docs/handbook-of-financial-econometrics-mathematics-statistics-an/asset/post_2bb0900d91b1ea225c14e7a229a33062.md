#### 52.5.1 Before and during May 1997

The Thai baht attracted the attention of speculators in 1997 because of the country’s slow GDP growth rate and unstable financial institutions. Several waves of speculative attacks took place in January, February, and May 1997. However, the estimation results of the SV family of models show no signs that the Thai baht was affected by these attacks. In May, the situation changed dramatically. The waves of attacks that began on May 12 sharply raised the estimated volatility of the SVIJ model, to 2.24 from 0.04. The model identified a jump in volatility during a particular period, which suggests that the Thai government failed to maintain the stability of its foreign exchange market.

#### 52.5.2 June–July 1997

Another jump in volatility was identified on June 11, while the speculators continued to attack the Thai baht. The scale was 8.873, pushing the estimated volatility to 10.05. This jump marked the beginning of a more volatile episode. Daily changes of more than 3% became common during this period. For example, the Thai baht appreciated 4.15% on June 17, depreciated 6.19% on June 19, appreciated 4.54% on June 27, and finally depreciated 17% on July 2. The SVIJ model estimated an 11.3% jump in the observation equation on July 2 and attributes the remainder of the 35% in changes to conditional volatility.

While the Thai baht continued to slide after July 2, the estimated volatility declined gradually, with the exception of a few sporadic occasions. The next major volatility jump happened on October 6, when the Thai baht depreciated 3.43% and appreciated 3.15% the following day. The SVIJ model identified this as a 4.9 jump in volatility.

### Abstract

This chapter attempts to explore the puzzle of post-earnings-announcement drifts by focusing on the revision of systematic risk subsequent to the release of earnings information. This chapter proposes a market model with time-varying systematic risk by incorporating ARCH into the CAPM. The Kalman filter is then employed to estimate how the market revises its risk assessment subsequent to earnings announcement. This chapter also conducts empirical analysis based on a sample of US publicly held companies during the five-fiscal year sample period, 2010–2014. After controlling for the revision of risk and isolating potential confounding effect, this chapter finds that the phenomenon of post-earnings announcement drifts, so well documented in accounting literature, no longer exists.

### 53.1 Introduction

The phenomenon of the post-earnings-announcement drifts (PEADs) first observed in Ball and Brown (1968) has been extensively investigated by modern accounting research (Cartwright and Lee, 1987; Bernard and Thomas, 1989; Bhushan, 1994; Ke and Ramalingegowda, 2005; Garfinkel and Sokobin, 2006; Sadka, 2006; Francis et al., 2007; Ng et al., 2008; Ayers et al., 2011; Chung and Hrazdil, 2011; Louis and Sun, 2011; Shin and Park, 2018; Wang et al., 2018). However, whether this phenomenon is due to improper research design or is a sign of market inefficiency has been inconclusive.

Among those who can hardly argue that the stock market is inefficient, Ball (1978), Foster et al. (1984) (referred to FOS hereafter) and Ball et al. (1993) (referred to BKW hereafter) attribute this phenomenon to the non-stationarity of systematic risk. They suggest that the systematic risk may not be fully adjusted, because the capital-asset-pricing model (CAPM) used to calculate abnormal returns is either incomplete or misspecified. As a result, the so-called abnormal returns represent fair compensation for risk bearing that is priced but not captured by the CAPM. Ball and Bartov (1996) further argue that predictable stock returns following earnings announcement remain anomalous and may be due to biases in sample selection or abnormal returns measurement. On the other hand, Bernard and Thomas (1989, 1990, referred to BT hereafter) contend that the inference of asset pricing model misspecification is unwarranted. After investigating several possible sources of the post-earnings-announcement drifts, BT conclude succinctly that the drift is damaging evidence against the belief in market efficiency.
