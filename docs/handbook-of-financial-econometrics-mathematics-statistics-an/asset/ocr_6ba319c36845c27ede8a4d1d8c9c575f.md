Recall that the first group of columns (labeled SV) in Table 33.3 contains the summary statistics for instantaneous correlation (column ρ), volatility of variance (column η), and the volatility scale (column σ) parameter estimates of model (33.5) fitted to the calibrated variance, {V^{SV}}_t. Similarly, the second group of columns (labeled RV) in Table 33.3 contains the summary statistics for the same parameters of the model (33.5) fitted to the realized variation, RV. Figure 33.4 depicts the parameter estimates obtained from Euler-discretized stochastic volatility model (33.5), fitted separately to realized variation and calibrated variance: Panel (a) depicts the instantaneous correlations {ρ^{RV}, ρ^{SV}}_t, while Panel (b) depicts the volatilities of variance {η^{RV}, η^{SV}}_t. Shaded areas in the graphs cover the 95%-confidence intervals. To better illustrate the relative dynamics of parameters, {ρ^{RV}, η^{RV}}_t are plotted against the primary vertical axes, while {ρ^{SV}, η^{SV}}_t are plotted against the secondary vertical axes. The different scales of the two axes clearly indicate the mismatches between magnitudes of the parameter estimates based on different data inputs: fitting the model to calibrated variance leads to much higher magnitude negative instantaneous correlation and significantly smaller volatility of variance, compared to the fit achieved with realized variation. These differences in levels of parameters are clearly significant because the corresponding 95%-confidence intervals rarely overlap (recall the difference in the scales of the primary and secondary axes). Greater instability of realized variation clearly contributes to the higher estimate of volatility of variance. This effect, however, cannot be attributed to jumps in the underlying, as is verified by fitting the model to only the continuous component of variation (captured with bipower variation). Notably, the overall evolution of volatility of variance for both, {η^{SV}}_t and {η^{RV}}_t, is similar: initial decline, period of stability, and then a period of growth. However, there is no discernible commonality in the evolution of instantaneous correlation parameters, {ρ^{SV}}_t and {ρ^{RV}}_t. These observations serve as evidence against the external consistency of the Heston stochastic volatility model, as captured by Hypothesis 2.

The implied volatility skew in the option market is driven by the product of instantaneous correlation and volatility of variance. Figure 33.5 (Panel (a)) demonstrates that the skew generated by model (33.5) fitted to realized variation follows the qualitative dynamics of the skew generated by the same model specification fitted to calibrated variance; however, the former is far less in magnitude and exhibits greater instability than the latter.
