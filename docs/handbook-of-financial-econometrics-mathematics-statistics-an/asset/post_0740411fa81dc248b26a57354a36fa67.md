Leone and Wu (2007) report that forecasts released by those analysts who master industry knowledge are regarded as more valued by the buy-side. Bradley et al. Analysts’ ties with other agents in the economy can also affect their performance. For example, social ties with management affect both analyst forecast behavior and coverage decisions (e.g., Westphal and Clement, 2008; Cohen et al., 2010; Brochet et al., 2014).

However, analysts do not work in isolation and few studies have examined how the interaction among competing peer analysts can affect the information aggregation process in the market. Our study explicitly models this potential interaction in a network constructed by peer analysts with overlapping firm coverage. Analysts not only obtain information from company disclosures and management, but may also collect more information from the conversation with or research outputs from their peers (Gomes et al., 2017). Thus, we expect that when two analysts cover the same firms, there is sharing of information among the analyst-pair on firms, industries, or any relevant macroeconomic development.

According to Burt (2003), people with similar behavior and opinion form a group and focus on activities inside their own group. This “group” structure makes information more homogeneous within than between groups and creates “structure holes” in the information flow between groups. If agents stand at the position of structural holes, they would create bridges connecting different groups and play a role of controlling the information flow between these groups. Similarly, when analysts occupy more structural holes in the network, they can get access to more diverse information about market, industries and firms from peer analysts and release more valuable forecasts. Therefore, analysts occupying more structural holes will have a greater ability to move stock price, and are more likely to be highly ranked in the profession.

We test the hypothesis that structural hole can be used to rank analysts by regressing excess stock returns on an interaction term of the structural hole measure and the unexpected component of the analyst’s earnings forecast. If investors value more the earnings forecasts released by analysts occupying more structural holes (measured by a lower constraint), the coefficient of the interaction term will be significantly negative. We discuss the variable of network constraint in detail in Section 31.4. We use data from the previous year to calculate the structural hole measures in order to eliminate any dependence of our classification procedures on the contemporaneous excess returns. Hypothesis 1 is presented as follows:

## Hypothesis 1: *Analysts who occupy more structural holes have greater ability to move stock price upon newly announced earnings forecasts.*

Analysts can strategically position herself in a position of structural hole. Given that every analyst is working under a constraint of limited time and attention, a more experienced analyst can stand to benefit the most by placing herself in a position with access to more diverse and varied sets of information. Similarly, analysts work for large brokerages having more resources and support available if they need to divide their time and attention among diverse sources of information and knowledge. We therefore develop Hypothesis 2 based on the above arguments.

## Hypothesis 2: *Those analysts occupying more structural holes are more likely to be experienced and/or work at large brokerages than others.*

Several methods have been proposed to rank analysts based on the accuracy, timeliness and consistency of analysts’ earnings forecasts. If all of these methods capture similar information on analysts’ (unobservable) capability to process information as that of network structure hole, analysts ranking by these methods will be positively correlated with the ranking based on structural hole. We calculate the accuracy and timeliness of analyst earnings forecasts following Cooper et al. (2001) and classify analysts whose earnings forecast accuracy ranked in the top decile as accuracy leaders, and analysts whose timeliness test statistics are significant at the 10% level as timeliness leaders. We also calculate earnings forecast consistency following Hilary and Hsu (2013), which report that analysts who release more consistent forecasts are more likely to be top-ranked analysts. Thus, we test whether analysts identified as accuracy or timeliness leaders are more likely to occupy more structural holes. In addition, we examine the correlation between analyst rankings by earnings forecast consistency and by network structural hole. Hypothesis 3 is as follows.

## Hypothesis 3: *Analysts identified as accuracy or timeliness leaders occupy more structural holes than others. Rankings of analyst by earnings forecast consistency and structural hole are positively correlated.*

Stickel (1992) and Hong and Kubik (2003) report that more accurate analysts are rewarded with better career outcomes, while Hilary and Hsu (2013) find that analysts associated with more consistent earnings forecasts are more likely to be nominated All Star analysts. In our setting, if analysts occupying more structural holes rank highly in the profession, they will have better career development than others. Analysts working in large brokerages are considered to have higher reputation and earn better compensation than others. We use the number of analysts for a given brokerage to measure the brokerage size (Hong and Kubik, 2003) and hypothesize that analysts who span more structural holes are more likely to move to larger brokerages later in their career. ## 31.4 Data and Variable Construction

This section describes the construction of the analyst coverage network, discusses the data collection and presents summary statistics on our sample.

### 31.4.1 Analyst coverage network

Prior studies suggest that information exchange and dissemination among peer analysts can be an important input in making accurate earnings forecasts. An analyst competes with her peers not only with her access to corporate management, but also to any information economically relevant for providing investors an accurate investment opinion and advice. For peer analysts covering the same firms, interaction inevitably and naturally arises due to their presence at conference calls, investment conferences and corporate visits. To capture such an effect of information sharing among peer analysts, we construct an analyst coverage network in which each individual analyst constitutes a node, with nodes of analysts linked (connected) if the analyst pair covers the same stock(s) with the weight of connection (linkage) as the number of companies the pair covers at the same time.

To construct the analyst coverage network, we collect FY1 forecast data from the detailed history file of I/B/E/S over the period of January of 1994 to December of 2016.
