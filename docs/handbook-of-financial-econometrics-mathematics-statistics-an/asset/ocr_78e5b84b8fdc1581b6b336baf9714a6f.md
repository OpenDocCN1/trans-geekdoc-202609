It is clear that Jensen’s regression equation can be regarded as the correspondent of the form in (8) with error in the measurement of $R_b$. Further, the measurement error of $R_b$ is decomposed into the constant part, B, and the random part, $e_{bt}$.

Using standard econometric methods we can express $\widehat{\alpha}'_j$ and $\widehat{\beta}'_j$ as estimated parameters in Jensen’s equation in (62.3) when the true model should be that in (62.8) as

```
$$\widehat{\beta}'_j = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \{(r_{jt} - r_{bt} + e_{bt})(r_{mt} - r_{bt} + e_{bt})\} / \sum_{t=1}^{n} (r_{mt} - r_{bt} + e_{bt})^2, \tag{62.9a}$$
```

```
$$\widehat{\alpha}'_j = (\bar{R}_j - \bar{R}_b + B) - \widehat{\beta}'_j (\bar{R}_j - \bar{R}_b + B), \tag{62.9b}$$
```

where $r_{jt} = R_{jt} - \bar{R}_j$, $R_{mt} = R_{mt} - \bar{R}_m$, $r_{bt} = R_{bt} - \bar{R}_b$, and $\bar{R}_j$, $\bar{R}_m$ and $\bar{R}_b$ are arithmetic means of $R_{jt}$, $R_{mt}$ and $R_{bt}$, respectively. Following Johnson (1972) and Cramer (1946), the components of the sample estimates $\hat{\beta}'_j$ and $\hat{\alpha}'_j$ can be written as$^4$

```
$$\widehat{\beta}'_j = \beta'_j + \{(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_e^2)\} + s_j, \tag{62.10a}$$
```

```
$$\widehat{\alpha}'_j = \alpha'_j - \{(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_e^2) + s_j\} (\bar{R}_m - \bar{R}_b) - \{(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_m^2 + \sigma_e^2) + s_j + (\beta'_j - 1)\} B, \tag{62.10b}$$
```

where $\widehat{\beta}'_j$ and $\widehat{\alpha}'_j$ are the population coefficients under the specification of (62.6); $\beta_j$ and $\alpha_j$ are the population coefficients under the specification of (62.2). Further, $\beta'_j = \beta_j$ and $\alpha_j = \alpha'_j + (1 - \beta_j)B$. $\sigma_M^2$ is the variance of $(R_{mt} - R_{bt})$. $s_j$ is the sample variance of $\beta_j$ and is distributed with zero mean and finite variance $\sigma_s^2$.

Equation (62.10a) implies that the sample estimate of the systematic risk can be decomposed into the population value $\beta'_j$, sample variance $s_j$, and the bias caused by the measurement error of $R_b$, i.e., $(1 - \beta'_j)\sigma_e^2 / (\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_e^2)$. Similarly, as in equation (62.10b), the sample estimate of the Jensen performance measure $\alpha'_j$ can be decomposed into three components. The effects of the components other than $\alpha'_j$ and $\beta'_j$ are usually ignored. Thus, the magnitude of $\beta'_j$ will affect both the signs and the magnitude of $E(\hat{\beta}'_j)$ and $E(\alpha'_j)$. In general, $E(\hat{\beta}'_j)$ will be larger (or smaller) than $\beta'_j$ when $\beta'_j$ is smaller (or larger) than unity.
