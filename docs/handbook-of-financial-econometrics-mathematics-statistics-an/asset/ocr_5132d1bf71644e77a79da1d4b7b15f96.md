At this point in time, the risk-free rate exceeds the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean when the market’s expected return is constant; in the two cases when the market’s equilibrium mean is equal to its sample mean, i.e., when the intercepts and slopes of the SMLs are θ_Int = r̄_ft and θ_Slt = μ_mt^s − r̄_ft, and θ_Int = r_ft and θ_Slt = μ_mt^s − r_ft, respectively; and when the slope and intercept of the equilibrium means are set by the GLS regression. The risk-less rate exceeding the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean is not consistent with MV theory, even though the time-series of equilibrium means closely track the sample means in the latter case. On the other hand, the market portfolio’s equilibrium mean exceeds the risk-free rate when the market portfolio’s risk premium and expected excess return to variance ratio are constant, and when the GLS regression of sample means on betas is constrained so that the intercept is greater than or equal to the riskfree rate and the slope is positive. While this is consistent with MV theory, these equilibrium means are far from their sample counterparts.

The means and betas estimated from the expanding and fixed 120-month windows may be startlingly different, especially the sample means, and the equilibrium means based on the unconstrained and constrained GLS regressions. Figure 47.2 shows that, with the fixed 120-month window, the means are both more variable over time and less variable at a point in time. It is much easier to see differences in the cross-section of betas, shown for the 10 size portfolios dataset in Figure 47.3. The betas estimated from the expanding window, in the top panel, are reasonably stationary, although the spread narrows over time.⁹ By way of contrast, the betas estimated from the 120-month fixed window, in the lower panel, are highly variable. In most of the months, the small (large) size portfolio has the highest (lowest) beta. Yet, in and around month 250, the pattern reverses itself.
