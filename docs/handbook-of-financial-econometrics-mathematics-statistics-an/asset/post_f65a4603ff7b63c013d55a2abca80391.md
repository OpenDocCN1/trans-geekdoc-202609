## Abstract

The objective of this chapter is to provide an update to the literature on initial public offering (IPO) performance and issuance focusing explicitly on the methodological approaches used to conduct these analyses and to develop a more general approach to evaluating aggregate IPO issuance and performance. Traditionally, empirical studies of IPO performance have been critically dependent on the general methodology that researchers use to adjust the individual IPO's returns to account for market performance and the time horizon of the study; however, more recent studies have examined the patterns of returns that IPOs emit, in general, sometimes prior to performance adjustments. In the US market, for instance, changes in the regulatory regime as a result of the introduction of the JOBS Act and events such as the financial collapse have led to a period of relatively benign issuance associated with IPOs. This has recently led to new questions about the true relationship between the volume of IPO issuance and performance. Historically, we have assumed that hot and cold market cycles affect performance; however, recently the methodology used to capture whether markets are indeed hot or cold has been questioned. In addition, there has been a renaissance of late as researchers critically examine the validity of research projects that claim to identify hot and cold markets or identify cyclicality in the performance of IPO. The research has evolved from a segmentation of a population of IPO returns into quartiles or terciles and referring to the segments of these populations hot and cold, to Markov two and three state regime-shifting models, to more recent applications of event specific and spline regression models; researchers have been working to uncover what actually causes the IPO markets to move and the cyclical nature of IPO performance and issuance seems to indicate that the current state of research on IPOs needs some restructuring and clarification. This chapter has important implications for financial markets participants, portfolio managers, investment bankers, regulatory bodies, and business owners. Furthermore, this review chapter can aid in the setting of benchmarks for the valuation of IPOs, help investors, business owners, and the managers of businesses to understand the relationship between IPO performance and issuance so that they are better positioned to make wise investment decisions when purchasing IPOs or when issuing their IPOs and enable researchers to think more critically about developing their models of IPO issuance and performance.

### 67.1 Introduction

The objective of this chapter is to provide an update to the literature on initial public offering (IPO) performance and issuance focusing explicitly on the methodological approaches used to conduct these analyses and to develop a more general approach to evaluating aggregate IPO issuance and performance. Traditionally, empirical studies of IPO performance (Ritter, 1991; Loughran & Ritter, 1995; Bradley et al., 2003; Gompers & Lerner, 2003; Bradley et al., 2004; Purnanandam & Swaminathan, 2004) have been critically dependent on the general methodology that researchers use to adjust the individual IPO's returns to account for market performance and the time horizon of the study and researchers have focused intently on developing methods to adjust for methodological issues in tests of performance, specifically, longer-run performance (e.g., Barber and Lyon, 1997; Lyon et al., 1999). Another vein of the IPO research has been focused on examining patterns of returns that IPOs emit (Ibbotson, 1975; Ibbotson and Jaffe, 1975; Ritter, 1984; Ibbotson et al., 1994; Loughran et al., 1994; Pagano et al., 1998; Stoughton et al., 2001; Benveniste et al., 2002; Lowry and Schwert, 2002; Lowry, 2003; Helwege and Liang, 2004; Purnanandam and Swaminathan, 2004; Alti, 2005; Benninga et al., 2005; Derrien, 2005; Pastor and Veronesi, 2005; Yung et al., 2008; Gao et al., 2013; Hu and Wang, 2013; Smith and Mumtaz, 2018), in general, sometimes prior to performance adjustments (i.e., aggregate pre-market IPO performance). This chapter focuses explicitly on the second vein of research because we feel that there have been some recent advances in how we account for aggregate IPO performance and the methods that we use to attempt to model aggregate-level IPO performance.

Historically, if we were to start at what some may call the beginning or pioneering studies that attempt to examine the cyclical nature of aggregate IPO performance, a good starting point would be Ibbotson and Jaffe (1975), in which the authors provide evidence of serial correlation in the residuals of IPO using correlational studies and the runs test. Ibbotson et al. (1975) found that there was a substantial correlation between the performance of the current month and subsequent month’s performance from 1960 to 1970. In addition, Ibbotson et al. (1975) used a regression framework and regressed volume of IPOs on the residual returns from the aggregate IPO performance adjusted for market performance and they identified that there was a strong relationship between the two variables but did not contend that it was causal due to the serial correlation issues. This set the stage for many researchers to question what the relationship between IPO performance and issuance was and what was causing both IPO performance and issuance.

Since this finding, research on IPO pricing and performance has become increasingly interesting and the influences on IPO pricing and performance through events or technological innovations have been noteworthy. For example, recently Liew and Wang (2016) examined the “tweet sentiment” from public Twitter accounts and found that this sentiment was indicative of IPO performance. Researchers such as Bajo et al. (2016) have shown that the role the lead IPO underwriter plays in the IPO process is significant and that the centrality of this lead underwriter in relation to other investment bankers affects the IPO’s offer price revisions, after-market valuations, initial returns, institutional holdings and analyst coverage, increases post-issue liquidity, and leads to better long-run performance. Similarly, Liu, Sherman and Zhang found that the media coverage of an IPO, pre-issuance, is positively related to the IPO’s long-term value, analysts’ coverage, and institutional investor ownership.

Another strain of research focuses on how the actual company that takes the shares public examines the role that management plays in the issuance of the IPO shares and the role that founders play. For example, Boone et al. (2016) indicate that firms that redact information prior to their issuance experience greater underpricing at the issuance, but over the long-run, they earn greater abnormal returns when compared to non-redacting firms; the assumption is that the redaction is an indication that these firms are protecting proprietary information from their rivals. Wang and Song (2016) focus specifically on the relationship between the founding owners of the firm and their board structure and find that as the non-founder component of the firm increases initially the level of the IPO price premium increase, but only to an extent because too much non-founder board representation leads to a decrease in the initial IPO price premium. This seems to indicate that founders have to have sufficient representation on the board to help steer decisions, but there is an optimal level of non-founder board members that provide investors and underwriters with the belief that both fractions of the board will keep the other inline and make good decisions for the firm. Boulton and Campbell (2016) illustrate that overconfident managers tend to use IPO underpricing as a signal of firm quality, which indicates that there is a relationship between managerial confidence and IPO underpricing, and that these managers access the capital markets quicker and more often following an initial offering; hence, overconfident managers use underpricing as a signaling process to investors. Managers, owners, and what they do leading up to the IPO's issuance and subsequently have an effect on firm value.

The goal of this chapter is to take a macro perspective and build a model of what these individual firm-level decisions are and these increases in the impact that events have on the aggregate level of IPO performance and issuance. To accomplish this, the chapter starts back at the beginning and leverages the sentiment behind the Ibbotson et al. (1975) paper to build a comprehensive model of the cyclical nature of IPO performance and issuance. Since Ibbotson et al. (1975), some researchers have concluded that there are cycles in IPO performance and issuance (Ritter and Welch, 2002), others refer to the patterns as sequential learning (Lowry and Schwert, 2002), others indicate that investor sentiment has a large impact on these variables (Lowry, 2003), and others refer to them as waves (Yung et al., 2008). There are, of course, similarities in all of these interpretations of what causes IPO issuance and performance to fluctuate so much (Lowry, 2003).
