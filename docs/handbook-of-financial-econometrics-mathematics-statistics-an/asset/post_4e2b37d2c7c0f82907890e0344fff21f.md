Equations (114.14a) and (114.14b) show that the gross return of long period can be presented as the product of consecutive gross rates of return of every individual sub-period which are disjoint. And this property is called multiplicativity. For example, if r_t and r_Mt represent monthly rates of returns and T/n = 3, then R_T and R_MT will represent the quarterly rates of return.

Levhari and Levy (1977) and Chen (1980) have analyzed the effects of temporal aggregation on beta estimates under multiplicative assumption. It has been shown that if the market rates of return are assumed to be identically distributed, but not independently distributed, the N-period systematic risk associated with a multiplicative model can generally be written as

β_N = [Cov(r_t, r_Mt) + μ_i μ_M]^N - (μ_i μ_M)^N + b_N / (σ_M^2 + μ_M^2)^N - μ_M^{2N} + c_N
= Σ_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t β_u^{N-1} (1 + α)^t + b_N / Σ_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t + c_N,  (114.15)

where r_t = the security rate of return, r_Mt = the market rate of return, μ_i = E(r_t), μ_M = E(r_Mt), σ_M^2 = Var(r_Mt); a_t = (N choose t)(σ_M^2)^{N-t}(μ_M^2)^t and α = (β_u - 1) μ_M - r / μ_M with r = riskless interest rate as defined by Levhari-Levy; b_N and c_N represent the effects of autocorrelation in the market rates of return on Cov(r1, r2, ..., rN, rM1, rM2, ..., rMN) and Var(rM1, rM2, ..., rMN), respectively. Equation (114.15) reduces to Levhari-Levy equation if the market rates of return are assumed to be independently distributed (b_N = c_N = 0). To investigate the impact of autocorrelation in one-period systematic risk associated with multiplicative rates of return, equation (114.15) is explored as given in Sections 114.2.2.1–114.2.2.3.

### 114.2.2.1 Neutral stock

The $N$-period systematic risk for a neutral stock ($\beta_u = 1$) can be shown to be

$$\beta_N^* = \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t + b_N}{\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t + c_N}. \tag{114.16}$$

Thus, $\beta_N^* = 1 = \beta_u$ only if $b_N = c_N = 0$. In other words, $\beta_N^*$ cannot be equal to one unless the terms, $b_N$ and $c_N$, associated with autocorrelation in one-period market rates of return are zero, which requires the assumption of the independent distribution of the market rates of return. Therefore, $\beta_N^*$ is not equal to one for a neutral stock if there exists autocorrelation in the market rates of return.

### 114.2.2.2 Aggressive stock

A stock which is more volatile than the market is called an aggressive stock; i.e., $\beta_u > 1$. In this case, $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta_u^{N-i} \ge \beta_u$, $i = 0,1,\dots, N - 1$. Then, following equation (114.15), it is easy to show that

$$\beta_N^* > \beta_u \left[ \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t + b_N/\beta_u}{\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t + c_N} \right]. \tag{114.17}$$

As indicated by Levhari and Levy, $\beta_N^* > \beta_u$ if rates of return are assumed to be identically and independently distributed. However, equation (114.17) implies that $\beta_N^*$ would have a lower bound greater or less than $\beta_u$, depending on the magnitude of $b_N$ and $c_N$. If $b_N/\beta_u$ is greater than $c_N$, $\beta_N^*$ has a lower bound greater than $\beta_u$. In this case, Levhari–Levy conclusion, $\beta_N^* > \beta_u$, is still valid. However, if $b_N/\beta_u$ is less than $c_N$, $\beta_N^*$ has a lower bound less than $\beta_u$. This means that $\beta_N^*$ could be less than $\beta_u$ for some $N$. This finding explains why for some stocks $\beta_N^*$ is not greater than $\beta_u$ for some $N$ in the Levhari–Levy empirical results. Thus, the existence of autocorrelation in the market rates of return can increase or decrease the magnitude of the $N$-period systematic risk associated with an aggressive stock.

### 114.2.2.3 Defensive stock

In this case, β_u < 1, α < 0, β_u^{N-i} ≤ β_u, i = 0,1,…, N − 1. Then, it can be shown that

β_N^* ≤ β_u [ (∑_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t + b_N/β_u) / (∑_{t=0}^{N-1} a_t + c_N) ] . (114.18)

If b_N/β_u is less than c_N, the same result, β_N^* < β_u, as found by Levhari and Levy, is obtained since β_N^* has an upper bound less than β_u. In addition, β_N^* will be less than −β_u if, for some N, b_N or c_N is negative such that either the numerator or the denominator of equation (114.18) becomes negative. Specifically, the effect of autocorrelation in the market rates of return may decrease the magnitude of β_N^* associated with a defensive stock. And, for some N, the N-period systematic risk may become negative. This result helps explain why some estimated N-period systematic risks associated with defensive stocks are negative in Levhari–Levy empirical results. Therefore, the existence of autocorrelation in the market rates of return can be used to interpret the empirical results found by Levhari and Levy.

## 114.3 Time Aggregation on Estimated R²

Jacob (1971) has found that the R² estimated from monthly data is smaller than that from both quarterly and annual data, Altom, Jacquillat and Levasseur (1974) have found that the R² estimated from quarterly data is smaller than that from both semiannual and annual data and McDonald (1974) has found that the R² obtained from monthly mutual fund data is smaller than that from both quarterly and annual mutual fund data. Schwartz and Witcomb (1977a,b) have tried to explain the above findings by the time-variance relationship. Now, a new approach is used to explain the impact of time aggregation on the estimated R².

Equations (114.19) and (114.21) imply that there exists a relationship between the variance of daily data and the variance of weekly data.

Based on the definition of R^2, the “daily” and “weekly” population goodness of fit measure, R^2_daily and R^2_weekly, can be defined as

R^2_daily = \frac{\beta_{daily}^2 Var(X_t)}{\beta_{daily}^2 Var(X_t) + \sigma^2}, (114.22)

$$R_{\text{weekly}}^2 = \frac{\beta_{\text{daily}}^2 (1\ 1\ 1\ 1\ 1)\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{X})\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}}{\beta_{\text{daily}}^2 (1\ 1\ 1\ 1\ 1)\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{X})\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}+5\sigma^2}.\tag{114.23}$$

Substituting (114.20) into (114.23) yields that

$$R_{\text{weekly}}^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{5\sigma^2}{\text{Var}(X_t)\left(5+8\rho_X^1+6\rho_X^2+4\rho_X^3+2\rho_X^4\right)\beta_{\text{daily}}^2}}.\tag{114.24}$$

And note that from equation (114.22), we have that

$$\frac{\sigma^2}{\beta_{\text{daily}}^2\,\text{Var}(X_t)} = \frac{1 - R_{\text{daily}}^2}{R_{\text{daily}}^2}.\tag{114.25}$$

Finally, we substitute (114.25) into (114.24) and obtain that

$$R_{\text{weekly}}^2 = \frac{R_{\text{daily}}^2}{R_{\text{daily}}^2 + k_{d-w}\left(1 - R_{\text{daily}}^2\right)},\tag{114.26}$$

where $k_{d-w}$ is the daily-to-weekly multiplier for $R^2$'s estimated by using daily data and weekly data:

$$k_{d-w} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{8}{5}\rho_X^1 + \frac{6}{5}\rho_X^2 + \frac{4}{5}\rho_X^3 + \frac{2}{5}\rho_X^4}.\tag{114.27}$$

Thus, if $k_{d-w} < 1$, then $R_{\text{weekly}}^2 > R_{\text{daily}}^2$, which implies that the estimated goodness of fit grows as the time interval increases. On the other hand, if $k_{d-w} > 1$, then $R_{\text{weekly}}^2 < R_{\text{daily}}^2$. It means that the estimation of $R^2$ using weekly data would be smaller than that estimated by daily data.
