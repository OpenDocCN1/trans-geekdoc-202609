The results of Merton (1976) illustrate that the jump component of the stock’s return represents non-systematic risk. And the main formal derived by Merton (1976) is BS solution but it had a larger interest rate component. According to Merton (1976), option prices increase with an increase in interest rates. Further, the probability in this scenario is conditional on Poisson jumps occurring during the life of an option. Given that his article is theoretical, he mapped out a possible empirical scenario. First, he finds out the ceteris paribus as an option becomes more valuable with the presence of stock price jumps.

Scott (1997) derived a closed option formula for a European option under jump diffusion process. According to Scott (1997), in prior studies, jumps were due to ordinary jumps and stochastic volatility, and his studies, incorporates all those elements. In order to make his pricing “elegant”, he used Fourier Transform. Scott (1997) states that jumps increases risk premium in options, phenomenon that will be illustrated the formulation section. For modeling section, he adopts an exponential model for pricing a European option that is driven by two Weiner processes. The derived Margrabe model adopts similar approaches.

Based on the relationship between interest rates and stock prices; results show (i) significant positive relationship on squared stock price changes, (ii) significant negative correlation between interest rates and stock changes, and (iii) significant negative correlation between volatility and stock price changes. Further, sample kurtosis increases dramatically after stock market crashes. He argues that models that do not incorporate jumps would not be able to illustrate the latter phenomenon. Moreover, Scott (1997) calibrated his model to second, third and fourth moments (variance, skewness and kurtosis). At the heart of the calibration was the inclusion of risk premium. The results improved with calibration of the model. On the other hand, those options were valuated using Monte-Carlo simulation (MCS). From MCS results, the following could be observed based on Pearson correlation (ρ). Firstly, a negative ρ produces higher values for out-of-the-money (in-the-money) puts (calls) and lower values for in-the-money (out-of-the-money) puts (calls). Secondly, value of options increases with an increase in stock jumps.

The three distinctive factors that affect the risk premium are (i) diffusive price shocks, (ii) price jumps, and (iii) diffusive volatility shocks.
