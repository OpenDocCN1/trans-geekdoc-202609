Many researchers have blamed the low interest rate environment for the GFC and the euro-area sovereign debt crisis. The combination of low interest rates and the abundant liquidity led financial institutions to take excessive risks and increase leverage. This theory touches upon the role of the loose monetary policy; observers argue that a more aggressive policy about interest rates would have prevented the development of the bubble that led to the GFC. Rajan (2006) investigates whether a low interest rate period may drive banks to search for higher yields by adopting a riskier behavior. Delis and Kouretas (2011) use various interest rates to investigate the relationship between interest rates and bank risk-taking and conclude that the short-term rates are negatively and strongly related to the bank risk-taking. Houweling and Vorst (2005), Hull et al. (2004) support that short-term rates have stronger impact on bank risk-taking than longer term interest rates since they refer to very liquid assets, they have no short sale constraints and they are not influenced by special tax regulations (see Clarida et al. 2001).

Although there have been numerous studies on the determinants of corporate CDS spreads, until recently, the number of cases where structural models had been applied for understanding the formation of credit risk in the banking sector has been small. The reason is often cited as being the high leverage ratios under which banks operate which would exaggerate the "credit-spread" puzzle. Also, capital ratios were considered as not being informational or useful since they were determined in compliance to the capital ratios regulatory demands and were susceptible to manipulations due to the regulatory capital arbitrage policies followed by many banks (e.g., Admati and Hellwig, 2013).

Recent studies on banks’ credit risk have attempted to expand the structural model in, at least, two directions; the first one introduces common risk factors extracted from financial markets data while the second one introduces balance sheet items expressing idiosyncratic risks. The importance of market variables in improving the explanatory power of the structural models can hardly be refuted. For instance, Eichengreen et al. (2012) applied a principal components analysis on the CDS prices of a number of international banks and showed that even in normal times four factors explain a high share of CDS spreads’ variation. Moreover, the CDS spreads were strongly associated with short-term global economic prospects which were best expressed by the US high yield spreads. Annaert et al.
