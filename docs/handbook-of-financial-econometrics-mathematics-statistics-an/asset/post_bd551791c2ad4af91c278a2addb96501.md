We partition the book-tax differences into quintiles. POSBTD is a binary variable equal to one for firm-years with scaled temporary book-tax differences in the highest quintile of firms in each year (positive book-tax differences) and zero otherwise. NEGBTD is a binary variable equal to one for firm-years with scaled temporary book-tax differences in the lowest quintile of firms in each year (negative book-tax differences) and zero otherwise.

Table 32.2 provides the Spearman correlations among the variables. The correlation between TOTPAY and BTD is negative and significant at the 1% level. Consistent with the efficiency hypothesis, CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences.

Table 32.3 presents the regressions of CEO compensation on book-tax differences and other determinants. In column (1), the dependent variable is salary and in column (2) the dependent variable is cash compensation comprising salary and bonus. This provides a baseline to compare with prior studies (such as Core et al. (1999)) that use salary and cash pay as proxies of level of compensation. In column (3), the dependent variable is stock-based compensation defined as options granted and restricted stocks granted to the CEO divided by total assets at the start of the year. In column (4), the dependent variable is TOTPAY computed as the sum of salary, bonus, long-term incentive payout, options granted, restricted stock granted and other annual pay paid to the CEO during the year divided by total assets at the start of the year. For all four measures of CEO compensation, the coefficient for BTD is negative and significant. Thus, consistent with the efficiency hypothesis, CEO compensation is negatively associated with book-tax differences. Furthermore, the result is consistent across different compensation components such as salary, cash pay and stock-based compensation. To assess the economic significance of the result, we consider the effect of an increase in book-tax differences on total compensation in column (4). Based on the mean assets of $4,886 million, an increase in the book-tax differences by one standard deviation reduces total compensation of the CEO by $1.04 million. Hence, the result is economically significant as the mean CEO compensation in our sample is $4 million.

In general, the control variables are in their predicted direction. Large firms, profitable firms and those with high growth opportunities tend to have higher CEO compensation. Firm risk, proxied by standard deviation of ROA and standard deviation of common stock return is positively associated with compensation. Total compensation is negatively associated with insider equity ownership. This is consistent with prior results (Lambert, 1993; Core et al., 1999) that higher insider ownership reduces agency costs. Consistent with Hartzell and Starks (2003), salary and cash compensation are negatively associated with concentrated ownership by institutional shareholders but no association is documented for total compensation. Consistent with Core et al. (1999), firms with CEO who is also chairman of board have higher compensation. Contrary to prediction and Yermack’s (1996) argument that large boards are ineffective, compensation is negatively associated with board size. The proportion of outside directors on the board appears to have little effect on CEO compensation.⁴
