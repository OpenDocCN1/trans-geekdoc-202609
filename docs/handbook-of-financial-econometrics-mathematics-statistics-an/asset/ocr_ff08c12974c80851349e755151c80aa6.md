(2009) investigated whether the provision of nonaudit services to audit clients impairs auditor independence, and whether auditor independence improved after the Procomp scandal. Using publicly disclosed fee data for the fiscal years 2003 and 2004 in Taiwan, they documented that the coefficient for the nonaudit fees ratio was negative and significant in 2003, but not in 2004. There results were consistent with the notion that auditors make a trade-off between gaining service fees and avoiding litigation and loss of reputation. Although the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX) banned most nonaudit services (NAS), it did not restrict auditors from providing tax NAS to their audit clients (Alsadoun et al., 2018). They fill a void in the literature by examining investors’ perception of auditor-provided tax NAS, as reflected in the implied cost of equity capital. Their results show that investors require higher cost of equity capital for clients that generate more tax NAS revenue for their auditor’s office. Their findings suggest that investors seem to negatively perceive tax NAS because of punitive and cash flow risks associated with tax NAS. Lisic et al. (2018) investigate the relation between the proportion of accounting firm consulting revenue to total revenue and audit quality and investor perceptions of audit quality. They find evidence suggesting that before SOX, higher proportions of audit firm consulting revenues negatively impacted both audit quality and investor perceptions of audit quality. However, they do not find a statistically significant association between audit firm consulting revenues and either audit quality or investor perceptions of audit quality following SOX.
