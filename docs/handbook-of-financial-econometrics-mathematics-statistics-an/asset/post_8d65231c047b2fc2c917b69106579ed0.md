
As a special case of equation (27.7), consider the so-called “Geometric Brownian motion,”

$$ dS(t,w)=\mu S(t,w)dt+\sigma S(t,w)dZ(t,w), \qquad (27.24) $$

in which $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are constants as in Sample Problem 27.2.1. Assume that equation (27.24) describes the price behavior of a certain stock with $S(0,w)$ given. The solution $S(t,w)$ of equation (27.24) is given by

$$ S(t,w)=S(0,w)\exp\big[\big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\big)t+\sigma Z(t,w)\big]. \qquad (27.25) $$

To show that the behavior of equation (27.25) is the solution of equation (27.24) use Itô’s lemma as follows. First, start with equation (27.25), which corresponds to the function $Y(t,w)$. In this case, equation (27.25) is a function of $t$ and $Z(t,w)$, and instead of equation (27.24)

$$ dZ(t,w)=0\cdot dt+1\cdot dZ(t,w). $$

Next, compute the first- and second-order partials denoted by $S_t$, $S_Z$, and $S_{ZZ}$

$$ S_t(t,w)=\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)S(t,w), $$ $$ S_Z(t,w)=\sigma S(t,w), $$ $$ S_{ZZ}(t,w)=\sigma^2 S(t,w). $$

Collect these results and use equation (27.15) to conclude that equation (27.24) holds

$$ dS(t,w)=\Big[\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)S(t,w)+\sigma S(t,w)\cdot0+\frac{\sigma^2}{2}S(t,w)\cdot1\Big]dt+\sigma S(t,w)\cdot1\cdot dZ(t,w)=\mu S(t,w)dt+\sigma S(t,w)dZ(t,w). $$

This result is not only mathematically interesting; in finance, it means that, assuming stock prices are given by an Itô process as in equation (27.24), then equation (27.25) holds as well. To see if equation (27.25) accurately describes stock prices in the real world, rewrite it in the form of log changes (or log returns) as

$$ \ln\frac{S(t,w)}{S(0,w)}=\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)t+\sigma Z(t,w), \qquad (27.26) $$

which is a random variable normally distributed with mean $(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t$ and variance $\sigma^2 t$. This means that if the stock price follows an Itô process, it has a log-normal probability distribution. Such a distribution for stock price is reasonable because it is consistent with reality, where negative stock prices are not possible; the worst that can happen is that the stock price reaches zero, but then again it would take infinite time.

Equation (27.25) is the conceptual cornerstone of Monte Carlo simulation as it is used in most cases for the pricing of more exotic¹ or path-dependent² options: replicating the distribution of a stock price process at any given future time requires us only to generate a large number of normally distributed random numbers with zero mean and variance t and apply repeatedly (27.23) by substituting them for dZ(t, w). Of course, in practice, the correct application of the Monte Carlo simulation involves certain complications regarding the drift or the volatility term, which we'll discuss later. Another way of looking at (27.26) is also motivated from applying the usual convention of continuously compounded returns z_i from S(t + Δt) = S(t) · e^{z_i}.

Successive continuously compounded returns are simply added together to produce the return for the longer time interval as in S(T) = S(0) · e^{z1+z2+⋯+zn}.

Empirical evidence prompts us to impose the assumptions that these returns are independently and identically distributed (without making any additional assumption on their distribution), ensuring thus that the stock price follows a random walk, a property which is tightly associated with the concept of efficient markets in Fama (1970, 1991). Furthermore, as described above, the empirically observed finite means and volatilities of stock returns force us to impose two more assumptions, namely that the continuously compounded stock return means and variances are scaled by time as the size of the time interval becomes smaller. Cox and Miller (1990) show with the use of the Central Limit Theorem that if these four assumptions hold, then the continuously compounded stock returns are normally distributed and therefore the stock price is log-normally distributed.

For a detailed analysis of the properties of a log-normal random distribution see Cox and Rubinstein (1985: 201–204); for a list of bibliographical references on the empirical distribution of stock-price changes see Cox and Rubinstein (1985: 485–488). Sample Problem 27.4.1 provides further illustration.

## 27.5 The Pricing of an Option

An option is a contract giving the right to buy or sell an asset within a specified period of time subject to certain conditions. The simplest kind of option is the European call option, which is a contract to buy a share of a certain stock at a given date for a specified price. The date the option expires is called the expiration date (maturity date) and the price that is paid for the stock when the option is exercised is called the exercise price (strike or striking price).

In terms of economic analysis, several propositions about call-option pricing seem clear. The value of an option increases as the price of the stock increases. If the stock price is much greater than the exercise price, it is almost certain that the option will be exercised; and, analogously, if the price of the stock is much less than the exercise price, the value of the option will be near zero and the option will expire without being exercised. If the expiration date is very far in the future, the value of the option will be approximately equal to the price of the stock. If the expiration date is very near, the value of the option will be approximately equal to the stock price minus the exercise price, or zero if the stock price is less than the exercise price. In general, the value of the option is more volatile than the price of the stock, and the relative volatility of the option depends on both the stock price and maturity.

The first rigorous formulation and solution of the problem of option pricing was achieved by Black and Scholes (1973) and Merton (1973). Consider a stock option denoted by C whose price at time t can be written

$$C(t,w)=C[t,S(t,w)],\tag{27.30}$$

in which C is a twice continuously differentiable function. Here S(t,w) is the price of some stock upon which the option is written. The price of this stock is assumed to follow Itô's stochastic differential equation

$$dS(t,w)=\mu[t,S(t,w)]dt+\sigma[t,S(t,w)]dZ(t,w).\tag{27.31}$$

Assume, as a simplifying case, that $\mu[t,S(t,w)]=\mu S(t,w)$ and $\sigma[t,S(t,w)]=\sigma S(t,w)$. For notational convenience w is suppressed from the various expressions, and sometimes t as well. Therefore, equation (27.31) becomes

$$dS(t)=\mu S(t)dt+\sigma S(t)dZ(t).\tag{27.32}$$

Consider an investor who builds up a portfolio of stocks, options on the stocks, and a riskless asset (for example, government bonds) yielding a riskless rate r. The nominal value of the portfolio, denoted by P(t), is

$$P(t)=N_{1}(t)S(t)+N_{2}C(t)+Q(t),\tag{27.33}$$

where $N_{1}$ is the number of shares of the stock, $N_{2}$ is the number of call options, and $Q$ is the value of dollars invested in riskless bonds.

Assume that the stock pays no dividends or other distributions. By Itô's lemma, the differential of the call price using equations (27.30) and (27.32) is

$$\begin{aligned}dC&=C_{t}dt+C_{S}dS+\tfrac12C_{SS}dS^{2}\\&=\left(C_{t}+C_{S}\mu S+\tfrac12C_{SS}\sigma^{2}S^{2}\right)dt+C_{S}\sigma S\,dZ\tag{27.34}\\&=\mu_{C}Cdt+\sigma_{C}C\,dZ.\end{aligned}$$
