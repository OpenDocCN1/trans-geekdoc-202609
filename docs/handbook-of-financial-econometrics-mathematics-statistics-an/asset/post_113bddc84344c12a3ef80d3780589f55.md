
The contribution reported in this chapter is a simple Markovian model for a class of cash conversion systems and the derivation of the model’s equilibrium state distribution, based on Melamed and Rogers (2015) and Chen et al. (2017). This state distribution is used to derive computable formulas for the associated equilibrium CCC statistics, as well as several financial equilibrium rate metrics. Finally, we use these results to optimize the system’s working capital and capital structure under a number of scenarios, including capital rationing and credit line availability. The numerical results provide some interesting insights into the firm’s optimal operational and financial designs (inventory base stock level and capital structure), as well as the correlation between the CCC and the abovementioned financial metrics.

## 46.3 Technical Preliminaries

In this chapter, we shall use discrete-state random variables and stochastic processes using the following notation (for simplicity, we assume that all mathematical objects exist and are well defined). The indicator function 1_A(x) of set A equals 1 if x ∈ A, and equals 0 if x ∉ A; similarly, the complementary indicator function is 1̄_A(x) = 1 − 1_A(x). For any random variable X, we denote its cumulative distribution function (cdf) by F_X(x) = Pr{X ≤ x} and its density by f_X(x); for a discrete-state X, the density is the probability mass function (pmf) f_X(x) = Pr{X = x}, while for a continuous-state X, it is the probability density function (pdf) f_X(x) = d/dx F_X(x).

Next, we clarify the two fundamental concepts that underpin the ERA approach: equilibrium and rate. In this chapter, we shall use the term equilibrium interchangeably with the terms stationary, invariant and steady state. Recall that a system is said to be in equilibrium if the probability law (distribution) of its state process is time invariant.² Stochastic systems may or may not have an equilibrium regime, but if they do, they might have multiple ones depending on their initial state distribution; if they evolve into equilibrium, such systems are said to have a conditional long-run distribution, obtained as a limiting equilibrium distribution. Mathematically, many stochastic systems converge to equilibrium (in distribution) as time tends to infinity regardless of the initial state distribution, in which case they are said to have a (unique) long-run distribution. ## 46.4 Cash Conversion Model

In this section, we define a Markovian model of the associated cash conversion system, which incorporates both SCOM and SCFM aspects, including financial constraints (funds availability, both debt and equity, to be used in purchasing product). This model will be used to derive the optimal capital structure and inventory sizing, simultaneously; see Myers (1977), Bradley et al. (1984) and Stulz (1990) for a discussion of optimal capital structures.

### 46.4.1 Cash conversion model description

Consider a firm that owns a single-product MTS inventory, a treasury, as well as a receivables pool (Accounts Receivable) which tracks all pending payments. Thus, the working capital of the system is its “wealth” in the form of funds, product units and receivables. The firm has access to a revolving credit line from which the firm can borrow up to a prescribed credit limit; the amount of untapped credit at any given time will be referred to as residual credit. Here, we abstract the notion of a working capital unit or untapped credit unit by viewing any of its forms as a product-equivalent monetary unit (PEMU), each equaling the product unit cost to the firm. Because the cost

of a borrowed PEMU is always additional to the cost of non-debt PEMU in working capital (in treasury, inventory, or receivables pool), the firm uses the following financial pecking order: it always uses its non-debt treasury funds first to pay for replenished product, and borrows only when such funds are exhausted; conversely, collected receivables are used to pay down outstanding debt first, and are directed to the treasury only when all credit debt is paid in full.

Figure 46.2 depicts this system as a queueing network. Thus, our model consists of two interacting supply chains: one of product and the other of funds. Mathematically, it is a population process of PEMUs circulating directionally among three pools (inventory, receivables and available funds), subject to identity changes. As such, it captures the turning of the “wheel of commerce” in that it describes how money turns into product and product into money. The system starts operations with a capital investment in the treasury of G > 0 PEMUs, a credit line of H ≥ 0 PEMUs, an empty inventory with base stock level S > 0 PEMUs, and no receivables. The reason for requiring a positive G is that borrowing is often subject to covenants that restrict the percentage of debt relative to equity, thereby requiring the borrower to risk some of its own funds. The term available funds refers to the sum of cash on hand in the treasury and residual credit. Furthermore, the quantity A = G + H is the firm’s available funds maximum. Without loss of generality, we assume that S ≤ G + H, since if S > G + H, the system’s

behavior is unchanged as compared to the case of S = G + H. Note that in the model of Figure 46.2 the pool of available funds commingles two PEMU pools: the firm’s cash on hand and the firm’s residual credit. This can be done without losing track of each pool, since the state of available funds, k, has the following interpretation: for 0 ≤ k < H, the treasury is empty and k is the residual credit (so the firm’s debt is H − k PEMUs), whereas for H ≤ k ≤ G + H, the credit line is untapped.

Demands of size 1 arrive at the inventory following a Poisson process with arrival rate λ > 0, but a demand that cannot be filled is lost. However, if a demand is filled, the corresponding revenue is allowed to become a receivable, which arrives at the available funds pool after an exponential time with rate parameter ν > 0. All times to payment are iid and independent of all other processes. We assume that each revenue inflow is larger than a PEMU, but the excess over the PEMU’s value is immediately disbursed to the firm owners as profit.

Replenishment in our model is subject to financial constraints (the availability of funds in the funds pool to pay for an arriving replenishment). Consequently, the traditional MTS replenishment regime is replaced by the Financial-Make-to-Stock (FMTS) regime as follows: If the number of PEMUs in the inventory is n and the available funds is k, then the number of product units undergoing replenishment is L_{n,k} = min{S − n, k}. The replenishment regime for each inventory unit has an exponential lead time with rate μ > 0, where all lead times are iid and independent of all other processes.

Next, define the cash conversion process to be the state process of the system depicted in Figure 46.2, namely, as the trivariate process {(I(t), C(t), R(t)) : t ≥ 0}, where I(t) is the number of product PEMUs in the inventory, C(t) is the number of available funds (in PEMUs) in the treasury and residual credit pool, and R(t) is the number of receivable PEMUs in the receivables pool, all at time t. We assume throughout that the process {(I(t), C(t), R(t)) : t ≥ 0} is PEMU conserving in the sense that

I(t) + C(t) + R(t) = G + H,  t ≥ 0.  (46.5)

To this end, we assume not only that any net profit resulting from a filled demand is immediately disbursed to the owners, but also that when costs reduce the number of PEMUs below G + H, the shortage is promptly made good by the owners, thereby precluding bankruptcy. This situation typically holds when the firm is a subsidiary of a parent company that serves as its guarantor.
