
The extended structural approach illustrates the values of closed-form solutions in the BT model. There is an interesting argument for elimination of moral hazard effect in Table 15.1; it states that the FDIC value accrues as asset volatility increases. Episcopos (2008) also explains the negative effect of an increase in volatility on actual equity value (down-and-out call option), thus claiming that the rise in “total equity” value would transfer from the shareholders to the FDIC. However, Episcopos (2008) doesn’t take into account the default risk for the FDIC. The total value of the FDIC is combined contingent asset (down-and-in option) with a promised payment for depositors (European put option). If the speed of transformation from shareholders’ equity to the FDIC is slow, the FDIC’s asset value cannot handle the payment of deposits but the shareholders still obtain a part of asset value; therefore moral hazard still exists.

### 15.3.2 Moral hazard problem

There is an alternative way to solve moral hazard in deposit insurance by the investigation of the relationship between the FDIC value and the policy of closure rule, that is, the level of closure barrier. Without the consideration of bankruptcy costs, Figure 15.5 shows the existence of moral hazard when the supervisory policy is of forbearance. That is, the lower closure barrier will cause a decrease in the FDIC’s value when the risk of a bank’s asset increases. Panel A of Figure 15.5 shows the overall impacts of the levels of a bank’s risk and closure barrier on FDIC value. Panel B, as a part of the cross-section of panel A, describes that the FDIC value declines and moral hazard happens under a lower barrier when the asset risk increases. In contrast, under a higher barrier, the increase of the FDIC value follows the increase of asset risk. This means the strict supervision policy would diminish moral hazard. Therefore, the FDIC should adopt the strict supervision policy to monitor the states of banks under the assumption of no bankruptcy costs.
