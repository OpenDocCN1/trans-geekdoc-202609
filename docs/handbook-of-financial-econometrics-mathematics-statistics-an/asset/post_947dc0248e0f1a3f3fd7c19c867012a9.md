To examine the external consistency of the Heston SV model, we conduct a rolling Wald test for the hypothesis \(\eta_t^{SV}\rho_t^{SV} = \eta_t^{RV}\rho_t^{RV}\). Figure 33.5 (Panel (b)) depicts the Wald test statistic and the corresponding p-values. The analyzed time period contains stretches where the hypothesis can and cannot be rejected. However, even the substantial number of trading days when the hypothesis is rejected casts doubt on the external consistency of the Heston SV model.

Figure 33.6 depicts the volatility scale estimates obtained from time series stochastic volatility model (33.5), fitted separately to realized variation and calibrated variance: \(\{\sigma^{RV}_t\}\) are parameters of model (33.5) fitted to \(RV_t\), while \(\{\sigma^{SV}_t\}\) are parameters of model (33.5) fitted to calibrated variance. Once again, to more clearly depict their relative dynamics, \(\{\sigma^{RV}_t\}\) are plotted against the primary vertical axis (on the left), and \(\{\sigma^{SV}_t\}\) are plotted against the secondary vertical axis (on the right). While the levels of \(\{\sigma^{RV}_t\}\) and \(\{\sigma^{SV}_t\}\) are clearly different, the patterns in their dynamics are similar. The 95% confidence intervals of \(\{\sigma^{SV}_t\}\) lie almost entirely below unity, which suggests that the calibrated variance overestimates the actual variation of the underlying process, invalidating Hypothesis 3.

Finally, to test the robustness to jumps in the underlying asset, we also estimate model (33.5) for the econometric measure of continuous variation, as captured with bipower variation. The final group of columns in Table 33.3 contains the parameter estimates of model (33.5) fitted to bipower variation, \(\{V^{BPV}_t\}\). The instantaneous correlation parameters resulting from fitting the model to \(V^{RV}_t\) and \(V^{BPV}_t\) are quite similar, while the model fitted to \(V^{BPV}_t\) suggests far smaller estimates of volatility of variance. The latter observation about volatility of variance estimates is to be expected because \(V^{BPV}_t\) captures only the continuous component of variation and jumps tend to cause high values of \(V^{RV}_t\). For the same reasons, the volatility scale \(\sigma\) is higher when the model is fitted to the \(V^{BPV}_t\). Carrying out the analysis along the template set for the comparison of calibrated variance and realized variations, one arrives at results that also lead to the rejection of the external consistency of the Heston model. Finally, substituting realized kernel variance estimates, \(V^{RK}_t\), instead of realized variation, \(V^{RV}_t\), in the analysis above yields qualitatively similar results.

This paper presents evidence that variance in stochastic volatility models does not satisfy the postulated model dynamics. Thus, calibrated variance based on stochastic volatility models is an artificial device for reproducing the desired features of the implied volatility surface. Furthermore, it does not reflect the dynamics of the true variance of the underlying returns. Thus, calibration to market prices of option contracts alone does not yield a process under the risk-neutral probability measure that is consistent with the actual evolution of variance. These results underscore the challenges of joint calibration to market prices of derivative assets and to actual processes for the corresponding underlying assets. The dissonance between calibrated and true variance may also provide a ready explanation for the parameter instability of calibrated stochastic volatility models.
