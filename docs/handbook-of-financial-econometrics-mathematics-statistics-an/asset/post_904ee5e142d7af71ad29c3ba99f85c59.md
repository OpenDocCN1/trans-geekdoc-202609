The impact of an improper observation horizon can be investigated first by finding the first derivative of the covariance defined as in (16) with respect to the holding period m. Thus,

```
$$\frac{d(\text{Cov})}{dm} = d[(1+R_f)^m \ln(1+R_f) - (1+\mu)^m \ln(1+\mu)]. \tag{69.20}$$
```

Then if $R_f$ is greater than $\mu$,

```
$$\frac{d(\text{Cov})}{dm} > d[(1+R_f)^m \ln(1+R_f) - (1+\mu)^m \ln(1+R_f)],$$\n\n since $\ln(1+R_f) > \ln(1+\mu)$ for $R_f > \mu$\n\n$= d \cdot \ln(1+R_f) \cdot [(1+R_f)^m - (1+\mu)^m] > 0,\ (R_f > 0),$\n\n since the factors $d$, $\ln(1+R_f)$, and $[(1+R_f)^m - (1+\mu)^m]$ are all positive. \tag{69.21}$$
```

The above inequality implies that the covariance between the estimated Sharpe’s measure and the estimated risk measure for the m-period case is a strictly increasing function of the length of the observation horizon, m, if the risk-free rate $R_f$ is greater than the mean rate of return on the market portfolio $\mu$ for the one-period case. Similarly, if $R_f$ is less than $\mu$,

```

```

Thus, the m-period covariance is a strictly decreasing function of the length of the observation horizon, m, if $R_f$ is less than $\mu$. Similarly, when R_f is less than μ, the m-period covariance increases in the negative direction when the observation horizon is longer than the true investment horizon. Hence, if an observation horizon longer than the true investment horizon is used, the m-period covariance is expected to increase in either positive or negative direction depending on the sign of the difference between the risk-free rate R_f and the mean rate of return on the market portfolio μ.

- (ii) **An observation horizon shorter than the true investment horizon:** When the observation horizon is shorter than the true investment horizon, the m-period covariance becomes smaller if the risk-free rate R_f is greater than the mean rate of return on the market portfolio. For the case where R_f is less than μ, a weaker negative covariance is expected if the observation horizon approaches zero.

Therefore, in conducting empirical research, an improper observation horizon will have a significant impact on the covariance between the estimated Sharpe’s measure and the estimated risk measure. It is interesting to note that an observation horizon shorter than the true investment horizon can reduce the dependence of the estimated Sharpe’s performance measure on its estimated risk measure. An observation horizon longer than the true investment horizon will magnify the dependence. These results indicate that a shorter observation horizon should be used in empirical research to reduce the bias associated with the estimated Sharpe’s measure.

The results of this section and previous sections have shown that the estimated composite performance measures may be highly correlated with its estimated risk proxies. In general, the sample size, the investment horizon and the market condition are three dominant factors in determining those relationships. Johnson and Burgess (1975) and Burgess and Johnson (1976) examined the effects of sample sizes and sampling fluctuation on the accuracy of both portfolio and security analyses. They concluded that the number of historical observations is important to yield efficient portfolio performance characteristics. Their conclusion is similar to the result associated with the impact of sample size on the relationship between the estimated composite measures and their risk proxies derived in this study.

## 69.4 Summary

In this paper, the statistical relationships between the estimated Sharpe performance measure and its risk proxy are derived in accordance with statistical distribution theory. The above-mentioned statistical relationships are generally affected by the sample size, the investment horizon and the market conditions associated with the sample period selected for empirical studies. In addition, a larger sample of historical observations and an appropriate investment horizon can generally be used to improve the usefulness of the Sharpe’s performance measure in both portfolio and mutual fund management. Finally, the standard sample estimate of Sharpe’s measure is not an unbiased estimator; the unbiased estimate of Sharpe’s measure can be derived for use by either portfolio managers or academic researchers, that is, the unbiased estimate is the sample estimate of Sharpe’s measure divided by the error factor.
