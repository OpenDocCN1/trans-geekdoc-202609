# 24.3.2 **The existence of a risk neutral measure and the absence of arbitrage**

Besides giving investor’s preferences to pursue the tighten option bounds, there is an alternative way to pursue the narrower bounds under a risk-neutral measure (or called an equivalent martingale measure). In an arbitrage-free and complete financial market, the price of an option can be evaluated by the expected value of its discounted cash flows under a risk-neutral measure if the price processes of the underlying asset are specified. For example, by assumption of the lognormal distribution of the underlying asset, European style options can be obtained in closed-form formulas by Black and Scholes (1973) and Merton (1973).

However, there is no unique specified risk-neutral measure to accurately evaluate option prices because of the lack of precise information on the underlying asset dynamics. Chazal and Jouini (2008) adopt a duality approach to derive the upper bound of European call option when the underlying asset dynamics are not specified. Rather than applying the restrictions to distributions and investor’s preferences, Ryan (2000) analyze arbitrage opportunities based on the existing set of options with same maturity. By using Ritchken’s (1985) linear programming method, the bounds of an options in Ryan (2000) are subject to the constraining prices of the underlying asset, the riskless asset, and other observed options with the same underlying asset and same maturity date.

Another stream consider the underlying asset’s fluctuations to search for the tighten option bounds (Christoffersen et al., 2010). Many studies utilize a stochastic volatility model or other advanced approach to precisely capture the movements of the underlying asset and then obtain option prices in accordance with this specification assumption (Hull and White, 1987). Frey and Sin (1999) determine the maximum and minimum of the set of possible European option prices for a large class of stochastic volatility models under no-arbitrage assumption. Lupu (2006) used Monte Carlo simulation to estimate tighten option bounds when the underlying asset follows a jump-diffusion process. Oancea and Perrakis (2007) also extend the stochastic dominance approach by Perrakis and Ryan (1984) to derive the narrow option pricing bounds under the different assumptions of the underlying asset following a jump-diffusion process, a stochastic volatility model, or a GARCH process. However, uncertainty of the model of the underlying asset dynamics might be subject to model risk problem (Cont, 2006). Thus, the calibration of option bounds under the specified price process of the underlying asset should be taken into account (King et al., 2005; Pinar, 2009).
