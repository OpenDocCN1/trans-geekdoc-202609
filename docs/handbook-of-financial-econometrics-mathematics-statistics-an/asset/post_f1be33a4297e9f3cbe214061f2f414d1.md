Besides Zhang (1994) and Lee et al. (2002), several papers try to extend or generalize Lo's (1987) approach. Grundy (1991) derives option bounds by considering higher moments of the underlying asset's distribution. Grundy (1991)'s upper bound of a European call option can be derived by given knowledge that the value of the $n$th moment multiplied by the $n$th power of current price of underlying asset should be equal to the $n$th power of the value of underlying asset at maturity date. Boyle and Lin (1997) apply Lo (1987)'s semi-parametric method to a European call option on maximum of several assets and a lookback option. Bertsimas and Popescu (2002) also extend Lo (1987)'s upper bound of a European call option by considering the correlation between European call options with different strike price. Lo's (1987) approach is also improved by Gotoh and Konno (2002). They propose an efficient cutting plane algorithm which can efficiently solve the semi-definite programming problem associated to the bound depending on the first $n$ moments. Chazal and Jouini (2008) put constraints on the two first risk neutral moments and use some qualitative information on the risk-neutral measure in order to improve the bound of Lo (1987).

## 24.2.4.2 Non-parametric method

The advantages of non-parametric method used in derivation of option bounds are no assumptions of the underlying stock's distribution, interest rates or volatility in option pricing framework. Rodriguez (2003) uses a non-parametric method and a single analytical framework to derive the option bounds. Rodriguez (2003) states that the disparate methods applied to option bounds can be viewed as a single analytical framework with the synthesis lying in the derivation of call option bounds which depends on the discount factor of its corresponding put option. The general expression for the value of a European call option in a single analytical framework can be written as

$C_0 = S_0 - K e^{-rT} + R_p \int_{0}^{K} F(S_T) d(S_T), \qquad (24.23)$

where $r$ is risk-free rate, $F(S_T)$ is the cumulative distribution function of underlying stock price, and $R_p$ is the discount factor of its corresponding put option. Given the suitable lower bounds of the put's discount factor, Rodriguez (2003) provides the tighter lower bound than previous work.

Lin et al. (2012) derived a tighter lower bound and re-interpret Ritchken (1985) upper bound of European options under non-parametric framework. The only variable used in these bounds is the dollar beta of an option which is defined as a covariance between an option price and its underlying asset’s price over the variance of its underlying asset’s price. Lin et al. (2012) utilize the general classical asset pricing model with a stochastic kernel to derive the lower bound at time t for a European call option as follows:

$C_{t,\min}^{E} = D_{t,T} E[C_T] + \beta_C \{S_t - D_{t,T} E[S_T]\},$

where $\beta_C = \frac{\text{Cov}(C_T,S_T)}{\text{Var}(S_T)}$ denotes the dollar beta of the call option, $D_{t,T}$ is the risk-free discount factor and can be written as $D_{t,T} = e^{-r(T-t)}$ if the risk free rate is non-stochastic. The lower bound of a European put option can be derived by the put-call parity as follows:

$P_t = C_t + D_{t,T} K - S_t \geq C_{t,\min}^{E} + D_{t,T} K - S_t = P_{t,\min}^{E}.$

By substituting the lower bound formula of a European call option, then we can get

$P_{t,\min}^{E} = D_{t,T} E[P_T] + \beta_P \{S_t - D_{t,T} E[S_T]\},$

where $\beta_P = \frac{\text{Cov}(P_T,S_T)}{\text{Var}(S_T)} = 1 - \beta_C$ denotes the dollar beta of the put option and can be calculated by the dollar beta of the call option. By using histogram method to calculate the expected payoffs of the stock and the option, then lower bounds of European options can be obtained under non-parametric framework.

## 24.3 Methods to Tighten Bounds for European Options

In this section, we review the previous studies that attempt to tighten bounds for European options by adding assumptions on investors’ preference, adding restrictions on pricing kernel, selecting some risk neutral measures, and adding frictions in financial markets. The tightened bounds for European options in markets with frictions are also discussed in detail.

### 24.3.1 Restrictions on investor preferences and pricing kernel

The assumptions of investor behaviors and the related pricing kernel (called a state-contingent discount factor or stochastic discount factor) have paved the way for searching tighter option bounds (Perrakis and Ryan, 1984; Levy, 1985; Ritchken, 1985; Ritchken and Kuo, 1989; Mathur and Ritchken, 1999; Basso and Pianca, 1997, 2001; Mello and Pyo, 2006, 2008). Kimball (1990) deeply and stringently discussed the standard risk-aversion concept to explain that investors will reduce current expense and save more for the uncertainty of future income. Therefore, previous papers usually assume risk-averse investors in markets to obtain narrower option bounds. For example, non-decreasing concave utility functions used in Levy (1985), assumption of decreasing absolute risk aversion (DARA) used in Ritchken and Kuo (1989) and Basso and Pianca (1997), application of standard risk aversion (SRA) rules in Basso and Pianca (2001), and non-increasing absolute risk-aversion utility functions utilized in Mathur and Ritchken (1999).

If the investor preference (utility function) is given, the pricing kernel in each state can be constructed by the discounted marginal utility of wealth in that state divided by the expected marginal utility. Thus, we can use this pricing kernel to evaluate all securities including options as well. By incorporating with no-arbitrage assumption, the option bounds can be obtained by searching minimum and maximum option prices which are determined by the pricing kernel (Ritchken, 1985; Ritchken and Kuo, 1989; Basso and Pianca, 1997, 2001; Mello and Pyo, 2006). Mello and Pyo (2006) and Pyo (2008) employ minimax deviation approach to derive the bounds for real options in accordance with the risk preference of the decision maker.

Alternative way to apply pricing kernel technique to the derivations of option bounds is to investigate the characteristics of pricing kernel. Bernardo and Ledoit (2000) propose “gain–loss ratio bounds” for options by linking pricing kernel to gain–loss ratio of investment’s excess payoff, i.e., the ratio of positive expected value of excess payoff to its expected negative value. The narrow option bounds can be obtained by maximizing gain–loss ratio. Cochrane and Saa-Requejo (2000) link the volatility of pricing kernel to Sharpe ratio and propose “good-deal bounds” for options that lower (upper) option bounds are the minimization (maximization) of option prices under the constraints of the volatility of pricing kernel less than Sharpe ratio of the option. Bondarenko and Longarela (2004) further modify “good-deal bounds” for an option which its underlying asset follows stochastic volatility model. Huang (2004) generalizes option bounds by using the elasticity of the pricing kernel and shows that Cochrane and Saa-Requejo’s (2000) approach is a special case of Huang’s (2004) approach when underlying asset follows lognormal distribution. Christoffersen et al. (2010) show that using a quadratic equivalent martingale measure (EMM) and GARCH framework also can derive good-deal bounds on option prices.

### 24.3.2 The existence of a risk neutral measure and the absence of arbitrage

Besides giving investor’s preferences to pursue the tighter option bounds, there is an alternative way to pursue the narrower bounds under a risk-neutral measure (or called an equivalent martingale measure). In an arbitrage-free and complete financial market, the price of an option can be evaluated by the expected value of its discounted cash flows under a risk-neutral measure if the price processes of the underlying asset are specified. For example, by assumption of the lognormal distribution of the underlying asset, European style options can be obtained in closed-form formulas by Black and Scholes (1973) and Merton (1973).

However, there is no unique specified risk-neutral measure to accurately evaluate option prices because of the lack of precise information on the underlying asset dynamics. Chazal and Jouini (2008) adopt a duality approach to derive the upper bound of European call option when the underlying asset dynamics are not specified. Rather than applying the restrictions to distributions and investor’s preferences, Ryan (2000) analyze arbitrage opportunities based on the existing set of options with same maturity. By using Ritchken’s (1985) linear programming method, the bounds of an option in Ryan (2000) are subject to the constraining prices of the underlying asset, the riskless asset, and other observed options with the same underlying asset and same maturity date.

Another stream considers the underlying asset’s fluctuations to search for the tighter option bounds (Christoffersen et al., 2010). Many studies utilize a stochastic volatility model or other advanced approach to precisely capture the movements of the underlying asset and then obtain option prices in accordance with this specification assumption (Hull and White, 1987). Frey and Sin (1999) determine the maximum and minimum of the set of possible European option prices for a large class of stochastic volatility models under no-arbitrage assumption. Lupu (2006) used Monte Carlo simulation to estimate tighter option bounds when the underlying asset follows a jump-diffusion process. Oancea and Perrakis (2007) also extend the stochastic dominance approach by Perrakis and Ryan (1984) to derive the narrow option pricing bounds under the different assumptions of the underlying asset following a jump-diffusion process, a stochastic volatility model, or a GARCH process. However, uncertainty of the model of the underlying asset dynamics might be subject to model risk problem (Cont, 2006). Thus, the calibration of option bounds under the specified price process of the underlying asset should be taken into account (King et al., 2005; Pinar, 2009).
