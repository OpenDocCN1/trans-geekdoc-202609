Prior studies (Blaylock et al., 2017; Clemons and Shevlin, 2016; Hanlon, 2003; Lev and Nissim, 2004) suggest that large positive book-tax differences (accounting income greater than taxable income) seem to signal low earnings quality and investors use information in book-tax differences to assess earnings persistence. Phillip et al. (2003) also show the incremental usefulness of deferred tax expense (which increases with book-tax differences) as a metric to detect earnings management.

Prior research documents that compensation committee do intervene in setting executive compensation. Dechow et al. (1994) and Gaver and Gaver (1998) find that compensation committees shield executive compensation from the adverse effect on current period earnings of potentially value-enhancing restructurings. Adut et al. (2003) extends this analysis by documenting that compensation committees evaluate the context of individual restructurings in determining the degree to which they will intervene to protect executive compensation from these charges. Specifically, compensation committees appear to: (1) completely shield initial and subsequent restructuring charges for CEOs with long tenure, provided that the firm had not recorded a charge in the two immediately prior years, (2) do not shield subsequent restructuring charges taken by short-tenured CEOs if the firm reported a prior restructuring charge within 2 years of the current charge, (3) and partially shield the other categories of restructuring charges. They interpret their evidence as suggesting that compensation committees assess a compensation penalty on CEO in contexts where the restructuring charge is more likely to be opportunistic. Duru et al. (2002) provide evidence that firms shield CEO compensation from income-decreasing effects of strategic expenditures such as research and development and advertising expenditures.

The board of directors is an important monitoring mechanism of managerial performance. Fama and Jensen (1983) argue that reputational considerations and managerial labor-market provide incentives to directors to properly monitor firm’s performance. As a result of difficulty in observing managerial effort, directors are likely to use observable signals of managerial effort. Prior studies typically model CEO compensation as a function of two performance signals: accounting earnings and stock price (Lambert and Larcker, 1987; Banker and Datar, 1989; Sloan, 1993).
