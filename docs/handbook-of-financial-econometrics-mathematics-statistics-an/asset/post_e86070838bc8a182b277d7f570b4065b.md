The main advantage of this technology is speed. Mobile banking also retains the benefits Internet banking offers. In the digital era, banking services have become more closely connected to mobile devices. For instance, some smartphones can be used instead of payment cards as a part of contactless payment services or using fingerprint scanning rather than passwords (KPMG, 2015). Mobile devices also allow customers to receive more personalized services.

Another significant factor in the model (−0.185) is the perceived effort that negatively influences the intention to use mobile banking. It is also similar to the results of the existing study on mobile banking in different countries (Luarn and Lin, 2005; Hanafizadeh *et al.*, 2014). The size of screens and keypads of the mobile device requires customers to concentrate on them more carefully, while using mobile apps, lowering the ease of use. Mobile banking helps banks offer many new services; however, this also makes mobile apps more complex. The easier an app is to use, the more likely the customer will use the app.

The next factor is perceived financial costs (−0.144). The same results are obtained in Luarn and Lin (2005) and Hanafizadeh *et al.* (2014). They showed that this factor was important in different countries. Extra costs have a strong negative impact on the intention to use mobile banking. Moreover, even small fees could be crucial for young people when deciding whether or not to use mobile banking (TASS, 2016).

Contrary to previous studies, the significance of social influence (Zhou *et al.*, 2010; Riquelme and Rios, 2010) and perceived risk (Koenig-Lewis *et al.*, 2010; Riquelme and Rios, 2010) are insignificant in our model. Actually, customers just did not perceive risk although it may exist. Thus, very often, Russian mobile banking users report the surplus of security procedures. Moreover, the high quantity of such procedures is reported even as a weakness of mobile banking (Deloitte, 2014a). Nevertheless, mobile banking may be really less risky than other remote services in Russia. Fraudsters prefer Internet banking because the transaction amounts are higher. Second, fraudsters also usually attack legal entities which rarely use mobile apps (Kostylev, 2013).

We suppose that the absence of the impact caused by social influence may also reflect trends in the Russian mobile banking market or cultural characteristics of Russian customers. Thus, social influence usually comes from a high level of awareness and financial literacy. However, according to the National Agency for Financial Studies (NAFI, 2016), 26% of adult population in Russia did not have a bank account and about a half of it did not use remote access to the bank account. In such conditions, there is no social pressure towards customers who use or do not use mobile banking in general. Another challenge is the low level of financial literacy: people from a group with high level of financial literacy use mobile banking four times more. However, only 12% and 21% of the Russian population correctly answered on all four or three questions that measure the level of financial literacy, respectively, in 2016, while 20% had a limited level of financial literacy (NAFI, 2016). At least, 41% estimated their financial knowledge and skills as unsatisfied or very unsatisfied. In such a case, the intention to use mobile banking could not be pushed by any social benefits like image improvement or “be like all” feeling.

However, we also find some significant indirect effect. Self-efficacy has a negative impact on perceived efforts (−0.535). The main item of self-efficacy is interface unattractiveness. If a customer finds it user-unfriendly, their efforts will be much lower. Ease of use is also demonstrated as an important factor in empirical studies (Luarn and Lin, 2005; Gu et al., 2009). However, we assume that young people are in general more experienced in terms of using mobile app.

Two important trends that may also explain this finding were presented by Deloitte (2014b). On the one hand, the leaders of Russian mobile banking market stopped burdening their apps with as much functions as possible and paid more attention in involving customers in mobile channels only in 2014. On the other hand, some banks started to develop a range of mobile apps that were less complicated and may be used only for specific goals and tasks. This means that more users may feel that they are ready to use mobile banking apps because they need to learn less.

Perceived efforts also have two significant relationships. First, this factor has a negative impact on perceived usefulness (−0.353). When a customer finds it difficult to use mobile banking, it means that the user will perform operations slower than assumed and will perceive the time saved as being less. According to Deloitte (2014a), some market leaders offer mobile apps that are not easy to use and do not have a user-friendly interface. Even if mobile banking fits the customer needs, it does not lead to a decrease in their perceived efforts. Second, high perceived efforts turn into fear of fraud or technical error, and customer perception of risk will change. Too many difficulties to use mobile banking apps cause an increase in potential risks (0.473) which might lead to a potential increase in financial losses.

Finally, we do not find a relationship between lifestyle compatibility and perceived efforts although some researchers report this relationship as significant (Koenig-Lewis et al., 2010; Gu et al., 2009; Hanafizadeh et al., 2014). We suppose that this is so because most Russian banks do not use modern technologies required by customers (Deloitte, 2014a). Actually, Russian customers use mobile apps mostly for small transactions (Frank Research Group, 2014). According to the experts interviewed by Frank Research Group, the most popular functions for mobile banking users (even for frequent) are basic ones like checking bank account balance and making transactions between own accounts, but in order to make more important financial decisions customers prefer to visit the bank. In such a case, even a high compatibility with lifestyle does not lead to the improvement of perceived effort because this compatibility relates only to few basic functions.

## 71.6 Conclusion

This chapter focuses on the adoption of mobile banking by smartphone and tablet users from 18 to 44 years. First, we find that our results for smartphone and tablet users are comparable with the existing literature. The most important positive factor is perceived usefulness while the most significant negative factor is perceived effort. This result also confirms the theoretical foundations of the basic theories because either these factors or their equivalents are used in all three main popular adoption theories (TAM, IDT, UTAUT). Other empirical studies which were run in different countries also found these factors the most powerful (Shaikh and Karjaluoto, 2015; Ha et al., 2012).

We show the importance of marketing policy, and that it is important for banks to develop methods of involving customers in mobile services. This result is consistent with the behavior of market leaders, who began to move from simple functional expansion towards building marketing strategies (Deloitte, 2014b). If a consumer finds mobile banking useful, he or she will probably use it. While benefits like the speed of transactions and ease of access seem to be incontestable, mobile banking is still not very popular. The main reason is the low rate of awareness. It is also crucial to improve the interface of mobile banking apps. The incentives to use mobile banking increase when it is easy to use it. Customers also feel more comfortable while working with an attractive-looking service. A user-friendly interface makes the relationship with the bank more understandable for customers. It helps consumers cope with psychological barriers like the fear of error or fraud.

Moreover, self-efficacy and perceived financial costs are also found to be important for customers. However, we do not find any impact of social influence, perceived risks and compatibility with lifestyle although these factors are found significant in the literature. We suppose that such results underline the importance of the country and cultural differences and more precisely of the development of banking system and required infrastructure (smartphone and tablets penetration, tariffs for mobile Internet usage). It may also be the features related precisely for smartphone and tablet users who are the target group of this study. At least, the number of factors may be wider than the ones discussed in this chapter.

There are some crucial limitations of this study. First, our sample consists only of smartphone and tablet users and the sample majority tends to be rather young (18-24 years). Although as discussed in the methodology section, Y and Z generations seem to be the most relevant target groups for mobile banking usage, we are aware that a sample bias might exist. In such a case, the results may change if updated and a larger sample beyond the national context or other criteria for sampling are used. Second, as a target variable, we use the intention to use, not real usage. Although such simplification is widely used in empirical studies (Shaikh and Karjaluoto, 2015), some longitudinal studies should be done in this area.

There are also some key directions for future research. It may be important to divide the initial intention to use mobile banking for the first time and the intention to use it again. Cross-country and cross-cultural studies may also be more informative. Moreover, due to different business strategies associated with different degrees of mobile app enhancement, it may not be the truth that all potential adopters especially in different regions face the same supply of mobile banking. At least, while the demand-side studies investigate mobile banking from customers’ point of view, the supply-side studies discuss banks’ interest in developing mobile apps, which together may shed some light on this field of study.
