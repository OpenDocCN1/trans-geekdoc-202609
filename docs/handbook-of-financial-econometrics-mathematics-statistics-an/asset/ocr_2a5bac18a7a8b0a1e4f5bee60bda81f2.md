# 60.2 Relevant Literature

Merton (1974) suggested a credit risk model that explains how the probability of company default can be inferred from the market valuation of companies under some assumptions on asset and loan values evolvement. More specifically, the event of default is determined by the market value of the firm’s assets jointly with the firm’s liability structure. If the value of the assets falls below a threshold, then the firm is considered to be in default. Many authors such as Black and Cox (1976), Geske (1977), Longstaff and Schwartz (1995), and Collin-Dufresne and Goldstein (2001) have expanded Merton’s model. Also, there exist a number of studies which compare the performance of alternative models but fail to establish their superiority to Merton’s model (see Eom et al., 2002; Gemmill, 2002).

Merton’s model uses a formula for credit spread which is based on asset growth, asset volatility and leverage as the key economic drivers for bankruptcy. The best measure to capture a firm’s asset value is through the firm’s equity value. The firm issues a zero coupon bond and equity shares to finance its assets. The leverage shows the bounds of default. When the assets’ value falls below the default boundary then the firm defaults. According to Christie (1982), the changes in the degree of financial leverage are related to the bank’s stock return (also see Alexander and Kaeck, 2008). If stock returns are positive, leverage will decrease, leading to lower credit spreads and hence a negative relationship between stocks returns and credit risk is expected.

A firm’s probability of default may be influenced by the firm value volatility. Benkert (2004) testes different volatility measures on CDS spreads and concludes that option-implied volatility is the most appropriate one. Merton’s model suggests that the higher the asset volatility, the higher credit spreads are going to be because the probability of default is greater. Chen et al. (2007) use historical volatilities while Cao et al. (2010) show that the implied volatility of individual stock options has significant explanatory power for CDS spreads. Alexander and Kaeck (2008), have tested historical volatility against implied volatility indices and conclude that the latter performs better.

In Merton (1974), the risk-free interest rate constitutes the drift in the risk neutral world. His model suggests that the risk-free interest rate should decrease the probability of default as the higher the rate is, the higher the risk neutral drift will be.
