Some researchers even combined IDT and TAM models as a theoretical framework. A strong negative relationship between risk and the intention to use mobile banking was shown in Riquelme and Rios (2010) and Koenig-Lewis et al. (2010).

At least, extended UTAUT-based models tend to be better in statistical terms than the original UTAUT model. Zhou et al. They found that the integrated model had more power in predicting consumer behavior than the separate one. Another important finding was the relationship between social influence and the intention to use.

Moreover, Afshan and Sharif (2016) not only combined UTAUT and task-technology fit factors but also the components of initial trust. Such combination allows them to explore behavioral, technological and environmental aspects of mobile banking usage simultaneously. In comparison with some other papers, they reported much higher explanatory power of the model that tested the intention to use mobile banking.

In addition, Baptista and Oliveira (2015) used UTAUT2 model with cultural moderators. On the one hand, they received both convergent and divergent results. Thus, some of their results were not consistent with the existing literature. The authors explained such inconsistency by the particular features of the explored region (Africa). However, their model provides more predictive power than UTAUT2.

Thus, in the literature, there are three most widespread adoption theories: Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT) and Unified Theory of the Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). However, these theories are not purely used and are usually introduced as a starting point while searching for the determinants of the intention to use mobile banking.

## 7.1.3 Research Model and Hypothesis

From the literature review, we can find two main constructs which are included in all three basic models (TAM, IDT, UTAUT) as well as in their modifications. These factors are “perceived usefulness” and “perceived efforts”. As shown in Venkatesh et al. (2003), there were quite similar constructs: perceived usefulness in TAM, relative advantage in IDT and performance expectancy in UTAUT. Baptista and Oliveira (2016) reported that the same unification could be done for the second factor and the perceived ease of use (TAM), ease of use (IDT) and effort expectancy (UTAUT) were also comparable constructs.

Recently, Shaikh and Karjaluoto (2015) provided a meta-analysis of 55 papers related to mobile banking adoption from 2005 to 2014. According to their results, both perceived usefulness and perceived efforts are also the most popular factors that can be found in most of the analyzed empirical studies. Baptista and Oliveira (2016) covered 57 papers from 2003 to 2016 and also found these factors as the most popular independent variables when predicting the intention to use mobile banking. Similar constructs were used in different cases like studying mobile banking adoption by millennial generation (Tan and Lau, 2016) or for purposes of a cross-cultural study (Mortimer et al., 2015). Based on these results, we also use these factors as a baseline framework.

The factor of perceived usefulness reflects the benefits gained from mobile banking usage, such as saving time and a decrease in transaction costs. The significance of this factor is often supported by the literature (Luarn and Lin, 2005; Gu et al., 2009). Moreover, the importance of perceived usefulness is proved not only by widespread SEM-utilizing explorations, but also by Verissimo (2016) who used a different method: fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA). At least, Mortimer et al. (2015) showed that perceived usefulness was still found to be a key factor predicting the intention to use mobile banking even in a cross-cultural study. This leads to the first hypothesis as follows:

- **H1:** Perceived Usefulness positively affects Intention to Use mobile banking.

Perceived efforts have a significant influence on perceived usefulness, perceived risks and the intention to use mobile banking. When a customer tries new services, it takes some time and knowledge to become a skillful user. The screen size of an ordinary mobile device might cause extra difficulties for new users and require extra effort (Riquelme and Rios, 2010). If the application is not adapted for small screens and keypads, it might be uncomfortable to navigate. Apps that are too complicated may also scare new customers. However, if it is easy for a consumer to operate via mobile banking application, the intention to use mobile banking will increase.

Many researchers used the factor of efforts or ease of use as a basic factor and found it to be significant (Riquelme and Rios, 2010; Koksal, 2016). However, some scholars reported the contradictory results and argued that there may be no connection between perceived efforts and the intention to use. For example, Mortimer et al. (2015) did not find such a relationship for Australian data which was also true for a previous exploration of other developed countries like Japan, Switzerland or the USA. The possible reason is that in such countries, mobile phone users are already extremely experienced, so they “did not need easy to use format and were moving on to the more complex systems” (Mortimer et al., 2015, p. 559).

Actually, the huge challenge caused by the complexity and difficulty of use is connected with the main mobile banking advantage. If the mobile application is not user-friendly, it makes the whole process slower and the advantage of fast access to bank accounts becomes neutralized. For the reasons discussed above, Hypotheses 2 and 3 are as follows:

- **H2:** Perceived Efforts negatively affect Intention to Use mobile banking.

- **H3:** Perceived Efforts negatively affect Perceived Usefulness.

In this chapter, we also explore the costs of mobile banking usage in more detail and add new factors due to the frequency of their appearance in empirical studies. “Perceived financial cost” reflects all financial expenses, “perceived risks” reflect possible losses, and “compatibility with lifestyle” measures extra costs from the inconsistency between the needs of the customer and mobile banking opportunities. All of them were identified by Ha et al. (2012) as main drivers of mobile banking adoption. “Self-efficacy” reflects the resources (e.g. skills or knowledge) that the customer needs to use mobile banking. We also add “social influence” from sociological and psychological theories. According to Shaikh and Karjaluoto (2015), these factors were used in 20% of studies (6th place) and 27% (4th place), respectively.

Thus, the advantage of mobility causes extra risks. The customer may not reach the goal because of technical errors, the consumer’s own mistakes, the end of battery life or an unstable Internet connection and the user may suffer financial loss. Personal and financial information sent through an Internet connection is vulnerable to hackers. Finally, mobile devices themselves can be lost or be stolen along with personal and financial data (Riquelme and Rios, 2010; Hanafizadeh et al., 2014). To sum up, mobile transactions need a higher level of trust than face-to-face transactions (Lin, 2011) because they are more risky. Moreover, “lack of trust is one of the most frequently cited reasons for customers not using mobile banking” (Lin, 2011, p. 254). At least, mobile banking is not a face-to-face service, so customers may not understand how they interact with their bank. In that case, they need a higher level of trust as the potential risks increase. Therefore, Hypotheses 4 and 5 are as follows:

- **H4:** Perceived Efforts positively affect Perceived Risks.

- **H5:** Perceived Risk negatively affects Intention to Use mobile banking.

Although mobile banking provides an opportunity to save time and money, it also generates some extra costs. Modern technologies are developing very quickly, and new devices with new features constantly arise. For example, some banks offer mobile services that use fingerprints instead of passwords, but only few models of smartphones have this feature. With the development of contactless payments, customers may use smartphones instead of payment cards, but smartphones with this feature are also limited. If the customer wants to use all the services of the bank, smartphones need to be regularly upgraded. Customers also pay for the Internet traffic, which increases with the use of mobile applications. However, both these expenditures are offset to a certain extent by the high speed of technological development, which reduces smartphone and Internet traffic prices.

Nevertheless, additional expenses still exist. Banks can charge fees for using mobile apps. If these fees are too high, customers will have less intention to use mobile banking (Zhou et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2006). On the contrary, the low cost of using remote banking services may be a reason for switching to mobile apps. This factor is important in this area because of its economic origin (Luarn and Lin, 2005; Hanafizadeh et al., 2014). Thus, we assume the following hypothesis:

- **H6:** Perceived Financial Costs negatively affect Intention to Use mobile banking.

When customers try a new service, they face two important challenges. The first is the lack of experience. A customer decides if he or she needs to learn to use the app. If a consumer can do it on his or her own, it takes less effort than if someone’s help is needed. In other words, the service which can be used independently, causes fewer difficulties. The visual appearance of the mobile banking app is also important. If it is too complicated, it requires more mental effort. There is also image barrier or anxiety towards computers (Laukkanen and Kiviniemi, 2010). Some customers, finding mobile devices too complex to use, will conflate difficulties with devices and difficulties with mobile banking apps. This factor is found to be significant in the empirical literature (Luo et al., 2010; Gu et al., 2009).
