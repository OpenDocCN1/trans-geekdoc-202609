Table 13.4 shows the potential benefits of international diversification for any domestic investor. Since the values are bounded, we apply the truncated *t*-statistics (Barr and Sherrill, 1999) to test their difference from zero. The no-short-selling international diversification on average yields an increase in the Sharpe ratio of 0.30, a reduction in volatility by 4.50%, and an increase in the realized risk-return of 0.13. The entire sample shows that 70% of which has an increase in Sharpe ratio greater than 0.2. Consistently, for the diversification benefits of a reduction in volatility, 58% of our sample yields a decrease in volatility between 3% and 5% while 36% of our sample has a decline in volatility greater than 5%. The results of realized return depict a different story. About 60% of our sample has positive diversification benefits measured by the realized Sharpe ratio from diversification greater than that of simple value-weighted strategy. The increase in mean–variance brought by the RMSR portfolio (\(\psi\)) is volatile due to poor predictability. Overall, the results show the increasing integration gradually diminishes but does not completely eradicate the benefits of diversification. There is no clear pattern for realized increase in Sharpe ratio. The result of truncated *t* tests indicates that the *ex ante* benefits of diversification are statistically significant but not the case for the realized return.

Figure 13.2 demonstrates the time-series of potential benefits and its long-term smoothing curve (H–P) proposed by Hodrick and Prescott (1997) to the US investor. The benefits under various diversity conditions move in the same direction but are not proportional in size. The economic values of diversification are positive though they seem to vary over time. The Sharpe ratio benefits in Panels A to D and volatility benefits in Panels E to H reveal different stories. For \(\delta\), diversification benefits decreased during 1993 and 2000 but jumped up from 2000 to 2007 and declined since the subprime mortgage crisis in 2008. This is consistent with previous evidence that the markets are highly integrated during bear market periods.

The diversification benefits measured by a reduction in volatility (\(\varepsilon\)) shown in Panels E to H follow a different pattern. The diversification benefit \(\varepsilon\) increased from July 1995 to 2000, decreased during 2003 and 2008, but slightly increased after 2008. The volatility of individual assets increase during the bearish period, the risk of a portfolio still can be reduced by an efficient diversification strategy, though the magnitude of reduction in risk is small.
