# 121.2.2.2 Bali, Peng, Shen, and Tang (2014)’s liquidity shocks

To study the effect of liquidity shocks on future stock returns, Bali et al. (2014) argue that market frictions such as investor inattention and illiquidity can lead to underreaction to liquidity shocks. Following Amihud (2002), Bali et al. (2014) measure the illiquidity of individual stocks as the average daily ratio of absolute return over dollar trading volume in a month, i.e.,

ILLIQ_{i,t} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{d=1}^{N} \frac{|r_{i,d}|}{VOL_{i,d}}, \quad (121.4)

where r_{i,d} denotes the daily return of stock i on day d, VOL_{i,t} denotes the daily dollar trading volume of stock i on day d, and N denotes the number of days with non-missing data within month t. Their first measure of liquidity shock is simply the negative difference between ILLIQ_{i,t} and its previous 12-month average. The second measure assumes ILLIQ_{i,t} to follow an ARMA(1,1) process so that the liquidity shock is defined as the negative difference between ILLIQ_{i,t} and its estimated value from the ARMA(1,1) model. The third measure of liquidity shock is the negative difference between volume-weighted effective relative spread (SPRD) and its previous 12-month average. In their univariate portfolio-level analysis, stocks are ranked on liquidity shocks and assigned into ten portfolios. Next, one-month-ahead portfolio returns are calculated. Contrary to the findings that are based on the assumption of perfect informational efficiency, Bali et al. (2014) show that the 10th portfolio (stocks with positive liquidity shocks) tend to produce higher subsequent returns, up to six months in the future, than the 1st portfolio (stocks with negative liquidity shocks).
