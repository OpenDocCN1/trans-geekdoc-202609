We also apply the bootstrapping method to get the threshold values of the threshold variables (Hansen, 1999), and the empirical results of the threshold effect show that the association between discretionary accruals and auditor services is nonlinear, and there is a structural change in both pre- and post-SOX periods.

We summarize the results of our tests as follows. The results for the pre-SOX period show that nonaudit fees and total fees are positively associated with earnings management, but audit fees are only negatively associated with earnings management in the subsample under the threshold value, suggesting that audit quality is improved only when the threshold variable, FEERATIO, is less than 0.67095. These findings support concerns that companies which purchase more nonaudit services receive more lenient treatment from their auditors. The results with regard to the post-SOX period show that when FEERATIO is higher than 0.298917, nonaudit services are significantly positive, revealing that nonaudit fees are the primary threat to auditor independence. Specifically, this independence is compromised when the nonaudit fees ratio is higher than 0.298917, which means the opportunity cost of losing the economic rents is higher than the potential litigation and reputation costs.

For the purpose to complete robustness, we also conduct several additional tests to extend the results. First, we show that our results are substantially unchanged when we use the different sample period. Second, we apply our method in Big five audit firm sample and find that the results are consistent with our main findings. Third, we replace the different economic bonding variables, and find that the results are unchanged.

The ultimate aim of SOX was to improve audit independence, and the most contentious change was the prohibition of nonaudit services. This has had a major impact on the auditing profession, although the associated increase audit fees has not been not helpful to audit quality. Specifically, the results reveal that the reduced nonaudit fees still has led to greater incentives to acquiesce to client demands, thus compromising auditor independence.
