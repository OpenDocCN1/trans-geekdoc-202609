If aggregate liquidity is priced, cross-sectional differences in expected stock returns should be related to the sensitivities of returns to fluctuations in aggregate liquidity (i.e., liquidity betas). Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) develop an aggregate liquidity measure based on order-flow-induced return reversals of individual stocks, and construct ten portfolios sorted on liquidity betas as their test assets. Empirically, Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) find that expected returns of their test assets are indeed related cross-sectionally to their liquidity betas.

A large empirical literature on liquidity has developed. Our study extends the literature along two dimensions. First, while previous research (e.g., Pástor and Stambaugh, 2003) typically focuses on one cross-section of stock returns (i.e., the portfolios sorted on liquidity betas), we examine various cross-sections of returns, as, if aggregate liquidity is a true state variable in the equity market, it should price other cross-sections of returns too. More importantly, as Lewellen, Nagel and Shanken (2010) point out, using test assets with tight factor structure could yield spurious results. Second, unlike prior research that typically employs only one liquidity measure<sup>2</sup> (e.g., Bali et al. 2014), we take into account various measures of aggregate liquidity. In particular, we employ both stock and money market liquidity measures, as (1) Chordia, Sarkar, and Subrahmanyam (2005) find that fluctuations in liquidity are correlated across stocks and bond markets and (2) a large literature (e.g., Schularick and Taylor, 2012; Gilchrist and Zakrajšek, 2012; Krishnamurthy and Muir, 2016; López-Salido, Stein, and Zakrajšek, 2017) suggests that banking and bond market conditions can exert significant influences on real economy.

Another plausible explanation is that with a liquid secondary market, investors may find it easier to invest in productive though illiquid long term projects (Levine, 1991; Bencivenga, Smith, and Starr, 1995; Levine and Zervos, 1998).

The strong evidence that stock market liquidity is a leading indicator of business cycles supports the idea that its variation could have a systematic impact on expected stock returns. Among others (Hasbrouck and Seppi, 2001; Huberman and Halka, 2001), Chordia, Roll, and Subrahmanyam (2000) find empirical evidence of commonality in stock market liquidity, likely due to the trading-induced co-movements in inventory fluctuations across individual stocks. The commonality in liquidity, therefore, implies the unanticipated changes in market liquidity pose a non-diversifiable priced risk.<sup>5</sup> If so, a stock whose return is more positively correlated with liquidity shocks shall require a higher rate of return.

Indeed, it has been well documented that time variation in aggregate market liquidity affects the cross-section of stock returns.<sup>6</sup> Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) construct a market-wide liquidity measure based upon order-flow-induced return reversals of individual stocks. They argue that less liquid stocks tend to experience larger return reversals on a daily basis than more liquid stocks.<sup>7</sup> Using data of common stocks on New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and American Stock Exchange (AMEX), they estimate “liquidity betas” as the measure of individual stock return’s sensitivities to innovations in aggregate liquidity. They find that stocks with higher liquidity betas (in magnitude) generate significantly higher subsequent returns.<sup>8</sup>

Acharya and Pedersen (2005) derive a liquidity-adjusted capital asset pricing model (CAPM), demonstrating that there are three forms of liquidity risks. They are linked to:

- The covariance between a security’s illiquidity and the market illiquidity. Since investors require higher returns for holding securities that become more illiquid when the whole market is illiquid, the risk premium associated with such covariance should be positive.
- The covariance between a security’s return and the market illiquidity. Because securities that generate higher returns in times of market illiquidity are more desirable, the implied risk premium should be negative. As described above, Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) find supportive evidence for this liquidity risk.
- The covariance between a security’s illiquidity and the market return. In a down market, investors prefer less illiquid securities. Thus, the associated risk premium should be negative.

Employing Amihud (2002) illiquidity measure, Acharya and Pedersen (2005) show that all three liquidity risks are priced in the cross-section of stock returns. The strongest effect is ascribed to the third form, i.e., the covariance between a security’s illiquidity and the market return, which contributes about 80 percent of the overall liquidity premium.

Liquidity is a broad and multifaceted concept. It encompasses transaction cost, price impact (the responsiveness of stock prices to changes in trading activity), market depth (trading volume), and its effect on real economic activities. The complex nature of liquidity makes it very difficult, if not impossible, to incorporate all its dimensions and sum it up with one single measure. There are, however, some commonly used proxies in the literature: bid-ask spread (Chordia et al., 2000), turnover ratio (Chordia et al., 2001), Amihud (2002) illiquidity measure, and Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity index.

In our study, we investigate the pricing impact of various market-wide liquidity measures. We would like to find evidence to answer the question: conditional on common risk factors (market, size, value, and momentum), is it the stock market liquidity, or the money market liquidity, or both that are priced in the cross-section of stock returns?
