Other studies which support the concept of reputational capital are based on the assumption that the loss of reputation and litigation costs are the most significant considerations for auditors, and that they are likely to encourage auditors to maintain their independence (DeFond et al., 2002). Although a larger client can create greater economic dependence, when auditors have more than one such client there is less reliance on a single one. Thus, a larger number of clients will mitigate the issue of economic dependence (Reynolds and Francis, 2001).

This study aims to investigate whether the provision of nonaudit services reduces the independence of auditors. In particular, this study determines in what kind of situation the benefits of economic dependence exceed reputation and potential litigation risk, causing auditors to treat clients more leniently. Previous studies only used linear regression models, which could not identify the nonlinear association between fees and earnings management. The main aim of this paper is thus to apply the panel threshold regression model, developed by Hansen (1999), to explore whether there is a nonlinear relation between discretionary accruals and nonaudit fees, or if there are any structural changes in the relation between auditor behavior and discretionary accruals. By using the panel threshold regression model, we can find the trade-off point between reputation concerns and economic bonding for auditors.

Threshold regression models are used widely in economics, such as the threshold autoregressive model that is prevalent in the nonlinear time-series literature. Hansen (1996, 1999, 2000) developed a series statistical theory for threshold estimation in the regression context. This technique can objectively divide a sample into subsamples by using a threshold variable which must be exogenous and continuous. We use FEERATIO (ratio of nonaudit fees to total fees) as the threshold variable, and the threshold values are significant in several subsamples.

For the purpose of investigating the effects of the SEC prohibiting auditor firms from providing certain kinds of nonaudit services for their clients in order to preserve their independence, we examine pre- and post-SOX data. We use the absolute value of discretionary accruals, which is the best measurement of management’s deliberate intervention in earnings management (Warfield et al., 1995; Francis et al., 1999). Following the same complementary specifications of auditor fees contained in Frankel et al. (2002), we use nonaudit fees (RANKNON) and audit fees (RANKAUD), which are measured as percentile ranks for each client of each auditor, and the last specification (RANKTOT) is the percentile rank of total auditor fees for each client of each auditor, which is used to capture the combined incentive effects of audit and nonaudit fees. We also apply the bootstrapping method to get the threshold values of the threshold variables (Hansen, 1999), and the empirical results of the threshold effect show that the association between discretionary accruals and auditor services is nonlinear, and there is a structural change in both pre- and post-SOX periods.

We summarize the results of our tests as follows. The results for the pre-SOX period show that nonaudit fees and total fees are positively associated with earnings management, but audit fees are only negatively associated with earnings management in the subsample under the threshold value, suggesting that audit quality is improved only when the threshold variable, FEERATIO, is less than 0.67095. These findings support concerns that companies which purchase more nonaudit services receive more lenient treatment from their auditors. The results with regard to the post-SOX period show that when FEERATIO is higher than 0.298917, nonaudit services are significantly positive, revealing that nonaudit fees are the primary threat to auditor independence. Specifically, this independence is compromised when the nonaudit fees ratio is higher than 0.298917, which means the opportunity cost of losing the economic rents is higher than the potential litigation and reputation costs.

For the purpose of completing robustness, we also conduct several additional tests to extend the results. First, we show that our results are substantially unchanged when we use the different sample period. Second, we apply our method in a Big Five audit firm sample and find that the results are consistent with our main findings. Third, we replace the different economic bonding variables, and find that the results are unchanged.

The ultimate aim of SOX was to improve audit independence, and the most contentious change was the prohibition of nonaudit services. This has had a major impact on the auditing profession, although the associated increase in audit fees has not been helpful to audit quality. Specifically, the results reveal that the reduced nonaudit fees still has led to greater incentives to acquiesce to client demands, thus compromising auditor independence.

## 22.2 Prior Literature

After the accounting scandals at the turn of the 21st century, regulators, market participants and scholars all focused their attention on whether or not the nonaudit services that auditors provide impair the quality of their auditing. In November 2000, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) issued its Revision of the Commission’s Auditor Independence Requirements, which required companies to disclose information about fees paid to the incumbent auditor in proxy statements on or after February 5, 2001. The SEC’s concern that the growth in the provision of nonaudit services compromises audit firm independence was based on the premise that the provision of such services increases the fees paid to the audit firm, thereby increasing its economic dependence on the client (Ashbaugh et al., 2003).

However, the research results about nonaudit services and auditor independence are mixed. There are two different opinions to explain the behavior of auditors, one of which focuses on economic dependence, as in DeAngelo's work (1981), which suggested that economic rents arise because of auditor learning over time or switching costs imposed on the client. Auditors therefore must weigh the costs of avoiding lawsuits and loss of reputation with the opportunity costs of losing the economic rents that may be associated with a particular audit engagement (Abbott et al., 2003). If a particular client is more important and thus can create greater economic dependence in an auditor firm, then the auditor firm has more incentive to acquiesce to client pressure, including pressure to allow earnings management. Simunic (1984) suggested that “knowledge spillovers” caused by the joint provision of audit and nonaudit services may lead to incremental economic rents beyond those described by DeAngelo (1981). Firth (1997) argued that the installation of new information systems can give rise to additional consulting opportunities; for example, a firm that has recently implemented a new information system may need assistance in developing the related financial reports to be produced by the system. Thus, in SOX 201, some services were prohibited as being outside the scope of practice of auditors, including financial information systems design and implementation. Frankel et al. (2002) developed three complementary variables to examine whether audit fees are associated with earnings management and the market reaction to the disclosure of such fees. Their data, collected from SEC files between February 2001 and June 2001, found a significant positive association between the purchase of nonaudit services and discretionary accruals, and a significant negative association between audit fees and the behavior of earnings management.
