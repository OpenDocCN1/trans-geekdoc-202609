## 53.3 Research Design

In this section, we introduce how we estimate standardized unexpected earnings (SUE). We then introduce research methodology.

### 53.3.1 Estimation of standardized unexpected earnings (SUEs)

We estimate standardized unexpected earnings (SUEs) by following FOS (1984) and BT (1989) who suggest that the following univariate time-series model provides the best description of quarterly earnings generating process. Expressed by equation (53.19), earnings are forecasted with historical earnings data.

$$E(Q_{i,t}) = Q_{i,t-4} + \phi(Q_{i,t-1} - Q_{i,t-5}) + \delta, \tag{53.19}$$

where $Q_{i,t}$ is quarterly earnings of the $i$th firm in quarter $t$ and $\delta$ is a drift term.

Expressed by equation (53.20), the SUE is calculated as follows:

$$\text{SUE} = \frac{Q_t - E(Q_t)}{\sigma[Q_t - E(Q_t)]}. \tag{53.20}$$

where $Q_{i,t}$ is quarterly earnings of the $i$th firm in quarter $t$. $E(Q_{i,t})$ is the forecasted quarterly earnings estimated from equation (53.19). $\sigma$ refers to the standard deviation over the estimation period.

### 53.3.2 Research methodology

We begin with a test of ARCH effect on daily stock returns, using simplified Lagrange multiplier. A number of studies find evidence of heteroscedasticity in the market model and subsequent related models, but most of them focus on unconditional and cross-sectional heteroscedasticity. For instance, Diebold et al. (1993) focus on conditional volatility movements of stock returns. They used Engle's (1982) autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (ARCH) model to study the temporal behavior of residual return variance in the market model. Their empirical results indicate that the ARCH effect is a rather common phenomenon for monthly returns for NYSE firms. However, it could be premature to suggest that the daily returns used to examine the phenomenon of the post-earnings-announcement also exhibit the ARCH effect. We employ the simplified Lagrange multiplier test proposed by Engle (1982) to examine whether the daily returns also exhibit ARCH effect. The test statistic is $T * R^2$; where $T$ is the number of observations and $R^2$ is the coefficient of determination of regressing the OLS squared residuals on a constant and a $k$-lag OLS squared residuals. This statistic has an asymptotic Chi-square distribution with $k$-degree of freedom under the null hypothesis.

We proceed to the examination of systematic risks around earnings announcement. We estimate systematic risk by regressing the daily stock returns against the equally weighted market indices. We use Chow test (Chow, 1960) to examine whether the systematic risks have been changed subsequent to the announcement of earnings. A rejection of the null hypothesis would provide us with preliminary information that the conventional market model, which states that the systematic risk is constant prior and subsequent to earnings announcement, may be misspecified in terms of estimating abnormal returns.

The sample firms are assigned to five portfolios based on the ranking of their SUE relative to the distribution of unexpected earnings in the prior quarter.³ Each sample firm contains 131 daily returns. There are 100 observations before earnings announcement and 31 observations subsequent to earnings announcement. Cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) are then calculated for each portfolio. Abnormal returns of each firm are estimated for the 31 trading days holding period. The test statistic developed by Brown and Warner (1985) is employed to test the significance of the CARs following the announcement of earnings.⁴

We then use the Kalman filter algorithm to estimate the time-varying systematic risk as illustrated in Appendix 53A. The CARs for each portfolio estimated from the time-varying risk model are also tested with the approach proposed by Brown and Warner (1985). The test statistic is the ratio of the mean excess return to the estimated cross-sectional standard deviation. CARs for each portfolio is plotted to examine whether the drifts are persistent in presence of conditional heteroscedasticity.
