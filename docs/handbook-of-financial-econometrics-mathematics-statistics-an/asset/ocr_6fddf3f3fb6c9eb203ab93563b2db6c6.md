# 38.4.1 Data descriptions

The data used in this empirical study are credit risk, market risk and operational risk data of Chinese banks. Covered in Pillar I of the Basel Accord, credit, market and operational risks are the three major risks faced by banks (BCBS, 2006). However, the lack of these risk data has always been an obstacle. Many studies seek risk data from academic studies, expert judgments, surveys and external databases (Rosenberg and Schuermann, 2006; Aas et al., 2007; Li et al., 2015). However, usually only scattered risk statistics or data for a single risk type can be obtained. Therefore, some other studies have tried to look for proxies for these risks (BCBS, 2010; Li et al., 2018). Financial statements present a relatively complete picture of a bank's performance, in which all earnings and losses are recorded and summarized into an income statement periodically. Therefore, a growing number of studies are mapping the income statement items to these risk types.

Market risk refers to the potential losses arising from changes in the value or price of an asset, such as those resulting from fluctuations in interest rates, currency exchange rates, stock prices and commodity prices (BCBS, 2001), which is nicely captured in income statements as “trading revenue” in American banks (Rosenberg and Schuermann, 2006; Inanoglu and Jacobs, 2009). However, unlike American banks, there is no direct “trading income” item in financial statements of Chinese banks. The sum of net gains from financial investments, net gains from foreign exchange and changes in fair value of investment properties is used as the proxy of market risk instead. Credit risk is defined as the potential loss caused by borrower’s or counterparty’s failure to meet its obligations (BCBS, 1999), which is usually considered close to the meaning of provisions (Kuritzkes and Schuermann, 2007). Operational risk is defined as the risk of direct or indirect loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal process, people and systems or from external events (BCBS, 2006). Non-operating expenses is a relatively proper proxy for it (Inanoglu and Jacobs, 2009).

Compared with the former approaches, this type of approach is able to obtain data of various bank risk types, thereby offering a relatively comprehensive dataset.
