In Fig. 2, the bottom row represents the predicted tumors, while the upper row represents the ground truth. It can be seen that almost all methods performed convincingly in segmenting/detecting the three classes of tumors (WT, TC, and ET) compared to the ground truth. Although it is difficult to spot the subtle differences between the segmentation results among the competing methods, Tables 4 and 5 show the superiority of our proposed approach in terms of the segmentation dice score and Hausdorff distance.

Figs. 3 and 6 show the accuracy and the convergence with a different number of training iterations. Here, the number of iterations indicates a completed update of parameters.

For all training, we assume the condition that no user dropped out of the network during the federated rounds. It can be clearly seen in Fig. 6 that the baselines, FedBN and FedAvg, need more training iterations before convergence is reached. This is likely due to statistical heterogeneity among the local clients. Our proposed approach introduces robustness into the model, as seen in the convergence graph for both training and validation.

The time required to train the local model also depends on the size of the dataset. The cost of blockchain increases with the data size as illustrated in Fig. 4b. It also shows that the cumulative time cost is likely to increase when the number of users is increased for the global model training. As observed previously, increasing the number of iterations results in higher computation costs, as shown in Fig. 7. It could also be observed that the proposed approach has the worst computational time as compared to other competing methods. This is due to the fact that the blockchain based systems needs additional time for consensus building besides the typical federated learning algorithm.
