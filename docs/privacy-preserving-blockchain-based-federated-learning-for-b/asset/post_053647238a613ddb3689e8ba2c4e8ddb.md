## ABSTRACT

Improved data sharing between healthcare providers can lead to a higher probability of accurate diagnosis, more effective treatments, and enhanced capabilities of healthcare organizations. One critical area of focus is brain tumor segmentation, a complex task due to the heterogeneous appearance, irregular shape, and variable location of tumors. Accurate segmentation is essential for proper diagnosis and effective treatment planning, yet current techniques often fall short due to these complexities. However, the sensitive nature of health data often prohibits its sharing. Moreover, the healthcare industry faces significant issues, including preserving the privacy of the model and instilling trust in the model. This paper proposes a framework to address these privacy and trust issues by introducing a mechanism for training the global model using federated learning and sharing the encrypted learned parameters via a permissioned blockchain. The blockchain-federated learning algorithm we designed aggregates gradients in the permissioned blockchain to decentralize the global model, while the introduced masking approach retains the privacy of the model parameters. Unlike traditional raw data sharing, this approach enables hospitals or medical research centers to contribute to a globally learned model, thereby enhancing the performance of the central model for all participating medical entities. As a result, the global model can learn about several specific diseases and benefit each contributor with new disease diagnosis tasks, leading to improved treatment options. The proposed algorithm ensures the quality of model data when aggregating the local model, using an asynchronous federated learning procedure to evaluate the shared model’s quality. The experimental results demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed scheme for the critical and challenging task of brain tumor segmentation. Specifically, our method achieved a 1.99% improvement in Dice similarity coefficient for enhancing tumors and a 19.08% reduction in Hausdorff distance for whole tumors compared to the baseline methods, highlighting the significant advancement in segmentation performance and reliability.

## 1. Introduction

The massive growth of Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) technologies, coupled with recent rapid developments in machine learning, has revolutionized the field of medical sciences [1–4]. The design of new surgical equipment for the partial diagnosis of diseases has opened up many possibilities for patient treatment. Over the last decade, AI approaches have been widely adopted to solve critical problems, with a significant percentage of researchers working on various issues such as brain MRI analysis [5], lung CT scan analysis [6,7], clinical prescription analysis [8,9], and many more [10,11]. Moreover, advancements in AI technologies have facilitated diagnostic accuracy improvements, exemplified by enhanced epileptic seizure recognition [10] and innovative approaches for classifying complex conditions like monkeypox using convolutional neural networks [12]. These methods underscore the potential of AI in revolutionizing diagnostics and patient treatment strategies, especially when integrated with IoMT devices. Unlike research problems in other domains, such as vehicle re-identification [13] and sensor data analysis [14], health data is highly confidential [15]. Consequently, limited AI approaches have been utilized, where only a small amount of data is used to build models. The privacy of health data is a primary concern for healthcare organizations, such as hospitals and pathological laboratories. However, the robustness of AI models often requires vast amounts of data. The dynamic nature of different patients’ cases and problems demands new technologies and tools to link and collaboratively combine data from various hospitals effectively. For this reason, an increasing number of hospitals are seeking secure methods to share data to predict and diagnose new types of viruses and disease symptoms.

To address the privacy challenges faced by healthcare organizations in the IoMT industry 4.0 [16–18], numerous efforts have been made to refine and optimize the protection of individual privacy [19, 20]. Currently, approaches based on federated learning have shown promise in resolving privacy concerns by preserving edge intelligence in distributed scenarios [21, 22].

Effectively handling data privacy also significantly reduces the cost of data transmission. This reduction occurs because each node in the centralized network optimizes a local model using its own data and only transmits the learned model parameters [20]. Generally, in most cases, the optimization of the local model employs the gradient descent algorithm. Through this process, each node (for instance, a hospital, pathology laboratory, or another healthcare organization) retains its local data. The shared learned parameters are then aggregated by a central curator to collaboratively develop a global model [23–27]. However, the collaboration scheme in federated learning still faces significant risks and challenges, particularly concerning the trustworthiness of participant nodes and the quality of their local models. For instance, while [28] proposed a distributed model for sharing gradients, their methods have shown vulnerabilities to passive attackers [29, 30].

### 1.1. Motivation

In the last few years, blockchain and federated learning have become emerging approaches that facilitate a distributed and secure solution to share the data of each node [27]. Features of blockchain, such as traceability, anonymity, and tamper-proof properties, have been given a significant amount of attention by researchers to enhance node and data security in many areas such as smart grids, vehicular networks, and healthcare data networks [3]. This transition aligns with the need for secure platforms to manage sensitive health data, as highlighted by various studies, including the use of blockchain to optimize data privacy and system security [9, 11, 31]. On the other hand, Shen et al. [1] designed a multi-collaborative system based on federated learning in the medical domain. The incentive of existing works only considers the server-based aggregation of the model [23–27, 32], while our work considers blockchain-based aggregation in the medical domain. Moreover, Kumar et al. [3] combined the blockchain and federated learning approach to create a decentralized model, but the drawback of [3] does not consider the trust mechanism between the new local models.

Bonawitz et al. [33] designed a privacy protection framework to safely aggregate gradients using a federated learning global model, whereas [34] proposed a homomorphic encryption (HE) scheme and threshold secret sharing to protect the gradient. However, each aggregation in these schemes requires multiple rounds, which are unsuitable for blockchain distributed networks. Additionally, their approaches were restricted to learning predefined categories (diseases in this case) and cannot learn from new categories. Overall, some important research issues must be addressed with appropriate answers. Some of these important research questions (RQ) are covered more below.

- **RQ1:** How can a collaborative processing system that integrates multiple parties be developed?
- **RQ2:** How can trust in new local models be built while maintaining the quality of the local models?
- **RQ3:** How can a mechanism be developed to ensure the privacy of hospitals?

### 1.2. Research approach and contributions

To enhance user privacy and trust, we integrate blockchain with federated learning techniques. The blockchain provides a reliable scheme for training a global model among untrusted participants, facilitating efficient data sharing [35]. This proposed scheme compares the accuracy of the new model with that of existing models to establish trust. In this way, blockchain fosters trust among parties and ensures accurate model training. We have also designed a blockchain-based distributed architecture for secure data sharing, minimizing the risk of privacy leaks. This is achieved by using an encryption scheme that secures model parameters, while the blockchain ensures accurate model training. The aim of this paper is to design a medical data-sharing framework based on AI federated learning and blockchain. This framework is beneficial for predicting new cases and securely sharing medical data. It provides a robust trust mechanism and guarantees privacy. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

1. We propose a quality-aware, blockchain-based federated learning approach to ensure the quality of the model, thereby ensuring end-to-end trust in a decentralized network.
2. This paper designs a keychain-based federated learning algorithm with a secure key-sharing mechanism to protect local model parameters and ensure data security, aiming to improve the security of federated learning in the healthcare sector.
3. We propose a blockchain-based method for collaboratively collecting locally trained model weights from different sources to train a global model.
4. We demonstrate the adaptability and scalability of our blockchain-based federated learning framework, showcasing its efficacy and its potential applicability in diverse healthcare scenarios beyond brain tumor segmentation.

## 2. Preliminaries

### 2.1. Background of federated learning

Federated learning is a decentralized and privacy-preserving approach to deep learning, allowing the development of a collective global model while ensuring the confidentiality of the data from all contributing clients. Furthermore, federated learning introduces a mechanism to collect data from various parties or hospitals without breaching their privacy [24, 36–38]. An advantage of the federated learning model is its ability to lower resource usage, such as memory and power, for each participant while simultaneously improving the quality of the trained model. In other words, federated learning learns the model collaboratively and shares the trained model with the participating local clients/machines. To be more specific, each user \(u \in U\) has his or her own private dataset \(D_u \subset D\).
