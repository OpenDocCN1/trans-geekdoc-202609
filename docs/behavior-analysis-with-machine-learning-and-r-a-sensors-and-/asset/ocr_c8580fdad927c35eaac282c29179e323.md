Nowadays, most smartphones with a tri-axial accelerometer sensor. This sensor mea-
gural forces from the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes. This information can be used to capture
movement patterns from the sensor and automate the monitoring of the type of physical
activity being performed.

In this section, we will use decision trees to automatically classify physical activities fromnacceleration data. We will use the SMARTPHONE ACTIVITIES dataset that contains ac-
elation records that were collected with a smartphone. This dataset is WISDM2
and was made by Kwaspis et al. (2010). The dataset has 6 different features:
‘walking’, ‘jogging’, ‘walking upstairs’, ‘walking downstairs’, ‘sitting’ and ‘standing’. The
data was collected by 36 volunteers with an Android phone located in their pant's pocket
with a sampling rate of $20Hz$ (1 sample every 50 milliseconds).

The dataset contains two types of files. One with the raw accelerometer data and the other
e after feature extraction. Figure 2.10 shows the first 10 lines of the raw accelerometer
values of the first file. The first column is the id of the user that collected the data and the
second column is the class. The third column is the timestamp and the remaining columns
are the $x$, $y$, and $z$ accelerometer values, respectively.

Usually, classification models are not trained with the raw data but with feature vectors
extracted from the raw data. Feature vectors have the advantage of being more,
thus, making the learning process more efficient. For activity recognition, the feature extrac-
ton consists of defining a moving window of size $w$ that starts at position $i$. In the
beginning, i is the index pointing to the first accelerometer readings. Then, $n$ statistical
features are computed on the elements covered by the window such as mean, standard-
diation, 0-crossings, etc. This will produce a $n$-dimensional feature vector and the process
is repeated by moving the window steps forward. Typical values of $s$ are such that the
overlap between the previous position and the next is one about 30% to 50%. An
overlap of 0 is also typical, that is, $s=w$. Figure 2.11 depicts the process.
