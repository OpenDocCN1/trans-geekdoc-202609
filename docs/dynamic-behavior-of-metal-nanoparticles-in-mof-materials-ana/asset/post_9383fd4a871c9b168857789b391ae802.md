## Analysis of Co NPs dynamic behavior with deep learning

To study the behavior of the resulting Co nanoparticles in the BiF-66 sample, we analyzed videos obtained by TEM using deep learning. To analyze the topological characteristics of the observed particles, we considered the metric Solidity (completeness), which characterizes the ratio of contour area to its convex hull area, the perimeter, and the area of the particle. The given graphs and histograms (Fig. 6) illustrate the dynamics of change and the law of distribution of the topology of nanoparticles.

According to the data of the particle solidity analysis, the average and maximum particle solidity remain constant during the experiment, i.e. close to a convex shape (Fig. 6A). The most expressed solidity dynamics turned out to be in the case of the change value. This indicates the instability and tendency to change in the shape of particles with a solidity in the range of 0.6–0.7. The solidity distribution of the particles showed during the analysis of the video that the solidity of most particles is in the range of 0.920–0.930 (Fig. 6B).

An analysis of the dynamics of the particle perimeter showed that the minimum and average values remain relatively stable and are approximately equal to 10 and 65 nm, respectively (Fig. 6C). This indicates that particles 2 and 10 nm in size (in the case of their rounded shape) retain the stability of their geometric parameters. At the same time, the maximum perimeter tends to change during the experiment. This indicates that particles with a perimeter in the range of 150–200 nm (diameter 24–32 nm) are the most unstable and tend to divide and merge with other particles. The distribution of particles along the perimeter showed that the perimeter of the largest number of particles was in the range of 60–70 nm (10–11 nm in the case of their rounded shape) during the experiment (Fig. 6D).

By analogy here. The “perimeter” metric, a trend in area is also observed here. The minimum and average values remain relatively stable at approximately 15 and 320 nm<sup>2</sup> (2 and 10 nm for round particles) (Fig. 6E). The maximum area tends to change, which indicates the instability of particles with an area in the range of 1250–2250 nm<sup>2</sup>. The area distribution of particles showed that the area of most particles was in the range of 310–370 nm<sup>2</sup> (10–11 nm) during the experiment (Fig. 6F).

The average velocity of moving particles is in the range [0, 1.6] nm s<sup>−1</sup> (Fig. 6G). The moving distance of the particles of the analyzed sample during the observation time is in the range of [0, 295.22] nm with an average value of 27.75 nm (Fig. 6H).

It should be noted that the observed nanoparticles are individual and do not represent an aggregate of subnanometer particles. Under the studied conditions, the Co NPs moved as a whole and did not disintegrate. In the case of aggregates of subnanometer particles, this would not be the case (uncorrected movement or disintegration should be the case). The integrity of the NPs was also evidenced by HR-TEM (Fig. S12 and S13, ESI†) and SAED (Fig. S14, ESI†) analysis.

## Conclusions

The relationship between the stability and decomposition of MOFs under an electron beam is a well-known problem, which complicates investigations of this class of nanoporous hybrid materials by electron microscopy, i.e., TEM. In summary, a systematic study of the resistance of MOFs to the action of an electron beam was carried out. Studied MOF structures with various compositions, topologies and porous structures show different stabilities under an electron beam, mainly depending on the type of metal coordination centers in the framework. Al- and Zn-MOFs are less likely to transform to M NPs, while Fe-, Co-, Ni- and Cu-MOFs degrade to form M NPs.

Subsequently, the behavior of M NPs under the action of an electron beam was studied. Thus, the Fe NPs turned out to be stable and possessed static images. In contrast, the formed Co NPs are liquid-like under an electron beam and show amazing dynamic behavior. Despite their large size up to 100 nm and the absence of additional heating, cobalt nanoparticles on the surface of a transformed MOF material change their shape, move along the support surface, merge with each other, and break down into several particles. The behavior of copper nanoparticles formed from HKUST-1 under the action of an electron beam appeared to also be interesting. Cu NPs sublimated in a few seconds and deposited on a carbon substrate a few micrometers away from the particle under study.

For a deeper understanding of the ongoing processes, an analysis of the dynamics of Co NPs was carried out using computer vision and deep learning. This made it possible to analyze the video of Co NPs movements obtained in the TEM experiment at high speed. It was found that particles with the lowest solidity and maximum area and perimeter are prone to change. Thus, the most dynamic were large particles with the least convex shape. Particles of a convex shape and smaller size turned out to be more stable in terms of geometric parameters.

The observed phenomena indicate the ease of phase transitions even for large nanoparticles under the conditions of studies using transmission electron microscopy. This indicates the need to control the operating conditions of nanodevices based on metal nanoparticles. At the same time, such phase transitions initiated by an electron beam can be further used to develop nanosized devices or catalytic systems.

## Experimental part

### Synthesis of MOFs

MOFs were synthesized according to published procedures.<sup>76-82</sup> The MIL-101(Fe) and NH<sub>2</sub>-MIL-101(Fe) samples were prepared by the MW technique under atmospheric pressure according to a modified previously reported procedure.<sup>78</sup> For the synthesis of MIL-101(Fe), benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (H<sub>2</sub>BDC) (0.230 g, 1.38 mmol) was dissolved in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) (60 mL). Then, FeCl<sub>3</sub> × 6H<sub>2</sub>O (0.748 g, 2.77 mmol) was added. The resulting mixture was stirred, transferred to a glass reactor, and heated in a domestic MW oven (145–155 °C, 40 min). The crude product was separated with DMF (2 × 15 mL), soaked in a fresh DMF portion (15 mL) for 24 h, rinsed with EtOH (3 × 15 mL), and evacuated (160 °C, 4 h, 10<sup>–3</sup> torr).

For NH<sub>2</sub>-MIL-101(Fe) synthesis, 2-aminobenzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (H<sub>2</sub>ABDC) (0.501 g, 2.77 mmol) was dissolved in DMF (60 mL). Then, FeCl<sub>3</sub> × 6H<sub>2</sub>O (0.374 g, 1.38 mmol) was added. The resulting mixture was stirred, transferred to a glass reactor, and heated in a domestic MW oven (145–155 °C, 45 min). The crude product was separated by centrifugation, rinsed with DMF (2 × 15 mL), soaked in a fresh DMF portion (15 mL) for 24 h, rinsed with EtOH (3 × 15 mL), and evacuated (160 °C, 4 h, 10<sup>–3</sup> torr).

### TEM analysis

A target-oriented approach was utilized for the optimization of the analytic measurements.<sup>83</sup> Before measurements, the samples were mounted on a 3 mm copper grid covered with carbon film from an isopropanol suspension. Sample morphology was studied using a Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope. Images were acquired in bright-field TEM mode at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV and an emission current of 8–17 mA.

### HR-TEM analysis

HR-TEM micrographs were obtained with a Themis-Z 3.1 instrument (TFS, USA) equipped with an X-FEG monochromator and CS/S double corrector at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Elemental analysis was performed with a Super-X EDS detector (energy resolution of approximately 120 eV) in HAADF-STEM mode. The samples for the TEM study were prepared by ultrasonic dispersion in ethanol and consequent deposition of the suspension upon a “holey” carbon film supported on a copper grid.
