One of the consequences of the easy diffusion of atoms in nanoparticles is their tendency to sinter or coalesce.16,17 This phenomenon is also of great importance in nanotechnology, catalysis, and the development of electronic nanosized devices. The sintering of nanoparticles in a TEM chamber can be used as cold welding to create nanoscale devices.12 Using in situ TEM, it was demonstrated that large silver nanoparticles (20–50 nm) can be fused at 300 °C.18 Even more interesting is that the fusion of Ag NPs and Au NPs (ca. 20 nm) could occur through diffusion initiated by an electron beam at 200 °C for 30–60 minutes.19–24 For smaller Ag and Au NPs (~3 and 7.5 nm), sintering was observed in the HR-TEM chamber under the action of an electron beam, which was accompanied by rearrangement of its crystal structure.25

The low phase stability of nanoparticles can lead not only to melting and sintering but also to sublimation. The sublimation of nanoparticles in the electron microscope is described for metals (mainly silver),26–28 GeSb2Te4,29 Bi2Te3, Bi2Se3,30 CdSe,31 GeTe,32 and MgO–Mg.33 In all cases, sublimation occurred with additional heating (> 300 °C). In particular, the sublimation of silver nanoparticles occurred by a direct transition without melting.34 The decrease in the size of Ag NPs with a diameter of 9 nm started at a temperature of approx. 575 °C. Higher temperatures (ca. 675 °C) are needed for the sublimation of larger Ag NPs (50 nm).28 The sublimation of 4 nm copper particles deposited on graphite upon additional heating up to 800 °C has been described. The authors note that the behavior of copper nanoparticles depended on the type of support. In the case of the CuO support, instead of sublimation, melting of copper nanoparticles occurred at 700 °C.35

When studying nanoparticles by electron microscopy, one should take into account the possible effect of an electron beam on a sample. The effect of the electron beam is not limited to heating of the sample. For example, it has long been described that an electron beam can induce chemical transformations and reduce certain compounds.21,36–39 Moreover, electron beam reduction has been proposed as a method for producing M NPs and even chemical bonds.40–43

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are an important class of porous coordination polymers composed of metal ions connected by organic molecules – linkers.44,45 MOFs are prospective materials for application in gas storage,46–48 electrochemistry,49–51 photocatalysis,52,53 catalysis,54–56 as well as sensors57 and nanoreactors.58–60 They can also be used as precursors for the synthesis of atomically dispersed catalysts and MOF-derived porous materials via pyrolysis.61–63 The instability of MOF structures under electron beam exposure is a known problem that makes it difficult to study these hybrid nanoporous materials using electron microscopy.64–68 It was shown that the stability of metal–organic frameworks under the action of an electron beam in a transmission electron microscope is determined primarily by the metal and not by the type of linker. In the present study, we report the dynamic behavior of M NPs formed when MOF materials are exposed to an electron beam. The M NPs behavior and stability of various MOFs in an electron microscope chamber are analyzed in detail. In the case of Co-MOFs (ZIF-67, BIF-66), the exposure of the electron beam resulted in the formation of nanoparticles with unusually high mobility. In the case of Cu-MOFs (HKUST-1), the electron beam initiated the instantaneous sublimation of the formed nanoparticles (Fig. 1). The behavior of Co-containing nanoparticles was studied using machine learning methods to digitally track the particle trajectories and properties.
