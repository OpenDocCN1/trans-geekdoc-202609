
## Dynamic behavior of metal nanoparticles in MOF materials: analysis with electron microscopy and deep learning

Kirill S. Erokhin, Evgeniy O. Pentsak, Vyacheslav R. Sorokin, Yury V. Agaev, Roman G. Zaytsev, Vera I. Isaev and Valentine P. Ananikov

Electron microscopy is a key characterization technique for nanoscale systems, and electron microscopy images are typically recorded and analyzed in terms of the morphology of the objects under study in static mode. The emerging current trend is to analyze the dynamic behavior at the nanoscale observed during electron microscopy measurements. In this work, the study of the stability of MOF structures with different compositions and topologies under conditions of an electron microscope experiment revealed an unusual dynamic behavior of M NPs formed due to the electron-beam-induced transformation of specific frameworks. The transition to the liquid phase led to spatial movement, rapid sintering, and an increase in the M NPs size within seconds. In the case of copper nanoparticles, instantaneous sublimation was observed. The dynamic behavior of Co NPs was analyzed with a computational framework combining deep learning and classic computer vision techniques. The present study for the first time revealed unique information about the stability of a variety of MOFs under an electron beam and the dynamic behavior of the formed M NPs. The formation of Fe, Ni, Cu, and Co NPs was observed from a molecular framework with a specific subsequent behavior – a stable form for Fe, excessive dynamics for Co, and sublimation/condensation for Cu. Two important outcomes of the present study should be mentioned: (i) electron microscopy investigations of MOF samples should be made with care, as decomposition under an electron beam may lead to incorrect results and the appearance of “phantom” nanoparticles; and (ii) MOFs represent an excellent model for fundamental studies of molecular-to-nano transitions in situ in video mode, including a number of dynamic transformations.

## Introduction

Metal nanoparticles (M NPs) of a small size are actively used in various fields of science and technology.1–5 Importantly, they can be unstable even at room temperature. Changes in the shape of M NPs can lead to critical changes in the characteristics of nanoscale devices.6,7 Therefore, it is known that the efficiency of heterogeneous nanocatalysis depends critically on their shape and size.8–10 The diffusion barrier for single metal atoms in nanoparticles on a clean metal surface is extremely low (1.0 eV).11 Consequently, even a small thermal activation is sufficient to cause the diffusion of atoms. This allows clean metal surfaces to flow like a liquid at temperatures far below the melting point.12 The melting points of the M NPs are lower than those of the bulk metal. Small M NPs are characterized by a quasi-molten phase at temperatures far below the melting point. In this phase, the particles continuously oscillate between different structures. In addition, particles of different shapes can have different phase regions.13 For the first time, melting of nanoparticles under the action of an electron beam in a microscope was observed in 1954 for low-melting metals, such as Pb, Sn, and Bi.14 A significant decrease in melting temperature occurs for particles smaller than 10 nm. A phenomenon of transition to a liquid state is also described for larger particles. For example, liquid-like pseudoelasticity of sub-10 nm crystalline silver particles was observed under pressure in a microscope chamber.6 It was shown that Ag nanoparticles can be deformed like liquid drops. Interestingly, at the same time, they remained crystalline inside. Regarding metals with high melting points, the liquid-like behavior of Pt particles in a CO flow in a TEM chamber has been described.15

One of the consequences of the easy diffusion of atoms in nanoparticles is their tendency to sinter or coalesce.16,17 This phenomenon is also of great importance in nanotechnology, catalysis, and the development of electronic nanosized devices. The sintering of nanoparticles in a TEM chamber can be used as cold welding to create nanoscale devices.12 Using in situ TEM, it was demonstrated that large silver nanoparticles (20–50 nm) can be fused at 300 °C.18 Even more interesting is that the fusion of Ag NPs and Au NPs (ca. 20 nm) could occur through diffusion initiated by an electron beam at 200 °C for 30–60 minutes.19–24 For smaller Ag and Au NPs (~3 and 7.5 nm), sintering was observed in the HR-TEM chamber under the action of an electron beam, which was accompanied by rearrangement of its crystal structure.25

The low phase stability of nanoparticles can lead not only to melting and sintering but also to sublimation. The sublimation of nanoparticles in the electron microscope is described for metals (mainly silver),26–28 GeSb2Te4,29 Bi2Te3, Bi2Se3,30 CdSe,31 GeTe,32 and MgO–Mg.33 In all cases, sublimation occurred with additional heating (> 300 °C). In particular, the sublimation of silver nanoparticles occurred by a direct transition without melting.34 The decrease in the size of Ag NPs with a diameter of 9 nm started at a temperature of approx. 575 °C. Higher temperatures (ca. 675 °C) are needed for the sublimation of larger Ag NPs (50 nm).28 The sublimation of 4 nm copper particles deposited on graphite upon additional heating up to 800 °C has been described. The authors note that the behavior of copper nanoparticles depended on the type of support. In the case of the CuO support, instead of sublimation, melting of copper nanoparticles occurred at 700 °C.35

When studying nanoparticles by electron microscopy, one should take into account the possible effect of an electron beam on a sample. The effect of the electron beam is not limited to heating of the sample. For example, it has long been described that an electron beam can induce chemical transformations and reduce certain compounds.21,36–39 Moreover, electron beam reduction has been proposed as a method for producing M NPs and even chemical bonds.40–43

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are an important class of porous coordination polymers composed of metal ions connected by organic molecules – linkers.44,45 MOFs are prospective materials for application in gas storage,46–48 electrochemistry,49–51 photocatalysis,52,53 catalysis,54–56 as well as sensors57 and nanoreactors.58–60 They can also be used as precursors for the synthesis of atomically dispersed catalysts and MOF-derived porous materials via pyrolysis.61–63 The instability of MOF structures under electron beam exposure is a known problem that makes it difficult to study these hybrid nanoporous materials using electron microscopy.64–68 It was shown that the stability of metal–organic frameworks under the action of an electron beam in a transmission electron microscope is determined primarily by the metal and not by the type of linker. For example, poor stability was shown for Cu- and Ru-MOFs.69 MOF decomposition under these conditions can be used as a methodology for the synthesis of ZnO, In and Cu nanoparticles and ordered silver nanostructures.70

In the present study, we report the dynamic behavior of M NPs formed when MOF materials are exposed to an electron beam. The M NPs behavior and stability of various MOFs in an electron microscope chamber are analyzed in detail. In the case of Co-MOFs (ZIF-67, BIF-66), the exposure of the electron beam resulted in the formation of nanoparticles with unusually high mobility. In the case of Cu-MOFs (HKUST-1), the electron beam initiated the instantaneous sublimation of the formed nanoparticles (Fig. 1). The behavior of Co-containing nanoparticles was studied using machine learning methods to digitally track the particle trajectories and properties.
