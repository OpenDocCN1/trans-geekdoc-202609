(Cu3(BTC)2, BTC = benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylate), Ni(BDC), Ni(BTC), and MOF-74(Ni) (Ni2DOBDC, DOBDC = 2,5-dihydroxy-1,4-benzenedicarboxylate), were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Some of them tended to transform with the formation of particles with a diameter of several nanometers to tens of nanometers when exposed to an electron beam with an accelerating voltage of 100 kV. The formation of the particles depended mainly on the metal ion in the framework and was observed for MOFs with Co2+ (BIF-66, ZIF-67), Fe3+ (MIL-101(Fe), NH2-MIL-101(Fe)), Cu2+ (HKUST-1), and Ni2+ (Ni(BDC), Ni(BTC), MOF-74(Ni)) (Table 1 and Fig. S1, S2, ESI†). MOFs with Al3+ (NH2-MIL-101(Al), MIL-53(Al), NH2-MIL-53(Al)) and Zn2+ (ZIF-8) were stable (Fig. S3, ESI†). An interesting observation is that this is consistent with the standard reduction potentials Mn+/M0 of metals: metals of stable MOFs have a lower value (−1.66 eV for Zn2+/Zn0, −0.76 eV for Al3+/Al0) compared to those of MOFs prone to transformation (−0.28 eV for Co2+/Co0, −0.25 eV for Ni2+/Ni0, −0.04 eV for Fe3+/Fe0, and 0.34 eV for Cu2+/Cu0)71. In addition, the formation of the particles depended on the emission current.

For the formation of NPs. NPs appearance was observed at different times of exposure to the electron beam for different particles of the same MOF. Interestingly, as we observed, not all framework particles were transformed into nanoparticles under the action of an electron beam even after a long exposure. Many MOF structures began to transform only when the emission current increased to 17 μA. The use of high magnifications initiated the formation of nanoparticles. A decrease in the emission current to 8 μA made it possible to obtain an image of the surface of the MOF particles without transformation. Thus, the transformation of MOFs and the formation of nanoparticles strongly depended on the current density. It should be noted that only partial formation of nanoparticles under the action of an electron beam was observed, while some particles of the same MOF sample remained unchanged.

We used the following parameters for the TEM studies: accelerating voltage of 100 kV, magnification range of 50–150 K, and detection with a CCD camera. The screen density was on the order of 10−12 A cm−2. Our observations have shown that the rate of transformation of the framework into NPs depends not only on the electron dose but also on the MOF nature and particle size. In the case of BIF-66 and ZIF-67, the transformation into NPs occurred almost instantly. For Ni MOFs, tens of seconds or even whole minutes were required

Most likely, the transformation of MOF structures occurs due to the generation of secondary electrons, which can lead to bond cleavage in the framework.72,73 The electron beam can reduce metal ions in the MOF structures, resulting in the formation of M NP. The sample can be heated due to interaction with the electron beam. However, previous studies have shown that heating the sample with an electron beam is limited to several tens or a couple of hundreds of degrees.28,72 Therefore, the transformation of the MOF material due to strong heating is unlikely.

To determine the nature of the formed nanoparticles, EDS analysis of the ZIF-67 sample was performed. According to element distribution maps for the MOF particle, on which nanoparticles were formed by the electron beam, regions with a high content of cobalt correspond to the location of the studied nanoparticles (Fig. 2).

The observed nanoparticles are insoluble in organic solvents. The analysis of one area of the sample before and after washing with acetone and chloroform showed that no noticeable changes occurred with cobalt nanoparticles after exposure to these solvents (Fig. 3). Thus, the resulting nanoparticles are stable. The presence of molecular organic components in the composition of nanoparticles is unlikely.

## Dynamic behavior of formed nanoparticles

Further TEM observation of the formed nanoparticles made it possible to establish their different behaviors under the action of an electron beam. The Fe nanoparticles were static. In the case of nanoparticles formed during the transformation of the MOF-74(Ni) structure, a gradual increase in particle size was observed (Fig. 4A and B). In addition, a particle “flicker” was observed under the action of an electron beam, which is probably due to a change in their orientation under the action of local heating and a magnetic field (Fig. 4C–F).74

Among the studied MOF materials, the dynamic behavior of cobalt nanoparticles formed during the transformation of the BIF-66 and ZIF-67 structures under an electron beam was the most interesting. In contrast, Fe NPs formed upon transformation of the Fe-based MOF (MIL-101) were stable and static (Table 1). The video obtained during the TEM study of the Co-MOFs clearly shows that the nanoparticles actively moved over the surface of the MOF particle, changed their shape, and, in some cases, merged with each other, increasing in size. In some cases, one nanoparticle was divided into several separate nanoparticles (Fig. 1). This indicates that Co NPs had a liquid-like nature. Notably, the size of the observed liquid-like nanoparticles varied from a few nanometers to several tens of nanometers.

The melting points of bulk Fe, Co, and Ni are 1812, 1768, and 1726 K, respectively. Although the values are close, there is a distinct difference of > 40 K between them. In this series, iron is the most refractory among the metals listed above. This is probably one of the reasons for the static nature of Fe NPs. In contrast, Co and Ni exhibit dynamic behavior. M NPs exhibit lower melting points compared to bulk metal. The decrease in melting point can be predicted using the Gibbs–Thomson equation. It is significant for particles smaller than 100 nm. For cobalt particles with a size of 10 nm, the melting point significantly exceeds 1000 °C. In the case of Co NPs with a size of ~ 1 nm, it approximates 500 °C. Thus, the size effect of a decrease in the melting temperature should not be strong for the observed nanoparticles. The transition to the liquid state requires temperatures well above a few hundred degrees.

Important factors of the thermal stability of bulk and surface atoms are the Tammann temperature (0.5Tmelt in K) and the Hüttig temperature (0.3Tmelt in K, respectively). At temperatures above the corresponding values, the individual atoms of the particle exhibit mobility. For bulk cobalt, the Tammann temperature is 877 °C, and the Hüttig temperature is 526 °C. Cobalt nanoparticles with a size of 5–22 nm have a Hüttig temperature in the range of 200–240 °C, while the Tammann temperature is above 500 °C.73 Thus, heating initiated by an electron beam can cause mobility of the surface atoms of cobalt nanoparticles. However, it is unlikely that this can lead to the melting of M NPs.

An even more interesting behavior was observed in the case of a copper-containing HKUST-1 (Table 1). The formation of spherical nanoparticles with diameters in a wide range of approximately 10–100 nm occurred upon focusing an electron beam. As the emission current increased, gradual sublimation of Cu NPs was observed. In some cases, the complete disappearance of nanoparticles with a diameter of approximately 50 nm was observed within a few seconds. In addition, the formation of Cu NPs on the lacy carbon film of the TEM grid was observed within a radius of several micrometers from the HKUST-1 particle that was exposed to the electron beam.
