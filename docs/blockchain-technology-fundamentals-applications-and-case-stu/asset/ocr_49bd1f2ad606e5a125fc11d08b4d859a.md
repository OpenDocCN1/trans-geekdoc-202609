# 12.10 PITFALLS IN CRYPTOCURRENCY

Cryptocurrencies have a big risk in the form of government or political intervention. But even a bigger risk is bugs in the code or problems with the protocol that leads to a complete lack of confidence. Though it is structured to overcome the weaknesses of trust-based models, the entire network is built on mutual trust among the peers. If trust is lost, then the entire network would crash to almost zero.

# 12.11 RISK AND FUTURE OF CRYPTOCURRENCY

Bitcoin was just an experiment, which turned out to be successful. As of now, cryptocurrencies have a high value, but it also has been quoted as “the big bubble”. This happens when people flock up to a new investment that drives prices like a “tulip mania”. The biggest question is: “Will bitcoin become widely accepted as a form of payment and a store of value?” In other words, the mass majority of people in the world use bitcoin as a means of exchange over other currencies. The first bubble was witnessed in June 2011, when the price of bitcoin soared from 50 cents to 32 dollars in about 60days and over the course of a few months to $2. Another bubble was observed between April 2013 and November 2013, when the rates went up from $36 and crashed back to $50. All these are small blips on a map as the exchange rate of bitcoin to be $8,725.77 (as Coinbase cliché on 21 January 2020). The future market is unpredictable, but there's an old cliché of Warren Buffett that says: “when people are scared, be greedy and when people are greedy be be scared”. It applies to every market and especially cryptocurrencies. Investing in cryptocurrencies using money that we cannot afford to lose brings significant risks.

# 12.12.1 CURRENCY CRISIS

ZWD is the cash condensing for the Zimbabwe dollar, which was the official money for the Republic of Zimbabwe from 1980 to 2009. In 2007–2008, ZWD experienced one of the most exceedingly scenes of hyperinflation at any point recorded. By printing much cash in the range of 2 years, the Zimbabwe government basically devalued their money to nothing. This prompted a cash emergency, which realized a sharp decrease in the estimation of money. Such decrease makes individuals pull back the entirety of their cash and convert it into outside money. This is alluded to capital flight. Subsequently, the rates deteriorate, bringing about a sudden spike in the demand for the cash. Two other mainstream money emergencies are Latin American Crisis of 1994 and Asian Crisis of 1997. Following a while of hyperinflation, the legislature of Zimbabwe reported the demonetization of the ZWD in 2009, which got last in 2015.
