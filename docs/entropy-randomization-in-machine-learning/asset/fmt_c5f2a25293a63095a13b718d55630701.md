
Denote by **x**(*τ*) an input vector composed of independent observations *x*[1](*τ*), …, *x*[*n*](*τ*) that are defined on a time interval 𝒯[*t*] = [0, *t*], where *τ* ∈ 𝒯[*t*]. At each time *t* > 0, the model outputs a vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t)$ composed of observations *ŷ*[1](*t*), …, *ŷ*[*m*](*t*), where *t* ∈ 𝒯 = [0, *T*]. A relation between the input **x**(*τ* | *τ*∈𝒯[*t*]) and the output $\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t)$ is described by a functional ℬ, i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
\left.
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x}(\tau),\mathbf{a}\, \middle| \,\tau \in \mathcal{T}_{t}),\quad\quad t \in \mathcal{T} = \lbrack 0,T\rbrack.
\right.
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.16)

Assume that this functional is parameterized by a vector **a** = {*a*[1],…,*a*[*r*]} of random elements that belong to intervals

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{A}_{i} & {= \lbrack a_{i}^{-},a_{i}^{+}\rbrack,\quad\quad i \in \lbrack 1,r\rbrack;} \\
\mathcal{A} & {= {\prod\limits_{i = 1}^{r}\mathcal{A}_{i}}.}
\\
\end{aligned}
$$

(4.17)

Three classes of the dynamic “Single Input–Ensemble Output” RPMs will be considered, namely, the linear ones, as well as the nonlinear ones with the power and polynomial nonlinearities.

### 4.2.3 Linear dynamic models

Consider a dynamic RPM with an input **x**(*τ*), *τ* ∈ 𝒯[*t*] and an output $\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t),\, t \in \mathcal{T}.$ The input vector elements $x_{i}(\tau),\, i = \overline{1,n},$ are *continuous time-varying functions*. A relation between the input and output of this RPM is described by a *linear* functional ℬ with the parameters **a** (4.16), i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t) = {\int_{0}^{t}B}(t,\tau,\,\mathbf{a})\mathbf{x}(\tau)d\tau.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.18)

In this formula, the matrix *B*(*t*, *τ*, **a**) of dimensions (*m* × *n*) consists of pulse characteristics $\left. \lbrack b_{ij}(t,\tau\, \middle| \,\mathbf{a}^{(ij)}),\, \middle| \, i = \overline{1,m},\, j = \overline{1,n}\rbrack \right.$ with random parameters $\mathbf{a}^{(ij)} = \{a[1]^{(ij)},\dots,a[k[ij]]^{(ij)}\}$, where *k*[*i**j*] is the number of parameters for a pulse characteristic *b*[*i**j*](*t*, *τ*).

According to a general concept, the parameters **a**^((*i**j*)) are interval-type, i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\mathbf{a}^{(ij)} \in \mathcal{A}^{(ij)} = \lbrack\mathbf{a}_{-}^{(ij)},\mathbf{a}_{+}^{(ij)}\rbrack,\quad\mathbf{a} \in \mathcal{A} = {\prod\limits_{i = 1}^{m}{\prod\limits_{j = 1}^{n}\mathcal{A}^{(ij)}}}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.19)

The model (4.19) generates an ensemble ${\hat{\mathcal{Y}}}_{\mathcal{T}}$ of random trajectories $\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t),\, t \in \mathcal{T}.$ These pulse characteristics can be parameterized in different ways. Some of them will be discussed below.

#### 1.1. Given functions with unknown parameters
As a rule, pulse characteristics are exponential functions or harmonic functions with exponential decay, i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{b_{ij}(t,\tau) = \alpha_{ij}(t)\exp\left( {-\beta_{ij}(t)\tau} \right)\text{or}b_{ij}(t,\tau)\exp\left( {-\beta_{ij}\tau} \right)\,\sin(\omega_{ij}(t)\tau + \phi_{ij}(t)),}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.20)

where, e.g., time-varying parameters have the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\alpha_{ij}(t)} & {= \alpha_{ij}^{0} + \alpha_{ij}^{1}t;\quad\beta_{ij}(t) = \beta_{ij}^{0} + \beta_{ij}^{1}t,} \\
{\omega_{ij}(t)} & {= \omega_{ij}^{0}\ln t;\quad\quad\phi_{ij}(t) = \phi_{ij}^{0} + \phi_{ij}^{1}t.}
\\
\end{aligned}
$$

(4.21)

In these expressions, *α*[*i**j*]⁰, *α*[*i**j*]¹, *β*[*i**j*]⁰, *β*[*i**j*]¹, *ω*[*i**j*]⁰, *ϕ*[*i**j*]⁰ and *ϕ*[*i**j*]¹ are random variables that take values within corresponding intervals.

#### 1.2. Approximation by given family of functions with unknown weights
Consider a family ℛ[*v*] of *v* deterministic functions, further referred to as the *basis* functions:

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\mathcal{R}_{v} = \left\{ \phi_{1}(t,\tau),\ldots,\phi_{v}(t,\tau) \right\}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.22)

Then the pulse characteristics are written as

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{b_{ij}(t,\tau) = {\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{K_{ij}}b_{k}^{(ij)}}\,\phi_{k}(t,\tau),}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.23)

where the coefficients *b*[*k*]^((*i**j*)) are randomized independent variables that take values within some intervals.

To a large extent, a proper choice of basis functions depends on the properties of an object under study. Here a widespread approach is to employ the families of basis functions formed by the exponential functions exp(*α*(*t*, *τ*)), the polynomial-exponential functions *β**t*^(*p*)exp(*α*(*t*, *τ*)) (with integers *p*), or the polynomial-exponential-harmonic functions *β**t*^(*p*)exp(*α*(*t*, *τ*) sin(*ω*(*t*, *τ*)).

If the elements of the matrix *B* have random time-invariable parameters, the expression (4.18) takes the form

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\mathbf{y}(t) = {\int_{0}^{t}B}(t - \tau)\,\mathbf{x}(\tau)d\tau.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.24)

The elements of the matrix *B*, which has dimensions (*m* × *n*), are the pulse characteristics *b*[*i**j*](*τ*) with the independent randomized time-invariable parameters.

#### 1.3. Discrete input functions
In most cases, the input and output of an object are measured at discrete times with a fixed step `Δ`. An object operates on finite time intervals, and hence the available information about its input and output is represented as one-dimensional numerical data arrays. These arrays are used to form the vectors of lattice functions, **x**[*k*Δ*] = **x**[*k*] and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}[k\Delta] = \hat{\mathbf{y}}[k]$ for the object's input and output, respectively. For details, the reader is referred to [[176](#refbib.xhtml#r176)].

In a linear approximation, a dynamic relation between the input and output lattice function vectors, **x**[*k*] and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}[k],$ can be described by a matrix `B[k,q]` composed of the lattice pulse characteristics `b[i j][k,q]` for the RPM with time-varying parameters or by a matrix composed of the lattice pulse characteristics `b[i j][q]` for the RPM with constant parameters. In both statements, the RPM contain independent random parameters taking values within given intervals. The pulse characteristics are assumed to have the so-called finite memory. For the RPMs with time-varying parameters, this means that

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{b_{ij}[k,q] = \left\{ \begin{aligned}
{b_{ij}[k,q]} & {\text{if}\, 0 \leq l \leq L_{ij}\,\text{and}\, k \geq 0,} \\
0 & {\text{if}\, l > L_{ij}\,\text{and}\, k \geq 0;} \\
\end{aligned}~ \right.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.25)

for the RPM with constant parameters,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{b_{ij}[q] = \left\{ \begin{aligned}
{b_{ij}[q]} & {\text{if}\, 0 \leq l \leq L_{ij},} \\
0 & {\text{if}\, l > L_{ij}.} \\
\end{aligned}~ \right.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.26)

Then the RPM with discrete inputs and outputs is described by the following formulas:

* for the time-varying parameters,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\hat{\mathbf{y}}[k] = {\sum\limits_{q = 0}^{L^{*}}B}[k,q]\,\mathbf{x}[q],\quad\quad k \geq L^{*};}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.27)

* for the constant parameters,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\hat{\mathbf{y}}[k] = {\sum\limits_{q = 0}^{L^{*}}B}[q]\,\mathbf{x}[k - q],\quad\quad k \geq L^{*}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(4.28)

Here `L*` = max[*i**j*] *L*[*i**j*].
