
The value `$q = {\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{s}i_{k}}$` is called the order of the moment function. In the class of moment functions, a special place belongs to the moment functions of orders 1 and 2, namely,

### the mean

$$
\begin{array}{r} {m(t_{1}) = m_{1}(t_{1}) = \mathcal{M}\left\{ x(t_{1}) \right\};} \\ \end{array}
$$

(2.20)

### the correlation function

$$
\begin{array}{r} {R(t_{1},t_{2}) = m_{11}(t_{1},t_{2}) = \mathcal{M}\left\{ \lbrack x(t_{1}) - m(t_{1})\rbrack\lbrack x(t_{2}) - m(t_{2})\rbrack \right\}.} \\ \end{array}
$$

(2.21)

If `t[1] = t[2] = t`, then `R(t, t) = sigma^2` are the variances of a random variable `x(t)` with a fixed `t`. If `R(t[1], t[2]) = 0`, then the values `x(t[1])` and `x(t[2])` of a random function `x(t)` are uncorrelated. In a special case where the two-dimensional joint probability distribution of random variables `x(t[1])` and `x(t[2])` is Gaussian and the uncorrelatedness condition holds, the random variables `x(t[1])` and `x(t[2])` are independent.

Continuity is an important property of a function. For a random function, this property has the probabilistic sense formalized in terms of the distribution of all pairs `x(t[1]), x(t[2])`. Introduce the distance `rho(t[1],t[1])=|t[1]-t[2]|` on the set `mathcal{T}`. A random function `x(t), t in mathcal{T}`, is called *stochastically continuous* at a point `t^*` if, for all `epsilon > 0`,

$$
\begin{array}{r} \left. \mathbf{P}\left\{ \middle| x(t^{*}) - x(t) \middle| > \varepsilon \right\}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\,\,\rho(t^{*},t)\rightarrow 0. \right. \\ \end{array}
$$

(2.22)

If condition (2.22) holds for all values `t in mathcal{T}`, then a corresponding function `x(t)` is stochastically continuous on `mathcal{T}`.

According to the definition (2.22), stochastic continuity is an “asymptotic” property to be declared only. A more constructive definition would operate some quantitative values. In fact, *uniform stochastic continuity* forms such a property. A random function is uniformly stochastically continuous on `mathcal{T}` if, for an arbitrary `epsilon > 0`, there exists a `delta > 0` such that

$$
\begin{array}{r} {\mathbf{P}\left\{ \middle| x(t) - x(\widetilde{t}) \middle| > \varepsilon \right\} < \varepsilon} \\ \end{array}
$$

(2.23)

for all the pairs `(t, tilde{t})` satisfying the inequality `rho(t, tilde{t}) < epsilon`.

Since the set `mathcal{T}` is compact, the stochastic continuity of a random function on `mathcal{T}` implies its uniform stochastic continuity on `mathcal{T}`.

Consider some classes of random functions (processes).

## 1. Gaussian random functions

In many applications, a crucial role is played by the random functions with Gaussian finite-dimensional probability distributions. As the matter concerns the finite-dimensional distributions, a random function can be described by a random `n`-dimensional vector `overset{rightarrow}{x} = { x(t_{1}),...,x(t_{n}) }.` A random vector `overset{rightarrow}{x}` obeys the Gaussian distribution if its characteristic function can be represented in the form

$$
\begin{array}{r} {\Phi(\overset{rightarrow}{u}) = \mathcal{M}\left\{ \exp\left( {j\left\langle \overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{x} \right\rangle} \right) \right\} = \exp\left( {j\left\langle \overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{m} \right\rangle - \frac{1}{2}\left\langle G\,\overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{u} \right\rangle} \right).} \\ \end{array}
$$

(2.24)

Here `overset{rightarrow}{m}` denotes the `n`-dimensional vector of the means for the corresponding elements of a vector `overset{rightarrow}{x};` `overset{rightarrow}{u}` is the `n`-dimensional vector of real variables defined on `R^{(n)}`; `G` indicates a nonnegative definite real symmetric matrix (the correlation matrix); finally, `langle bullet, bullet rangle` stands for the scalar product of appropriate vectors.

The moments of a Gaussian random function can be calculated by expanding its characteristic function. If the central moments (`m[1](t[1]) = 0`) suffice, then

$$
\begin{aligned} {\Phi(\overset{rightarrow}{u})} & {= \exp\left( {- \frac{1}{2}\left\langle G\,\overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{u} \right\rangle} \right) = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\left\langle G\,\overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{u} \right\rangle + \frac{1}{2^{2}2!}\left\langle G\,\overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{u} \right\rangle^{2} - \ldots} \\ & {+ {( - 1)}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\left\langle G\,\overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{u} \right\rangle^{n} + \ldots.} \\ \end{aligned}
$$

(2.25)

Hence, the odd-order moment functions are

$$
\begin{array}{r} {m_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{s}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}) = 0,\text{\\quad\\quad}{\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{s}i_{k}} = 2n + 1;} \\ \end{array}
$$

(2.26)

the even-order moment functions take the form

$$
\begin{aligned} {m_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{s}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})} & {= \frac{\partial^{2n}}{\partial t_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{s}^{i_{s}}}\left( {\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\left\langle G\,\overset{rightarrow}{u},\overset{rightarrow}{u} \right\rangle} \right),} \\ {\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{s}i_{k}} & {= 2n.} \\ \end{aligned}
$$

(2.27)

The Gaussian random functions are of paramount importance in different (theoretical and applications-oriented) studies. Really, under rather nonrestricting conditions, the sum of a large number of independent random functions with small values can be approximated by a Gaussian random function, regardless of the probability-theoretic nature of the terms. This significant result—the normal correlation theorem—is a generalization of the central limit theorem [[166](#refbib.xhtml#r166)].

## 2. Random processes with independent increments

The research in the field of random processes with independent increments started with Brownian motion and the Wiener process as its appropriate mathematical model. At a later time, the generalized mathematical models with integration in the class of processes with independent increments were developed.

A random process `overset{rightarrow}{x}(t), t in mathcal{T},` taking values in `R^{(n)}` is called a process with independent increments if, for any `n`, `t[0] <= t[1] <= ... <= t[n] = T`, the random vectors `overset{rightarrow}{x}(t_{0}),overset{rightarrow}{x}(t_{1}) - overset{rightarrow}{x}(t_{0}),...,overset{rightarrow}{x}(t_{n}) - overset{rightarrow}{x}(t_{n - 1})` are mutually independent.

The vector `overset{rightarrow}{x}(t_{0})` is called the initial state (value) of the process, and its probability distribution is called the initial distribution of the process. For defining a process with independent increments, it suffices to define an initial distribution `P_{0}(mathcal{B}) = mathbf{P}\left\{ overset{rightarrow}{x}(t_{0}) in mathcal{B} \right\},` a collection of distributions `P(t,h,mathcal{B}) = mathbf{P}\left\{ Delta overset{rightarrow}{x}(t,h) in mathcal{B} \right\}` for the vector `Delta overset{rightarrow}{x}(t,h) = overset{rightarrow}{x}(t + h) - overset{rightarrow}{x}(t),` where `t in mathcal{T}`, `h > 0`, and `mathcal{B} in R^{(m)}` is a set of elementary events.

If a distribution `P(t, h, mathcal{B})` does not depend on `t`, i.e., `P(t, h, mathcal{B}) = P(h, mathcal{B})`, then a corresponding process with independent increments is called *homogeneous*.
