## 7.4 Multiplicative Algorithms with *p*-Active Variables

Many problems arising in RML and ML procedures are reduced to systems of nonlinear equations, e.g., the balance equations (7.9–7.12). Let us illustrate a method for solving such equations that are associated with the entropy maximization problems on polyhedrons [140].

Consider a system of equalities that are interpreted as the balance constraints between data **q** ∈ *R*^(*r*) and an output **y** ∈ *R*^(*n*) of some linear parametric model:

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{q},\quad(T,\mathbf{y}) \geq 0,\,\,\mathbf{q} \geq \varepsilon > 0,\text{\quad\quad}r < n.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.134)

Here *T* denotes a matrix of full rank *r*. For the system (7.134), there exists a set of solutions and a unique solution among them that maximizes the entropy

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{H(\mathbf{y}) = - {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}y_{i}}\ln\frac{y_{i}}{ea_{i}},\text{\quad\quad}e = 2.71,}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.135)

with *a*[1], …, *a*[*n*] acting as parameters.

Consider the following constrained optimization problem for the entropy (7.135):

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \left. H(\mathbf{y})\Rightarrow\max, \right.
\\
& {T\,\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{q}.}
\\
\end{aligned}
$$

(7.136)

The corresponding Lagrange function has the form

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{L(\mathbf{y},\overline{\lambda}) = H(\mathbf{y}) + {\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{r}\lambda_{k}}\left( {q_{k} - {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}t_{ki}}y_{i}} \right).}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.137)

The first-order optimality conditions are given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\nabla_{y_{i}}L(\mathbf{y},\overline{\lambda})} & {\frac{\partial H(\mathbf{y})}{\partial y_{i}} - {\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{r}\lambda_{k}}t_{ki} = 0,\quad i = \overline{1,n};}
\\
{\nabla_{\lambda_{k}}L(\mathbf{y},\overline{\lambda})} & {q_{k} - {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}t_{ki}}y_{i} = 0,\quad k = \overline{1,r}.}
\\
\end{aligned}
$$

(7.138)

The first group of equations yields a relation between the variables **y** and the Lagrange multipliers $\overline{\lambda}$ in the form

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{y_{i}(\overline{\lambda}) = \frac{a_{i}}{{\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{r}\exp}(t_{k}i\lambda_{k})},\text{\quad\quad}i = \overline{1,n}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.139)

This relation allows us to eliminate the primal variables **y** from the definition of the Lagrange function (7.137), leading to the following expression for the dual function (which depends on the dual variables—the Lagrange multipliers):

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\widetilde{L}(\overline{\lambda}) = L\lbrack\mathbf{y}(\overline{\lambda}),\overline{\lambda}\rbrack.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.140)

The elements of its gradient are

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\nabla_{\lambda_{k}}\widetilde{L}(\overline{\lambda}) = q_{k} - {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}y_{i}}(\overline{\lambda}),\text{\quad\quad}k = \overline{1,r}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.141)

In terms of the dual function, the second group of the optimality conditions (7.138) can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\nabla_{\lambda_{k}}\widetilde{L}(\overline{\lambda}) = 0,\text{\quad\quad}k = \overline{1,r}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.142)

Hence, the balance constraints take the form

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{{\widetilde{B}}_{k}(\overline{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{q_{k}}{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}t_{ki}}\frac{a_{i}}{{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{r}\exp}\left( {\lambda_{j}t_{ji}} \right)} = 1,\quad\overline{\lambda} \in R^{r},\text{\quad\quad}k = \overline{1,r}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.143)

Often it seems convenient to transform these equations using the nonnegative variables $z_{k} = \exp(\lambda_{k}),\, k = \overline{1,r},$ known as the exponential Lagrange multipliers. Then equations (7.143) are reduced to

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{B_{k}(\mathbf{z}) = \frac{1}{q_{k}}{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}t_{ki}}a_{i}\left( {\prod\limits_{j = 1}^{r}z_{k}^{- t_{ji}}} \right) = 1,\quad\mathbf{z} \in R_{+}^{r},\text{\quad\quad}k = \overline{1,r}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.144)

Now, for the dual function (7.140), the Hessian matrix has the form

$$
\begin{array}{r}
\left. \Gamma(\overline{\lambda}) = \lbrack\nabla_{\lambda_{j},\lambda_{l}}\widetilde{L}(\overline{\lambda})\, \middle| \,(j,l) = \overline{1,r}\rbrack, \right.
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.145)

where

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\nabla_{\lambda_{j},\lambda_{l}}\widetilde{L}(\overline{\lambda}) = - {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}t_{kj}}\frac{\partial y_{j}}{\partial\lambda_{l}}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.146)

The primal variables expression (7.139) implies the inequality

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\frac{\partial y_{j}}{\partial\lambda_{l}} \leq 0,}
\\
\end{array}
$$

(7.147)

holding for all *λ* ∈ *R*^(*r*). Therefore, the Hessian matrix (7.143) consists of nonnegative elements. Taking into account (7.145), the quadratic form $K = \left\langle \Gamma\overline{\lambda},\, \overline{\lambda} \right\rangle$ is strictly positive definite for any $\overline{\lambda} \in R^{r}.$ This means that the dual function *L̃* is strictly convex. The system of equations 7.143 guarantees boundedness of its solution, i.e., there exists a constant *c* > 0 such that $\left| \overline{\lambda} \middle| \leq c < \infty. \right.$ Consequently, *L̃* ≥ *L*[min].

For solving equations (7.138), utilize the multiplicative algorithm with *p*-active variables as follows.

*Initial step:*

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{\mathbf{z}^{0} > \mathbf{0}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

*Iterative step:*

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k_{1}(s)}^{(s + 1)} & {= z_{k_{1}(s)}^{s}B_{k_{1}(s)}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z}^{s}),}
\\
\cdots & {= \cdots\cdots\cdots,}
\\
z_{k_{p}(s)}^{(s + 1)} & {= z_{k_{p}(s)}^{s}B_{k_{p}(s)}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z}^{s})}
\\
z_{k}^{(s + 1)} & {= z_{k}^{s},\quad k \neq k_{1}(s),\ldots,k_{p}(s),}
\\
& {(k,k_{1}(s),\ldots,k_{p}(s)) \in \overline{1,n}.}
\\
\end{aligned}
$$

(7.148)

Further analysis will need an additive form of the algorithm (7.148) in the variables *λ*[*k*] = ln *z*[*k*]. This form is written in the following way.

*Initial step:*

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{{\overline{\lambda}}^{0} \in R^{n}.}
\\
\end{array}
$$

*Iterative step:*

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{k_{1}(s)}^{(s + 1)} & {= \lambda_{k_{1}(s)}^{s} + \gamma\ln{\widetilde{B}}_{k_{1}(s)}({\overline{\lambda}}^{s}),}
\\
\cdots & {= \cdots\cdots\cdots,}
\\
\lambda_{k_{p}(s)}^{(s + 1)} & {= \lambda_{k_{p}(s)}^{s} + \gamma\ln{\widetilde{B}}_{k_{p}(s)}({\overline{\lambda}}^{s})}
\\
\lambda_{k}^{(s + 1)} & {= \lambda_{k}^{s},\quad k \neq k_{1}(s),\ldots,k_{p}(s),}
\\
& {(k,k_{1}(s),\ldots,k_{p}(s)) \in \overline{1,n},}
\\
\end{aligned}
$$

(7.149)

where ${\widetilde{B}}_{k}(\overline{\lambda}) = B_{k}(\exp(\overline{\lambda})),\, k = \overline{1,r}.$

A major difficulty for such algorithms is to select active variables. This selection can be organized by a cyclical rule, e.g., for *p* = 1,

$$
\begin{array}{r}
{k_{1}(s) = s(\text{mod}\, n + 1).}
\\
\end{array}
$$
