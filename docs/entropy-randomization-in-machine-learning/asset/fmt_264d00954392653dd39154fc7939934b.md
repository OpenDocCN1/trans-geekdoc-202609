
# 1. Frobenius norms of matrices

Above, it has been supposed that the direct `Q` and inverse `T` projector matrices have nonnegative elements. Such an assumption is due to the use of cross-entropy to characterize the “distance” between them. However, in applications-relevant machine learning problems, it often makes sense to narrow the class of projector matrices. This can be achieved by considering the Frobenius matrix norm:

# 2. Information capacity of data matrix

In the course of reduction, some information contained in a data matrix is lost. Therefore, it should be measured somehow. Introduce the concept of *the information capacity* `ℐ(A)` of a nonnegative matrix `A[n × q]`, which is given by

$$\begin{array}{r} {\mathcal{I}(A) = {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{q}a_{ij}}}\ln a_{ij}.} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.59)

Clearly, the information capacity corresponds to the entropy taken with the opposite sign.

# 3. Cross-entropy reduction algorithm

The dimension reduction procedure of a data matrix is to minimize the cross-entropy:

$$\begin{array}{r} \left. H(Q,T\, \middle| \, U)\Rightarrow\min\limits_{Q,T}, \right. \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.60)

where

$$\begin{array}{r} \left. H(Q,T\, \middle| \, U) = {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{m}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{s}x_{ij}}}(Q,T\, \middle| \, U)\ln\frac{\left. x_{ij}(Q,T\, \middle| \, U) \right.}{u_{ij}}, \right. \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.61)

$$\begin{array}{r} \left. x_{ij}(Q,T\, \middle| \, U) = {\sum\limits_{\mu = 1}^{r}{\sum\limits_{\nu = 1}^{s}t_{\mu,j}}}q_{\nu,\mu}u_{i,\nu},\text{\quad\quad}i = \overline{1,m};\, j = \overline{1,s}, \right. \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.62)

subject to the following constraints:

* —the nonnegative values of the projector matrices,

$$\begin{array}{r} {Q \geq 0,\text{\quad\quad}T \geq 0;} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.63)

* —the upper bounds (`a`, `b`) on the norm of the projector matrices,

$$\begin{array}{r} {n_{Q}(Q) = {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{s}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{r}q_{ij}^{2}}} \leq a,\quad n_{T}(T) = {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{r}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{s}t_{ij}^{2}}} \leq b;} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.64)

* —the `δ`-admissible decrease of the information capacity,

$$\begin{array}{r} {\frac{\left. \mathcal{I}(U) - \mathcal{I}(Y(Q \middle| U)) \right.}{\mathcal{I}(U)} \leq \delta.} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.65)

In this inequality,

$$\begin{array}{r} \left. \mathcal{I}(Y(Q\, \middle| \, U)) = {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{m}{\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{r}y_{ij}}}(Q\, \middle| \, U)\ln y_{ij}(Q\, \middle| \, U), \right. \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.66)

$$\begin{array}{r} \left. y_{ij}(Q\, \middle| \, U) = {\sum\limits_{\nu = 1}^{s}u_{i,\nu}}q_{\nu,j}. \right. \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.67)

Due to the properties of the objective function (an entropy-bilinear form) and the constraints specified by quadratic entropy inequalities, the cross-entropy reduction problem (3.60)–(3.64) is nonconvex; for details, see [[134](#refbib.xhtml#r134), [171](#refbib.xhtml#r171)]. Therefore, there exists a certain (a priori unknown) number of local optimums, and this problem should be treated as a global optimization one.

Consider the problem (3.60)–(3.64) as a local minimization problem on the nonnegative orthant `R[+](2*s*r)`. Here the variables are the elements of the matrices `Q` and `T` each containing `rs` entries. Using *matrix vectorization*, i.e., the transition to a complex vector through the sequential joining of rows, construct the vectors `q`, `t` and `u`, corresponding to the matrices `Q`, `T` and `U`, respectively. Then the cross-entropy minimization problem can be represented as

$$\begin{array}{r} \left. \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t}\, \middle| \,\mathbf{u})\Rightarrow\min, \right. \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.68)

$$\begin{aligned} & {(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t}) \in R_{+}^{(2sr)},} \\ & {N_{Q}(\mathbf{q}) = - {\widetilde{N}}_{Q}(\mathbf{q}) + a \geq 0,\quad N_{T}(\mathbf{t}) = - {\widetilde{N}}_{T}(\mathbf{t}) + b \geq 0,} \\ & \left. \mathcal{I}_{Y}(\mathbf{q}) = I_{Y}(\mathbf{q}\, \middle| \,\mathbf{u}) - \gamma(\delta) \geq 0,\quad\gamma(\delta) = (1 - \delta)I_{U}. \right. \\ \end{aligned}$$

(3.69)

Recall that the problem (3.68) is considered as a local optimization one, for which the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions are necessary. The Lagrange function for this problem has the form

$$\begin{array}{r} {L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\alpha,\beta,\gamma) = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) - \left\langle \alpha,\mathbf{q} \right\rangle - \left\langle \beta,\mathbf{t} \right\rangle,} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.70)

where `α` = `{α[1],…,α[r*s]}`, `β` = `{β[1],…,β[r*s]}`, and `γ` = `{γ[1],γ[2],γ[3]}` are the Lagrange multipliers related to the corresponding constraints.

The function

$$\begin{array}{r} {\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) = \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t}) - \gamma_{1}N_{Q}(\mathbf{q}) - \gamma_{2}N_{T}(\mathbf{t}) - \gamma_{3}\mathcal{I}_{Y}(\mathbf{q})} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.71)

will be called the truncated Lagrange function.

Assume that there exists a local minimum point (`q*`, `t*`) in the problem (3.68, 3.70) and a neighborhood

$$\begin{array}{r} {\mathcal{O}_{(\mathbf{q}^{*},\mathbf{t}^{*})}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t}) \in \|\mathbf{q}^{*} - \mathbf{q}\|^{2} + \|\mathbf{t}^{*} - \mathbf{t}\|^{2} \leq \varrho} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

(3.72)

such that the problem (3.68), (3.70) becomes convex on the set (3.72). Then the Kuhn–Tucker conditions [134] are satisfied on the set (3.72) in terms of the truncated Lagrange function (3.71), i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} & {\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) \geq \mathbf{0},\text{\quad\quad}\nabla_{\mathbf{t}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) \geq \mathbf{0},} \\ & {\mathbf{q} \otimes \nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) \geq \mathbf{0},\text{\quad\quad}\mathbf{t} \otimes \nabla_{\mathbf{t}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) \geq \mathbf{0},} \\ & {\mathbf{q}^{*} \geq \mathbf{0},\text{\quad\quad}\mathbf{t}^{*} \geq \mathbf{0},} \\ & {\nabla_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) \leq \mathbf{0},\text{\quad\quad}\gamma \otimes \nabla_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) = \mathbf{0},} \\ & {\gamma \geq \mathbf{0}.} \\ \end{aligned}$$

(3.73)

This system of equations and inequalities can be solved using the multiplicative algorithm [[143](#refbib.xhtml#r143)]. Introduce the following notations:

$$\begin{aligned} & {\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) = \mathbf{1} - \theta\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma),} \\ & {\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) = \mathbf{1} - \theta\nabla_{\mathbf{t}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma),} \\ & {\mathbf{K}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma) = \mathbf{1} + \theta\nabla_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{t},\gamma).} \\ \end{aligned}$$

(3.74)

The multiplicative algorithm, further referred to as **Minimum Cross-Entropy with constraints (MCEC)**, has the following structure.

* *a) Initial step:*

$$\begin{array}{r} {\mathbf{q}^{0} > \mathbf{0},\quad\mathbf{t}^{0} > \mathbf{0}.} \\ \\ \end{array}$$

* *b) nth iterative step:*

$$\begin{array}{r} {\mathbf{q}^{(n + 1)} = \mathbf{q}^{n} \otimes \mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}^{n},\mathbf{t}^{n},\gamma^{n}),} \\ \\ \end{array}$$
