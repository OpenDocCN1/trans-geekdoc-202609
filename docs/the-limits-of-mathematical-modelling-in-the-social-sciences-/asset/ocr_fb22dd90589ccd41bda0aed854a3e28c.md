Importantly, CASs are characterized by positive feedback processes that run counter to the more typical negative feedback effects with which we are familiar. Common negative feedback systems involve the muting of signals over time, that is, the consequences of some action or event become less that action or rec rec into the past. In contrast, at least of the feedback processes taking place within CASs exhibit positive feedback, where consequences become more significant over time. This is important because scholars in the social and other sciences have tended to think in terms of linear, negative feedback regimes. This approach is limiting; for example, consider rush traffic in a major city. In terms of traffic flow, the initial consequences of an accident along a major highway might be relatively minor, with only one or two of traffic being impacted. However, as traffic in these lanes backs up and impacts other lanes, emergency vehicles arrive on the scene and additional lanes of traffic, backups along the feeder roads, and so forth; the consequences of the initial event become more significant over time, not.¹
