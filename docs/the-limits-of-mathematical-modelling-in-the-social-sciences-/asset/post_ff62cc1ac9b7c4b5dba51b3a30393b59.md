# Markets in equilibrium may have noncomputable prices

Those results have an immediate consequence for a question of both historical and practical importance: the controversy over economic planning between L. von Mises and O. Lange (see, on that controversy, Seligman (1971, I, p. 155)).

The central problem of economic planning is an allocation problem. Very frequently, allocation is to be done on the basis of maximizing (or minimizing) simple functions over finite sets. We proved trouble to be expected even when the problem of planning is reduced to the problem of determining equilibria in finite noncooperative Nash games, which is formally equivalent to the determination of equilibrium prices in a competitive market.

# Oscillating populations or a chaotic demography

The Lotka-Volterra equations (LV) describe two interacting populations, namely a “preyed” $x$ and a “predator” $y$. If left by themselves, the $x$ blow up exponentially, while the $y$ decays exponentially. When $x$ and $y$ interact, they start to move in nonlinear cycles. The LV model describes in a reasonable number of ecological cycles (see Goel et al., 1971; Nicolis and Prigogine, 1977).

Now consider the following situation: let $\langle x,y \rangle$ and $\langle x',y' \rangle$ be two coupled LV systems. We show in Section 20 that they can be described by a Hamiltonian system $\Xi$, and that there is a perturbed system

$\Xi' = \Xi + \epsilon Y$

where all populations are (in general) coupled and $\Xi'$ has a Smale horseshoe. So $\Xi'$ is chaotic in its behavior.

We slightly modify $\Xi'$ and get

$\Xi^* = \Xi + \epsilon \beta Y$

where $\beta$ is given in Corollary 14.16. Then the assertions “$\Xi^*$ describes two sets of oscillating, nonchaotic, uncoupled populations” and “$\Xi^*$ describes four coupled, chaotic populations” are both undecidable in our formal theory $T$.

# Will the middle class survive?

Section 20, equation (6), describes a beautiful model (elaborated by Prigogine) for the interaction of three coupled populations, $x, y, z$, where $z$ is a small-sized “upper” or “warrior” class, $y$ is a “lower” or “working” class, and $x$ is a “middle class.” The model predicts two stable equilibria: in the first, the “middle class” vanishes; in the second, the “warrior” class vanishes while “workers” and the “middle class” thrive. The “middle class” will grow to a steady state value if

$\kappa(N_0 - y_0 - z_0) - \delta - \rho^*z_0 > 0$

$\rho$ is a measure of the aggressiveness of the “upper class” against the “middle class,” and $N_0$ measures the total value of the ecological niche; $y_0, z_0$ are constant values obtained of the system’s parameters, like $\delta$ (see Section 20).

We now write $\rho^* = \beta\rho' + (1-\beta)\rho'$, where $\rho' \gg \rho'$, and $\rho'$ doesn’t satisfy the preceding condition, while $\rho'$ satisfies it. Then, of

$\kappa(N_0 - y_0 - z_0) - \delta - \rho^*z_0 > 0$

the sentences “The middle class will thrive” and “The middle class will fade away” are both undecidable in $T$.

# Markets in equilibrium may have noncomputable prices

Those results have an immediate consequence for a question of both historical and practical importance: the controversy over economic planning between L. von Mises and O. Lange (see, on that controversy, Seligman (1971, I, p. 155)).

The central problem of economic planning is an allocation problem. Very frequently, allocation is to be done on the basis of maximizing (or minimizing) simple functions over finite sets. So, the main argument by Lange in favor of a planned economy (by the way, an argument also shared by von Mises) clearly breaks down.
