It should be regarded as a natural consequence of Menger's rejection of Brouwer's intuitionism (Menger, 1979; London, 1998) and his adherence to Hilbertism. A caveat: even though since the late 1920s, Menger had clearly rejected Brouwer's intuitionism, Brouwer's influence on Menger has been kept alive, so to speak, by his interest in nonclassical (or nonstandard) logics. Contrary to standard logic (ordinary 2-valued logics), Menger severely criticized the idea that logic was unique. He strongly supported the n-valued logics of Lukasiewicz and Post in which neither the law of excluded middle nor the law of noncontradradiction operated.15

Many-valued logic was created by the logician Lukasiewicz (1920), and it developed first in Poland. His first intention was to use a third, additional truth-value for “possible”, and to model in this way the modalities “it is necessary” and “it is possible that”. This intended application to modal logic did not materialize.
