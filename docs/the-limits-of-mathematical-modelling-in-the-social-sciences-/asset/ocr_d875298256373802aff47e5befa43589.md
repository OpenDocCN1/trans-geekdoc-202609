# **“Smooth” problems equivalent to hard number-theoretic problems**

Common wisdom among mathematicians has that number-theoretic problems are in general much more difficult than “smooth” problems. We showed that it is definitely not the case. We gave an explicit example of a dynamical system where the proof that chaos is equivalent to the proof of Fermat’s last theorem (or the proof of Riemann’s hypothesis, or the decision of the $P$ vs $NP$ question). We also proved that (given some conditions) these “nasty” problems are dense in the space of all dynamical systems (see da Costa et al., 1993).

# **Simple problems worse than any number-theoretic problem**

The language of analysis is much richer than the language of arithmetic, as we can express explicitly construct “natural”-looking and quite simple problems with our techniques that lie beyond the arithmetical hierarchy (see da Costa and Doria, 1994a). For example, we can explicitly define a procedure to obtain an expression $	heta^{(\omega)}(n)$ for a characteristic function in $	heta^{(\omega)}$, That function equals either 0 or 1, but the actual computation of $	heta^{(\omega)}$’s values reaches beyond the arithmetic hierarchy.

# **Generic, faceless objects**

One of the features of the main set-theoretic constructions is that we add “generic,” faceless sets to our formal theories. However, there are no explicit expressions for those objects. With the help of our techniques, we exhibited an expression for a “faceless” Hamiltonian (see da Costa and Doria, 1994a): the only thing we can prove about that it is definitely a Hamiltonian, and nothing more.
