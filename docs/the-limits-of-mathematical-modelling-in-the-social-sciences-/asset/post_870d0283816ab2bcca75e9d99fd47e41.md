According Menger, an exact theory of wishes and commands is a useful and proper tool for a mathematical approach to ethical problems.

Although a first attempt in this direction has been made by Mally, who introduced “lp” = $p$ ought to be (Mally, 1926), according to Menger, Mally made a mistake: he founded his system on a 2-valued logic, which is not applicable to wish and commands, because they are doubtful. Mally set up five postulates:

- (1) $[(p \rightarrow !q)\&(q \rightarrow r)] \rightarrow (p \rightarrow !r)$;
- (2) $[(p \rightarrow !q)\(!q\&r)] \rightarrow [p \rightarrow !(q\&r)]$;
- (3) $(p \rightarrow !q) \leftrightarrow(p \rightarrow q)$;
- (4) $u$; that, there exists a proposition $u$ for which $!u$ holds;
- (5) $(!v)$; that is, there exists a proposition $v$ for which $!v$ holds.

After deriving 50 theorems from these assumptions, Mally arrived at the conclusion:
$p \leftrightarrow !p$.

Hence, according to Menger, the introduction of the sign $!$ is superfluous in the sense that it may be cancelled or inserted in any formula. Stressing the importance of substituting 2-valued logic with $n$-valued logic, Menger's conclusions were:

- (A) The objects of human wishes ($Dp$) and commands ($Cp$) are neither necessities nor impossibilities. Hence, propositions are neither asserted nor negated: they are doubtful. Mally's mistake can be founded on the fact that he used 2-valued logic.
- (B) The words “command” and “wish” differ from each other.
- (C) The expression “wish” in everyday language is incomplete and ambiguous.
- (D) A theory of wishes should account some results of economic theory, in particular the following:
- The distinction of certain classes of pairs of goods, for example, complementary goods; or goods that can be substituted for each other and of which is wanted without.
- For each individual, the combinations of goods constitute a set that is partially ordered by a preference relation such that absence of preference is transitive; that is, if $M$ and $N$ are two combinations of goods, then either $N$ is preferred to $M$, or $M$ is preferred to $N$, or neither is preferred to the other. In the last case (absence of preference), $I$ is indifferent to $M$ and $N$ and we can apply the properties of the logic.

How can these remarks be applied to optative logic in Menger’s system? Even the most elementary wishes presuppose at least normal conditions in some respect; while the number of conditions is practically unlimited, a theory must start with simplifying assumptions; for instance, with the restriction to finite sets of doubtful propositions and $p$ and its negation $p'$:

1. If $Dp$, then $p$ belongs to $\mu_0$, only $Dp$ are objects of human wishes;
2. $Dp'$ doesn't belong to $\mu_0$, no doubt as to whether or not $I$ wishes $p' = Ex(y)$;
3. $(Dp\&Dp')$ belongs to $\mu_1$, $I$ does not wish $p$ and wishes $p' = I$;
4. $Dp\&Dp'$ is ruled out by the law of optative consistency.

The attitudes $Dx(y)$, $Ex(y)$, $Ix(y)$ are interpreted in terms of preferences between the pairs $(x,y)$ and $(x',y)$ respectively by $(x',y) < (x,y)$, $(x,y) < (x',y)$, $(x,y) \sim (x',y)$.

Hence, Menger introduced substitutive goods: when we consider two objects of desires, $p$ and $q$ (with their negations, $p'$ and $q'$) such that either affects the optative attitude toward the other. As before, $Ax(y)$ denotes the attitude toward $x$ under the condition $y$ (where $A$ is $D$ or $E$ or $I$, $A^*$ will denote $E, D$, or $I$, respectively, and $A^{**} = A$) then we have the following:

- (1) If $Ax(y)$ then $Ay(y)$
- (2) If $Ax(y)$ then $A^*x(y)$

There are eight attitudes that can be cyclically arranged:
$A_1p(q)$ $A_2q(p)$
$A_8p'(q)$ $A_3q'(p)$
$A_7q'(p')$ $A_4p(q')$
$A_6q'(p')$ $A_5p'(q')$

From assumption (1) it follows that $A_{i+1} = A_i$ for $I = 1, 3, 5, 7$.

From assumption (2) it follows that $A_{i+1} = A^*_i$ for $I = 2, 4, 6, 8$.

Jointly the assumptions imply that:

Complementary objects illustrate the case $A_1 = D$; incompatible objects correspond the case where $A_1 = E$. Attitudes can again be interpreted in terms of preferences between (unordered) pairs; for $Dp(q)$ by:

- $(p',q) < (p,q), (p,q') < (p',q), (p',q) < (p',q'), (p,q') < (p',q')$.

As we realize, Menger's imperative logic is found on the concept of deterrence (the idea that norms are threats is far from being universally accepted in legal philosophy), and his optative logic is based on a theory of wishes that should take account of some results within economic theory, that can be situate up in the following terms.

Pairs, triple and n-tuples of goods that are complementary or substitutive, and for every individual, the combinations of goods constitute a set that is partially ordered by preference relations. Menger did build a normative logic (including wishes) based on logic of preference.
