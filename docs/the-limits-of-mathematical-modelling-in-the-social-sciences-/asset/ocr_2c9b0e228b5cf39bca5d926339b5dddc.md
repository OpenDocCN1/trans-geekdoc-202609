(But they are a necessary condition.) So, arose the question of an algorithmic procedure to anticipate whether a nonlinear system would exhibit chaotic behavior.

The nonexistence of such an algorithm was proved, under general conditions, by da Costa and Doria in 1991. So, only nonlinear systems may turn out to be unpredictable; we cannot even say for (arbitrary systems and conditions) whether they will be unpredictable or not! Moreover there will be systems that are chaotic in one model for mathematics, and perfectly predictable in another model.

Here the Gödel phenomenon enters the picture. If we could make calculations with infinite precision, we could predict with all sharpness the future behavior of chaotic systems. But there would be situations where not one of these calculations are possible.

So, we have a higher-order difficulty here — one that anticipated by our fictional sage, Hari Sed own. Chaotic systems are deterministic: therefore, given infinitely precise calculations, all future behavior can be predicted. However, when the Gödel phenomenon enters the game, no calculations might exist because we have no algorithms to perform them; no algorithms exist (and the standard concept for mathematical proof subsumes an algorithmic construction).

A. Lewis and V. Veluppilli, among others, were pioneers in the discussion of undecidability and incompleteness in mathematical economics. We now know that, for example equilibrium prices in efficient markets are noncomputable, in the general case (this follows from a 1995 result by Lewis). In 1998, Tsuji, da Costa, and Doria entered the fray, with their result on the incompleteness of game theory — which is valid for a wide range of axiomatizations of Nash games and analogous theories.
