
## Applications of Nonstandard Logic to the Social Sciences

The intellectual framework provided by the theory of complex adaptive systems (CAS) captures at least part of the logic that our arguments seek to convey. CAS are webs or networks of agents that interact with each other — where changes in the behavior or properties of one agent can produce effects that ripple throughout the system. Agents are bound in loops of mutual influence; agents are interconnected whereby all action is the result of actions by others. Arthur, Holland, and others assert that markets possess these important features of radical social interdependency, in that each agent’s expectations of all other agents’ behavior include anticipation of others’ expectations, resulting in a massively interdependent system.

Different agents have different experiences and evaluate those experiences differently, which includes the way those experiences affect observers’ observations of the same phenomenon. Indeed, agents could experience the identical phenomenon, but, unless their prior experiences were identical, they evaluate that phenomenon differently.

As observers, we do not merely observe some fixed reality. Rather, what we may observe at least, in part, be a function of the expectations of what we observe (i.e., “A’s” observation of “B” alters “B’s” behavior — a kind of uncertainty for the social sciences). This, in turn, changes what “A” observes. What we observe is an endogenous function of the expectations of others, which, in turn, affects our own expectations. It must certainly be true that the way in which a researcher evaluates a particular discovery in the social world, whether at the cosmological or quantum-mechanical level, bases on the prior beliefs and expectations that have developed about the world around them through the development of heuristic learning (Gigerenzer and Brighton, 2009; Gigerenzer, 2008 available to multiple observers may be largely the same or completely disjoint). Regardless, no observer can know what the other knows. In the case of financial markets, for example, each agent acts on the basis of those empirical facts they observe. There are obvious implications for nonstandard logic. One might speculate that the economic assumptions of utility-maximizing agents come close to the analogy of multiple observers in different locations, arriving as different conclusions. But an overarching defined set of states that can be “coarsened” in different ways to model unchanging views of the same reality. Equivalently, descriptions of the system always depend on the position from which the system is observed. Different systems vary in the extent to which the family of all descriptions corresponding to different positions overlap or contradict each other.

## 5. Conclusion

The long-standing belief of social sciences in classical logic and its attendant reliance needs revision. While not suggesting that there is a need to abandon classical logic, we suggest that the social sciences will benefit from certain nonstandard approaches to understanding phenomena in the social world. As has been the case in the past, some of the inspiration for this recommendation comes from physics and related disciplines. In particular, epistemological problems that confront cosmologists suggest a rethinking of the traditional paradigm. The assumptions of intuitionistic theory and constructivist mathematics, the paraconsistent logic of da Costa and the extension of aspects of their topos theory and related mathematical innovations point to new possibilities in the social sciences. This is particularly the case given the structure of a world in which we find ourselves in complex, interacting systems where cause and effect are not always clear and we are forced to rely upon heuristics and cognitive roadmaps in making decisions. In such a world, it seems reasonable to relax traditional assumptions of logic, and ground it in contextual frameworks. McAllister's work suggesting the indeterminacy of empirical data partially illustrates the problem, as does Velupilli's path breaking in algorithmic economics. While not advocating a revolution in the social sciences, we suggest a rethinking of the traditional standards of logic may be justified and worthwhile.
