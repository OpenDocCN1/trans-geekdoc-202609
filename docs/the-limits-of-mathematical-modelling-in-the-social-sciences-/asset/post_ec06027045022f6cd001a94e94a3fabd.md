
# Applications of Nonstandard Logic to the Social Sciences

As described by Smolin (2001), there are two fundamental issues in attempting to understand the universe: first, nothing can travel faster than light, and second, the universe appears to have created a finite amount of time in the past, that is, nearly 14 billion years ago. These two facts together mean that it is impossible to observe the entire universe. 14 billion light years away from earth, but only as they appeared 14 billion years in the past. But for reasons that are unnecessary to explain in detail here, the universe itself is far larger than 14 billion light years in any direction.

A fundamental problem emerges: no observer existing inside the universe we can only hope to have knowledge, albeit imperfect, of that part of the universe that we can observe. For example, we might, in principle, be able to determine exactly how many galaxies exist in the observable universe, but we would have no knowledge of knowing how many galaxies exist in the unobserved part of universe. Even extrapolating from the known to the unknown portions of the universe is impossible, because we have absolutely no knowledge of (including the size of) the unobservable portion of the universe, and conventional extrapolation techniques based on classic logic require such knowledge. The utility of nonstandard logic, extending our knowledge into situations as this, will require us to reevaluate some of the logical premises that we take for granted.

In the social sciences, and particularly in economics, it is traditional to assume that there is a single observer with full knowledge (the idea of a social welfare function is consistent with this notion), or that there are multiple observers who see the world in the same way and have the same information, that is the notion of homogeneous agents. Borrill and Tesfasion state, regarding the theory of relational quantum dynamics (RQM), “there are no observer dependent states... The distinction between observed and observed, cause and effect, are replaced by a symmetric notion of mutual observers interacting with each other... Moreover, physics shares with the social sciences the need to account for multiple observers with multiple perspectives on reality where measurements necessarily entail perturbative interaction (information flow) traces” (Borrill and Tesfasion, 2011, pp. 244–245). This basic concept is quite similar to what Soros (Smolin, 2001, pp. 31–32) describes in his theory of reflexivity, which essentially assumes a kind of positive feedback process in which one observer makes a decision on the basis of what they observe another observer doing, in turn, influencing the original observer. This kind of positive feedback system can explain bubbles, or antubbles, in financial markets and suggests that market participants are just that, participants who are unavoidably part of an evolving and coevolving self-referential kind of process. There is no objective reality outside of the market, because everyone participating is inside the market’s frame of reference.

The observer in the universe, as we have noted, is limited by the speed of light, so different observers will draw different conclusions from different facts. The observer in the world is limited by the behavior of others around them and the continually evolving interactions with their human and physical environment. We think of those in the hard sciences as observing and measuring the systems they observe; they are “outside the frame.” (Rovelli, 1996), physics cannot be complete until it confronts the problems posed by having multiple observers embedded in the universe (or world where measurements are local and relative to each other and whose interactions can potentially alter its course (Borr and Tesfatsion, 2011). Indeed, quantum physics long ago reconciled itself to the idea with the critical insight that one cannot know simultaneously both the location and momentum of a particle. One can know one or the other, but the more one tries to know one variable, the greater the uncertainty regarding the other. We agree, as Borrill and Tesfatsion (2011), some of the lessons drawn from physics should be applicable to our efforts to better understand the observer-observed relationship in the behavioral and social sciences. The notion that there may not necessarily be a single GEV perspective regarding all phenomena has support in areas of research ranging from quantum physics to cognitive science. Fuchs’ (2014, 2010) research into quantum Bayesian approaches to reality provide new insights. The traditional assumption of quantum theory is that a wave function describes the complete state of some aspects of some, and the shape of the wave function essentially encodes the probabilities of the outcomes any observer chooses to make. Whereas the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum behavior requires there be an observer representing a GEV of the world, Fuchs and his colleagues interpret the wave function as Bayesian probabilities or subjective degrees of belief about the system. According to Quantum Bayesianism (QBism), the wave function’s collapse is the observer updating her beliefs after taking a measurement. Fuchs (2014, 2010) treats the wave function as one’s subjective knowledge about the world, still much grounded in the traditional notion of a third person observer. One might consider certain analogs to Fuchs’ quantum Bayesianism as being applicable to social interactions with multiple observers.

The cognitive scientist David Hoffman suggests, controversially, that the traditional assumption that our perceptions represent a reasonably accurate, although not perfect, representation of the world has to be discarded. Otherwise, so goes the argument, evolution would have prevented the human race from achieving its current status because of its inability to adapt to the environment. Evolution, so it is argued, must produce organisms whose perception of environment matches reasonably well with reality. Hoffman rejects this argument, stressing that it assumes a strong correspondence between truth and fitness, when in fact truth, objective facts have little to do with or not the organism is fit from an evolutionary standpoint. For Hoffman, what describes the world are conscious agents, all who interact with each other. There is no single, with a first-person point of view, objective third-person perspective. This “conscious realism” is based upon a perspective that says the idea that we as individuals are capable of measuring, or perceiving, the same object at the same time in the same place and get the same results is simply wrong, and runs counter to everything learned in quantum physics over the last several decades (Hoffman, 2008, 2014; Hoffman and Singh, 2012).
