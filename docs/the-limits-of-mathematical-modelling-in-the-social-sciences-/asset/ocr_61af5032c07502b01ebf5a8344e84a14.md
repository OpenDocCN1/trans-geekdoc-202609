# Shocked Disbelief

Citing Wolpert (1996), Kopp and Rosser (2002, pp. 354-358) show that his results about the limits of prediction apply to social science as well. Wolpert (2001, pp. 016128-3 and 016128-14) notes that his results are robust to the interpretation of the universe in his analysis; they require only that the computers considered exist within the system in question.

The model of the universe that Wolpert uses to get his unpredictability is so broad that it “does not rely on temporal ordering of events.” Thus, unpredictability also shows that “no computer can infallibly predict the past” either (Wolpert, 2001, p. 016128-1, emphasis in original). This limit, in turn, implies that memory is fallible. Now can there be “an infallible general purpose observation device” or “an infallible general purpose control device” (p. 016128-2).

Wolpert’s analysis would seem to have an intriguing theological implication. If God intervenes in the world actively, then God is not omniscient. If God is omniscient and unerrring, it is only because he does not interact with the world he created. This theological insight raises a political question. If we imagine a personal god to be fallible, how much faith can we place in human regulators, lawgivers, and overlords?

Wolpert’s concept of “god computer” helps to reveal limits to error correction. He defines a god computer as “any physical computer in a set of computers such that all other computers in that set are predictable to the god computer.” Any set of (“pairwise-distinguishable”) computers can have at most one god computer. A god computer is omniscient if it can predict not merely any other individual in the set, but all of simultaneously (Wolpert, 2001, p. 016128-10). Notice that an omniscient god computer is “omniscient” only with respect to the outputs of other computers in the set. One might expect that a cooperative combination of fallible computers might be able to achieve perfection through error correction. This does not seem to be the case, however.
