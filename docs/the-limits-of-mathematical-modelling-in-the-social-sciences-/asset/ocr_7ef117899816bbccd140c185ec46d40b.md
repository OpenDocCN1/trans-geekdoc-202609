+   The group of gauge transformations $\mathcal{G}$ is the subgroup of all diffeomorphisms of $P(M, G)$ that reduce to a diffeomorphism on $M$ and to the group action on the fiber.
If $\ell(G)$ is the Lie algebra of $G$, we get:
- Connection-form, or the space of potentials, $\mathcal{A}$, is the space of all $C^k$-cross-sections of the bundle of $\ell(G)$-valued 1-forms on $M$.
 Curvature space, or the space of fields $\mathcal{F}$, is the space of all $C^k$-cross-sections of $\ell(G)$-valued 2-forms on $M$, such that $F \in \mathcal{F}$ is the field with potential $A \in \mathcal{A}$.
- Source space $\mathcal{I}$ coincides with $\mathcal{A}$, but is acted upon a different way by the group $\mathcal{G}$ of gauge transformations. (Currents in $\mathcal{I}$ are tensorial 1-forms, while gauge-potentials in $\mathcal{A}$ are transformed by an inhomogeneous transformation.)

+   - The space of physically different fields is $\mathcal{K}/\mathcal{G}$, where $\mathcal{K}$ is any of the above fields spaces.
- Dynamics are given by the usual gauge-field equations, which are a nonlinear version of the electromagnetic field equations. There is also a Dirac-like equation for gauge fields (see Doria et al., 1986). Or, again, we can start from a variational principle.

To sum up with the help of the schema presented at the beginning of the section, we can say that the structure of a physical theory is an ordered pair $(\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{G})$, where $\mathcal{F}$ is an infinite-dimensional space of fields, and $\mathcal{G}$ is an infinite-dimensional group that acts upon field space. To about the Suppes predicate, we must add the information equations $D(\phi)=0, \phi \in \mathcal{F}$, for the fields $\phi$.

Notice that general relativity can be seen as a kind of degenerate gauge field theory, more precisely a gauge theory of the $O(3,1)$ group.
