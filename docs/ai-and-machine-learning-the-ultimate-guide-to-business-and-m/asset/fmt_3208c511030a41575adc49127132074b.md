
# The first feature is that the resolution method works with applications - with quantifier-free disjunctions of literals

These disjunctions have no direction. In fact, the order in which the letters appear in a sentence is very important because all algorithms linearly scan and analyze the information in the order in which the letters appear in the formulas. This order can be important.

If we compare the solution method with the usual proof method based on the modus ponens rule, then this rule itself contains an explicit reasoning. The modus ponens rule looks like this:

```
A , A → B
──────────
B
```

It is clear that in this case you must start with `A`, and then we come to `B`. There is an explicit reasoning given by the arrow itself. But if we eliminate the implication arrows when reducing formulas to propositions, this direction can be lost.

**Example:**

```
(¬A ∧ ¬B) → C    A ∨ B ∨ C
(¬B ∧ ¬C) → A    A ∨ B ∨ C
```

The above are two completely different implicative rules reflecting different semantics, and after converting them into sentences, this difference disappeared. The resolution method doesn't know where to start and where to go. It has all the letters the same; it just sorts them mechanically, tries to deduce an empty sentence, but in which direction to move in order to output an empty sentence - this information is not available, it is lost, although it may have been present in the original formulation of the problem.

Setting up a problem for an automatic proof system is the job of an expert filling the knowledge base. He knows which way to use the implication and could prompt the system. For example, an expert may know that instead of a direct rule `¬A → B` should use the contrapositive form `¬B → A`. But when they are reduced to sentences, clues are forgotten. The resolution method only sees Formula `A ∨ B`. This is the first disadvantage of the resolution method. This property is called **loss of implicitivity**.

## 4.4.3. Propagation of literals

The second disadvantage of this method is the following. When converting into sentences, we use the subjunctive form, and at the same time the formula can be expanded, that is, the total number of occurrences of predicates in formulas can increase. In other words, what is known as **literal multiplication** can occur. With the resolution method it can turn out (and turns out in the worst case) that the coefficient of expansion observed here is exponential in nature.

**Example:**

> Vlasenko is a distinguished student in group 5057.
>
> All who study in this group are students, all students know the solving method.
>
> High performers are smart.
>
> Let's try to show the following fact: There is a student who knows the solving method and is either a programmer or a smart guy.

We introduce the corresponding predicates and write down the formulas.

| # | Formula | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | `∃x (D(x) ∧ (P(x) ∨ U(x)))` | Target |
| 1 | `5057(Vlasenko)` | Fact |
| 2 | `O(Vlasenko)` | Fact |
| 3 | `∀x 5057(x) → C(x)` | Rule |
| 4 | `∀x C(x) → P(x)` | Rule |
| 5 | `∀x O(x) → U(x)` | Rule |

Let's turn formulas into sentences. According to the rule of the resolution method, a negation must be attributed to the target in front. In this assignment, the first statement is split in two, and the predicate `P`, which was present once in the original version, appears twice after the transformation. Now let's start with the resolution.

```
1) ¬P(x) ∨ ¬D(x)
2) ¬P(x) ∨ ¬U(x)
3) ¬O(x) ∨ D(x)
4) ¬5057(x) ∨ C(x)
5) 5057(Vlasenko)
6) ¬O(x) ∨ U(x)
7) O(Vlasenko)
8) ¬P(x) ∨ ¬C(x)          (1, 3)
9) ¬P(x) ∨ 5057(x)        (8, 4)
10) ¬P(x)                 (9, 5)
11) ¬U(x) ∨ ¬C(x)         (2, 3)
12) ¬U(x) ∨ ¬5057(x)      (11, 4)
13) ¬U(Vlasenko)          (12, 5)
14) ¬O(Vlasenko)          (13, 6)
15) ⊥                     (14, 7)
```

Points 8, 9, and 10 are deductions, resolutions obtained in the simplest way. We take the first two matching sets and start solving; points 8, 9, and 10 in this example are the first matching, and it's easy to figure out that we don't need the resulting `¬P(x)` at all. This result is then not used anywhere. Programmers were not mentioned at all in the source data. This is a waste of machine time and memory running unneeded output. Step 11 (resolution of sentences 2 and 3) had to be started immediately; sentence 1 did not have to be taken into account. The atomic formula `¬P(x)` that appeared in Step 1 resulted from the multiplication of literals and of course proved detrimental to solving the problem.

The conclusion is built, but along the way there was a false move caused by the multiplication of literals that follows from the conversion to conjunctive normal form taking place. As a result, the number of occurrences of literals in sentences increased and the output lengthened. In the worst case, such an increase can be exponential.

## 4.4.3. Natural withdrawal direction

Let's look at the same problem from the other side. We will not reduce the original formulas to propositions and apply the resolution rule, but we will apply the usual modus ponens rule. We omit the proof by contradiction, but instead use the direct deduction. This makes everything much better, easier, and faster:

| # | Formula | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `∃x D(x) ∧ (P(x) ∨ U(x))` | Target |
| 2 | `5057(Vlasenko)` | Fact |
| 3 | `O(Vlasenko)` | Fact |
| 4 | `∀x 5057(x) → C(x)` | Rule |
| 5 | `∀x C(x) → P(x)` | Rule |
| 6 | `∀x O(x) → U(x)` | Rule |
| 7 | `C(Vlasenko)` | Modus Ponens 2, 4 |
| 8 | `P(Vlasenko)` | Modus Ponens 7, 5 |
| 9 | `U(Vlasenko)` | Modus Ponens 3, 6 |
| 10 | `P(Vlasenko) ∨ U(Vlasenko)` | Rule ∨+, 8 |
| 11 | `D(Vlasenko) ∧ (P(Vlasenko) ∨ U(Vlasenko))` | Rule &+, 7, 9 (Note: Original text says 7.9, likely 7 & 9) |
| 12 | `∃x D(x) ∧ (P(x) ∨ U(x))` | Rule ∃+, 11 |

Steps 2 and 4 of modus ponens give `C(Vlasenko)`, 7 and 5 give `P(Vlasenko)`, 3 and 6 give `U(Vlasenko)` and apply rules that have no analogue in the method of resolution, but which are apparently consistent; we get the goal. We see that the direct system (ordinary reasoning and not a method of proof by contradiction) is simple reasoning according to the modus ponens rule, and the introduction of additional connectors much faster than in the first case allows you to get the same answers.

Here lies the main idea. We want to keep the advantages that the representation of knowledge by sentences offers us and eliminate the disadvantages that this representation gives us. We want to get rid of literal multiplication and keep the direction of implication so that we can make efficient and short derivations as in the second case compared to inefficient and long derivations in the first case.

## 4.4.4. AND/OR Form

Let's try to convert the original formulas with which our knowledge of the subject is written (arithmetic formulas of the first order) not into the form of sentences, but into the so-called **AND/OR form**, by simply carrying out fewer conversion steps. Just as when reducing to sentences in the resolution method we eliminate the implication, pull in the negation, eliminate the existential quantifier, construct the prenex form, eliminate the universal quantifier, and separate the variables in the main conjunctions (see paragraph 0).

As you have noticed, the subjunctive phase has been skipped. Whatever form it was, that form has remained; we just got rid of quantifiers and separated the variables. It is easy to see that the number of literals is conserved as a result of eliminating the implication, drawing the negation, eliminating the existential quantifier, constructing the prenex form, eliminating universality, and renaming variables.
