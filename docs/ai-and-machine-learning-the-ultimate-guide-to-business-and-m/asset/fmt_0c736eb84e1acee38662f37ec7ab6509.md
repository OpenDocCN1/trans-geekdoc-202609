
Note that the AND/OR form can be obtained from any predicate calculus formula.

Example:

The formula is given (Fig. 49):

`∃ u ∀ v (Q(v, u) ∧ ¬((R(v) ∨ P(v)) ∧ S(u, v)))`.

In order to use the AND/OR form, it is proposed to represent the formula in the form of a hypergraph as follows. Where the main operation is `∧`, two separate 1-arcs are placed; where the main operation is `∨`, a 2-arc is placed. The justification is very simple. If `A ∧ B` is known, you can take the argument one way by using what is given to `A`, and you can go the other way by using what is given to `B`. If given `A ∨ B`, we don't know which direction to move concretely; we have to move in both directions at the same time.

`∃ u ∀ v (Q(v, u) ∧ ¬((R(v) ∨ P(v)) ∧ S(u, v))) Q(w, A) ∧ ((¬R(v) ∧ ¬P(v)) ∨ ¬S(A, v))`

![Hypergraph representations in AND/OR form](Images/image00370.jpeg)

**Figure 49. Hypergraph representations in AND/OR form**

Obviously, the set of propositions into which the root formula is transformed can be read as the set of terminal vertices of all solution graphs lying on this hypergraph (Figure 49). There are three solution graphs on this hypergraph. One goes from the root to `Q`; the second goes from the root to the next level and then needs to be connected to `S` since there is a 2-arc. We are interested in terminal nodes. If the original form is reduced to sentences, there are three of them: `Q`, `¬R ∨ ¬S`, `¬P ∨ ¬P`.

Note this circumstance: the AND/OR form stores the same information that we had in the solution method, as well as other information.

Consider the following way of constructing a proof engine—the core of an intelligent inference system in the form of a rule-based deduction system.

### 4.4.5. Direct Trigger System

As is usual in logic programming, we need to divide the entire set of formulas—all the information we have on a given topic—into three classes: facts, rules, and goals.

This results in the following syntactic restrictions:

*   What we consider facts is actually not limited by anything and can be presented in any form AND/OR;
*   What we consider rules must necessarily be of the form `L → W`, where `L` is a literal and `W` is any formula represented in the form AND/OR;
*   The target must be a disjunction of literals, that is, it must be a sentence. In other words, one can formulate a termination condition: the target formula is a sentence, the solution graph ends in the target literals.

The meaning of the method of growing the AND/OR graph using rules is simple. Let's take a fact (or set of facts) and represent it in AND/OR form to get a hypergraph. Let's start by attaching rules to the leaf nodes of this diagram. Each leaf node of the constructed graph is unified with the left-hand side of the rule. If the unification is successful, we grow the graph (or tree) AND/OR around the right-hand side of the rule until a decision graph appears in this graph whose leaves fall in the goal nodes (i.e., unify with goal literals). If the left side of the rule is identified with a leaf node in the diagram, some substitutions may need to be made to unify. It is clear that when different rules are applied in different decision columns, they are applied independently. But in a solution graph, it is impossible to replace the same variable with different terms at the same time. Such cases should therefore be ruled out. In fact, the mechanism now described is a special case of the resolution method performed using graph rebuilding.

Let's look at this with an example.

**Fact:** Vlasenko is smart and knows the solution method or he is not a student:
`¬S(V) ∨ (R(V) ∧ U(V))`.

**Rule 1:** All students in group 5057 are students: `¬C(x) → ¬5057(x)`.

**Rule 2:** He who knows the method of dissolution is an excellent student:
`P(y) → O(y)`.

**Objective:** There is someone who is either not studying in group 5057 or is an excellent student:
`∃z (¬5057(z) ∨ O(z))`.

The example of Rule 1 shows the advantage of a direct rule-based system. The designer of the knowledge representation system for a specific department can write down this rule—"all students in group 5057 are students"—in a contrapositive form: "If he is not a student, then he is not studying in group 5057". Therefore, the designer of the knowledge representation system (human) gives the inference program a hint: in deductive systems it is more profitable to infer from the general to the particular, and not from the particular to the general. That is exactly what Rule 1 does. That is why staying implicit is so important and useful.

We build direct deductive reasoning and do not use the method of proof by contradiction. Unlike the resolution method, negation should not be applied to the target. The goal should be used as it is in the problem statement. In this case, it is clear that after Skolemization the goal will be a quantifier-free disjunction of literals, which is required in the syntactic condition of the direct deduction system.

The rationale is as follows (Figure 50).

![Extending a form AND/OR applying rules](Images/image00371.jpeg)

**Figure 50. Extending a form AND/OR applying rules**

Initially, the fact graph has the following list of leaf nodes: `¬C(B)`, `P(B)`, and `Y(B)`. We use our Rule 1 to grow the tree. The left-hand side of the rule is unified with the "B" substitution for `x`. The tree is incremented by the right side that is merged with the first target literal. This isn't a solution yet because we haven't created a decision graph that ends with target literals yet. So we continue to apply the rules. Rule 2 is triggered and the result is identified with the second target literal.

A decision graph is created ending with goal literals (circled in the figure). In order to save information about the substitutions, we introduce suitable arcs (in the figure they are represented by thick unfilled arrows) connecting the literals to be replaced and the left parts of the rules.

Let's consider another example.

Given a fact: `P(x) ∨ Q(x)`

**Rule 1:** `P(A) → R(A)`

**Rule 2:** `Q(B) → R(B)`, where `A` and `B` are concrete constants.

**Target:** `R(A) ∨ R(B)`

Note that the goal is not a logical consequence of the facts. This example explains the meaning of the condition that the substitutions on the matching arcs in the same decision graph must be consistent (Figure 51).

It can be seen that the replacements are incompatible: you have to replace `A` with `x` and `B` with `x` at the same time. As a result, the substitutions are not consistent; the decision graph is not constructed as it should be because the goal is not a logical consequence of facts and rules.

The example discussed in the previous section was quite instructive. We have performed resolving inference in the technique of graphs, but the applicability of this technique is limited by the fact that we have syntactic restrictions on the form of facts, syntactic restrictions on the form of rules, and syntactic restrictions on the form of goals.

If `f(x₁, …, xₙ)` is a boolean function, then the boolean function `f*(x₁, …, xₙ) = ¬f(¬x₁, …, ¬xₙ)` is called the dual Boolean function.
