The above example is formalized as follows.

Let α be the lower bound on the value of the score at a given node, β be the upper bound on the value of the score at that node.

Note that the value of α for the Max node cannot decrease and the value of β for Min cannot increase as the nodes are opened. Therefore, the following rules apply.

1. Min node if its upper bound β is less than or equal to the lower bound α for any of its Max parents.
2. Max node if its lower bound α is greater than or equal to the upper bound β for any of its min parents.

Please note that the formula for the cutoff inequality to be tested is formally the same: in the first case β ≤ α , in the second case α ≥ β , but these formulas have different meanings.

In the first case, we take the estimate β just obtained for the node Min and compare it with the estimates α for the nodes Max above it . In the second case, we take the estimate α just obtained for node Max and compare it with the estimates β for the nodes Min above it . But in both cases, the fulfillment of the inequality is the basis for truncation.

So we see that the order in which positions are generated and the order in which moves are considered are one of the key factors in programming intellectual games. Unfortunately (or maybe fortunately?) there are no general methods of shunting trains. The enumeration methods in all game programs are roughly the same and similar to those described (published a long time ago and well studied), but the heuristics of the order of considering moves for each game are different (and constitute the author's know-how of the game program).

Practice shows that the strength of the game program is directly proportional to the search depth. For example, a beginner chess player rarely scans a position more than three or four moves ahead. This. The depth of the search tree for a beginner is about 3. Chess masters build a tree in their mind for a dozen half-moves, and if a forced variation is planned, then more. Grandmasters and champions see even further, two dozen half moves. Since they are in the middlegame, they can already predict the endgame.

Modern chess programs, taking advantage of the dramatically increased capabilities of computers, build and evaluate huge game trees, including millions of positions. How come sometimes people still don't lose chess against computers? [\[59\]](#part0005.xhtml#_ftn59)The fact is that a human chess player builds a much narrower tree in his head. In a complex chess position, a strong chess player immediately sees 2-3 best moves out of two dozen possible moves and only analyzes and calculates them in detail. And a chess program discards on average only half of the possible moves , even with the most well-known tricks like α - β cutting . If we conditionally assume that the human branch width in the game tree is constant and equal to 2, and the program is constant and equal to 10, then to search to a depth of 10 using the minimax method, the person must look through 2^11 = 2048 positions and the program 10^11 = 100,000,000,000 items.
