
## 2.1.1 Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs)

The concept of Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) emerged in the late 1970s, originating from the idea of combining both *quantity* and *quality of life* by Joseph S. Pliskin, then it became a widely used metric for evaluating the *cost-effectiveness of healthcare interventions*. One QALY corresponds to one year in perfect health, which ranges from 1 (perfect health) to 0 (dead). However, this metric, in various sectors such as health insurance, is criticised due to its lack of addressing disparities among individuals. It may result in discrimination against individuals with specific medical conditions or disorders.

A controversial outcome emerged after 25 years of research and studies, raising concerns about the metric's validity and inclusivity. Started in the early 1980s, the step in 2010 with the European Commission¹⁰ which started the largest-ever study dedicated to testing the assumptions of the QALY.¹¹¹ ending in 2013 with the recommendation "not using QALYs in healthcare decision making", arguing that patients with disabilities are valued less under a QALY-based system than individuals with no disabilities. This result has been further criticised and remains a topic of debate.

> “Medicine cannot itself determine the quality of life. It can only help people to achieve the state of health that enables them to cultivate the art of life—but in their own way. This implies that the ability to enjoy the fundamental satisfactions of the biological vie. It implies the ability for each person to do what he wants and become what he wants to, according to human values transcendent judgement” D. Dubes¹²

Used in health to measure the value of medical interventions by balancing both the quantity and quality of life, QALYs help decision-makers determine which treatments or programs offer the greatest health benefits relative to their costs. For example, a health policy might prioritising treatments that deliver high QALYs per dollar, ensuring resources are allocated to maximise outcomes across the population. This approach guides decisions in healthcare budgeting, insurance coverage, and prioritising interventions for chronic illnesses, preventive care, or new therapies.

DALYs are connected with QALYs in that metrics seek to quantify the impact of health quality and length of life. However, while QALYs focus on the benefits with and without medical intervention, DALYS measure the overall burden of diseases.

The D metric is calculated by summing the **years of life lost (YLLs)** due to premature death and the **years lived with disabilities (YLDs)**. This metric provides a comprehensive measure of a population's health status by highlighting both mortality and disability. For example, consider a population with a life expectancy of 80 years. Individuals who die before reaching this age contribute to the YLLs, while those living with disabilities add to the YLDs. The sum of these values gives the total DALYs for the population, which can then be compared globally to assess health status and identify areas needing improvement in facilities, research, and investment.

## 2.1.3 Health-Adjusted Life Years (HALY)

Adjusted Life Years (HALY) is a metric that combines both of DALYs and QALYs to provide a comprehensive measure of health outcomes in a population. HALYs quantify the burden of disease and the overall quality of life experienced by individuals, measuring the number of years of healthy life lived by a population accounting for both mortality and morbidity. The impact of these conditions on individuals' quality of life is incorporated in the calculation of HALYs.

HALYs adjust expectancy levels based on the prevalence of health conditions and the associated disability. Weights reflect the severity of different health conditions and their impact on daily functioning. By applying these weights to the prevalence of each condition, researchers can estimate the overall burden of disease in terms of years healthy life lost, similar to the calculation of DALYs. Unlike DALYs, which focus on mortality, HALYs provide a more nuanced view of health.

## 2.4 Health-Adjusted Life Years (HALE)

Healthy Life Expectancy (HALE)¹⁷ is a measure of health that combines both quantity and quality of life. It specifically focuses on the number of years an individual can expect to live in good health, rather than simply the total number of years lived. HALE provides a more nuanced view of health than traditional life expectancy, accounting for the prevalence of conditions.

HALE is a useful tool for health practitioners and policy makers. This information can help public health interventions and prioritize resources, as well as track changes in health outcomes over time. Overall, the HALE metric provides a valuable perspective on the overall health and well-being of a population, is calculated by subtracting from life expectancy the amount of time lived less than perfect health.

## 2.5 Healthy Life Years (HLY)

There is one more health metrics that is worth mentioning, the Healthy Life Years (HLY), also known as Disability-Free Lifeancy (DFLE) or Sullivan's Index², assesses the number of years an individual can expect to live in good health, free from significant health problems or disabilities. It is a measure of the qualityadjusted expectancy, focusing on the years lived in full health rather than simply the total number of years lived. It takes into account both mortality and morbidity, providing a comprehensive view of overall health and well-being.

HLYs differs from HALE in that it focuses on the number of additional years a person can expect to live in good health after reaching a certain age, typically 65. This makes HLY relevant for assessing health in aging populations and evaluating interventions to improve the quality of life for older adults.
