The 20th century marked significant advancements in epidemiology, driving the expansion and refinement of health metrics. This period saw the widespread adoption of life tables and the modification of expected survival time to incorporate weighted metrics, such as disability-adjusted years (DALYs). These developments enabled more comprehensive assessments of population health by accounting for both mortality and the quality of life, laying the foundation for modern health evaluation tools.

The term Summary Measures of Population Health (SMPH)⁵ was established in the field, originating from the need to encapsulate both fatal and non-fatal health outcomes into a single comprehensive measure, representing a significant evolution in health metrics. Used for various purposes, the SMPH compares health of populations and includes the contributions of different diseases, injuries and risk factors to the total disease burden in a population. SMPH, also referred to as a composite indicator,⁶ is divided into two parts: health expectations and health gaps.

Health expectations are summary measures that cover information on mortality to represent the average number of years that an individual can live in good health. Such as healthy life expectancy (HALE) and the quality adjusted expectancy (QALE) using health-related quality of life.

Health gains, on the other hand, represent the difference between the current health status of a population and an ideal health status, providing a measure of the potential for improvement in health outcomes. Divided into the disability adjusted life years (DALYs)⁷ in the global burden of disease (GBD) and the quality adjusted life years (QALY)⁸ as the outcome index of cost-utility analysis.

In the contemporary era, the integration of computing power and big data has transformed health metrics. Today, we have complex models, machine learning algorithms, and global health databases for real-time tracking of diseases and health outcomes. John Grant’s work has been the bedrock of a scientific approach to understanding population health. From Grant’s early analysis of the Bills of Mortality⁹ to the development of today’s advanced health metrics, this journey reflects the ongoing effort to understand and manage the complexities of human health and disease.
