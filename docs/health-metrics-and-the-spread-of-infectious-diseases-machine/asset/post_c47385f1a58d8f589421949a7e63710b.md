## 2.1 The History of Health Metrics

The history of health metrics is a fascinating journey that spans centuries, evolving from rudimentary observations to sophisticated statistical methods, as illustrated by Figure 2.1.

One of the earliest forms of health metrics can be traced back to the work of John Graunt, an Englishman, in the 17th century². In 1662, Graunt published a landmark work titled “Natural and Public Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality.” Graunt, defined as the father of demography, was largely self-educated, and his interest in mortality data was driven by the Bills of Mortality, which were weekly and annual records of deaths in London, and provided information on the mortality data and produced tables that presented various patterns and trends. His notable contributions were the concepts of life expectancy and mortality rates. One of Graunt’s key observations were the consistent pattern of higher mortality among infants and young children. He noted the difference in life expectancy between males and females and provided insights into factors influencing mortality, such as epidemics and seasons.

Over time, advancements in mathematics, medicine, and statistics led to the development of more sophisticated health metrics. The 19th century saw the emergence of life tables, providing a systematic way to analyse mortality and life expectancy.

The concept of health metrics can be traced back to ancient civilizations where observations on life, experience, birth rates, and mortality were made. However, these were often anecdotal and lacked system.

In the 17th century, John Graunt, an English haberdasher, made significant contributions to health metrics in 1662, *Natural and Public Observations Made upon Deaths in London*. Graunt's work laid the foundations of demography, introducing expectancy and mortality rates.

The 19th century saw the development of life tables, a systematic approach to analysing mortality and life. This period marked a transition from descriptive observations to more structured records in health metrics.

QALYs, another important health metric, emerged in the late 20th century, combining both the quality and quantity of life commonly used in economics and healthcare decision-making.

A significant milestone in contemporary health is the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study started in 1990. J. L. Murray and Alan D. Lopez, this collaborative effort between Health organisation and World Bank aimed to quantify the impact of diseases and injuries on global health.

The GBD DALYs as a metric in 1990: years of life lost to disease and mortality years became more sophisticated through a comprehensive methodology. Real-time tracking, machine learning, and global databases contribute to a more dynamic understanding of population health.

With advancements in technology, including computing power and data analytics, health metrics have become more sophisticated. Three datasets track various social determinants of health.

Today, health metrics encompass a wide range of measures, traditional mortality rates, disease prevalence, and broader indicators. They play crucial roles in shaping public health policies, interventions, and global health assessments.

## The History of Health Metrics

More specifically, Mary Dempsey in 1940s wrote an article³ for the National Tuberculosis Association about the concept of Years of Life Lost (YLLS), assessing time-loss as a metric for the first time. She was then followed by other more advanced improvements spanning throughout the rest of the century.

> “The quality of life also depends on the scale of “public health”—namely on factors that affect the biological welfare of the community as a whole.”⁴

The 20th century marked significant advancements in epidemiology, driving the expansion and refinement of health metrics. This period saw the widespread adoption of life tables and the modification of expected survival time to incorporate weighted metrics, such as disability-adjusted years (DALYs). These developments enabled more comprehensive assessments of population health by accounting for both mortality and the quality of life, laying the foundation for modern health evaluation tools.

The term Summary Measures of Population Health (SMPH)⁵ was established in the field, originating from the need to encapsulate both fatal and non-fatal health outcomes into a single comprehensive measure, representing a significant evolution in health metrics. Used for various purposes, the SMPH compares health of populations and includes the contributions of different diseases, injuries and risk factors to the total disease burden in a population. SMPH, also referred to as a composite indicator,⁶ is divided into two parts: health expectations and health gaps.

Health expectations are summary measures that cover information on mortality to represent the average number of years that an individual can live in good health. Such as healthy life expectancy (HALE) and the quality adjusted expectancy (QALE) using health-related quality of life.

Health gains, on the other hand, represent the difference between the current health status of a population and an ideal health status, providing a measure of the potential for improvement in health outcomes. Divided into the disability adjusted life years (DALYs)⁷ in the global burden of disease (GBD) and the quality adjusted life years (QALY)⁸ as the outcome index of cost-utility analysis.

In the contemporary era, the integration of computing power and big data has transformed health metrics. Today, we have complex models, machine learning algorithms, and global health databases for real-time tracking of diseases and health outcomes. John Graunt’s work has been the bedrock of a scientific approach to understanding population health. From Graunt’s early analysis of the Bills of Mortality⁹ to the development of today’s advanced health metrics, this journey reflects the ongoing effort to understand and manage the complexities of human health and disease.
