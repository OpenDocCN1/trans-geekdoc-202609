## Institutional design elements

The institutional design of a DLT setup works in tandem with its technical configuration to form the whole. Typically, decisions must be made regarding what roles the various institutions will play in the arrangement. These decisions may include who will be responsible for its operation and who will have access to it.

## Functioning of the arrangement

An important aspect of the institutional design of a DLT arrangement is determining which entity or entities will be responsible for managing the arrangement. This includes the ability to modify or update the protocol and source codes, as well as the ability to grant access and assign permissions to other entities so that they can carry out specific roles. At one end of the spectrum, there is the possibility that a single entity might host and operate all of the nodes in an arrangement on behalf of the participants, and this entity would also be the only entity accountable for the upkeep of the ledger. Alternately, maintenance could be split throughout a large number of entities, with each entity being responsible for possessing a copy of the ledger and carrying out the responsibilities that have been prescribed.

### Access to the configuration of the arrangement (unrestricted or restricted)

The arrangements do not impose any restrictions on the maximum number of participants that may take part in the event. Unrestricted arrangements are those that are planned and built in such a way that there are no limitations placed on who can access the arrangement or what roles nodes can play within the arrangement. This is because there are no restrictions placed on the planning and construction of the arrangement. In environments in which access is available to absolutely everyone, it is quite unlikely that the entities who are in charge of running the nodes are acquainted with one another.

It could be difficult to exercise control over such arrangements and the whole set of rules that are supposed to regulate interactions between nodes need to be written primarily “on-ledger” (encoded in the computer protocol).

On the other hand, limited platforms make it possible to oversee and monitor participants’ access to the arrangement. This is because they prevent unauthorized access. Due to limited access, the rules that govern interactions may be kept “off the ledger” if it becomes essential to do so.

At the most basic level, an arrangement has to strike a balance between unrestricted and limited resource access. The need for tiered payment, clearing, and settlement processes may be reduced when unrestricted arrangements are used since they make it possible for new types of participants to take part. Due to the difficulties of achieving an agreement among a big number of unknown persons, unlimited access may result in scalability and security concerns. For participants to remain anonymous, the arrangement’s design and legislation need to include appropriate safety measures (that is, for these issues to be addressed “on-ledger”). These safety measures should protect against any criminal activity or cyberattacks. As a result of these problems, the vast majority of current DLT implementations for payment, clearing, and settlement are based on constrained arrangements that are meant to fit preexisting legal and regulatory frameworks.

The assignment of certain responsibilities to a large number of things and the connecting nodes between them may result in additional challenges. If just certain nodes are delegated in order to obtain a consensus, it is possible that this process will be simpler as well as more expedient. It’s possible that the ledger’s integrity may be compromised by any one of these nodes. It’s possible that reaching an agreement will be simpler (and take less time) if just a subset of the nodes is assigned to do so. Participants are required to recognize and acknowledge this organization as a reliable source. When additional nodes are added to a network, the network’s resilience may improve, but the latency may increase.

The more decentralized the responsibilities in DLT are, the more likely it is that governance, settlement, and operational risk management will be difficult. The selection of validation and consensus processes is determined by the access rules as well as the responsibilities that are specifically assigned to entities and nodes.

A solution-in-search-of-a-problem approach will not be adequate to handle payment, clearing, and settlement difficulties due to the vast number of ways DLT might be implemented.
