All transactions, as well as account balances, are recorded in ledgers. A blockchain is a distributed ledger that records transactions that have already taken place. When blockchain technology is used, the information about transactions is stored in blocks. Following the validation of a block, that block will then be linked to all of the ledger transactions that came before it.

One kind of distributed ledger is known as a blockchain, although not all of them use to block or chain transactions. There are several categories of distributed ledgers. One alternative is to use the more conventional accounting method, which entails solely altering the account balances.

More than only the ownership of assets could be recorded in a ledger depending on the circumstances. The provisions of a financial contract, or a copy of the contract itself, may be stored on a distributed ledger.

It is permissible to keep either the original contract or a copy. Certain DLTs make it possible to create “automated contract tools.” Users can automate their contractual commitments by embedding code on the ledger, which is made possible by these technologies. This includes the payment of interest and principal on specific dates, the collection or distribution of cash in response to defined events, and the automated termination of contracts according to the terms outlined in such contracts. This kind of agreement is referred to as a “smart contract” in the commercial world.

### Making necessary corrections to the ledger

The fact that several nodes are collectively responsible for maintaining the ledger’s most recent version is one of the most noticeable aspects of DLT. These nodes can be dispersed throughout a broad number of places, institutions, and even nations where it is lawful to do so, as will be shown in the next section.

### Validation, in addition to the overall consensus

In order to bring a synchronized distributed ledger up to date, an arrangement will typically use several protocols for communication between nodes and for facilitating consensus among nodes regarding the current state of the ledger as well as its historical record.

These protocols will be used to facilitate communication between nodes and will also be used to facilitate consensus among nodes. This is done to make upgrading process easier to complete.

- **Cryptography**: Cryptographic techniques, such as public key cryptography and public key infrastructure play a crucial role in DLT. These tools are used to identify and authenticate authorized participants, validate data records, and facilitate agreement on ledger revisions. The use of public key cryptography is what enables this to become a reality. Participants who wish to make changes to the ledger must first authenticate their identities by submitting cryptographic digital signatures for the changes they intend to make. This is necessary in order for the ledger to be updated. Validators will verify, via the use of cryptographic technologies, to determine whether the participant owns the requisite credentials to do so. Validation will take place. Another method for limiting access to data and ensuring that only those who are allowed to do so are able to examine the information is to make use of cryptographic procedures.

- Consensus: The term “consensus mechanism” refers to the method by which the nodes in a network come to an agreement on the state of the ledger as a whole. This agreement may be reached in a variety of ways. In order to accomplish its goals, this strategy often makes use of cryptographic methods, a set of rules or procedures that are encoded in the protocol, and either financial incentives (which are applicable to any network configuration) or governance systems. The process of arriving to a consensus often consists of two steps, which are as follows:

- Validation: it is the responsibility of each validator to determine whether or not a state change is in accordance with the criteria that have been established for the arrangement (that is, assets are available to the originator, and the originator and beneficiary are entitled to exchange the assets). In order to do this, each validator will need to depend on a record of states that have gone before it. This record may either take the form of a “last agreed state” or a “chain of preceding states.”

- Nodes: It must reach a consensus before making any changes to the ledger in order for the agreement to take effect. At this point in the process of reaching a consensus, the procedures or algorithms that reconcile conflicting modifications to the ledger. The primary obstacle is to ensure that legitimate modifications are performed once and only once, which can be accomplished by ensuring that the distributed ledger’s many nodes synchronize their respective state updates.

## The roles of nodes from a technical perspective

There is a wide range of possible technical functions that can be performed by nodes in the network. The following are some examples of those roles, none of which are exclusive to the others:

The system administrator is the gatekeeper who regulates access to the system and offers specific services for the arrangement. The administrator is also responsible for providing those services. These services include the role of a notary public, the arbitration of disputes, the establishment of standards, and the reporting of regulatory violations. In addition to this, the administrator of the system may provide certain services relevant to the arrangement.

- Asset issuer: A node that has been granted authorization to issue new assets.
- Proposer: It is a node that has been granted authorization to suggest improvements to the ledger.
- Validator: A node that has the authorization to verify the correctness of proposed state changes.
- Auditor: A node that is granted authority to see the ledger but not to make any changes to it.

Additionally, the capacity of particular nodes to read the records that are maintained on the ledger may vary from instance to instance. Even if it has permission to view the entire ledger, a node may only be able to view those transactions in which it is involved as a counterparty or in which one of its customers has a relevant interest. This is the case even if the node maintains a copy of the entire encrypted ledger and stores it locally.
