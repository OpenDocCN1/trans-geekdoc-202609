## Agreement mechanisms

The motivation behind the blockchain is to enable peer-to-peer transactions that are validated, grouped into blocks, and then stored in a distributed ledger. To achieve this objective, the blockchain is governed by decentralized consensus algorithms that decide how and when a set of transactions can be included in the ledger. Specifically, each new block can be appended to the blockchain only if most nodes in the network agree on its contents, that is, only if consensus among blockchain users is reached. Every node in the network maintains a local copy of the blockchain. When another block reaches consensus, it is broadcast.

## Computational aspects

Although Proof of Work (PoW) is a successful mechanism to achieve consensus, it requires overwhelming amounts of energy and computational power, which increase steadily as more and more miners and transactions are added to the blockchain. Therefore, other consensus mechanisms have been considered in many blockchain architectures. For example, Proof of Stake (PoS) systems use deterministic rules based on the amount of coins, i.e., the stake, to select which node in the network will append the next block to the blockchain. Similarly, Proof of Importance (PoI) considers the stake as an important measurement as well – however, it also accounts for measurements that measure the miner’s involvement in the network, such as number and volume of transactions.

## Security aspects

Overall, consensus algorithms ensure the reliability of a blockchain. However, there are situations where malicious users can exploit the blockchain structure to alter, copy, or delete blocks. Specifically, it is sufficient for an attacker to control more than half of the nodes in the network to take over the entire blockchain. This attack, also known as the 51% attack, aims to compromise the consensus mechanism to control a blockchain. These attacks have been shown to be successful against numerous minor cryptocurrencies, such as Verge, Bitcoin Gold, and Zencash; however, they have also compromised even widespread cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin. Double spending, which consists of duplicating one or more transactions, is the main reason for 51% attacks. However, it has been shown that double spending can be achieved even without reaching the 50% threshold.

## Centralized systems

In centralized systems, users rely on authority to conduct transactions. For example, in banks, clients rely on a financial system that updates the user's account balances after making transactions. In a unified system, the central authority can modify the entire system by directly changing and updating databases at the back end. Centralized services do not allow the distribution of power and thus are single service providers. Online payments, cloud systems, governments, and courts are other examples of centralized systems. Using these systems has established ways of utilizing the essential properties of a good system, including integrity, transparency, community, and trust.

## Need for decentralized systems

The basic idea behind the use of decentralized frameworks is to provide a fault-tolerant distributed computing system where authority can be distributed without trusting a central system. This guarantees various properties such as trust, transparency, data integrity, etc., to provide an openly available system and achieve interoperability; the need for blockchain is growing. It enables the building of decentralized applications and distributed software infrastructures for countless untrusted participants. The problem with the centralized system is that it is prone to single-point failure and the system does not provide transparency, fair access to resources, integrity, non-repudiation of transactions performed, and data immutability.

## Blockchain with IoT

The combination of blockchain and IoT has disruptive potential. Surely, blockchain can help IoT's entry into our society by providing the following key benefits:

### Anonymity

IoT entities can participate in the blockchain with a public/private key, which (if desired) does not in itself reveal the true identity of the entity.

### Decentralization

Traditional centralized systems require every transaction to be approved through a central authority (e.g., a central bank) – which inevitably becomes a performance bottleneck. Conversely, third-party approval is no longer required in the blockchain, since consensus algorithms maintain data consistency.

### Non-repudiation

The blockchain guarantees that:

- Transactions can be easily approved; and
- Invalid transactions are not admitted – it is almost impossible to delete or roll back transactions once included in the blockchain
