This chapter presents a methodology for evaluating and analyzing DLT arrangements in the conceptual, experimental, or operational stages by central banks and other authorities to comprehend use cases and find any risks. The findings may also aid market participants. The framework focuses on efficiency, safety, and financial market effects. # Technology based upon distributed ledger

The term “Distributed Ledger Technology” is used in various ways, reflecting the ever-changing nature of technology, business, and new applications. In this chapter, “Distributed Ledger Technology” (or DLT) refers to the processes and related technologies that allow nodes inside a network (or arrangement) to reliably propose, validate, and record state changes (or updates) to a synchronized, distributed ledger. DLT enables payment, clearing, and settlement transactions without a central authority maintaining a single “golden copy” of the ledger. This is made possible through the utilization of established procedures and protocols made available by DLT.

The word “arrangement” is used throughout the paper to refer to any application or implementation built on distributed ledger technology. It is possible to characterize an arrangement by referring to its technological design and the institutional structure it possesses. The industry has only a limited amount of standardization when it comes to nomenclature; some of the developing arrangements include systems, platforms, and layers. A self-contained system can function without outside help. A platform is another type of organization in which programs are built on a common base to share functionality. Other technology implementations may act as a layer, facilitating interconnectivity across setups.

# Historical content

Satoshi Nakamoto published a paper in 2008 outlines Bitcoin’s mechanics and a peer-to-peer solution for internet transfers without known and trusted third parties. This method permitted user-to-user internet transfers. The solution integrates several tried-and-true technologies into a single system to validate transactions and add them to a “block.” Following a predetermined set of procedures and rules, this block, also known as a batch of transactions, is appended to a chain that contains a history of transactions and is referred to as a blockchain. The newly created block is then communicated to the network so that all of the nodes can converge on the same version of the blockchain and update their own ledgers. This process of agreement across nodes is known as consensus, and it entails cryptographically tying the new block to the block that came before it in the blockchain.
