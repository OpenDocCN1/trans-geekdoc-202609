# # Discussions

While BC's significance is being widely discussed, actual realistic implementations are still under development. A lot of focus has been on the technology itself rather than adoption and selection difficulties. BC adoption should occur when a given blockchain technology meets requirement, such as transparency, immutability, ledger privacy, reliability, and safety. Transaction-based costs can be reduced, new services added, organizational boundaries can be defined, and decision-making can be decentralized using value drivers.

The advantages of implementing blockchain technology are inextricably related to the incentives for value generation. For example, decentralized and distributed ledger systems cannot ensure data immutability for property registration or personal identification since stakeholders in the latter case can follow changes made. Increasing service innovation and value generation levels requires management to select where their companies fit into the blockchain technology development lifecycle. As pointed out, “structural changes in how value is created and appropriated within a given market take time”. Consider the dangers of blockchain adoption and start small when integrating it into existing or new processes and offers. When it comes to transactions with a minimal digital footprint, blockchain is the best option. The implementation of BC in the healthcare business may enhance medical and pharmaceutical supply chain management, as well as dynamic patient consent, data exchange, and access permission management. There are numerous advantages to preserving actors’ privacy through the immutability, greater transparency, and traceability of data. By distributing information to other nodes when one is attacked, distributed data exchange improves a system’s ability to withstand any conceivable cyber-attack.

# # Contributions

First, the chapter provides a roadmap for successful BC adoption in sustainable health care by defining the important elements and determining how to interact with the patient population. A key contribution of this chapter is to offer propositions that link institutional, technological, and environmental issues for blockchain adoption. These assertions are informed by a variety of organizational theories, such as force field, stakeholder, resource-based view, and relational and institutional theories as well as the TEO as its whole. Incorporating theoretical underpinnings into the examination of organizational innovation adoption hurdles to better understand the challenges to blockchain technology adoption in health care management.
