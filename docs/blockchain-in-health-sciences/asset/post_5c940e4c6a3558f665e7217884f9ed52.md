### Abstract

Human genomic data possess distinctive information about an individual and present unparalleled prospects for healthcare. Genomics research has made remarkable advancements, offering unparalleled understanding of human genetic variation, vulnerability to diseases, and the categorization of risk. By providing clinical interpretations, large genomic databases may greatly improve healthcare and pave the way for the creation of individualized medicines. Projections suggest a substantial rise in the production of genomic data in the next years. Together, with the rapid progress in science, will give rise to novel social, ethical, and legal dilemmas. Sharing genomic datasets presents significant obstacles to the unique nature of genomic data. This type of data might greatly indirectly disclose information about the owner's offspring and relations, and it can retain valid information long after the owner's death. Consequently, strict rules for data ownership must be set up and strictly enforced when dealing with genomic information. In this intricate and interdependent setting, the transfer of genomic data gives rise to questions of responsibility, authenticity, and openness: blockchain technology offers a novel and innovative way to address these concerns. Enhanced personal control over the use and disclosure of genomic data is possible with the use of distributed ledger technology in conjunction with a database. With this tool, people can choose who gets their data and how they are used, giving them more agency over their privacy. To overcome the many challenges that come with sharing genetic data, lawmakers and data owners must work together to effectively deploy blockchain technology. A detailed explanation of genomic research and blockchain will be covered extensively in this chapter.

### 19.1 Introduction

Genomic research holds great potential to revolutionize medical treatment. A key component in the development of precision medicine, genomic data produced by high-throughput sequencing (HTS) technology can provide unique, non-invasive insights into people's health [1]. Estimates put the potential number of sequenced human genomes at 100 million and 2 billion by 2025 [2]. The challenges they pose are growing in tandem with the exponential growth of genomic data and genomic technology. Can you tell who has access to the data and what safeguards are in place to keep them safe? How can we use and share data responsibly so that researchers and patients in the future can reap the benefits of sharing? When dealing with genetic material, the importance of maintaining anonymity be considered alongside the necessity of sharing [3]. Some people believe that those who have benefited from genetic testing should be required by law to divulge this information to the public [4].

Genomic data possess distinct attributes that distinguish them from conventional health. Firstly, they have longevity as they retain valuable information even after the individual's demise. Secondly, they have an impact on the descendants and relatives of the data owner. Lastly, the size of genomic data is substantial, particularly when the entire genome is sequenced, with BAM files reaching a size of 138 GB [5]. Utilizing genomic data for patient diagnosis, screening, and treatment has significant benefits, despite the fact that improper use of genetic information might have far-reaching societal effects. Genetic data storage and dissemination [6] are the main technical issues highlighted in the literature. For genetic data acquisition, analysis, and exchange, there are no need for efficient methods, but most importantly, secure and privacy-preserving technological frameworks. Metadata describe the data and everything needed to access them, including their raw format and notations, but there are no universally accepted norms and conditions for this information. This creates another impediment. These issues hinder data exchange and reduce the efficacy of the process of discovering new information [7].

Blockchain is a decentralized technology that has the potential to revolutionize data management in the healthcare business, particularly in the field of genetic research [8]. The blockchain enables the storage and processing of extensive amounts of genetic data without relying on a single point of failure, thanks to the utilization of several nodes.

Quite few companies have seen the light and are using blockchain technology for this same reason. Integrating with a wide variety of analytical tools is made easy with blockchain-trustworthy and versatile platform. On the other hand, its peer-to-peer architecture has several limitations, as a lack of storage space for data and high consensus costs [8]. Blockchain technology might be able to fix some of the problems that have been genetic study, which are discussed in this chapter. Here, we present and evaluate current research papers that demonstrate blockchain technology's applications. We also give a thorough assessment of the timeline for blockchain technology. Using blockchain technology is something we wholeheartedly support when it comes to building a Data Ecosystem for genomic applications.

### 19.2 Overview of Blockchain Technology

Blockchains are a type of DLT that uses a distributed ledger to keep track of events in order. This is different from regular databases. Even though they work a lot, blockchain and DLT are two different platforms. Instead of one person being in charge of all the data in a distributed ledger, a team of peers oversees the database all in the network. One distinguishing feature of blockchains compared to other types of distributed ledger technology is the presence of data blocks, which serve as distinct data structures. Hashgraph and DAG are two additional contemporary applications of DLT that are readily apparent in the world [11].
