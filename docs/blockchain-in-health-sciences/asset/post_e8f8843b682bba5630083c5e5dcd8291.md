### Abstract
Pharmacies must efficiently handle their data. A new technology called blockchain can make many pharmaceutical processes safer and more transparent. In 2013, the US created the Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA) to prevent the distribution of stolen, contaminated, or counterfeit drugs. Blockchain technology employs cryptographic methods to serve as an answer to this problem. To ensure the safety of a peer-to-peer network of transactions, blockchain technology creates a distributed ledger that is not centralized. Incorporating them into a product ensures its safety and marketability. Blockchain technology offers solutions to enhance the validity, reliability, and efficiency of medication manufacturing by facilitating compliance with legislation. Additionally, it offers an opportunity to tackle a major problem: the proliferation of counterfeit drugs that are making their way into the system and eventually into the hands of consumers. The pharmaceutical industry claims that counterfeit drugs cause a significant danger to society. Patients' health is negatively affected in both direct and indirect ways by false drugs, which may result in severe complications or even death. This counterfeit puts patient safety at risk and harms the manufacturer's image; it is caused by the pharmaceutical industry's deficient supply chain management. Supply chain management may benefit from blockchain technology’s transparency and immutability at every step of the process, which increases confidence, security, traceability, and openness. There is hope for openness and responsibility in blockchain, a new technology for managing electronic data. Every user of a computer network has access to the same immutable record of transactions known as blockchain.

## 15.1 Introduction

Blockchain is a distributed digital ledger that systematically, chronologically records transactions into “blocks”. Globally, a network of nodes controls a blockchain. Due to the distributed nature of the system, it is impossible to change a recorded transaction between two parties on the blockchain without affecting all blocks that come after it [1–3]. An innovation and architectural platform, blockchain was first revealed by Nakamoto in 2008 and released in 2009. Distributed ledgers, created by peers and kept in a decentralized manner among computer devices that make up the blockchain infrastructure, are the backbone of blockchain technology [3]. Originally developed for use with cryptocurrency, blockchain technology has the dual goals of cutting out intermediaries like banks and protecting users from the high probability of fraud and theft. Transparent and immutable record keeping may be necessary for certain health sector transactions, as the purchase and shipment of pharmaceuticals and medical equipment in supply chains, or the oversight of staff access rights and permissions to facilities, medical records, and other health data [4]. By using blockchain technology in the pharmacy, the supply and monitoring of medical supplies might be legally and reliably secured. Even for authorized businesses, this kind of system may make audits and controls for pharmaceutical products easier. There are currently no known flaws in blockchain in the 21st century. Blockchain may increase trust and help eliminate bias in traditional supply chain systems since it is designed to prevent any individual from changing transactions or data [5].

## 15.2 Blockchain and Supply Chain

As a highly intricate process involving many transactions, the pharmaceutical supply chain must adhere to stringent regulations at all times. Manufacturers’ present lack of candor on the authenticity verification process in the supply chain is an issue [6]. Security in the supply chain has lately come to the forefront due to the passage of the Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCA) in the US. One of the main reasons the Act was put into action was to address the problem of fake medications. Contraband medication poses a health risk to patients since it lacks the necessary active ingredients. Distributing, wholesaling, and manufacturing are all links in the intricate pharmaceutical supply chain that ultimately leads to the patient (Figure 15.1). Authenticity may be tracked by manufacturers with minimal visibility in the supply chain [7].

In the drugstore, retail, and manufacturing industries, manufacturers are responsible for ensuring their pharmaceutical inventory is prepared for delivery to wholesalers as part of the supply chain. After the wholesalers and distributors will provide their orders, manufacturers arrange the goods for safekeeping in their warehouses. To ensure complete transparency, including inventory data. The goal of the wholesalers is to make purchasing prescription drugs easier and faster. Manufacturers work with the wholesalers who supply thousands of pharmacies and dispensers. Instead of shipping pharmaceuticals to pharmacies one by one, manufacturers may send large quantities to a relatively small number of wholesalers. The wholesaler then offers a range of services, including packaging, computerized order processing, and medication delivery, after they have ownership of the goods. The final links in the supply chain are healthcare facilities and pharmaceutical companies.
