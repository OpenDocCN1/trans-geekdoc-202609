## 19.7.1 Adoption Barriers

For the general public to use blockchain technology in genomics and healthcare, it is essential to have user-friendly interfaces and reliable software performance. Blockchain platforms undergo constant evolution and necessitate specialized expertise for implementation. This issue has been recognized as a significant obstacle in the implementation of solutions, as stated in numerous academic studies. The presence of instability and the absence of user-friendly interfaces act as significant obstacles to wide-spread public acceptance, hence restricting its utilization to those with advanced technological skills [33].

## 19.7.2 Interoperability

To implement blockchain technology in the field of genomics, enterprises must establish seamless integration and connectivity between blockchain platforms and pre-existing non-blockchain platforms. The fact that there is a wide variety of blockchain implementations, which of may not be intrinsically compatible, makes this problem much worse. Blockchains that are able to communicate with one another reduce dependency on any one blockchain platform. When it comes to genomics, most of the solutions that have been suggested are platform and feature specific. Adopting a multi-blockchain strategy, independent of any particular blockchain platform, will enhance scalability and mitigate the security vulnerabilities inherent in the chosen platform. Nevertheless, the existing method is intricate and requires intricate inter-chain communication. The field of blockchain interoperability is experiencing significant growth, and it is possible that multi-blockchain techniques could become viable in the future [33].

## 19.7.3 Smart Contract Security

The development of advanced applications that go beyond financial transactions is made possible by blockchains, enabling the use of smart contracts. Smart contracts provide more functionality, but they also make the system more vulnerable to attacks like the 2016 DAO compromise [34]. Security holes in smart contracts are becoming more common [34], and they can cost a lot of money or cause people's personal information to be compromised. Smart contracts were employed in several academic articles to accomplish diverse functions, including the provision of access to confidential data [35]. An obstacle in implementing these contracts to manage real patient data is the inherent vulnerability they pose. Implementing established protocols and through rigorous assessments can mitigate this risk, while current research continues to explore smart contract security [34].

## 19.7.4 Data Privacy

Despite a lot of study, the privacy issues surrounding blockchain technology in genomic applications remain unanswered. Some parts of privacy need more research, like user anonymity and the possibility of re-identification by correlation attacks. It is critical to prevent the disclosure of patient data for whatever purpose, even when re-identification might be required in some research circumstances. When time and money are needed to conduct additional investigations, re-identification becomes essential. But steps must be done to stop patients' personal information from being reidentified and leaked. An extra challenge is maintaining the privacy of financial and personal information. In a perfect world, it would be practically impossible to identify a user from their specific interactions with businesses. The feasibility of this in applications has demonstrated by recent research on the use of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) [36]. Even after the full de-identification process, re-identification is still possible. Potentially more complex re-identification efforts might emerge at a later date. Therefore, it is not known if there are other ways to protect privacy to prevent future attacks [37].

## 19.7.5 Reproducibility and Validity

To prove the study's validity in genomics and overall research, it is essential to do a replication. To replicate a study, researchers or auditors need to find the same data, maintained in the same format over several years. It is essential to mention blockchain's use for data interchange in scientific research when off-chain storage is used. This problem surfaces in systems that depend on centralized off-chain storage as many in those that use decentralized off-chain storage, such as IPFS. Consider the possibility that the data may be removed from the cloud provider at a later date [38]. Following the FAIR guidelines for data management does not seem to be a priority for genomic blockchains. When patients are part of many organizations with different assigned IDs, data redundancy is another potential issue. The possibility of duplicate data publication, when identical patient data is available under different anonymous identifiers, is a barrier to the decentralized sharing and analysis of genetic data. This is a major problem since it could affect the reliability of the study, which is especially important when the data is used for research. In an effort to circumvent this, the CG [38] enlisted the help of two reviewers and developed a grading system based on rules. Various methods already exist for detecting duplicate entries in separate databases, such as the methodology described in reference [39]. This method employs secure multi-party computation to achieve privacy-preserving record linkage across multiple databases. Nevertheless, additional investigation is required to tackle this issue in a decentralized blockchain network [39].

## 19.7.6 Verifiability

An obstacle that arises when assigning processing jobs to untrusted parties is the need to authenticate the precision of the outcomes. An effective approach involves delegating the analysis task to numerous analysis nodes and subsequently comparing the findings to verify accuracy. Nevertheless, the expense of this method might be substantial, particularly when the identical work is allocated to a significant number of analysis nodes. Hence, the need for a reliable and scalable verification technique to confirm the accurate execution of outsourced computation/analysis by untrusted nodes in a blockchain network remains an unresolved matter that necessitates additional investigation. Exploration of verified computation improvements in a blockchain system is now underway [40].

## 19.7.7 Key Management

Ensuring that patients (data owners) can securely maintain their keys and identities, particularly with regards to health data, is a complex task. After patients gain complete autonomy over their data, it is imperative to establish educational processes to equip them with relevant knowledge about optimal data management techniques. Additionally, it is essential to establish effective key management schemes and processes for emergency access to genomic/healthcare data, sometimes referred to as “break glass” access [40].

## 19.7.8 Ethical Challenges

Defrancesco & Klevecz [41] and Ahmed & Shabani [42] have investigated the moral challenges brought on by genetic marketplaces. The authors argue that when financial incentives are involved, the validity of informed consent is called into question, which could lead to data sharing without discrimination. The success of these monetary incentives in encouraging people to share personal information for scientific studies is debatable. Nonetheless, other non-financial incentives should be seriously considered. To encourage people to participate in animal conservation efforts, Mofekeng & Fatima [43] showed how to use non-fungible tokens as digital collectibles. As a result, it would be beneficial to promote medical research by doing more studies on the non-financial factors that encourage participation in genetic research.
