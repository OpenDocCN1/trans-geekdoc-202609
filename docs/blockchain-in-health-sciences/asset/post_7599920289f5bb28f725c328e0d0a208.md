## 20.4.2 Non-Commercial Applications

The primary purpose of blockchain non-commercial applications is to enable genomic data exchange with hassle so that researchers may further progress. Data can be shared in categories, analysis, storage, and access control foci of the following studies about blockchain usage.

Blockchain technology provides secure, transparent, and decentralized solutions for quite a number of challenges in the senescence of genomic research data management. It offers improved solutions to sharing, analysis, storage, and access controls for the data, solving the many problems related to data privacy, security, and interoperability [13].

Data sharing: A prime example of using blockchain technology for safe genomic data sharing is the Cancer Gene Trust (CGT). CGT carried out a real-world patient data study to show how their system worked to achieve safe, efficient, and economical dissemination of genetic data. Informed consent was given by patients for the collection of their data, and de-identification processes protected patient privacy while allowing public access for research. A comparable strategy is presented by CryptDist [14], which provides improved procedures for exchanging whole-genome data.

Additionally, work is being done to create blockchain protocols that are especially meant to be used in the disciplines of genetics and medicine. Coini [15], for example, suggests a new consensus method to take the place of the widely used Proof-of-Work (PoW) architecture. Regarding their techniques, tokens are awarded to competitors that map High-Throughput Sequencing (HTS) correctly.

These reads are mapped, concatenated, and uploaded to the blockchain to validate blocks.

Processing and Analysis of Data: Numerous studies have looked into the possibility of using blockchain technology to process and analyze genetic data in a safe and efficient manner. Zhang et al. [16], proposed a strategy for Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) that prioritizes data privacy.

Their approach combines a framework for gene fragmentation with a privacy-preserving sharing protocol. Users can create data blocks with any genomic file type using this framework. Then, a decentralized-blockchain network made up of service providers stores these blocks. With a distributed method, there are no longer worries about centralized data storage, where one supplier may have undue control, and it is possible to share, analyze, and conduct collaborative research. To create decentralized-machine learning models, another line of inquiry focuses on fusing blockchain technology and machine learning. Continuing in this spirit, [17] suggests Swarm Learning (SL), a blockchain-based method for safe distributed peer-to-peer peering. SL makes safe participation without a central coordinator possible, which helps with the first phase of decentralized-machine learning. After that, the data are trained, and model parameters are combined to allow control and ownership of substantial amounts of genetic and medical datasets to stay near the data owners.

Secure Storage: A number of the publications that have evaluated examine the use of blockchain technology for the safe storage of genetic data. For example, the work reported in [18] shows that blockchain technology can be used to store and query pharmacogenomic data. The authors gives an example of how to store and retrieve genomic data that work using the Ethereum blockchain and smart contracts. When data items are stored in smart contracts, they are assigned unique identifiers that act as mapping keys when the objects are later retrieved.

This method uses genome-wide, multi-source data extraction to enable quick responses to data queries. It is noteworthy, therefore, that the amount of genetic data used in these investigations is small compared to the quantity of other kinds of genomic data. A follow-up study [7] looks into the problem of storing large data files, such as Sequence Alignment Maps (SAM) files, which can be larger than terabytes. The authors offer a novel remedy for this problem by fusing a private blockchain network with data compression.

Data Management and Access Control: Consent management systems are emphasized as a major motivator in a number of papers that emphasize the potential of blockchain technology for controlling and regulating access to genetic data. For example, [19], Dwarna suggests a web-based platform that uses blockchain technology to make it easier for researchers and research participants to gather and share dynamic consent. This platform complies fully with the requirements of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). By documenting consent on the distributed ledger, the blockchain's intrinsic architecture empowers participants and researchers to own own data. After that, access to the data is only permitted with the data owner's consent.

The paper argues that managing consent for individual genomic data is a challenging issue for multi-stakeholder consent management, or MSCM. Since genetic information can reveal personal information about an individual and their family, consent from relevant parties is necessary to preserve privacy.

The authors propose to obtain particular consent from a variety of stakeholders by means of a blockchain-based consensus technique. Furthermore, this approach logs user-generic database interactions. The partitioned logging system has benefits over centralized logs as a distributed, cross-site replacement. Specifically, it eliminates the potential for single points of failure and less the likelihood of manipulating historical logs.

Interoperability: Blockchain technology has great promise for achieving highly sophisticated degrees of interoperability between various individuals and operating their own data silos. This happens so that data may be exchanged more easily and seamlessly, blockchains offer transparent mechanisms for data storage and access management. The kind of blockchain that is chosen crucial, and it needs to match the resources that system actors have at their disposal. Consensus algorithms that need specialized hardware or favored entities with significant financial resources, for example, may discourage participation from less-equipped health organizations and individuals.

The use of tools like online blockchain browsers and wallet managers, as well as the use of synchronized hardware settings, undoubtedly contribute to the rise in adoption rates. Although there are additional problems that hinder interoperability, technological barriers are not the only ones.

To interface with the present healthcare data system, businesses utilizing blockchain technology to manage protected health information (PHI) must adhere to applicable “HIPAA” or “GDPR” rules. Although the majority of the systems were intended to address these issues, only a very small proportion of them really did so [21].
