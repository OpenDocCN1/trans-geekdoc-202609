# 15.1 Introduction

Blockchain is a distributed digital ledger that systematically, chronologically records transactions into “blocks”. Globally, a network of nodes controls a blockchain. Due to the distributed nature of the system, it is impossible to change a recorded transaction between two parties on the blockchain without affecting all blocks that come after it [1–3]. An innovation and architectural platform, blockchain was first revealed by Nakamoto in 2008 and released in 2009. Distributed ledgers, created by peers and kept in a decentralized manner among computer devices that make up the blockchain infrastructure, are the backbone of blockchain technology [3]. Originally developed for use with cryptocurrency, blockchain technology has the dual goals of cutting out intermediaries like banks and protecting users from the high probability of fraud and theft. Transparent and immutable record keeping may be necessary for certain health sector transactions, as the purchase and shipment of pharmaceuticals and medical equipment in supply chains, or the oversight of staff access rights and permissions to facilities, medical records, and other health data [4]. By using blockchain technology in the pharmacy, the supply and monitoring of medical supplies might be legally and reliably secured. Even for authorized businesses, this kind of system may make audits and controls for pharmaceutical products easier. There are currently no known flaws in blockchain in the 21st century. Blockchain may increase trust and help eliminate bias in traditional supply chain systems since it is designed to prevent any individual from changing transactions or data [5].
