# 4.4 Health Promotion and the HBM

Coordinated by WHO, Sustainable Development Goal 3 (health and well-being) emphasizes the need for health promotion [20]. Health promotion enables people to better control their health, positively impacting quality of life. Health promotion addresses social conditioning factors and determinants of health. Behaviors, beliefs, and perceptions form elements of social conditioning and, when revealed, help in selecting the best interventions.

Health behaviors are either within an individual's immediate control or are beyond their control due to social, economic, and environmental conditions. Individual health behaviors are shaped by normative beliefs, self-efficacy beliefs, self-representations, and attitudes [21]. Behavioral theories such as the TPB, the Transtheoretical Model, HBM, Social Cognitive Theory, and the Social Ecological Model have aimed to design, implement, and evaluate health behavior interventions.

HBM, created in 1966 by Rosenstock [22], became importance as a psychosocial framework in health research and practice. Maiman and Becker [23] refined Rosenstock's seminal work to predict better an individual's engagement or disengagement with preventive health-related actions [24].

The Rosenstock HBM uses six key constructs to measure diverse populations' conditions and allied behaviors. Depending on the health condition, population dynamics, and health behavior, measures are introduced to the key constructs of the model. The last and sixth construct, “self-efficacy”, served as an extension of the primary HBM by Rosenstock [25]. These six constructs help predict how individual will act for disease prevention, get screened, or manage the disease. Health behaviors are indirectly influenced by perceptions which are further influenced by demographic, sociopsychological, and socioeconomic variables [26].

Each construct of HBM is individually engaged to create prosperous health communication interventions for behavior change [27]. Though construct definitions remain consistent with the primary HBM theory, HBM is not generalized. For example, the construct of perceived barriers to Vitamin D screening may have different measures than those of barriers to colonoscopy [26].
