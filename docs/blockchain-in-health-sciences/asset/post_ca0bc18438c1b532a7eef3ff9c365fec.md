## 19.2.2 Smart Contracts

As a consequence of Ethereum's popularity, smart contracts on the blockchain have been on the rise. But the concept of smart contracts was first proposed by Szabo in 1997 [17]. The idea has evolved since its start, but at its core it means letting a smart contract software run on a decentralized network and change the system's state independently, and verifiably without any third parties getting in the way. The notion has successfully implemented and utilized in various domains like finance, identity management, and healthcare; thanks to the advent of blockchain technology [18]. Multiple blockchain systems exist that facilitate the execution of smart contracts in various programming languages, with solidity being one of the most often used languages. The process of creating a smart contract consists of delineating a sequence of conditions and directives that, when fulfilled, initiate an automated response. Smart contracts possess numerous benefits in comparison to conventional contracts, including autonomous execution, public verification, and the elimination of intermediaries [18].

## 19.2.3 Security and Privacy of Blockchain Technology

Using several cryptographic techniques is crucial to blockchain's security and privacy features. While earlier iterations of the Bitcoin blockchain made use of specific methods, subsequent iterations of the blockchain made use of other strategies to enhance privacy and security. By utilizing its core security and privacy features, the blockchain ensures that the system meets the requirements for secure and private online transactions. By using a pre-established consensus procedure, blockchain ensures that ledger consistency is preserved across several nodes. Both validating miners and outside attackers trying to modify blockchain transactions cannot alter them. Digital signatures and cryptographic hashing make it possible to detect tampering with transaction data by checking the validity of the digital signature [17].

In addition, because the distributed ledger of transactions is maintained by all nodes within the network, altering blockchain transactions requires changing the data contained in every node. Due to its distributed ledger technology, the blockchain network is impervious to distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks. By utilizing public key infrastructure (PKI) pseudonymity, blockchain technology guarantees privacy. Instead of using real identities, the public addresses are used to identify the nodes or users. Nevertheless, this approach lacks complete anonymity due to the potential for associating the public address with actual identities through the observation of transactions among various entities [19]. One further constraint on privacy in the initial blockchain implementation is the public visibility of transactions and their associated data. The immutability of blockchain data and transactions makes it suitable for a variety of uses, including the protection of sensitive personal information. A number of privacy and security measures, such as mixing, anonymous signatures, and Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs), have been proposed to overcome the aforementioned limitations [19].

## 19.3 An Overview of Genomics Research

Genomics is the scientific discipline that examines the entirety of an organism's genetic material. Within genomics, sequencing enables researchers to explore the connections between genetics and diseases, and can aid in the identification of genetic mutations that are responsible for diseases. This, in turn, can lead to personalized diagnoses and treatment strategies. Recent progress in DNA sequencing technologies has substantially reduced the cost of sequencing the human genome, making data more accessible to individuals at a price below $1000 USD [20]. Nevertheless, the augmented volume of genomic sequencing presents two concerns: firstly, the expanded quantity of genomic data necessitates significant computing capacity and data storage. Furthermore, the inclusion of genomic data raises privacy concerns due to its sensitive nature, and the expanding volume of genomic data exacerbates these concerns.

Present genomics research utilizes a powerful technology called high throughput sequencing that can simultaneously sequence hundreds of millions of DNA molecules [21]. Although useful, this technology produces an immense amount of data, requiring substantial computational power [20]. As a result, computational analysis is the bottleneck of genomics research [20]. A seemingly effective solution to address the increasing need for advanced computational power is to use cloud services [20, 22]. Cloud computing is a service that allows individuals to access and utilize additional computational processing power or store data by their hardware's capabilities from a shared supply of computational resources provided through the medium of the Internet [23]. While this model is effective and scalable, aggregating data at one data center, as is the case with cloud services, increases the risk of a single point of failure. Security breaches of data centers can result in the unauthorized access of genetic data and associated health records, as was the case in the breach of the DNA-testing Veritas Genetics in November 2019 [23]. This issue cannot be easily resolved by building additional data centers, as they are inherently challenging to create and sustain. The implementation of blockchain alternatives can enhance the security of these vulnerable data networks and encourage secure commercialization practices of genetic data between data providers and buyers. The subsequent sections of this review will demonstrate this, after providing an introduction to the complexities of cryptocurrencies and blockchain [22].
