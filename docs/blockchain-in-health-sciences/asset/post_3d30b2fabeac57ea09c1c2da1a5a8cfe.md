## 4.3.3 Insufficient Levels of Vitamin D in the Nation

A literature review with keywords Vitamin D deficiency, Youth, and India interesting articles with valuable insights. In a city in western India, a study found that seventy per cent (70%) of apparently healthy adults had severe vitamin D deficiency, with women having a slightly higher prevalence of seventy-six per cent (76%). This study was limited to a particular geography [15].

In North India, Delhi, the seeming health of individuals was assessed. Variables selected exposed to direct sunlight, the time of year, skin tone, calcium intake, and hormonal shifts like pregnancy. It was discovered that healthy people in Delhi remained naturally Vitamin D deficient despite the city receiving plenty of sunlight. This study, too, was limited to a particular geography [16].

A study of 200 families in a North Indian village located east of Delhi during February (winter) found though men possessed noticeably more Vitamin D values than women, these were six and threefold for higher males and females, respectively, compared to their counterparts. Despite better Vitamin D levels in rural subjects, of these, 70 per cent still lacked enough vitamin D. Therefore, irrespective of a rural or urban setting, the necessity of a national scheme for D-vitamin dietary fortification was indicated in this study [17].

Another longitudinal research through simple randomization studied the effects of seasonal variability on the D-vitamin levels of healthy young adults and college students (Eighteen to twenty-five) in Chandigarh. Seasons (End of Summer & End of winter) were taken as variables. The majority of healthy young adults were found to be vitamin D sufficient. The article stated its limitations as a lack of assessment of being in contact with sunlight and the effect of winter. Moreover, this study was too limited to a small geographical area [18].

Micronutrient malnutrition not only increases a nation's disease burden also burdens its economy. All micronutrient deficiencies are associated with an estimated economic cost of 0.8%—2.4% of the GDP in India [19]. Therefore, health interventions are needed for a nation's holistic growth.

## 4.4 Health Promotion and the HBM

Coordinated by WHO, Sustainable Development Goal 3 (health and well-being) emphasizes the need for health promotion [20]. Health promotion enables people to better control their health, positively impacting quality of life. Health promotion addresses social conditioning factors and determinants of health. Behaviors, beliefs, and perceptions form elements of social conditioning and, when revealed, help in selecting the best interventions.

Health behaviors are either within an individual's immediate control or are beyond their control due to social, economic, and environmental conditions. Individual health behaviors are shaped by normative beliefs, self-efficacy beliefs, self-representations, and attitudes [21]. Behavioral theories such as the TPB, the Transtheoretical Model, HBM, Social Cognitive Theory, and the Social Ecological Model have aimed to design, implement, and evaluate health behavior interventions.

HBM, created in 1966 by Rosenstock [22], became importance as a psychosocial framework in health research and practice. Maiman and Becker [23] refined Rosenstock's seminal work to predict better an individual's engagement or disengagement with preventive health-related actions [24].

The Rosenstock HBM uses six key constructs to measure diverse populations' conditions and allied behaviors. Depending on the health condition, population dynamics, and health behavior, measures are introduced to the key constructs of the model. The last and sixth construct, “self-efficacy”, served as an extension of the primary HBM by Rosenstock [25]. These six constructs help predict how individual will act for disease prevention, get screened, or manage the disease. Health behaviors are indirectly influenced by perceptions which are further influenced by demographic, sociopsychological, and socioeconomic variables [26].

Each construct of HBM is individually engaged to create prosperous health communication interventions for behavior change [27]. Though construct definitions remain consistent with the primary HBM theory, HBM is not generalized. For example, the construct of perceived barriers to Vitamin D screening may have different measures than those of barriers to colonoscopy [26].

Exemplified in the osteoporosis prevention study, questionnaires, based on the HBM model were used in scales and subscales to identify, explain, and predict interventions for risky health behaviors. The Osteoporosis Health Belief Scale crafted by [28] determined the relationship between health attitudes and osteoporosis prevention practices, such as calcium consumption and physical activity. A limitation of this scale, the self-efficacy construct, was later included in 1998, [29] to develop the Osteoporosis Self-Efficacy Scale (OSES) to evaluate self-efficacy of behaviors.

## BLOCKCHAIN IN HEALTH SCIENCES

HBM has been applied to study diabetes self-care, medication adherence, vaccination, contraceptive use, breast cancer screening, HIV protection, and other behaviors that require actions to avert a threat to health [7, 30, 31]. Interventions drawn from the HBM have improved voluntary Pap smear screening rates for cervical cancer, smoking cessation counts, and dental care and also promoted healthy eating lately [32].

During COVID-19, community pharmacists recently applied the HBM to help patients select healthy behaviors. They used educational tools to combat misinformation and reinforced social distancing as a COVID-19-limiting behavior. Demographics, socioeconomic characteristics, and the patient’s knowledge base were considered during communication [33]. An education program on osteoporosis using the HBM in Iraq showed a significant impact on osteoporosis prevention behaviors in post-menopausal nurses.

### 4.4.1 Use of HBM in the Indian Context

A three-decade old study on behavior prevention methods in India as of its geographic locations employing the Rosenstock [35]. Between 1990 and now, the HBM approach has been used to understand many different health-related behaviors in India. Though, the few of decade 2010–2020 have much more published studies regarding HBM in the Indian setting compared to the decades of 1990–2000. 

In the context of COVID-19, Kerala they used the HBM to predict preventive health behaviors in terms of the utilization of government health services. A cross-sectional investigation was conducted. Most of the state’s Population found it self-efficacious to follow their government’s preventive and protective measures. The HBM identified the barriers and altered their health behavior when the pandemic was only being started [36].
