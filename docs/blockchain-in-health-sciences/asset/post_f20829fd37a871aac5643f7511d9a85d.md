**Types of blockchains**

There are three main types of blockchains:

- **Public Blockchain**: The information within this blockchain is accessible to everyone, and anyone is welcome to participate and make consensus. Although participants' identities are pseudonymous through the use of public-key, if an attacker is able to couple a participant's realworld identity with the public key, he can then access the entire history. Public blockchains, like major cryptocurrencies, are best represented by Bitcoin and Ethereum, under which they fall under the term of permission permission.

- **Private Blockchain**: A single business oversees a distributed, typically centrally managed network. Compared with public blockchains, which are accessed by users, private blockchains are restricted to a few nodes and usually operate under centralized administration. While this is quite useful in the enforcement of legislation and data protection policy, it does not gain the features of full decentralization of ledgers. As a result, just like centralized systems, it is susceptible to insider assaults and security lapses. MultiChain is an example of a private blockchain.

- **Consortium Blockchain**: A consortium blockchain is partially centralized, allowing access to a restricted set of specific entities to participate in the consensus protocol. The main directions of such a blockchain will be directed by some powerful group or a single control entity that decides on which all entities have the right to read, append, and validate transactions. More than public blockchains, consortium blockchains still benefit from things like faster validation and throughput compared to private blockchains, while the majority of the network remains under the control of single entities. One example of a consortium blockchain is Hyperledger.

## 20.2 General Applications of Blockchain

Cryptocurrencies are based on blockchain technology that allows peer-to-peer transactions without the interference of financial institutions like commercial banks [1]. Verification of transactions and creating an immutable record of the same allow anonymity by protecting the user’s personal identity information.

Blockchain runs a digital record of transactions by binding them via a consensus procedure, enhancing trust in a distributed network. Therefore, trust is built-in within the decentralized system itself; one does not need to trust middlemen or unknown people.
