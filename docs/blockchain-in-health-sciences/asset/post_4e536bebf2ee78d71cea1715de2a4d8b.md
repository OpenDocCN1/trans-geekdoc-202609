## 2.5 Internet of Things (IoT) Health Security Concerns

More than 10 billion IoT devices are currently wirelessly joined, according to the "ABI (Allied Business Intelligence, Inc) Research report" [24]. Plus, this number will have soared 30 billion devices by the year 2026. Deploying IoT devices will result in a multitude of applications that greatly enhance our everyday life. With time, e-health will likely become one of the prominent worries [25]. Many problems, however, have emerged as a result of IoT incorporation into health sciences sector. Data management and storage, unified and ubiquitous access, privacy and security, and data transfer between devices are just a few of the many areas that fall under this umbrella of worries [26]. Data breaches can damage the credibility of companies and users alike. On top of that, hackers can spy on consumers' personal life if they manage to acquire access to their Internet of Things devices. Security remains a concern with IoT, even if it is growing at a rapid pace. Several new applications have emerged as a result of the expansion of the Internet of Things, with a focus on the health sciences. Nonetheless, sensitive information about individuals, such as medical records and patient identities, is often linked with the health sciences. Because of this, concerns about the safety of such systems are well-founded. Therefore, secured connections are essential for overseeing an IoT rollout of this magnitude. There is also the possibility of data theft or profiling due to the database's security measures, which store all of the information. Many obstacles exist in the realm of security for healthcare IoT devices. Privacy, secrecy, integrity, and authentication of devices are among these, along with information flow traces, persistence, and data transfer flows. There is a high prevalence of data theft, data privacy, and confidentiality at sensitive, active, and gateway environmental nodes, among others. Here, hackers acquire authorization to access confidential information through an unsafe household Wi-Fi, which they then sell on the black market. Data profiling is another common issue. This occurs when an attacker tries to identify the victim and their harm method by using stolen data. All healthcare-related IoT systems suffer from these two issues. In order to achieve their goal, attackers use a range of tactics to prevent IoT systems from functioning properly. The five main categories into which these assaults fall are as follows [27, 28]:

- **Selective-Forwarding Attacks:** Attackers can initiate Denial of Service (DoS) assaults by using selective-forwarding techniques [29], in which rogue nodes forward packets in a certain way. This exploit aims to interfere with routing pathways. Using this Computer Methods and Programmes in Biomedicine Update 2 (2022) 100071 3 as an illustration I. Using the Sadek et al. approach, criminals disrupt networks from sending out an alert when an alarming condition (such as a heart attack) is detected by a sensor.
- **Sinkhole Attacks:** A malicious node may launch this attack by falsely advertising a beneficial routing path to other nodes in the network in the hopes that would direct traffic to it. In other cases, this attack does not even affect the network; nevertheless, it can be quite effective when combined with other attacks, such as selective-forwarding attacks. Because of this, instead of trustworthy healthcare institutions receiving the data, it is sent to hackers who will be in charge of keeping and handling the recorded data. As a result, hackers will have access to private patient data, which they can use to eventually profile the patient [29].
- **Jamming:** An example of a machine-to-machine assault is the use of a noise signal to obstruct wireless communication. This interference then uses the wireless spectrum, making it impossible for Internet of Things devices to communicate with one another [30].
- **Overflowing:** The goal of the attack is to exhaust the target's processor, memory, bandwidth, and battery. For instance, attackers generate a large number of connection requests in order to consume bandwidth and deplete batteries. Interfering with ongoing data transmission and recording (such as sensor data) is the impact of this technique.
- **Email scams:** Environmental nodes' data collecting is vulnerable to attacks that steal personal information. Asking users for details, including the Wi-Fi password is one way this attack gets access to IoT resources. Security concerns hinder the correct operation of IoT nodes and can spread throughout IoT healthcare systems, limiting their large-scale deployment.

### 2.5.1 Protection and Integrity for the Internet of Things

Concerns like privacy and security are considered paramount in IoT designs. Because of this, a great deal of research has been done to address these issues. Here are a few succinct descriptions of the current solutions:

- (a) Lightweight authentication for wearable devices using basic cryptographic hash functions was proposed by Gupta et al. [31] to ensure secure network connections. Reason being, these devices aren't superpowers when it comes to memory and processing speed. This is why conventional methods of protecting user data on mobile devices are inadequate for usage with wearable gear. By masking device IDs with XOR and hashing techniques, the proposed system maintains privacy and anonymity.
- (b) Gope and Sikdar [32] introduced a lightweight two-factor authentication solution to safeguard the privacy of Internet of Things devices. Authors have emphasised device authentication more due to the open and public deployment of IoT devices, which makes them vulnerable to physical and closing threats. This technique uses physically irreplaceable functions constructed of ICs and unique one-way hash values to introduce random physical deviations into the micro-structure of ICs, making them distinct.
- (c) IoT; the subsequent iteration of devices, will be connected using IPv6, which was created especially for this. Due to the fact it provides many features and a larger address space than the antiquated IPv4 Internet Protocol [33]. However, because to its design, it is vulnerable to denial-of-service (DoS) assaults during IP address configuration, which could obstruct communication between the two nodes in a network [34]. Liu et al. [33] proposed a unique approach called Addressless IoT Server to solve this issue and enable safe communication between the Internet of Things client and server. Similar to this, Rehman and Manickam have developed a straightforward solution called Secure-DAD, which is deployed on IPv6-capable Internet of Things devices to ensure that communication between devices is limited to permitted ones. Can thus provide secure communication in an Internet of Things environment between the devices [35].
- (d) Sharma et al. [36] introduced a privacy-preserving paradigm for Internet of Things (IoT) healthcare-based systems based on data-mining techniques to monitor healthcare information. One of the most important aspects of modern life is the Internet of Things (IoT) healthcare system, [37] which has significantly expanded the big data-driven medical system. IoT-based wearables are already being used by medical professionals to expedite the diagnostic, monitoring, prediction, and treatment processes. The ease of use and low cost of adoption of IoT are starting to transform healthcare services. The authors offered potential solutions to these problems to offer practical data analytics for IoT-based healthcare systems that protect privacy. The different IoT sensors can monitor the physical surroundings and record changes in the environment in real-time make up the IoT sensor data [38]. A self-adaptive filter in the recently added framework automatically recovers missing or incorrect data. As a result, the system can withstand errors.
- (e) Srinivas et al. [39] introduced a cloud-based user authentication method to enable safe authentication of medical data in the Internet of Things healthcare monitoring system. Utilising this procedure, a confidential session key is generated to facilitate secure communication between the authorized user and the wearable sensor node. A security analysis was conducted by the authors of the proposed mechanism, utilising the Real-Or-Random (ROR) paradigm, in order to assess its resilience against several widely recognised attacks.
- (f) Raifa Akkaoui [40] proposed a decentralised authentication technique for Internet of Things healthcare systems that utilises blockchain’s decentralised features in order to lessen the detrimental effects of distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks on Internet of Things environments. The technique has been evaluated by the author for security and privacy research on the Ethereum network. The researcher believes that the plan safeguards the privacy, confidentiality, integrity, and anonymity of IoT users and devices.
