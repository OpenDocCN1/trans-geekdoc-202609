
Given the large data and variety of machine learning algorithms, data mining allows us to build complex predictive models with nonlinear relationships or interactions between variables. Since we do not know what the data may tell us, it is impossible to assume any type of relationship we are dealing with (linear or nonlinear) or how many variables will be in the final model. Advanced machine learning methods, such as decision trees, neural networks, or ensemble modeling, can model the complexity of the data. On the contrary, traditional statistical methods often use simpler models, such as t-tests, ANOVA, linear regression, or logistic regression. They rely on proposed hypotheses and may not be able to capture complex, nonlinear models.

Last but not least, interpretability is a key difference. Data mining can detect complex, nonlinear models with interactions of variables, often considered “black box” models because the relationships of variables are too complex to follow. Note that the model is not literally uninterpretable. Data mining outcomes allow us to examine those relationships using either trees or mathematical formulas. However, it requires a deeper understanding of the scientific background of the method. Note that the focus of data mining is not really to explain the underlying mechanism but rather to achieve accurate predictions. On the contrary, traditional statistical methods focus on explaining the relationship between variables and making causal inferences. The emphasis of these studies is often on parameter estimation and hypothesis testing, which makes their results easier to interpret.

The comparison highlights the strengths of data mining, but it is not without its weaknesses. The most obvious weakness is the tendency to overfit the model to the data, given the size of the data. Overfitting means training a predictive model with a lot of data to achieve very high accuracy. However, the model fits the training sample so well that it is not generalizable to other samples. When this model is run with another dataset, it achieves very low accuracy. Thus, the model is overfitted to the training sample and is not useful in real life since it lacks generalizability. Overfitting can usually be evaluated by partitioning the data randomly into training and validation samples. The training sample is used to train the model, and the validation sample is used to validate it. If the model fit metrics are similar between both samples, we have a reliable model without overfitting issues. We will delve deeper into overfitting in [Chapter 6](#chapter6.xhtml).

Additionally, some machine learning algorithms may require substantial computational power to complete the analysis. More specifically, neural network algorithms often require a very high number of iterations to search for the optimal solution. This search process demands a lot of computation, requiring high computing power and extended running time. An effective search algorithm and a high-power computer are needed to handle this. More importantly, in data mining, the most time-consuming part is data preparation and treatment. Typically, 80% of the time in data mining projects is dedicated to data cleaning and preparation.

These are general characteristics of data mining. When we cover each machine learning method, we will discuss its specific advantages and disadvantages. The rule of thumb is that *there is no perfect machine learning method, but there is the best method for a specific dataset.* While some methods are more complicated or advanced than others, all methods have pros and cons. The best method depends on the data. If there were a 21. perfect method that worked well for all types of data, we could always use that one for all projects. However, the reality is that we don’t know which method will work best for our data until we train the model with the data. Hence, the common strategy is to use multiple methods and develop multiple predictive models with various configurations. By comparing these models, we can identify the best predictive model, also known as the champion model. We will discuss this step in detail in [Chapter 6](#chapter6.xhtml).

## Big Data

![](images/ufig1_22.jpg)

I want to provide a quick introduction to big data. First, what is big data? A common-sense answer would be “a lot of data,” which is not wrong. The AI in the TV show *Person of Interest* has to collect and process a huge amount of data in different types and formats coming from many sources in real time. Of course, it would require a supercomputer to store and process that amount of data promptly. Predictions would not be meaningful or useful if they took days to make.

SAS Institute defines big data as “large volumes of data—both structured and unstructured—that inundate a business on a day-to-day basis. But it's not the amount of data that's important. It's what organizations do with the data that matters. Big data can be analyzed for insights that lead to better decisions and strategic business moves.”

Thus, big data is not only about the amount of data but also about what we do with it. Essentially, big data can be characterized by five dimensions: volume, velocity, variety, variability, and veracity ([Figure 1.7](#chapter1.xhtml#fig1_7)).

![FIGURE 1.7 Big data dimensions. Return to text.⏎](images/fig1_7.jpg)

FIGURE 1.7 Big data dimensions. [Return to text.⏎](#chapter1.xhtml#b1fig1_7)

*   **VOLUME** pertains to the quantity of data, encompassing various sources such as business transactions, smart devices, industrial equipment, videos, social media, and more. Historically, managing such data would have posed challenges, but advancements in cloud computing and the availability of cost-effective storage solutions like data lakes and Hadoop have alleviated this burden.

*   **VELOCITY** relates to the speed at which data are generated and transmitted. With the proliferation of the Internet of Things (IoTs), data stream into servers at an unprecedented rate, necessitating timely processing. RFID tags, sensors, and smart meters are driving the demand for handling these torrents of data in near real time.

*   **VARIETY** refers to the diverse nature of data, which spans a wide array of formats. This encompasses structured, numeric data typically found in traditional databases, as well as unstructured data such as text documents, emails, videos, audio recordings, and financial transactions.

*   **VARIABILITY** pertains to the diversity and fluctuations in data flows. In addition to the increasing speed and variety of data, data flows exhibit unpredictability due to frequent changes and significant variations. Businesses must stay attuned to trends in social media and effectively manage daily, seasonal, and event-triggered surges in data loads.

*   **VERACITY** denotes data quality. Given the multitude of data sources, linking, matching, cleansing, and transforming data across systems can be challenging. Businesses must establish connections and discern relationships, hierarchies, and multiple data linkages; otherwise, their data may quickly become unmanageable.
