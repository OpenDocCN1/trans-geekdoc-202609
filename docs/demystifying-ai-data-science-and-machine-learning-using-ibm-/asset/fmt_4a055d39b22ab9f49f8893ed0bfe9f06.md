
All three strategies are advantageous for businesses and organizations. However, in this book, our focus lies on the predictive strategy. Within business operations, predictive modeling plays a crucial role. It enables us to develop and validate predictive models that accurately predict a predetermined outcome (target variable). Additionally, we can identify significant predictors that contribute to these predictions. Subsequently, by extrapolating potential or forecasted future values of these predictors, we can anticipate outcomes in the future. Furthermore, we have the capability to conduct various what-if scenarios, modifying the values of specific predictors to observe how the outcomes change. Such scenarios empower decision-makers to determine the most effective strategies or decisions to tackle business problems with minimal uncertainty.

## Data Mining and Data Science

![](images/ufig1_16.jpg)

In recent years, analytics and broader fields have seen a surge in the popularity of the term **data science**. This surge has led to an increase in job openings for data scientists and the frequent appearance of titles like **data scientist** or **data science expert** on resumes. Despite its widespread use, **data science** often remains a buzzword—commonly spoken yet challenging to define clearly. This ambiguity largely stems from the lack of a universally accepted definition of data science. Before the rise of data science, the industry was buzzing with terms like **data mining, data analytics, business analytics,** and **big data**. It's important to note that in these terms, including **data science**, the emphasis is less on the **data** itself and more on what we do with it. We all know that we need data. The real value lies in the application of the data, not just its existence.

While numerous comparisons are available online, they often lack consistency and, in some cases, contradict each other, leading to more confusion than clarification. It's worth mentioning that some of these comparisons inaccurately mix the concepts of data mining and data science, further muddying the waters. **Data science** is not a new term. Actually, an official definition of **data science** was introduced back in 1998 by [Hayashi (1998)](#bibab10c.xhtml#refbib_53). According to the author, data science is a “concept to unify statistics, data analysis and their related methods” in order to “understand and analyze actual phenomena” with data. In 2013, Dr. Vasant Dhar provided a definition of **data science** at a higher level in the Communications of ACM. He defined data science as “the study of the generalizable extraction of knowledge from data” ([Dhar, 2013](#bibab10c.xhtml#refbib_32)). It uses theories and techniques from various fields, including mathematics, statistics, computer science, and domain knowledge ([Cao, 2017](#bibab10c.xhtml#refbib_20)). **Data scientist** is a professional who “creates programming codes and combines them with statistical knowledge to create insights from data” ([Davenport & Patil, 2012](#bibab10c.xhtml#refbib_30)).

Thus, we can say that **data mining** and **data science** serve the same purposes of enabling us to explore and uncover patterns from large data to provide organizations and individuals with newly discovered information so they can make informed decisions. These terms overlap or can even be considered the same in some cases since both involve machine learning, big data, and analytics. A common understanding is that data mining is a method or interdisciplinary subfield, while data science is a discipline. From that perspective, we can consider data science broader than data mining, but data mining is an essential component of data science. Let's put it this way. Data mining covers methods of using machine learning to uncover patterns from large data to predict outcomes. Statistics, mathematics, and other rule-based methods are the core of data mining. On the contrary, data science is a larger and multidisciplinary field, focusing on capturing and extracting knowledge from data and communicating the outcomes. Data science consists of data mining as an essential part of analyzing data and other components such as data collection and management, data treatment, data visualization, computer programming, software engineering, and AI development.

![](images/ufig1_18.jpg)

Data scientists are often tasked with a variety of responsibilities that can include data collection and preparation, data mining, machine learning, computer programming, and software engineering. Consequently, their role may focus solely on data mining or require multidisciplinaries. If their primary responsibility is data mining, they concentrate on analyzing data and employing various machine learning methods to build predictive models. While computer programming skills can be advantageous, they are not strictly necessary because many data mining applications enable users to perform tasks without writing code (this book, for instance, focuses on machine learning for 17. non-programmers). However, if the job includes additional responsibilities such as big data collection, data warehousing and management, developing an intelligence dashboard, creating complex deep learning algorithms, and deploying predictive models in practical applications, then programming skills become essential. This is why job qualifications and skill requirements for data scientist positions can vary significantly.

It's clear that as we move forward, the definitions of **data mining** and **data science** will continue to evolve and diversify. However, this book will also touch upon essential concepts of data visualization and treatment, offering valuable knowledge for those keen to explore these areas.
