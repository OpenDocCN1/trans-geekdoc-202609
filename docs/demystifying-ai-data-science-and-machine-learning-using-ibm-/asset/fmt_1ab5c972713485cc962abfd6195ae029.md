
## 181. First, let's clarify some terminology

**Model fit** and **predictive power** are commonly used terms in data mining, but their distinction is often unclear. In many books and online resources, these terms are used interchangeably, leading to confusion. While they do overlap, they serve different purposes. **Model fit** is more commonly associated with traditional statistical analyses, whereas **predictive power** is used specifically in the context of predictive modeling. Although predictive power is less precise than model fit, it remains a crucial concept in data mining. I will differentiate these terms based on their purposes rather than their measurement since there are overlaps in how they are assessed.

### Model Fit

![](images/ufig6_4.jpg)

What is model fit? Imagine a model, represented by mathematical expressions, logic, or rules, that describes the relationship between the target variable and its predictors. This model is used to predict the value of the target variable, whether it is developed from a theoretical foundation or derived from a machine learning algorithm. The critical question is how well this model fits the actual data, which we refer to as **model fit**. The difference between the model's predictions and the actual data is called **residuals** in association models or **misclassification** in classification models. The smaller the residuals or misclassifications, the better the model fit.

Model fit is assessed through **goodness-of-fit** metrics, which estimate how well the model predicts the target variable based on the predictors. Some goodness-of-fit metrics are universal and apply to all models, while others are specific to certain machine learning methods. For instance, **prediction accuracy**, **misclassification rate**, **sensitivity**, **specificity**, **Lift charts**, and **ROC charts** are applicable to all classification models. Similarly, **root mean squared error**, **mean absolute percentage error**, and **residual by predicted charts** apply to all association models. However, metrics like the **F-test** and **R²** are specific to linear regression models, while **−2LL difference** and **pseudo R²** are used for logistic regression models. It's important to recognize both general model fit metrics and specific ones, with the former being more common since not all machine learning methods have their own unique metrics.

![](images/ufig6_5.jpg)

It's important to note that the term **goodness-of-fit** is commonly used in other statistics books and materials. I understand this shift in terminology may cause some confusion. Essentially, **goodness-of-fit indices** are specific metrics used to evaluate model fit. To maintain consistency and simplicity throughout this book, I will primarily use the term **model fit** instead of **goodness-of-fit**. Likewise, I will refer to **model fit metrics** instead of **goodness-of-fit indices**. This approach should make it easier for you to follow along.

One important thing to note is that model fit is typically evaluated during the model training phase using the training sample. However, achieving a good model fit on the training data does not guarantee that the model will perform well in real-world predictive tasks. This is where predictive power comes into play.

### Predictive Power

![](images/ufig6_6.jpg)

In terms of measurement, predictive power uses the same metrics as model fit, which assess how well the model fits the data. However, evaluating predictive power goes beyond just assessing model fit on the training sample. To truly evaluate a model's predictive power, we need to test it on new sets of data, such as test and validation samples. This can be confusing, so let's explore an example.

## 183. Example of Predictive Power Evaluation

Suppose we build and train a predictive model using a training sample, and we achieve a good model fit, indicating the model fits the training data well. Next, we want to see if this model works with new data. To do this, we apply the model to a test sample and, later, to a validation sample for the final model assessment. Does the model still fit the data well? If yes, then the model is generalizable and can make good predictions with real-world data, meaning it has good predictive power. If not, the model lacks predictive power because it fails to generalize to different datasets, making it unreliable for real-world use. In this case, the model may suffer from **overfitting**, which we will discuss shortly.

In short, while model fit and predictive power use similar metrics, they serve different purposes. Model fit evaluates the model's performance on a specific dataset, such as the training data, while predictive power assesses the model's performance on new data, ensuring it can be generalized to other datasets. In essence, predictive power ensures that the model can make accurate predictions in real-life scenarios. Predictive power is a broader concept than model fit because it requires the model to have a good fit across all datasets, not just the training set. Achieving good predictive power should be our ultimate goal.

## Overfitting

Overfitting is a significant concern in both statistical analyses and predictive modeling because it affects a model's predictive power. It is a common issue, especially when working with large datasets in data mining. However, overfitting can be a confusing concept, often inconsistently explained in various materials, leading to reader confusion. This confusion arises from a lack of clear definitions or explanations of the problem.

### 184. What Is Overfitting?

The distinction between **model fit** and **predictive power** helps clarify the concept of overfitting. To break it down step by step, [Figure 6.2](#chapter6.xhtml#fig6_2) illustrates this problem visually. By following these steps, we can identify overfitting in a data mining project:

1.  We train our model using a training sample and achieve an excellent model fit, meaning the model fits the training data very well.
2.  Next, we test the model with a new dataset (the test sample), but the model fit is very poor. What happened?
3.  We try again with another dataset (the validation sample), but the result remains unsatisfactory—the model fit is still poor.
4.  What does this mean? It means our model is biased toward the training sample and only works well with that particular dataset. It cannot generalize to new datasets and, therefore, lacks predictive power, making it unusable for real-world predictions.
5.  What caused this? The model was overtrained on the training sample, capturing even the noise and minor fluctuations in the data, leading to overfitting. This issue could stem from several factors, such as an imbalanced dataset, sampling bias in the training sample, or improper configurations of the machine learning algorithm used.
