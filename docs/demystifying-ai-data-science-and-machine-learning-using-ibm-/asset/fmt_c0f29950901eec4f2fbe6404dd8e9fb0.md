## Introduction

![](images/ufig16_1.jpg)

In this chapter, we explore one of the most robust and complex machine learning algorithms: the Bayesian network. Rooted in probabilistic reasoning and grounded in Bayes’ theorem, this method provides a powerful approach to inferring future events based on existing evidence. Central to its functionality is the construction of a causal network—a directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing causal relationships between predictors and target variables, supported by a set of conditional probabilities. With its inherent capacity for causal inference, the Bayesian network is a formidable tool for uncovering cause-and-effect relationships within large datasets.

This chapter offers a comprehensive introduction to the application of Bayesian network methods in data mining. We cover various supervised learning algorithms, including Naive Bayes, Tree Augmented Naive Bayes (TAN), Bayesian Network Augmented Naive (BAN) Bayes Classifier, and Markov Blanket. This chapter also discusses the causal data mining process and provides real-world examples to illustrate the practical application of Bayesian network methods.

Given the complexity of the mathematical framework underlying Bayesian networks, our focus is on key concepts and the analytical process rather than detailed mathematical formulations. We deliberately omit in-depth mathematical details to prevent confusion. As Bayesian network modeling is often automated by software, the emphasis is placed on understanding the fundamental principles, strengths, limitations, and interpretation of results. By presenting the probabilistic relationships between predictors and target variables in an accessible manner, we aim to improve comprehension of analysis outputs while maintaining the analytical rigor that is essential to the Bayesian approach.

### Correlation vs. Causation

Before defining a Bayesian network, it is important to understand the distinction between correlation and causation. Data mining has proven to be an effective method for detecting patterns in large, noisy datasets. In other words, it is an excellent tool for exploring vast amounts of data to uncover meaningful, previously unknown patterns that support decision-making processes in organizations. However, there are ongoing debates about the limitations of conventional data mining methods, such as regression. The most common criticism of these traditional methods is their inability to infer causation among variables ([Cox, 2010](#bibab10c.xhtml#refbib_29)).

While causation often implies correlation, correlation does not necessarily imply causation. To clarify this distinction, let's consider an example related to injury severity during a car accident (Figure 16.1). In this scenario, we examine the effects of two variables: age and failure to wear seatbelts. Suppose data analysis reveals a high correlation between age and injury severity. Although age may be highly correlated with injury severity, it is not the cause of the injury. The correlation simply suggests that people of different ages may experience varying levels of injury. For risk assessment purposes, age can serve as one of the predictors for injury severity, but it is not a factor that can be controlled to mitigate the risk of injury.
