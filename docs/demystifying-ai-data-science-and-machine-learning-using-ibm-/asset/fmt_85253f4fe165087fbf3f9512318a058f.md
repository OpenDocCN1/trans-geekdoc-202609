
AI systems require vast amounts of data to train effectively. A well-known example is Tesla's Full Self-Driving (FSD) system, which relies on extensive data collection to improve its autonomous capabilities. Similarly, smart assistants like Amazon Alexa, Apple Siri, and Google Assistant continuously process and analyze speech data to enhance their natural language understanding. Streaming services such as Netflix and Spotify rely on vast datasets of user preferences and viewing/listening habits to refine their recommendation algorithms. Facial recognition systems used in security and authentication, such as Apple's Face ID or airport biometric scanners, require massive image datasets to improve accuracy and reliability. Even healthcare AI systems, such as DeepMind Health, leverage vast amounts of medical records, imaging scans, and diagnostic histories to assist in disease prediction and treatment planning. With AI becoming increasingly integrated into various industries, the question remains—where do all these data come from, and where do they exist?

![](images/ufig1_3.jpg)

We are living in a world of shared information, AI, social networks, wireless connectivity, and digital communications. In this world, data are streamed and collected continuously in real time. Data exist in many forms, including numbers, texts, images, voice, and videos. Big tech companies like Apple, Amazon, Meta (Facebook), and Tesla continue to collect and archive user data on their servers. Manufacturing companies collect data on their products, operations, supply chains, inventories, logistics, and retailers. Service providers, such as hotels, resorts, casinos, banks, and airlines, gather data on their markets and customers. Our normal lives are also captured constantly via security cameras installed almost everywhere. We also choose to share our own data by sharing information, thoughts, pictures, and videos on social networks. If we go to the Internet and google, we can find everything that we need. It is not an understatement that we are overwhelmed with the amount of data that we have access to, not to mention the proprietary data that are not accessible to the public. Remember, not seeing the data does not mean they don't exist. They are on the CLOUD, waiting to be used for AI-driven advancements.

## Has Anyone Seen the TV Show *Person of Interest*?

![](images/ufig1_4.jpg)

With that vast amount of data stored in cloud-based servers, the question is how can we make use of the data to improve our lives, businesses, or customer experience? Let me start with one of my favorite TV shows, *Person of Interest*. It is a CBS sci-fi thriller show that aired from 2011 to 2016. The main character is Harold Finch, a math and computer genius. He received substantial government funding to develop an advanced machine learning algorithm and build an AI called *the Machine*. The main goal of this AI is to process and analyze all data being fed into it from various sources, including personal information, social networks, traffic data, business information, security information, government information, banking transactions, and others. Basically, the Machine receives all kinds of data that the government can provide, including numbers, texts, videos, images, and voices. The data are fed to the system in real time. So, you can imagine the volume of data the Machine receives every second. It certainly requires multiple supercomputers with very large bandwidth and very high-end servers to store that much data, not to mention the amount of energy needed to maintain the servers. Furthermore, computers with very high computing power are needed to process and analyze that huge amount of data promptly and effectively to produce practical outputs.

Finch developed a very robust and novel machine learning algorithm for the Machine, which took him several years to make it work properly. It was a tedious process with a lot of statistics, data analytics, computer programming, model training, and validation. But he finally succeeded. (Of course, we are never presented with the actual algorithm on the show.) Using the algorithm, the Machine is capable of discovering unknown patterns from the data to do one thing and one thing only, which is to predict a specific individual, a person of interest, that will be involved in crime or national security-related incidents. The prediction is not the name or address but the individual's social security number since it is a unique number assigned to each citizen. However, the Machine does not know whether this individual is a good guy or a bad guy; it only predicts that this person will be involved in an incident. It also does not know the reason or what caused the incident. In order to prevent the incident from happening, Finch hired an ex-Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) operative, John Reese, who has a set of special skills in hand-on-hand combat, weaponry, tactics, and covert operations. Reese's job is to find and follow that person of interest and intervene to stop the bad thing from happening. In each episode, the Machine makes a prediction, and the team rushes through time to follow the person of interest. Their goal is to determine whether the individual is a victim or perpetrator, enabling them to either save the person or prevent them from committing a harmful act.

While the synopsis may sound complicated, how the Machine functions is fairly straightforward. I presented that system in [Figure 1.1](#chapter1.xhtml#fig1_1) to demonstrate it. The input of the system is all data collected from various data sources in different forms, such as numbers, texts, voices, and images. The data are collected, stored, and fed to the cloud in real time. Imagine the amount of data the system has to process every second. The model uses machine learning algorithms to mine all of that data. It is a highly intelligent model that can learn, adapt, and improve itself, hence the name Artificial Intelligence. Of course, a lot of complicated algorithms are used in this model. The interesting thing is that the more data we collect and feed to the system, the more the model can learn and improve itself with new data via the model training process. In other words, the Machine learns from data (now you can see where the term *Machine Learning* comes from). Finally, the output of this system is the prediction of the target variable, more specifically, the person of interest represented by the social security number. It is a unique number assigned to one person, so there are no duplications.

The key takeaway from this series is that the Machine does not try to explain how the incident will happen because it has not yet happened, nor does it explain contributing factors based on any theory. It only analyzes the big data given to it and predicts what will happen in the near future. It does not know whether the person of interest is a victim or a perpetrator. Nonetheless, its prediction is never wrong; something terrible will happen involving that individual. Sounds fictional, right? Remember, it is a sci-fi show. Realistically, we should always account for the margin of error in prediction. In essence, it does not try to explain the incident but rather predicts it. Once Reese intervenes and stops the incident, then we will know what caused or may have caused the incident, but it is what we learn at the end of each episode. It is a great TV show, and if you are a fan of sci-fi thrillers, I strongly recommend watching it.
