
# An Institutional Research Board (IRB)

An Institutional Research Board (IRB), alternatively known as an independent ethics committee (IEC), ethical review board (ERB), or research ethics board (REB), constitutes a committee tasked with the application of research ethics. Its role primarily involves scrutinizing proposed research methods to ascertain their ethical validity. These boards are officially authorized to either grant approval or withhold it, oversee, and evaluate research that involves human subjects. Frequently, they engage in a thorough risk-benefit analysis to determine the ethical suitability of conducting the research. The primary objective of the IRB is to ensure the implementation of necessary measures for safeguarding the rights and well-being of individuals participating as subjects in a research study.

When a researcher conducts a study involving human subjects, they need to submit an application and get approved by two different IRBs: one at their own institution and another at the data provider's organization. For example, if I conduct a study involving individuals at an airline, I will need to submit applications to two different IRBs: one at the university where I work and another at the airline (which may use a different name than IRB). Both IRBs will review my application, and upon approval by both, I can proceed with my research.

## IRB Application Process

Each organization has different rules, processes, and requirements for IRB applications. The typical process includes the following steps:

1.  **Application submission**: The researcher completes an application for IRB review, detailing the research purposes, methodology, potential use of human subjects, data collection process, experimental design, questionnaire, potential harms to human subjects, and efforts to avoid those harms.

2.  **Initial review**: The IRB coordinator reviews the application and decides whether it should be reviewed by an IRB committee. If not, the researcher is informed of the exempt decision and can proceed with the research. If yes, an IRB committee, including experts in the field, will be formed.

3.  **Committee review**: The IRB committee reviews, discusses, and votes on the application. They may request additional information or clarification from the researcher.

4.  **Decision communication**: The IRB committee's decision is communicated to the researcher. The decision may include several options: exempt, approved, approved with conditions, or declined (resubmission required). Based on the decision, the researcher can either proceed with the research or must make changes and resubmit.

## How to De-Identify Data?

![Figure](images/ufig1_26.jpg)

Data de-identification is the process of removing or altering identifying information from a dataset to protect individuals’ privacy and anonymity. It is typically unnecessary for publicly available data since the data provider already de-identifies the data before posting it online. Nonetheless, it is essential to review the terms and conditions posted by the data provider regarding how to use the data. In many cases, the researcher must agree to these terms and conditions before being granted access to the data.

If we have access to secondary data with sensitive and private information, it is crucial to de-identify the data before analyzing it. First, we should check with the data provider for any specific information they want to be removed and any conditions they have regarding how the data is used. For example, some organizations may not allow us to publish the findings or may require us to hold the publication for a certain period before submitting it to a journal. Other organizations may require us to submit the final report with detailed findings for their review, while others may want to hold the rights to the reports for various reasons. All these conditions should be discussed and agreed upon in advance. Additionally, any relevant privacy rules must be reviewed and discussed. Once we have a complete list of information that must be removed from the data, we can proceed with de-identification.

The de-identification process usually includes the following steps.

1.  **Identify risks for re-identification**: In this step, we need to evaluate any harm to individuals or organizations if the data are re-identified. The severity of the impacts must be reviewed and recorded for risk assessment purposes. Additionally, we must assess the possibility that someone may be able to re-identify the data based on the information in the de-identified dataset. This means that sometimes the de-identified data may still contain some information that allows others to guess the hidden information, which is a serious issue.

2.  **Develop and apply de-identification measures**: Once we know the risks of re-identification, we can develop and apply de-identification measures. The common approach is to remove personal identification and sensitive information or replace it with anonymous information or a temporary ID. While this sounds simple, we need to develop a clear and detailed protocol to avoid missing anything. The protocol should include the variables that should be removed or changed, the values used for replacement, and the location of those variables and data in the dataset. Since we are dealing with large datasets, some macros or programs can be written to automate the de-identification process to save time and avoid missing important data.

3.  **Examine the data again**: Once we de-identify the data, it is vital to examine the new dataset to ensure the data are properly and completely de-identified. Since we are the ones who de-identified the data, there may be bias or oversights. Therefore, it is recommended to use external reviewers to examine the data. As they review the data for the first time, they are objective and can detect any issues we may have missed. This step is necessary to ensure the validity of the data de-identification.

4.  **Document the method**: It is important to document the de-identification methods and processes carefully. We also need to document how we archive the original data and de-identified data and how we ensure the safety of the original data. This documentation can be used as evidence that we have followed a proper protocol to minimize any harm to human subjects.

5.  **Keep the codes and guidelines for re-identification by authorized parties**: These codes and guidelines must be kept confidential and shared only with authorized parties, as they allow for the re-identification of the data.

These steps are essential for ensuring that the de-identification process effectively protects the privacy and anonymity of individuals involved in the data. It's always a good practice to stay updated with the latest guidelines and techniques in data privacy to enhance your de-identification protocols.
