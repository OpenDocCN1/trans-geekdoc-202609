
# Convenience Sampling

Convenience sampling involves selecting individuals who are readily accessible to the researcher. While it offers a straightforward and cost-effective means of collecting initial data, it falls short in producing results that can be generalized to the broader population. For instance, in a scenario where we’re researching opinions on employee services within our organization, asking only colleagues working at the same level to complete a survey is a convenient approach. However, this limits the sample's representativeness as it doesn’t encompass all employees at all levels within the organization.

## Voluntary Response Sampling

Voluntary response sampling relies predominantly on accessibility. In this context, individuals volunteer to participate rather than being actively selected and contacted by the researcher. It's important to note that voluntary response samples tend to carry at least some degree of bias, as certain individuals are naturally more inclined to volunteer than others, potentially leading to non-representative results. For example, we send out a survey to all employees at the organization regarding health insurance benefits, and a lot of them decide to complete it. While this approach can provide us with some insights into the subject, it's worth noting that the respondents are more likely to hold strong opinions about the services. Therefore, we cannot definitively assert that their viewpoints accurately reflect those of all employees.

## Purposive Sampling

Purposive sampling, also known as judgment sampling, requires us to use our expertise to select a sample that is most useful for the purposes of the research. It is often employed in qualitative research, especially when the researcher seeks in-depth insights into a specific phenomenon rather than making statistical generalizations. It is particularly useful when dealing with a small and specific population. To ensure the effectiveness of purposive sampling, it's essential to establish clear criteria and a rational basis for inclusion. For example, if our goal is to gain a deeper understanding of the opinions and experiences of disabled employees within the organization, we deliberately select several employees with diverse support needs. This deliberate selection allows us to gather a wide range of data regarding their experiences with employee services.

## Snowball Sampling

Snowball sampling involves recruiting participants through other participants. The researcher contacts a few people they know and asks them to refer others who might also participate. As more people are contacted, the sample size “snowballs,” hence the term. For example, if we are researching the experiences of the LGBTQ+ community in our city, and there is no list of all LGBTQ+ individuals, probability sampling is not feasible. We might start by interviewing one person who then refers us to others in the community.

As you can see, non-probability sampling methods are easier and cheaper to implement, but they come with a higher risk of sampling bias and may result in a sample that is not representative of the population. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to a larger population. In data mining research, where we typically work with larger datasets, probability sampling should be used to ensure the data are representative. This approach allows us to develop predictive models that are more generalizable and applicable to other datasets within the same population.

# Secondary Data and Sample Representativeness

![](images/ufig3_10.jpg)

When we collect data ourselves, we can select the sampling frame and apply one of the sampling methods described above to ensure the sample's representativeness. However, in many data mining projects, we rely on data collected by other organizations or parties—referred to as secondary data. In these cases, we treat the entire dataset as the sampling frame and use one of the sampling methods to draw a sample from it. But since we often don’t know how the original data were sampled or collected, how can we be sure that our sample is representative?

To address this uncertainty, I recommend an effective approach: analyzing the demographic information of the sample and comparing it with that of the target population. By demonstrating that our sample shares similar demographic attributes and characteristics with the population, we can confidently conclude that our sample is representative, even if we are unaware of the original data collection methods. This approach is also useful when we collect data ourselves. Even if we choose the sampling frame and use probability sampling, there are still uncertainties in the data collection process that may introduce sampling bias—factors beyond our control that can affect representativeness. For example, the individuals from whom we collect data may not be as representative as we anticipated. Therefore, to ensure representativeness, it is prudent to compare the demographic information of the sample with that of the population.

[Table 3.2](#chapter3.xhtml#tbl3_2) provides an example of comparing demographics between the sample and the target population. In most cases, demographic information about the population can be obtained from online sources such as the U.S. Census Bureau or other organizations’ publications. As shown in the table, while the characteristics of the sample and population may not be identical, the differences are minimal, and the data distributions are very similar. This comparison indicates the similarity between the sample and the population. In other words, our sample is representative of the population, and the findings from our sample can be confidently applied to the broader population.

# Sample Size

## What Is Sample Size, and Why Is It Important?

Sample size refers to the number of individuals or subjects included in a sample. In data mining, sample size is a crucial consideration, as an adequate sample is vital to ensuring the quality and reliability of the findings.

Below are some key reasons highlighting the importance of sample size:

![](images/ufig3_12.jpg)

1.  **Representative patterns**: Data mining aims to discover meaningful patterns and relationships within data. A larger sample size provides a more representative subset of the population, increasing the likelihood of capturing accurate and reliable patterns. This leads to more precise and representative results of the population being studied.

2.  **Increased generalizability**: A larger sample size enhances the generalizability of research findings. When the sample is more diverse and includes a greater number of individuals or cases, the results are more likely to apply to the broader population from which the sample is drawn, thereby improving the external validity of the study.

3.  **Reliable model building**: Data mining often involves building predictive models or classifiers. A larger sample size allows for more robust and accurate model building, as the model can capture a wider range of variations and complexities in the data, leading to more reliable and generalizable predictions.
