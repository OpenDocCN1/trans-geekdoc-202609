First, data mining is a data-driven method. We use data mining to discover unknown or new patterns from large datasets. In other words, we use machine learning algorithms to extract information or uncover unknown patterns solely from the data. Essentially, we want to know what the data tell us. It differs from traditional statistical research, which tends to be theory-driven. Traditional research constructs relationships and hypotheses from existing theories, collects data to test these hypotheses, and confirms with evidence whether they are supported. These studies focus mainly on variables of interest, supported by literature, and often use traditional statistical methods such as `t-tests`, `ANOVA`, `MANOVA`, multiple linear regression, logistic regression, and structural equation modeling for testing correlations. In contrast, data mining does not rely on pre-existing hypotheses or theories because we want to find new patterns directly from the data. Hence, the quality of the data plays a very important role since noisy and incomplete data may provide meaningless results or even false outcomes. Therefore, to make data mining useful, we need large datasets with a large number of variables and records. We will discuss the characteristics of big data later.

Second, data mining research is exploratory; that is, we use data mining to explore new variables and relationships within data. We do not try to speculate in advance on what relationships are significant and which variables are important. Essentially, we want the data to tell us these outcomes. On the contrary, traditional statistical research is explanatory and tends to focus on confirming hypothesized relationships. Hence, those studies are limited to pre-identified variables of interest and their relationships based on the literature. A common question in data mining research is, “Do you discover any new variables, relationships, or patterns?” In other words, the findings of data mining research may surprise us, which makes them meaningful in predictive research.

19. Of course, to discover new variables and relationships, we cannot limit ourselves to small datasets with limited variables. Therefore, the third characteristic of data mining is large data. Large data means datasets with a large number of variables and records in different forms and structures. Since we do not know what we may find, we need large data. As illustrated in the TV show *Person of Interest*, AI needs access to a huge amount of data, many of which are rather sensitive, to make accurate predictions. This is different from theory-driven, explanatory research, which usually uses smaller datasets with limited variables. When performing statistical analysis techniques such as `ANOVA` and multiple regression, or even structural equation modeling, we usually use a formula to find the minimum sample size needed to detect relationships at a particular significance level and expected test power. Typically, a sample size between 100 and 300 is sufficient, with models having 3–10 variables. On the contrary, in data mining research, we need to analyze data with hundreds of variables and millions of rows. Since data mining is data-driven, noise and missing values may negatively affect the quality of outcomes. Having larger data helps address these issues.

20. Due to the size of the data, data mining methods are not subject to strict statistical assumptions and can handle noisy and unstructured data. They usually do not require specific underlying data distributions. Many machine learning algorithms can perform well with missing values and outliers since they are rule-based rather than statistics-based. However, we still need to ensure data quality to obtain meaningful outcomes. The phrase “garbage in, garbage out” still applies here. On the contrary, theory-driven studies tend to use statistical techniques with a smaller sample size, which requires meeting strict statistical assumptions, such as normality, non-multicollinearity, linearity, and homogeneity. These requirements tend to create obstacles in the data analysis process and limit the inclusion of variables in the model.
