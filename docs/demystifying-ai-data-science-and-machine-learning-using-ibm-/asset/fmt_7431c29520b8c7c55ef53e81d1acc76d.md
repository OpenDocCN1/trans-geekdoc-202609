
*   `x[1] to x[n]` are predictor variables 1 *− n*.

*   `β[0]` is the intercept

*   `β[1]` – `β[n]` are regression coefficients for the corresponding predictors,

*   `ε` is the residual or error.

Another form of this equation, which is easier to interpret, expresses the odds ratio as follows:

$$Odds\left( Y \right) = \left( \frac{p}{1 - p} \right) = e^{(\beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2} + \ldots + \beta_{n}x_{n} + \varepsilon)~}$$

Don’t be intimidated by these equations! We won’t use them directly in the analysis or interpretation of the results. I will explain the results in a simpler way.

### Estimation Approach

Logistic regression estimates the target variable and regression coefficients differently from linear regression. The method used is **maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)**. Simply put, we first assume a probability distribution for the target variable. Then, a likelihood function is used to calculate the probability of an outcome based on the predictors. This method is useful for estimating the probability of an event and does not require strict assumptions like **OLS**.

### Assumption Requirements

As mentioned, due to the nature of the MLE method, logistic regression does not require strict assumptions like normality, homoscedasticity, or linearity. However, logistic regression typically involves the following assumptions:

*   **Binary target variable**: The target variable must be binary, meaning it has two distinct levels (e.g., 1—accident, 0—no accident).

*   **Multiple predictors**: Logistic regression requires multiple predictors but does not require them to be continuous. It works well with binary or nominal predictors, offering flexibility in scale. This is advantageous when analyzing large datasets with predictors on different scales.

*   **Non-multicollinearity**: Similar to linear regression, predictors must be independent of each other. However, with large sample sizes, the effect of multicollinearity is often tolerable.

*   **Linearity**: There must be a linear relationship between the predictors and the logit of the target variable. The equation shows a linear regression relationship between the logit of the target variable and the predictors.

In conclusion, logistic regression offers more flexibility in its assumptions, making it a robust method for data mining across various types of data.

### How to Read and Understand Logistic Regression Results

Before interpreting logistic regression results, we must ensure we have a good model fit. Let's assume that we have already checked the logistic regression model fit metrics, such as −**2LL**, the **Chi-square test for** −**2LL**, **pseudo R²**, **RMSE**, **RSE**, **EP**, **misclassification rate**, **accuracy**, **sensitivity**, **specificity**, **F1 score**, **Gini coefficient**, **ROC chart**, and **Lift chart**, and found the model fit to be satisfactory.

The next step is to interpret the logistic regression results to identify significant predictors and their effects on the target variable.

Typically, the logistic regression estimation results include the following:

*   Logistic coefficient (B)
*   Standard error
*   Wald statistic—a test for the significance of the coefficients
*   Degrees of freedom
*   Probability value (p-value)
*   Exp(B) or odds ratio
*   95% confidence interval for Exp(B)

We focus on the key outputs that help us interpret the results. Logistic regression can be more challenging to read due to the use of the logit function. To simplify the process, we will primarily focus on the **p-value**, **logistic coefficients**, and **odds ratio**.

For example, suppose the regression equation for a model predicting students’ decision to choose major A is as follows:

`Logit(majorA_choice) = 0.5 – 0.3 * gender + 0.4 + age`

In this equation, 0.5 is the intercept, −0.3 is the logistic coefficient for **gender**, and 0.4 is the coefficient for **age**. The sign of the coefficient indicates the direction of the effect. In this case, **gender** has a negative effect on the choice of major A, while **age** has a positive effect.

While coefficients indicate the size of the effect, interpreting them in relation to the logit value (instead of the actual target variable) can be difficult. To make interpretation easier, we use the **odds ratio**. The equivalent equation in terms of odds is as follows:

`Odds(majorA_choice) = e^((0.5 – 0.3 * gender + 0.4 * age))`
