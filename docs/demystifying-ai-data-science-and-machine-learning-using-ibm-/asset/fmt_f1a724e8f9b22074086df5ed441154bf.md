
# CRISP-DM Process

In [Chapter 1](#chapter1.xhtml), we defined data mining and discussed its primary characteristics. It is an exploratory, data-driven method to detect unknown patterns from large datasets and provide insights that enable informed business decisions. To properly perform data mining, we need to follow a process that guides us through specific steps to explore and analyze the data accurately. Since large datasets tend to be noisy, the analysis outcome may be affected by this noise, leading to less accurate results and potentially wrong decisions. In the business world, mispredictions can have substantial consequences. Imagine an airline sending a promotion package to the wrong passenger and losing another passenger, a bank executive opening a new branch in an incorrect location and losing money, or a false negative prediction in healthcare delaying a patient's treatment. These incorrect business decisions could be catastrophic for both the business and its customers.

We all know that any prediction has a margin of error. Our goal is to keep this margin within an acceptable range, which varies by organization and context. Depending on the organization's strategies, this could be 1%, 5%, or even 10%. For instance, in the service industry with high demand fluctuations, executives may accept a 10% error. On the other hand, in healthcare or aviation safety, a 1% error might be the maximum acceptable threshold due to the significant impact on outcomes.

So, how can we avoid or at least minimize such problems? The best practice is to follow a valid data mining process when analyzing the data. A good process guides us through essential steps to ensure the analysis is conducted correctly, avoiding common data mining mistakes. On the Internet or in other books, you may find various steps for the data mining process. Many of these are based on the authors' opinions and experiences, and some can be useful, especially for specific project types. However, in some cases, we might question whether such a process has been validated and can be generalized to other types of projects. Using an invalid process may result in questionable outcomes.

As stated in [Chapter 1](#chapter1.xhtml), data mining is an exploratory, data-driven method. This means the study's outcome is dependent on the quality of the data and the analysis process. It is imperative to conduct the analysis correctly to produce quality outcomes. Following incorrect steps could lead to invalid results, which, in turn, will lead to wrong business decisions. For example, if we train a model without first cleaning the data, the noise in the data might lead to messy outcomes that are neither interpretable nor meaningful. The prediction outcomes may be erratic or contradictory, making them useless for decision-making. In another scenario, we might overtrain the model with a lot of data, achieving high accuracy. However, without validating the model with a separate sample, it might not generalize to other datasets due to overfitting. When applied to new datasets, such a model may produce predictions with a high margin of error, rendering it invalid and unusable in the real world. Consequently, decision-makers might be misinformed and make wrong decisions based on these incorrect predictions. Therefore, it is crucial to follow a valid data mining process to ensure the quality of the prediction outcomes.

In this book, I will introduce data mining processes that are validated, widely accepted, and used in both academia and industry. These include Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM) and Sample-Explore-Modify-Model-Assess (SEMMA).

## CRISP-DM Process

CRISP-DM, which stands for Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining, is based on the European Strategic Program on Research in Information Technology initiative (ESPRIT). It is more popular in the industry than in academia, and IBM has adopted and implemented this process in its software SPSS Modeler.

CRISP-DM breaks the process of data mining into six major steps: Business Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Modeling, Evaluation, and Deployment ([Figure 2.1](#chapter2.xhtml#fig2_1)). Below is a detailed overview of each step in the CRISP-DM process:

### Business Understanding

*   **Define the business problem or objective**: Clearly articulate the business goal you want to achieve through data mining.
*   **Translate the business goal into a data mining problem**: Understand how the business goal can be addressed using data and analytics.
*   **Identify success criteria**: Define measurable metrics that indicate the success of the project.

### Data Understanding

*   **Collect initial data**: Gather relevant data from various sources, including databases, files, and external systems.
*   **Describe data**: Document the structure and content of the collected data, including variables, formats, and data quality issues.
*   **Explore data**: Perform initial exploratory data analysis to identify patterns, trends, and potential issues.
*   **Verify data quality**: Assess the quality, accuracy, and completeness of the data.

### Data Preparation

*   **Clean and preprocess data**: Address missing values, outliers, and inconsistencies in the data. Transform and format data for analysis.
*   **Integrate data**: Combine data from different sources into a unified dataset.
*   **Select relevant variables**: Choose the variables (features) that are most relevant to the analysis and discard unnecessary ones.
*   **Create derived variables**: Generate new variables or features that might enhance the predictive power of the model.

### Modeling

*   **Select modeling techniques**: Choose appropriate algorithms or models based on the nature of the problem (classification, regression, clustering, etc.).
*   **Build models**: Apply the selected algorithms to the prepared dataset to create predictive or descriptive models.
*   **Assess model quality**: Evaluate the performance of the models using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, etc.
*   **Fine-tune models**: Adjust model parameters to optimize performance or prevent overfitting.

### Evaluation

*   **Evaluate model performance**: Assess how well the models perform on unseen data using validation techniques (e.g., cross-validation).
*   **Compare models**: Compare the performance of different models to select the best one for the business problem.
*   **Review results with stakeholders**: Present the findings and insights to stakeholders and gather their feedback.

### Deployment

*   **Plan deployment**: Create a strategy for deploying the selected model in a real-world business environment.
*   **Implement the model**: Integrate the model into the operational systems and processes.
*   **Monitor model performance**: Continuously monitor the model's performance and update it as needed to maintain accuracy and relevance.
*   **Document the process**: Document the entire data mining process, including decisions, methods, and outcomes.

Note that the sequence of the steps is not linear. Actually, the process requires moving back and forth between specific steps. The process diagram employs arrows to signify the primary and recurring interconnections between phases. The outer circle within the diagram serves as a representation of the iterative aspect inherent to data mining. The data mining process continues even after the deployment of a solution. Insights gained throughout this process have the potential to instigate fresh business inquiries, and future data mining endeavors stand to gain from the insights accumulated in prior iterations.
