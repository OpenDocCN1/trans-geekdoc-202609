
# Market Relations and Order Types

## Market Depth and Order Queues

In market relations, buyers establish the price at which they wish to acquire an asset, while sellers set the price at which they wish to sell an item. Simply put, it is a form of bargaining in which a buyer proposes a lesser price while a seller insists on a higher price. On the bitcoin exchange, this is the condition that has been witnessed.

Customers' purchases with defined prices are arranged in a sort of queue. If you want to be the first in this line, you must provide the most competitive pricing. At the same time, sellers who are willing to sell for the asking price are lining up. The first person in this line is also the one who has the most competitive pricing. Each pricing includes an indication of the volume of goods a buyer or a seller wishes to purchase or sell. Traders refer to such lines of pending orders as "market depth" on both the bid and ask sides of the market.

## Market Orders vs Limit Orders

Here's an illustration: Consider the following scenario: you place a market order to purchase coins for $5,000, but the value of the coins available is just $1,000.

The price you pay for the coins at the time your purchase is processed will be somewhere in the middle of these two ranges of prices. I should also point out that the exchange is also artistic and always delighted to earn a little money from you. If you place a market order, you can be almost certain that you will never receive the first asking price. As a result, you've submitted your order and, for example, have received a price of 244.78 dollars.

You can also take advantage of the simplest approach to make money on the exchange without taking any risks: by widening the gap, which is the difference between the bid and ask prices, in this case.

To avoid similar occurrences in the future, I propose putting in a limit order. A limit order is a purchase or sale order for a specific quantity of an asset at a defined price that is specified in advance. In this case, we are concerned with two parameters: price and volume. For example, you might make an order to purchase coins worth $5,000 at a cost of $243.47 each coin. To begin with, you can place a buy limit order in the bid line or anywhere else on the market. As a result, you join the queue of buyers who are waiting for their orders to be fulfilled. There is another option: you can go to the sellers and negotiate a price that is not lower than yours, but that is not lower than theirs.

On the other hand, it is possible that there is not enough inventory to fulfill your request. What should you do in order to achieve the desired volume? You should consider the general liquidity of the market and the depth of the market when placing your order. In other words, you must look into the orders that have been placed. Despite the fact that you theoretically have a chance to acquire the best price and volume positions, there is always the risk that someone else has already made an order and taken this volume because the information is occasionally shown with a delay. Alternatively, if you still want to complete your order (without using a market order to avoid significant slippage), you can put a limit order at a price at which your order will definitely be executed, rather than at a price at which you wish your order would be executed. This signifies that you have a very good chance of filling your purchase at a price that has been quoted by a vendor. If these orders are still available in the market and you've been successful in filling them, you've increased your chances of getting your order filled by offering a somewhat inflated price.

## Stop Orders and Stop-Limit Orders

The subject of stop orders will now be discussed further. A stop order is an order to purchase or sell a stock when the price of the stock exceeds a predetermined threshold.

Consider the following scenario: you have fixed your exit price at $200. As a result, whenever the price exceeds $200, your stop order is converted into a standard market order and executed. I, on the other hand, do not recommend that you employ this form of order.

Stop-limit orders are also available, which combine the traits that make stop orders unique with those that make limit orders unique. Stop-limit orders require the establishment of two price points in order to be effective. The first point, which is referred to as the stop, marks the beginning of the defined action, while the second point, which is referred to as the limit, represents the outside of the investor's goal price, which is referred to as the limit.

In my opinion, a stop-limit order is even worse than a stop order in terms of execution. Even if a price hits a specified stop price, it does not guarantee that the order will be executed (because the limiter might not work). As a result, I do not recommend utilizing stop-limit orders to close positions with the goal of limiting losses (stop-loss). Furthermore, not all brokers are willing to accept this form of trading request.

Stop orders should shield you from losses because they allow you to close a position if anything unexpectedly begins to fall in value. Rather than working in this manner, cease-and-desist orders will be rendered ineffective. Even if you believe you have placed a stop order, it will almost certainly be converted into a market order once the price hits a specific level, and your position will be closed. When you utilize a stop-limit order, on the other hand, it is reasonable that your stop will be converted into a limit. This means that your limit order will be executed if there is a price available in the market to execute it. You will, however, simply stand in line and wait if the market does not provide you with a reasonable price. A stop-limit order, in my opinion, does not make any sense in this situation.

## GTC and Fill or Kill Orders

Orders such as GTC and Fill or Kill are examples of this. Until an investor cancels or the deal is performed, a GTC order remains in effect. In this sense, a pre-arranged order is really convenient. A fill or kill order is a variation on a limit order. If you placed a limit order in the preceding scenarios, you may have received only a portion of the volume you sought. However, with a fill or kill order, your order must be completed in full or it will be cancelled.

# Margin Trading

To put it another way, the essence of margin trading is fairly straightforward: you trade with borrowed money. With margin trading, you pay a portion of the stock price (referred to as the margin) and borrow the balance from other players who are willing to lend you money. It is only necessary to use the balance of your margin account to serve as this borrowed money and to settle loan expenses when this is required. In other words, margin trading permits you to trade with money that you don't actually have on hand to trade with. Leverage is the term used to describe the borrowed funds you use to trade.

Consider the following scenario: you have $10,000. It is, in a relative sense, your margin of safety. Consider the following scenario: you decide to trade with 1:4 leverage. This signifies that you are permitted to do a transaction of $40,000 or more. You have a 25 percent profit margin. You purchase a currency for $40,000, and if the value of the purchased currency increases, your profit increases fourfold; however, if the value of the purchased currency decreases, your losses increase fourfold. As a result, if you purchase cryptocurrencies for $10,000, you will only lose all of your money if the value of your asset (for example, Bitcoin) collapses to zero. If you purchase bitcoin for $40,000 using leverage on a margin account, you will lose all of your personal funds ($10,000) if the asset falls by 25% in value.
