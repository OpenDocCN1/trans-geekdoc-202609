# Chapter 11. ICOs

We'll go through the fundamentals of ICOs (Initial Coin Offerings), how they differ from initial public offerings (IPOs), how to tell the difference between a legitimate ICO and a scam, and how to participate in an ICO.

## ICOs vs. IPOs

All stock investors are aware of the abbreviation IPO (Initial Public Offering), which refers to the first time a company's stock is made available to the public. The purchasers of stocks become co-owners of the corporation, and the corporation itself receives money from sales, which is typically greater than its annual return. ICO, or Initial Coin Offering, is a new abbreviation that has just appeared in the cryptocurrency industry that is similar to the previous one:

An initial public offering (IPO) is a valid (from a legal standpoint) activity. An initial public offering (IPO) is the process by which a company with an established and maturing corporate structure seeks to raise capital. The simplest and most cost-effective method of raising funds is through the sale of stock. At the same time, the process of launching an initial public offering (IPO) is extremely expensive. In order for a firm to be admitted to the exchange, it must meet a number of standards, including meeting the required capitalization level and passing an audit. In addition, the corporation should seek out underwriters who will be responsible for selling the company's stock. Once the word has gotten out, money and investors will be attracted, and the company will begin trading its stock on the stock exchange. It will be a long road ahead. As a result, it is a time-consuming and expensive process.

Aside from that, a deficit is purposefully established within the scope of an IPO, and only a limited number of the company's shares are offered for sale on the stock exchange.

The developers of ICO campaigns also state that the quantity of coins available is limited, and as a result, many people panic, believing that if they do not purchase these coins now, they will be forced to purchase this currency later at a higher price.

Both initial public offerings (IPOs) and initial coin offerings (ICOs) are riskier ways to generate money, but they may also be extremely successful. People join in initial coin offerings (ICOs) in the hopes of making quick cash. And as soon as these individuals receive their money back, they promptly withdraw it from their accounts.

As a result, when people begin to sell their coins, the price of the coins rises dramatically at first, before falling rapidly. Speculators employ a variety of strategies, one of which is this.

In contrast to initial coin offerings (ICOs), such speculations are relatively infrequent in initial public offerings (IPOs) due to the so-called "lock-up period." People who purchased shares prior to the initial public offering (IPO) will not be able to sell them until three months after the IPO. With initial coin offerings, you can purchase coins even at the pre-ICO stage.

Let us list the reasons why so many individuals are willing to bear the risk of investing in initial coin offerings (ICOs). While some individuals spend money on these ventures just to show their support, other people will buy and sell them rapidly. A third group of people invests money in these projects because they have heard about them somewhere or, even worse, because they want to replicate the success of their friends.

## How to Distinguish Between Promising ICOs and Pyramid Schemes

All of the companies that have launched initial coin offerings (ICOs) are startups that do not even have a so-called Minimum Viable Product (MVP). They simply have a vague notion. In fact, the majority of these businesses are rapidly resembling pyramid scams in that you are guaranteed that if you put money at the first level, your investments will be doubled at the second stage and will rise fourfold at the third level. In other words, investors are offered a game-like experience in order to give them the notion that they will be able to get rich soon.

Let's have a look at how an initial coin offering is carried out.

Prior to launching their official crowdsale or initial coin offering campaign, the companies hold a pre-ICO, which is a token sale event. This event is often performed to gather funds for marketing purposes, namely to pay for a public relations campaign during the initial coin offering (ICO). It sounds something like this: "We raise money for advertising in order to prepare an even stronger campaign later on and collect money again."

In addition, certain initial coin offerings (ICOs) will no longer proclaim that a specific number (or greater number) of funds is required for the actual project implementation, allowing the market to gather the largest feasible quantity. This is how some people manage to establish a firm with a market capitalization in the billions of dollars.

The IOTA cryptocurrency is a shining example. It is a highly scalable currency that can handle a large number of transactions. IOTA is currently worth about $2 billion, according to market estimates. There is, however, one snag. The organization makes no guarantees and holds no assets (except for the site, of course). That is to say, the design of the company's website is valued at about $2 billion by the general public. Isn't it clear that this is a bad investment?

I admit that some initial coin offerings (ICOs) see significant increases in real value, and individuals who are able to participate in them can make a significant profit. However, all such initial coin offerings (ICOs) suffer the same fate, which might be compared to an elevator that cannot go higher when it is completely packed. Because of this, there is a tremendous amount of speculative money in such initial coin offerings; every price increase causes these investors to sell everything, close their positions, and therefore impose pressure on the stock market.

Having said all of this, I do not aim to lead you to assume that all initial coin offering (ICO) projects are fraudulent. In fact, I simply want to demonstrate to you that the majority of these startups are vastly overestimated, with barely 2 percent to 3 percent of them surviving to maturity.

To make money from initial coin offerings (ICOs), you must first and foremost understand why you are investing in them. As a result, the reason for your transaction entry should be the same as the reason for your transaction exit. If you invest in an ICO just because you believe in the potential of a future product, you are most likely not making the best choice. First and foremost, allow the company to access the market and begin trading. If you still believe in the company's product, you can always purchase it at a later date, perhaps even at a discount.
