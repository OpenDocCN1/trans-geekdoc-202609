## ABSTRACT

Researchers have extensively used convolutional neural networks to detect meteor falls on Earth. However, when dealing with limited available data, these networks may need more robustness to classify new real-world images correctly. This study proposes an optimized training approach of a pre-trained model with an attention mechanism to achieve better generalization results in such a scenario. We compare two architectures, an optimized base model and another version with an attention mechanism. Furthermore, we present a new and publicly available optical meteor dataset that merges several public data sources. We used the merged dataset to train classification models combined with a stratified five-fold cross-validation strategy to determine the reliability of the prediction. The experimental results from both architectures showed good and similar performance. To further determine the best architecture, we performed an additional analysis with visual explanations in new observations. The architecture with an attention mechanism was the best model achieving a false alarm ratio of 2.6% and an accuracy of 97%.

## 1. Introduction

When meteoroids in space enter Earth's upper atmosphere and begin to glow, they become meteors. When a meteor falls, the excitation of air molecules produces the light and color of its trail. Meteors travel in different orbits around the Sun and can strike Earth from any angle. Unlike other pursuits of observation in astronomy, meteor observers do not need expensive equipment to spot them (Lunsford, 2009).

Detecting meteor falls plays a critical role in understanding the formation of the Solar System, because we can obtain much relevant information from meteor observations, such as direction, speeds, and masses (Koschny et al., 2019).

There are geographically distributed meteor network observation communities worldwide with the help of citizen scientists. The Brazilian Meteor Observation Network (BRAMON) is one of the largest meteor monitoring networks in the world, considering the number of stations and the covered area, (Betzler, 2022). However, cameras inevitably capture events similar to meteors, such as aircraft, satellites, clouds, flashing lights, and atmospheric events, which challenge the detection by humans or systems. Therefore, it is essential to establish a robust classifier to discriminate meteors from other sorts of objects correctly.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) have achieved remarkable results in computer vision, surpassing humans in applications such as object recognition (He et al., 2015). Therefore, we can attach attention mechanisms to CNNs to select relevant features. Previous studies have adopted CNN classification models to predict the “meteor” or “non-meteor” class of optical observations. Although this approach leads to compelling results, it is much more data-hungry due to the number of parameters to include in the estimation process (Yamashita et al., 2018). This situation can lead to a lack of robustness when dealing with limited training data due to overfitting.

In this study, we investigate the use of attention mechanisms with optimized CNN architecture training to achieve better generalization results when detecting meteors in sky images. We show that this approach has the potential to overcome the need for large volumes of data and improves the prediction robustness in the application of meteor detection. Furthermore, we present a new and publicly available optical observation of 7000 balanced meteors and non-meteors images by merging three public data sources to aid future work. We also performed a visual explanation analysis with the Grad-CAM algorithm on new images to determine the best architecture by analyzing the features to which the classifiers pay more attention.

We organize the rest of this paper as follows. Section 2 presents the related work. Then, Section 3 describes the methodology adopted in the study. Section 4 shows and discusses the results of the experiments we conducted in this research. Section 5 presents the final remarks on this work.

Automatic meteor detection has received more attention as astronomers employ machine learning methods. Researchers have proposed several approaches with different data sizes and sources, with successful results. For instance, Cicco et al. (2017) explored the application of machine learning deep learning algorithms by applying random forest (RF), long short-term memory (LSTM), and CNNs for the Cameras for All-Sky Meteor Surveillance (CAMS) datasets. Their work claims the capacity of deep learning techniques to achieve state-of-the-art results without performing a manual feature selection as in machine learning.

However, the same amount of labeled data is only sometimes readily available. Therefore, this section discusses the methodologies used in our study to implement the optimization of a VGG16 model attached to CBAM attention mechanisms to improve generalization with a dataset of 7000 image data.

In turn, Gural (2019) performed a recurrent neural network (RNN) and a CNN named MeteorNet to automate the post-detection screening of potential meteor tracks in the CAMS pipeline. He obtained 100,000 meteors and 100,000 non-meteors from video imaging, with no data augmentation, to train and evaluate the algorithms. Since CNNs are suitable for handling spatial information, MeteorNet leveraged this feature to capture 99.94% of meteor falls. In contrast, RNNs processing temporal or sequential data achieved 98.1% positive images of meteors. Because our study’s available training data is much smaller, we focus on achieving comparable results by optimizing the CNN architecture and its training.

### 3.2. Meteor image dataset

More recently, Al-Owais et al. (2023) introduced object detection using YOLO algorithms of meteor and non-meteor objects by reaching 98.5% recall. They achieved this result by employing 16,965 imbalanced images from the U.A.E. Meteor Monitoring Network. Although one can easily integrate both works to operate as a detection system, the current research focuses on improving the meteor detection stage in the scenario of limited labeled data.

The meteor image dataset we used contains 7000 unique sky photos of the occurrence or non-occurrence of falling meteors. Different instruments recorded the images in the period from 2014 to 2023. Therefore, we balanced the dataset with a 55:45 ratio of meteors to non-meteors to prevent the model from becoming biased towards one class. Table 1 presents the summary statistics of the dataset.

In close connection to our work, Galindo and Lorena (2018) introduce the application of a pre-trained model and exploit different approaches. They performed their experiments in a dataset of 1000 images of meteors and 660 images of non-meteors. They merged this dataset with data from the Universidade do Vale do Paraíba and the EXOSS website. Marsola and Lorena (2019) also applied transfer learning with a smaller dataset using the same data sources. Both works showed that fine-tuning was an essential technique to improve performance accuracy. However, Galindo and Lorena (2018) demonstrated how data augmentation worsens performance accuracy in their results, even when using small augmentation data.

Three different data sources provided a diverse set of sky scenes, as Fig. 1 exemplifies. The instruments captured the images mainly at night with the presence of other events, such as aircraft lights, satellites, clouds, flashing lights, image errors, and atmospheric events.

In our work, we performed fine-tuning and did not use data augmentation as an essential element to leverage the model performance. Although other works mentioned here achieved successful accuracy results, it is unclear whether the robustness capacity is suitable to correctly distinguish meteors from other events, such as aircraft lights or other atmospheric occurrences. In our work, we concentrate on attaining the same results concerning the robustness of focusing on the relevant features with attention mechanisms.

In our experiments, we resized all the images in the dataset to 224 × 224 pixels in the RGB color space, and then we rescaled each image by a factor of 1/255 using a data generator. We did not perform any data augmentation as it can decrease performance accuracy (Galindo and Lorena, 2018). We also cropped 24 pixels from the lower part of each image to remove the captions, which can potentially be used as a feature in the prediction task.

### 3.2.1. Data sources

The meteor dataset we used in this work derives from three primary data sources: the Brazilian Meteor Observation Network (BRAMON, 2023), the UK Meteor Network (UKMON, 2023), and the Base des Observateurs Amateurs de Météores (BOAM, 2023) repositories. Since observers can spot meteors from different angles depending on locations, they are encouraged to contribute by mounting their stations, which provide valuable scientific data on meteor falls.

There are successful attempts to use deep neural networks by leveraging substantial amounts of data, as many meteor monitoring stations produce vast amounts of video data of the skies daily.

BRAMON has approximately 130 stations and a covered area of 1.3 × 10^6 km^2 on Brazil’s territory. BRAMON is considered one of the largest meteor monitoring networks in the world according to the factors described (Betzler, 2022). Therefore, the images from BRAMON in our dataset are responsible for approximately 66% of meteors and 87% of non-meteors. Moreover, most raw images have diverse characteristics, such as resolutions, aspect ratios, brightness, and colors.
