As defined in the 802.11 standard, WEP supports only a 40-bit cryptographic key for the size used. However, numerous vendors offer nonstandard extensions of WEP that support keys from 40 to 84 bits. At least one vendor supports a keysize of 128 bits. The 104-bit key, with a 24-bit Initialization Vector (IV) becomes a 128-bit RC4 key. In general, all other things being equal, increasing the key size increases the security of the cryptographic technique. However, it is always possible for flawed implementations or flawed designs to prevent long keys from increasing security. Research shows that key sizes of greater than 80-bits, for robust designs and implementations, make brute-force cryptanalysis (code breaking) an impossible task. For 80-bit keys, the number of possible keys—a keyspace of more than 10^24—exceeds contemporary computing power. In practice, most WLAN deployments use 40-bit keys. Moreover, recent attacks have shown that the WEP approach for privacy is, unfortunately, vulnerable to certain attacks regardless of keysize. However, the cryptographic standards, and vendor WLAN communities have developed enhanced WEP, which is available as a prestandard vendor-specific implementations. This security service was designed to reject any messages that had been changed by an active adversary “in the middle.” This technique uses a simple encrypted Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC) approach.
