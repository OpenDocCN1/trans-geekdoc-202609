
Data Compression: Digital signals are well-suited for data compression techniques, enabling efficient storage and transmission of information.

Processing and Manipulation: Digital signals can be easily processed, manipulated, and converted using digital logic circuits and algorithms, making them suitable for various digital devices and applications.

Error Correction: Digital signals allow for error correction and detection through error-correcting codes and algorithms.

Applications: Digital signals are commonly used in modern communication systems, computers, smartphones, digital audio, video, and data transmission over the Internet.

# Analog Signal:

- Continuous Values: Analog signals are continuous in nature and can take on any value within a certain range. They are represented as varying voltage levels or continuous waveforms.

- Susceptibility to Noise: Analog signals are more susceptible to noise and interference during transmission, which can lead to signal degradation and quality loss.

- Signal Degradation: Analog signals are prone to degradation over long distances due to factors like attenuation and distortion.

- Limited Processing: Analog signals are more challenging to process and manipulate directly, requiring analog circuits and specialized techniques for amplification and filtering.

- No Exact Reproduction: Analog signals cannot be perfectly regenerated or reproduced without some loss of quality.

- Applications: Analog signals are used in traditional telephony, audio systems, radio broadcasting, analog television, and various sensors that measure continuous physical quantities like temperature and pressure.

Digital signals offer advantages in terms of noise immunity, error correction, and ease of processing, making them suitable for modern digital communication and computing systems.

Analog signals, on the other hand, are essential for applications that require continuous representations of physical quantities but are more susceptible to noise and signal degradation. Both types of signals have their roles in today's diverse and interconnected world of electronic communication and technology.

# Asynchronous vs. Synchronous Transmission Modes

Data is transmitted through communication devices in multiples of fixed-length units, typically 8-bits. For example, if the computer is transferring a source program, the data will be made up of a block of 8-bit binary-encoded characters. On the other hand, if the data is in the form of a compiled object code of the program, the data will be made up of a block of 8-bit bytes. At the receiving end, the following parameters are determined to decode and interpret the message correctly.

- The start of each bit period.
- The start and end of each character or byte.
- The start and stop of each complete message block.

These three parameters are known as bit synchronization, character, or byte synchronization, respectively. In general, there are two transmission modes; they are synchronous and asynchronous modes. In a synchronous mode, the transmitter and the receiver use the same clock. But in an asynchronous mode, two independent clocks are used on either side.

# Synchronous mode

Asynchronous transmission is used only when the rate at which characters generated is unknown or the synchronous data rate is too low. For the transmission of a large block of data at relatively higher bit rates, synchronous transmission is used. In synchronous mode, the sending and receiving devices are synchronized with a common clock signal. This eliminates the need for the start and stop bits. The complete block of data is transmitted with fixed time interval between the bits. Before the start of transmission, clocks at both ends are to be synchronized.

This is achieved by sending special character bytes called sync bytes or sync characters between the sender and the receiver. The sender informs the receiver about the start of a block. The receiver figures out the start of each character by knowing the coding scheme. If the sender is idle or does not transmit any character, the receiver searches for the next group of sync characters. The devices are then resynchronized to receive the next block of characters. The block length varies from few bytes to many hundreds of bytes. The most commonly used protocol is the BISYNC or Bit Synchronous Protocol.

# Asynchronous mode

Asynchronous mode is also known as start-stop mode. This mode is used when data to be transmitted generated at random intervals. For example, when a user communicates with a computer using a keyboard, the time interval between successive keystrokes is random. This means that the signal on the transmission line will be in idle state for a long time interval between characters. With this type of communication, the receiver must be able to resynchronize at the start of each new character received.

To accomplish this, each transmitted character or byte is encapsulated between an additional start bit and one or more stop bits. This mode is mainly used for the transmission of characters between a keyboard and a computer. Asynchronous transmission can also be used for the transmission of a block of characters or bytes between two computers. The time interval between successive characters is a variable entity.

At the receiving end, the modem demodulates the analog signals back into digital data, allowing the receiving device to interpret and process the information. This intermediary device bridges the gap between digital devices and analog communication channels, enabling long-distance communication and facilitating data exchange over traditional networks.

# SUMMARY

- Data communications is the process of exchanging data or information between devices or systems over a communication medium. It serves as the backbone of modern communication, enabling seamless transmission and sharing of data across local and global networks.

- In the data transmission process, errors may occur due to noise or interference. Therefore, error detection and correction techniques are employed to maintain data integrity. These methods enable devices to identify errors and request retransmission, ensuring accurate data delivery.

- Security is a paramount concern in data communications. Encryption, firewalls, and other security measures are implemented to protect data from unauthorized access, interception, or tampering.

- Data communication refers to the exchange of data between a source and a receiver via form of transmission media such as a wire cable.

- Communication channels are the medium that connects two or more workstations. Workstations can be connected by either wired media or wireless media.

- Communication media refers to the means of delivering and receiving data or information. In telecommunication, these means are transmission and storage tools or channels for data storage and transmission.

- Modulator converts information from digital to analog mode at the transmitting end and demodulator converts the same from analog to digital receiving end.

- Delay distortion is particularly critical for digital data. Consider that a sequence of bits is being transmitted, using either analog or digital signals.

- Thermal noise is uniformly distributed across the frequency spectrum and is often referred to as white noise; it cannot be eliminated and therefore places an upper bound on communications system performance
