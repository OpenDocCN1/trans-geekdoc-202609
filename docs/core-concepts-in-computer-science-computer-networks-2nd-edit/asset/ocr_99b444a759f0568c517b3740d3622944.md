Snoop can use queue length information to heurically distinguish congestion from wireless errors. When receiving an ELN notification, the TCP sender retransmits the packet without invoking congestion control. Although ELN is applicable to most topologies, it requires changes to router algorithms. Also, a packet can only be retransmitted after a round trip time has elapsed, when acknowledgment with the ELN bit is returned.

CC system RLPs avoid the layering violations of Snoop, which examines TCP headers at the IP level, but may retransmit data in parallel with TCP. This occurs rarely with fully reliable RLPs and is prevented by abandon recovery after several failed attempts. Link layer schemes operate at the local level with low round trip delays that allow recovery, in contrast to TCP modifications. Their main limitation is that they offer a single level of recovery, which may not be appropriate for higher layer protocols and applications.

# Wireless System Evolution and TCP

The trend for CC systems is to provide increased speeds and better support for packet data services. The highest data rates will be offered in small areas, or microcells, where user densities are higher. The High Speed Circuit Switched Data (HCSCD) system is a GSM extension providing bit rates of up to to 56 Kbps by reserving multiple TDMA slots for each data circuit. The General Packet Radio Service (GPRS) is a packet switched GSM extension. GPRS provides bit rates of up to 171 Kbps via dynamic TDMA slot reservation. Current implementations provide 20-40 Kbps of user throughput. Experiments show that Internet packet loss rates are around 2%. The third generation European CC system, UMTS, is based on wideband CDMA, supporting both circuit and packet switched modes, at various bit rates. Phase one includes services similar to GPRS, providing bit rates of up to 384 Kbps, with promises up to 2 Mbps in mobile areas. In the USA, the GSM EDGE/S-136 system will provide bit rates of 270-722 Kbps, or over 20 Mbps in mobile areas.

Many short (in room) systems, or Personal Area Networks (PANs), have been designed for low bit rates, such as Bluetooth, a FH frequency spectrum system providing bit rates of 400-700 Kbps. Bluetooth should provide TCP performance for low end WLANs, where there are serious problems concerning its radio link level interoperability with IEEE 802.11. The IEEE 802.15 project specifies a PAN standard based on Bluetooth is working on this issue.
