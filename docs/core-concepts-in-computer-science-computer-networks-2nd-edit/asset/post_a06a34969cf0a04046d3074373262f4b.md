In RLNC, coded packets are random linear combinations of the original packets over a finite field of size q. Each coded packet is in the form:

```
i=k
\sum_{i=l}^{k} \alpha_i \times P_i
```

$\alpha_i$, and $P_i$ are random coefficients chosen from a Galois field and the packet respectively, while $k$ is the number of packets that are coded together in a single transmission. A Galois field is any field that contains a finite number of elements. The probability that the receiver(s) will obtain linearly independent combinations approaches 100%. Linear independence is important in ensuring uniform distribution, thus reducing redundant information being forwarded to a given node. The smaller the size, the less complex the resulting system, which is especially advantageous in wireless networks. Instead of transmitting the original packets, the source node generates and transmits random-coded packets over the $k$ packets. In order to decode the coded packets and retrieve the original packets, the destination nodes need to receive $k$ linearly independent coded packets. They can use Gaussian elimination to decode the coded packets.

As powerful as this encoding scheme is, it does have its challenges, chief among them that if a receiver gets an insufficient number of packets, it is extremely unlikely that it can recover any of the original packets. This is known as the index coding problem. This can be fixed by sending additional random linear combinations until the receiver receives the required number of packets. Other challenges include the high decoding computational complexity due to the Gauss-Jordan elimination method and the high transmission overhead of adding large coefficient vectors to encoded blocks.

# Triangular Network Coding

In order to intrinsically fix the problem with RLNC detailed above, where receivers getting an insufficient number of packets cannot recover the original packets, triangular network coding. The triangular pattern-based packet coding scheme is performed in two stages. First, redundant “0” bits are selectively added at the head and tail of each packet to make sure that all packets are of uniform bit length. The packets are then XOR-coded bit by bit where the “0” bits are added in such a way that they generate a triangular pattern, known as tri-angularization, as shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3: Triangular Pattern.

The decoding process is similar to the LNC decoding process, which involves Gaussian estimation. However in this case, it is simplified since the coded packets are in a triangular pattern and such as the receiver only needs to perform back-substitution, where each row is solved from the last to the first row. This is far less computationally intensive and gives triangular coding the bandwidth performance of LNC, affording the computation cost of XOR coding.

# Opportunistic Network Coding

In ONC, the sensor nodes can snoop on all transmissions in their neighborhood and store the overhead data packets, whether they are intended for them or not. As such, the sensor nodes know the overhead and routed packets that each neighboring node possesses, and can perform network coding operations based on this information. Each node has its own queue of received uncoded packets $p_1,p_2,...,p_n$, whose destinations, determined by their packet headers and the node's routing table, are $r_1,r_2,...,r_n$. The node then dequeues the first packet, $p_j$, and while making sure of all next recipients can immediately decode the resulting combination, steps through the greedy to add packets for combination. A recipient can immediately decode a combined packet if it knows all one coded packet. For example, a combined packet $p_i+p_j+p_k+p_l$ is valid if $r_i$ knows $p_i$, $r_j$ knows $p_j$, and $r_k$ knows $p_k$ and $p_l$.

# Physical Layer Network Coding

Physical-layer network coding as a concept was proposed in 2006 for application in wireless networks. PNC's main idea is to take advantage of the natural network coding operation that occurs when electromagnetic (EM) waves are superimposed on one another. This simple idea led to numerous advancements, with subsequent works by various researchers leading to many new results in the domains of wireless communication, wireless information theory, and wireless networking. We shall attempt to give a brief overview of both the theory behind this concept and the implications of the results in the three fields listed above.

# Theory behind PNC

Interference is often treated as a destructive phenomenon in wireless communication networks today. When multiple transmitters send radio waves to their respective receivers, a receiver may receive signals from its transmitter as well as from other transmitters at the same time. The radio waves from the other transmitters are often treated as interference that corrupts the intended signal, as a result of their overlapping. In Wi-Fi networks, for example, when multiple nodes transmit together, packet collisions can occur leading to none of the packets being received correctly. PNC takes advantage of the fact that when multiple EM waves come together within the same physical space, they either superimpose, increasing in amplitude. This mixing of EM waves is a form of natural network coding, which is utilized in PNC.

The concept of PNC can be most easily illustrated with a network model known as a Two-Way Relay Channel (TWRC). TWRC is a three-node linear network in which two nodes, nodes $T_1$ and $T_2$, communicate via a relay $R$. This is illustrated in Figure 4:

A real-world example of such a system is a satellite network in which $T_1$ and $T_2$ are ground stations, and the relay is the satellite. The half-duplex constraint is often imposed on wireless systems. With the half-duplex constraint, a node cannot transmit and receive at the same time. This means that each packet from $T_1$ to $T_2$ must use at least two slots. Thus, possible exchange throughput is two packets for every two slots.

# Advantages and Challenges in PNC

PNC can potentially achieve 100% throughput increases compared with traditional transmission in multi-hop networks by reducing the needed slots for the exchange from four to two. Unfortunately, PNC is yet to be widely used in wireless networks, since in a general multi-hop network, MAC-layer and network-layer issues will take on an increasingly important role, particularly with regard to the management of complexity when there are many synchronous flows in the network.

One of the key issues in PNC is how to deal with the asynchronies between the signals transmitted by the two ends. Symbols transmitted by the two ends could potentially arrive at the receiver with misalignment as carrier-phase offset. This could result in significant penalties to performance. Reliability of transmission is also one of the challenges that face PNC. Channel coding is typically used to solve this issue, and its application to PNC is the subject of research today.
