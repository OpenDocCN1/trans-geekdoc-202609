
# The Dangers of Software Patents

Patents are a curious anomaly in capitalist economies, since they are the grant of a private monopoly by the State. Adam Smith roughly condemns monopolies in *The Wealth of Nations*.

In modern times patents enjoy widespread support from a broad range of businesses—from small vacuum cleaners to giant pharmaceutical companies. But when it comes to *software* patents, it is difficult to find anyone who positively supports them except for patent trolls (companies that buy and lease patent rights but which create nothing themselves), and their lawyers. In 1991 Bill Gates said, "If people understood how patents would be granted when most of today's ideas were invented, and had taken out patents, the industry would be at a complete standstill today." Of course, his view appears to be somewhat more nuanced today.

Software patents affect every business that produces software—whether for sale or in-house use. Even non-software goods such as Kraft Foods and Ford Motor Co are spending large sums of money defending against patent lawsuits. But every programmer is exposed to risk. For example, linked lists have been patented—but not by their inventors Allen Newell, Cliff Shaw, and Herbert Simon when they came up with the idea back in 1955-6, by someone else, 50 years later (https://www.google.com/patents/about?id=26jaAAEBAJ&linked=list). The same thing has happened with skip lists, invented by William Pugh in 1990 and patented over a decade later by someone else. Sadly, there are tens of thousands of other software patents that could be cited as examples, although we will mention just one more, "A system and method causes a computer to detect and perform actions on structures identified in computer data" granted to Apple in 1999 and covers all software that manipulates data structures (https://www.google.com/patents?id=aFEAAAAAEBAJdq=5,946,647).

It is easy to imagine that overly broad, obvious, or otherwise meritless patents would be easy to invalidate, but in practice, even giants like Google found themselves paying out millions of dollars in legal fees to defend themselves. How then startups and small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) hope to bring innovative software to market without being shaken down—again and again—by patent troll "businesses" which feed like parasites on the work of others?

## Appendix B. The Dangers of Software Patents

Here is how the system works in the U.S.—and anywhere else that has similar patent systems. First of all, keep in mind that patents are enforceable if the “violator” doesn’t know about the patent. Also, fines for patent violation can be huge. Now imagine that software developer working on some closed-source software completely develops a smart algorithm for performing some operation. The troll hears on the grapevine that the developer’s company has a new innovation that might make some money. So the troll files an injunction against the developer making a violation of one of their really patents (such as the one mentioned above). The source code must be submitted for independent analysis. Naturally, the analysis will not cover just the cited patent, but all of the patents held by the troll. The troll now gets to see the developer’s smart algorithm and might even try to patent it—after all they have deep pockets for legal fees and SMEs usually don’t. Of course, trolls don’t want to count their “business model” is based on extortion: They want the developer to sell their products and pay license fees for the general patents that the troll has violated. Of course, if not all of the patents are unenforceable, in that they have no merit—but fighting in the courts would bankrupt most SMEs, who end up paying license fees. And once they are paying this gives legitimacy to the troll who can cite their list of licensees to their next victim.

Big companies afford to acquire patents and defend themselves from patent trolls, and not necessarily have sympathy for SMEs which might, or grow to, potential rivals—most of them seem to be concerned. Some companies, including open source Red Hat, are filing software patents as a defense that can be used to cross-licensing deals and minimize legal costs. How effective such action will be when giants like Apple, Google, and Microsoft have built or acquired portfolios at a cost of billions of dollars, remains to be seen. Companies that have made such investments are unlikely to want the patent regime—however despite it—as this would incur massive write-offs that would be hard to justify to shareholders or to CEOs whose compensation depends on their company’s share price.

SMEs and individual innovators usually don’t have the financial muscle to defend themselves from patent troll shutdowns. But most will try their luck by moving overseas (going out of the process), or paying license fees on meritless patents. Software patents are already having a chilling effect on individual and SME innovation in the U.S., making it harder and more expensive for these businesses—and reducing their capacity to expand and create more jobs for programmers. Of course, many lawyers may do benefit from software patents—to the tune of US$11.2 billion in 2008 alone*—while even pro-software patent economists don’t seem to be able to count a single cent in economic benefits deriving from software patents.

* See esp.wikidot.com/local---files/2008-state-of-softwarepatents/feb_08-summary_report.pdf.

## Appendix B. The Dangers of Software Patents

Nor software developers outside the U.S. have cause for joy either; some have already had to withdraw their software from the U.S. market to avoid the patent toll protection racket. This means that giving innovative software is no longer available in the U.S., potentially giving a competitive advantage to non-U.S. businesses. Furthermore, the Anti-Counterfeiting Trade Agreement (ACTA) successfully protects patents of all kinds, and is adopted by many countries throughout the world—including the European Union—but not, at the time of writing, Brazil, China, or Russia. And in addition, the European so called 'unitary patent' (www.unitary-patent.eu) is likely to bring U.S.-style patents to the entire EU.

Software is a form of intellectual property that is perfectly protected by copyright. (For example, Bill Gates was the world's richest person—upon the basis of software copyright, and prior to the pernicious idea of patenting software.) Despite the success of applying copyright to software, the U.S. and many other countries have chosen—or been forced by international agreements to incorporate software into their regimes. Let us imagine for a moment that the meritless patents (e.g., those that are overly, obvious, or for which there is prior art) were to disappear. This would greatly reduce the shakedowns and would be a boon for innovation.

In many countries—including the U.S.—it is not possible to patent mathematical formulas, no matter how original or new they are. Yet mathematical formulas are ideas (which is what patents are designed to “protect”). And we know from the Church-Turing Thesis that the logic of any software can be reduced to a very particular form. This is exactly the argument Donald Knuth makes against a mathematical formula, so reality, software is mathematics written in a very particular form. The patenting of software. (See Professor Knuth's short and fascinating letter on this topic: www.progfre.org/knuth-to-pto.txt.)

The problem is solvable—but will require legislation to outlaw software as mathematics (which is what it is), and to reign in patent offices (where income is often linked to the number of patents they grant, irrespective of merit). This is difficult gaining the attention of politicians is expensive, and those who can afford to acquire patents can certainly afford to lobby. Also, the subject is so dry and industry-specific that it will hardly make any politician's career. But there are people lobbying for change, and so in a bipartisan starting point to learn more about why software patents are so disastrous and how to combat them endsoftwarepatents.org (and in Europe, www.nossoftwarepatents.com).
