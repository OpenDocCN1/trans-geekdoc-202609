# Appendix B. The Dangers of Software Patents

Nor software developers outside the U.S. have cause for joy either; some have already had to withdraw their software from the U.S. market to avoid the patent toll protection racket. This means that giving innovative software is no longer available in the U.S., potentially giving a competitive advantage to non-U.S. businesses. Furthermore, the Anti-Counterfeiting Trade Agreement (ACTA) successfully protects patents of all kinds, and is adopted by many countries throughout the world—including the European Union—but not, at the time of this writing, Brazil, China, or Russia. And in addition, the European so called 'unitary patent' (www.unitary-patent.eu) is likely to bring U.S.-style patents to the entire EU.

Software is a form of intellectual property that is perfectly well protected by copyright. (For example, Bill Gates was, the world's richest person—upon the basis of software copyright, and prior to the pernicious idea of patenting software.) Despite the success of applying copyright to software, the U.S. and many other countries have chosen—or been forced by international agreements to incorporate software into their patent regimes. Let us imagine for a moment that the meritless patents (e.g., those that are overly, obvious, or for which there is prior art) were to disappear. This would greatly reduce the shakedowns and would be a boon for innovation. But it still leaves the key question: Should software be patentable?

In many countries—including the U.S.—it is not possible to patent mathematical formulas, no matter how original or new they are. Yet mathematical formulas are ideas (which is what patents are designed to “protect”). And we know from the Church-Turing Thesis that the logic of any software can be reduced to a very particular form. This is exactly the argument Donald Knuth makes against a mathematical formula, so reality, software is mathematics written in a very particular form. The patenting of software. (See Professor Knuth's short and fascinating letter on this topic: www.progfre.org/knuth-to-pto.txt.)

The problem is solvable—but will require legislation to outlaw software as mathematics, which is what it is), and to reign in patent offices (where income is often optional to the number of patents they grant, irrespective of merit). This is difficult gaining the attention of politicians is expensive, and those who can afford to acquire patents can certainly afford to lobby. Also, the subject is so dry and industry-specific that it will hardly make any politician's career. But there are people lobbying for change, and so in a bipartisan starting point to learn more about why software patents are so disastrous and how to combat them endsoftwarepatents.org (and in Europe, www.nossoftwarepatents.com).
