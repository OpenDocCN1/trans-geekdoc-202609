
# Survey Methodology

The survey was conducted online and divided into three sections.

## Section 1: Demographic Factors
Demographic factors such as age, sex, level of education, and social status were considered.

## Section 2: Perceived Sensitivity of Information Types
Participants assessed the perceived sensitivity of 39 different forms of information. Respondents were asked: "How sensitive are the following data types to you?" They graded sensitivity using a 6-point Likert scale ranging from "not at all sensitive" (1) to "extremely sensitive" (6). To avoid sequence effects, all data types were presented in a randomized manner.

## Section 3: Privacy Attitudes, Experiences, and Risks
The final section assessed individuals' privacy attitudes, experiences with privacy violations, and risks. All items were graded on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = "I do not agree at all", 6 = "I entirely agree"). Trust in institutions (e.g., governmental bodies, insurance firms) was assessed on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 ("I don't trust them at all") to 6 ("I completely trust them"). Cronbach's Alpha was derived for reliability analysis. The scale for "encounters with privacy violations" fell short of satisfactory dependability (.7) and was removed from further consideration.

# Selection of Information Types

This study is based on the hypothesis proposed by Markos, Milne, & Peltier (2017). It employs the same data types as other studies to enable a descriptive comparison of Delhi users with users from the United States, Brazil, and Germany. The researcher examined and updated the data types to select appropriate items for Delhi participants, estimating which information types correspond to Delhi's laws and culture globally.

Markos, Milne, & Peltier (2017) conducted qualitative research to examine the narrative coherence of data types and identify additional sensitive information types. Following a brief introduction, participants pondered what data they share when using the internet or smart gadgets. All information kinds analyzed were provided via an online questionnaire; participants evaluated their sensitivity and scored them on a 6-point Likert scale.

Some items were incomprehensible to Delhi online users. Additional elements were omitted due to translation issues and significant disparities in the importance of certain information categories between cultures (e.g., the security number, as this ID is not prevalent in Delhi). To allow for comparability, the final list includes 39 information categories identical to those in the Markos, Milne, & Peltier (2017) study (*Information Sensitivity and Willingness to Provide Continua: A Comparative Privacy Study of the United States and Brazil*).

The final section assessed participants' privacy disposition, experiences with privacy infractions, and risk proclivity. All items were graded on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = "I do not agree at all", 6 = "I entirely agree"). Trust in organizations (e.g., government units, insurers) was graded on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("I don't trust them at all") to 6 ("I completely trust them"). Cronbach's Alpha was derived for reliability analysis.

# Statistical Analysis

The comparison of Delhi results with those of Markos, Milne, & Peltier (2017) is descriptive. Rankings of data categories are evaluated using rank-order correlation. Hierarchical cluster analysis categorizes data types based on perceived sensitivity. Regression studies investigate the impact of user characteristics on the impression of sensitivity (method: enter). For all analyses, the level of significance was fixed at 5%.

# Results

This section summarizes the descriptive findings of Delhi Internet users' perceptions of information sensitivity and their attitudes toward privacy. It compares Delhi results to American, Brazilian, and German results. Furthermore, it examines sensitivity results, emphasizes their characteristics, and identifies factors affecting the sensitivity of Internet users in Delhi.

## Information Sensitivity of Internet Users in Delhi

Results show the rank-order of each information type by average sensitivity ratings (M) based on survey results, ranked from highest to lowest sensitivity.

*   **Password**: Highest sensitivity (`M = 9.64`).
*   **Biometric**: `M = 9.23`.
*   **Financial information**: `M = 9.01`.
*   **Religion**: `M = 3.2`.
*   **Occupation**: `M = 4.12`.
*   **Lowest item**: `M = 2.27`.

Delhi Internet users demonstrated high sensitivity for all 39 values shared across nations, including passwords, fingerprints, digital signatures, passport numbers, credit scores, economic level, and political affiliation. They were less sensitive than counterparts in the US, Brazil, and Germany in seven items: license plate number, ZIP code, mother's maiden name, place of birth, etc.

Delhi, Brazil, and Germany had the highest password sensitivity scores (9.67, 9.2, and 9.34, respectively). The social security number was the most sensitive form for American consumers (9.6). Delhi users are clearly more sensitive to verification information (passwords, fingerprints, digital signatures, passport numbers) than users in other nations. The political affiliation category shows the largest variation when comparing Delhi users to other countries.
