## Abstract

The study focuses on prevailing technological cybersecurity challenges. It covers technologies used in healthcare and how digitalization has introduced a new regime. Cybersecurity challenges poised to affect digitalized healthcare are examined, as cyber threats and security vulnerabilities can jeopardize patient protected health information and distract healthcare professionals. Finally, the study covers solutions to handle technical and cyber-related problems, which can be considered possible solutions.

## Introduction

Enhancing the quality of health care and improving ease of access to health records while keeping practical costs low is challenging for health-care businesses globally. The problem is further exacerbated by the rapidly growing world population, specifically the growth rate of senior citizens (65 years old and older). According to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2018), the number of senior citizens will increase to about 1.5 billion by 2050. An aging population implies an increase in chronic diseases that require frequent visits to health-care providers, as well as elevated hospitalization needs. The rise in the number of patients requiring constant care substantially increases medical treatment costs.

Over the past few decades, Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) have been broadly adopted in the health-care environment to make health-care access and delivery easier and more cost-effective. When considering technology's future role in digital healthcare, it is important to weigh the benefits of better patient outcomes against the risks of increased physician burnout due to poor implementation and increased complexity (Agrawal and Prabakaran, 2020). The use of ICT has led to the development of Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems. EHRs incorporate complete patient health history (current medications, immunizations, laboratory results, modern diagnosis, etc.) and can be easily shared among multiple providers. They have proven to enhance patient-provider interaction. The adoption of ICT in the health sector is typically referred to as digital health care. Some clinicians, patients, and healthcare providers are wary of the sudden influx of technology, which is compounded by a lack of solid control (Duggal and Bagenal, 2018).

Over the years, digital health care has extended from primarily preserving digital patient records and offering patient web portals to allowing further flexibility and convenience in health-care management, often referred to as connected health. Consumption of self-healthy foods and body image management is rapidly growing (Shen, Y. 2019). Connected health uses smart phones and mobile applications, together with wireless technologies (such as Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and Long-Term Evolution) to allow patients to connect easily with their providers without visiting them frequently. For example, a standard hypertensive patient would see his/her doctor once in six months to report daily blood pressure readings. With a monitoring application, the patient can transmit daily or weekly blood pressure readings, thereby enabling his/her physician to detect a problem and intervene earlier. Connected health has evolved into smart health whereby conventional mobile devices (such as smart phones) are used together with wearable medical devices (such as blood pressure monitors, glucometers, smart watches, smart contact lenses, and others) and Internet of Things (IoT) devices (such as implantable or ingestible sensors) to enable continuous patient monitoring and treatment even when patients are at their homes. Smart health is expected to keep hospitalization costs low and provide timely treatment for a range of medical conditions by placing IoT sensors on health monitoring equipment. The data accumulated by these microchips can then be sent to any remote destination. For example, wearable sensors (such as a temperature sensor and a heartbeat sensor) can act as data collection units, gathering physiological signals from the patient's body. The collected data are then forwarded to a local gateway server via a Wi-Fi network such that end-systems (such as a physician's laptop) can retrieve the accumulated data from the gateway server. Regular server updates allow doctors access to real-time patient data. These devices work together to create a unified medical record that can be accessed by multiple providers. This data is not only beneficial for the patient but can be pooled together to study and predict healthcare trends across cultures and countries. While recent lockdowns and business closures have heightened the need for digitized health resources, disabled people have always needed safe digitized health resources that can be easily accessed from home, and would have benefited from a wider range of and easier access to exercise resources long before the COVID-19 outbreak (Stratton et al., 2020).
