Things get trickier when you consider connections between the offices. Should traffic route from Office A to B? The shortest hop count is the direct T1. But if link only has 1.5 megabits: taking the two-hop route from Office A down to the data center and back up to Office B offers 45 megabits, at the expense of an extra hop.

A distance vector routing protocol such as RIP would choose the direct T1 connection and consider one hop at 1.5 megabits “faster” than two hops at 45 megabits. Most Engineers (and all Link state routing protocols, as described in the next section) would disagree.

Distance vector protocols use metrics such as hop count, and are prone to routing loops, which packets loop between routers. # RIP

RIP (Routing Information Protocol) is a distance vector routing protocol that uses hop count as its metric. RIP will route traffic from Office A to Office B in Fig. 5.2 via the direct T1, since it is the “closest” route at 1 hop.

RIP does not have a full view of the network: it can only “see” directly connected routers. Convergence is slow. RIP sends updates every 30 seconds, regardless of changes. RIP routers that are on a network that is converged for weeks will send updates every 30 seconds, around the clock.

RIP’s maximum hop count is 15; 16 is considered “infinite.” RIP2 added support for CIDR. RIP is used by the UNIX command and is the only routing protocol universally supported by UNIX.

RIP uses split horizon to avoid routing loops. In Fig. 5.22, the circuit between the NYC and BOS routers has gone down. That moment, the NYC and BOS routers know the route is down; but others do not. The network lacks convergence.
