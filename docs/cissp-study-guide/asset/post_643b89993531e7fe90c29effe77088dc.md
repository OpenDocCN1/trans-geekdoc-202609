# Repeaters and Hubs

Repeaters and hubs are Layer 1 devices. A repeater receives bits on one port, and “repeats” them out the other port. The repeater has no understanding of protocols; it simply repeats bits. Repeaters are often used to extend the length of a network.

A hub is a device with more than two ports. It receives bits on one port and repeats them across all ports. Hubs were quite common before switches became common and inexpensive. Hubs provide no traffic isolation or integrity; all nodes send all traffic by the hub. Hub means an attacker connected to a hub may read and potentially alter traffic via the hub. Hubs are also half-duplex devices; they cannot send and receive simultaneously. Any device connected to a hub will negotiate to half-duplex mode, which can cause network congestion. Hubs also have “collision domain”: any node may send traffic with another (for more information on collisions, see “CSMA section). The lack of security, half-duplex mode, and large collision domain hubs are unsuitable for most modern purposes.

# Bridges

Bridges and switches are Layer 2 devices. A bridge has two ports and connects work segments together. Each segment typically has multiple nodes, and the bridge learns the MAC addresses of nodes on either side. Traffic between two nodes on the same side of the bridge will not be forwarded across the bridge. Traffic sent from a node on one side of the bridge to the other side will forward across. The bridge provides traffic isolation and makes forwarding decisions by learning the MAC addresses of connected nodes.
