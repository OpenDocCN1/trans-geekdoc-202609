# Google’s Hidden Mic

In 2019 Google faced a significant backlash from privacy advocates when it became known that the company had integrated a microphone into its Nest Secure device without customers’ knowledge.^4 The microphone was not listed on any documentation that came with the device. Google called the inclusion of the microphone an “error,” but privacy advocates labeled it another serious misstep by the technology giant.

# Border Protection

In 2019 a firm named Perceptics experienced a data breach and lost photos of travelers and license plate numbers for 100,000 people.^5 Perceptics was contracted by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) to provide surveillance at U.S. ports of entry. Privacy advocates argued that this data should not be stored if it cannot be adequately protected. This argument becomes even more relevant because the CBP wants facial scans to be mandatory at all U.S. airports by 2020.

# Banning Huawei

In 2019 President Trump effectively banned U.S. firms from using any equipment made by Chinese hardware giant Huawei due to national security concerns.^6 Concerns were related to potential vulnerabilities intentionally built into Huawei hardware that could be used by the Chinese government for spying or potentially offensive actions. Subsequently, Google also banned Huawei from getting any Android updates. This was particularly troublesome for Huawei because Android was the primary operating system for its mobile devices. The trade war between China and the United States seems likely to continue to affect both economies and their respective technology companies.

# HIPAA Time

In 2019 Google and the University of Chicago were sued for allegedly violating the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) requirements. The University of Chicago Medical Center shared hundreds of thousands of medical records with Google in an effort to use predictive analytics to improve medical care. However, the lawsuit claims that the shared data included dates and doctors’ notes that Google could use to identify patients. It reasoned that Google could correlate the location data it collects with the dates on the shared patient records to identify individual patients. Google and the University of Chicago claim they followed the law and the lawsuit is without merit.

# GDPR and British Airways

In 2019 British Airways was fined $222 million (£183 million) for violation of the new EU privacy law General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).^7 In 2018 British Airways lost 500,000 customer records in a data breach containing credit card and personal information. The GDPR, passed in 2016, became enforceable on May 25, 2018, and the UK’s Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) wasted no time levying fines. British Airways is appealing the record fine, arguing there’s no proof that any of the stolen data has been used or that it was negligent in storing customer data.
