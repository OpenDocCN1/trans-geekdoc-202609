Once inside Target’s network, the attackers compromised an internal Windows file server. From this server, the attackers used malware named Trojan.POSRAM (a variant of BlackPOS) to extract customer data from POS terminals. BlackPOS was developed by a 17-year-old from St. Petersburg, Russia, and can be purchased from underground sites for about $2,000.14

Customer data was continuously sent from the POS terminals to an extraction server within Target’s network. It was then funneled out of Target’s network to drop servers in Russia, Brazil, and Miami. From there, the data was collected and sold on the black market.

# THE DAMAGE

For the attackers, the “damage” was substantial. It’s estimated that the attackers sold about 2 million credit cards for about $26.85 each for a total profit of $53.7 million.15 The prices for the remaining cards dropped due to the large supply available. The hackers could have chosen to hold back some of the stolen credit cards rather than flooding the market, but they had to sell them as quickly as possible because the stolen cards were being canceled and replaced by banks. The prices of stolen credit cards from Target, sold in batches of 100,000, dropped by 70 percent after 2 months.16 This was likely due to a reduction in credit card validity rates from nearly 100 percent to about 60 percent.

From the hackers’ point of view, the operation was a spectacular success. The payoff was substantial for just a few weeks of work. Unfortunately, payoffs like these encourage even more data breaches.

Target, on the other hand, incurred much greater losses than the hackers’ gains. It was forced to upgrade its payment terminals to support chip-and-PIN-enabled cards
