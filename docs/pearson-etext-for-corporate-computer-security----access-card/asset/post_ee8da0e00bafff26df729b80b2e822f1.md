## In the News

## Snowden

Edward Snowden, a former NSA employee, disclosed classified details about multiple mass-surveillance programs being run by the U.S. government. Some of the information disclosed by Snowden included mass collection of telephone metadata for all U.S. calls, PRISM system that allowed government real-time access to e-mail, web searches, and all Internet traffic, and numerous other spying activities on foreign officials. The revelations about these surveillance programs have generated substantial discussion about national IT security policy and its effect on privacy. Snowden is currently living in Moscow, Russia, where he has been granted asylum. His memoir detailing these events was released in 2019.^3

## Google’s Hidden Mic

In 2019 Google faced a significant backlash from privacy advocates when it became known that the company had integrated a microphone into its Nest Secure device without customers’ knowledge.^4 The microphone was not listed on any documentation that came with the device. Google called the inclusion of the microphone an “error,” but privacy advocates labeled it another serious misstep by the technology giant.

## Border Protection

In 2019 a firm named Perceptics experienced a data breach and lost photos of travelers and license plate numbers for 100,000 people.^5 Perceptics was contracted by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) to provide surveillance at U.S. ports of entry. Privacy advocates argued that this data should not be stored if it cannot be adequately protected. This argument becomes even more relevant because the CBP wants facial scans to be mandatory at all U.S. airports by 2020.

## Banning Huawei

In 2019 President Trump effectively banned U.S. firms from using any equipment made by Chinese hardware giant Huawei due to national security concerns.^6 Concerns were related to potential vulnerabilities intentionally built into Huawei hardware that could be used by the Chinese government for spying or potentially offensive actions. Subsequently, Google also banned Huawei from getting any Android updates. This was particularly troublesome for Huawei because Android was the primary operating system for its mobile devices. The trade war between China and the United States seems likely to continue to affect both economies and their respective technology companies.

## HIPAA Time

In 2019 Google and the University of Chicago were sued for allegedly violating the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) requirements. The University of Chicago Medical Center shared hundreds of thousands of medical records with Google in an effort to use predictive analytics to improve medical care. However, the lawsuit claims that the shared data included dates and doctors’ notes that Google could use to identify patients. It reasoned that Google could correlate the location data it collects with the dates on the shared patient records to identify individual patients. Google and the University of Chicago claim they followed the law and the lawsuit is without merit.

## GDPR and British Airways

In 2019 British Airways was fined $222 million (£183 million) for violation of the new EU privacy law General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).^7 In 2018 British Airways lost 500,000 customer records in a data breach containing credit card and personal information. The GDPR, passed in 2016, became enforceable on May 25, 2018, and the UK’s Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) wasted no time levying fines. British Airways is appealing the record fine, arguing there’s no proof that any of the stolen data has been used or that it was negligent in storing customer data.

## 2.2 Compliance Laws and Regulations

## 2.2 Describe compliance laws and regulations.

## Driving Forces

Many companies have relatively good security plans, protections, and response capabilities. To plan for the future, however, even well-prepared companies need to understand the driving forces that will require them to change their security planning, protections, and response.

> Driving forces are things that require a firm to change its security planning, protections, and response.

Perhaps the most important set of driving forces for firms today are compliance laws and regulations, which create requirements for corporate security. In many cases, firms must substantially improve their security to be in compliance with these laws and regulations. This is especially true in the areas of documentation and identity management. These improvements can be very expensive. Another problem for corporate security is that there are so many compliance laws and regulations.

> Compliance laws and regulations create requirements to which corporate security must respond.

## Sarbanes–Oxley

Around the year 2000, there were several massive financial frauds that cost billions of dollars and depressed the stock market. Congress responded by creating the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002. This act produced the greatest change in financial reporting requirements since the Great Depression.

Under Sarbanes–Oxley, companies must report whether they have any material control deficiencies in their financial reporting process. Companies that report material control deficiencies are likely to take a hit in their stock price, and most chief financial officers in these companies are gone within a few months. If there was deception by the chief executive officer (CEO) or chief financial officer, they could go to jail.

In a material control deficiency, there is “a material deficiency, or combination of significant deficiencies, that results in more than a remote likelihood that a material misstatement in the annual or interim financial statements will not be prevented or detected.” Avoiding material control deficiencies is difficult.

Under Sarbanes–Oxley, companies have had to take a detailed look at their financial reporting processes. In doing so, they uncovered many security weaknesses, and in many cases, they realized that these security weaknesses extended to other parts of the firm. Given the importance of Sarbanes–Oxley compliance, most firms were forced to increase their security efforts.
