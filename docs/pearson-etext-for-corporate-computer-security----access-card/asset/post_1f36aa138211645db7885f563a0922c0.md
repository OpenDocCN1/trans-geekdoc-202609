
## Are Password Resets Secure?

Although password resets are a fact of corporate life, they are a serious security threat. A chain of security is only as strong as its weakest link, and the password reset is potentially the weakest link in the use of passwords—especially the self-service password reset.

One controversial way to strengthen password security is to eliminate self-service password resets completely for higher-security accounts. This will add to help desk costs, but the reduction in risk may be well justified.

In fact, for high-risk accounts, help desk password resets over the phone might be banned. If you lose your ATM PIN, you usually are required to go into a bank branch and show identification before you get a new PIN. In addition, you may have to wait a few days while the bank’s central headquarters approves the revised PIN.

Convenience is good, but it may need to be limited in password resets when security risks are high.

## PASSWORD STRENGTH

It is important for corporations to have policies that require strong passwords. For example, a corporate policy might require that passwords have the following length and complexity characteristics:

- Be at least 12 characters long.
- Have at least one change of case, not at the start of the password.
- Have at least one digit (0 through 9), not at the end of the password.
- Have at least one non-alphanumeric character, not at the end of the password.

An example of a password that fits all of these rules is tri6#VialOne!. It is 13 characters long. Although it uses mostly lowercase letters, lowercase brute-force cracking will not work because of the digit (6), the special symbols (# and !), and the capital letters (V and O).

It may also be useful to create passwords from long phrases with complex character mixes. For instance, “In 1492, Columbus sailed the ocean blue!” could give the password “i1492,Cstob!” (if a comma is allowed).

## PASSWORD AUDITING

Usernames and passwords are a primary target for hackers. All passwords must be stored using a secure hashing algorithm and regularly tested to ensure that they are not easily cracked. It is important to always obtain permission before running a password-cracking program on your company’s computers.

One of the best ways of auditing users’ passwords is to test them as if you were an attacker. Try to crack them using the same dictionaries attackers would use and see how many are cracked within a given amount of time. In fact, it would be best if you could ban all commonly known passwords in the first place. This would slow attackers down and keep them out of internal systems.

## List of Known Passwords

Security researcher Troy Hunt has collected a growing list of more than 555 million real-world unique passwords that were collected and anonymized from 8.4 billion stolen accounts in thousands of data breaches.12 Figure 5-6 shows the top 20 unique passwords across all data breaches. The password "123456" is the most commonly used password chosen by users. Systems administrators can use Troy Hunt's list to ban previously compromised passwords from internal systems. Preventing users from choosing weak passwords will make internal systems more secure.

## Data Breaches Expose Passwords

Password auditing becomes even more important when you consider that while operating systems automatically hash and store your password, online applications and e-commerce sites do not. For example, in 2009, hackers used a well-known SQL vulnerability to steal 32 million passwords from RockYou.com. RockYou.com was storing all usernames, passwords, and partner data as plaintext.13 RockYou.com would also send plaintext passwords to users via e-mail.

Even if you are using a strong password, the website you are using may not store it securely. If an attacker steals it, your strong password will go on a list of known passwords and then be used by attackers to access your accounts. Regular auditing using known passwords will ensure that passwords used on internal systems are secure. Consider the comparison of Troy Hunt's list with passwords taken from separate data breaches.

## The End of Passwords?

Although passwords are widely used, there is nearly total agreement among security professionals that passwords are no longer safe. Growing computer power has made simple passwords so fast to crack that only the longest and most complex passwords are safe. However, when users are forced to use very long and complex passwords, they usually write them down or circumvent their strength in other ways. Passwords are likely to be phased out in the fairly near future. Quite a few firms have already largely phased them out, and many others will soon do so. We will now begin to look at alternative authentication technologies for companies to use in the post-password world.

## 5.4 Access Cards and Tokens

**5.4** Explain how access cards and tokens work.

One approach to replacing reusable passwords is to have people carry small physical devices to authenticate them. These physical devices usually are divided into two categories—access cards and tokens.

### Access Cards

Figure 5-8 shows that an **access card** is a plastic card that usually is the size of a credit or debit card. Normally, the person wishing access to a door or a computer slides the access card through a reader or pushes it into a reader. This is exactly what you do when you use a credit or debit card at a retail store.
