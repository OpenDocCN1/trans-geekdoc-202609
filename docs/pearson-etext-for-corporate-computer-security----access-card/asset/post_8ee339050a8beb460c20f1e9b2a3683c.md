
## How Do Data Breaches Happen?

The first step in protecting yourself from data breaches is understanding how they happen. Each one is different because hackers are continually developing new tools and techniques that enable them to steal more data. They experiment with new attack vectors, or ways of attacking a target. They might use a phishing scam one week in an effort to trick users into donating funds for a natural disaster. Then, the following week, they might develop an exploit, which is software used to take advantage of a newly discovered vulnerability in a target’s application or operating system.

The following paragraphs detail the data breach at Target Corporation that happened in late 2013. While it’s tempting to think less of Target because of the breach, it’s not entirely fair to do so. Yes, there were missteps on Target’s part, and we will discuss those. But Target has been extremely forthcoming with the details surrounding the data breach, and it has handled the fallout from the breach very well. Other companies involved in data breaches have not been as forthcoming. LinkedIn, for example, lost 117 million customer accounts in 2012 but failed to notify users about the breach until 4 years later, in mid-2016.¹⁰ It also provided few details about the attack.

## Case Study: The Target Data Breach

Now let’s look at a specific attack to put these terms into context and to show how complex security attacks can be. We will begin with one of the most well-known losses of customer information—the data breach at Target Corporation.

On December 18, 2013, Target Corporation announced that it had lost 40 million credit and debit card numbers to attackers. Less than a month later, Target announced that an additional 70 million customer accounts had been stolen, including names, e-mails, addresses, and phone numbers. After accounting for some overlap between the two data losses, about 98 million customers were affected.¹¹ That’s 30 percent of all 330 million people in the United States (including children and those without credit cards).

Customer records were stolen from point-of-sale (POS) systems at Target retail stores during the holiday shopping season (November 27 to December 15, 2013). If you were shopping at a Target during this time, it’s likely your data was lost.

### HOW DID THEY DO IT?
As shown in Figure 1-5, the attackers first purchased malware designed specifically for the attacks they planned to carry out. They then used **spear phishing**, or a targeted phishing attack aimed at a single individual, to infect a Target third-party vendor named Fazio Mechanical Services (which provides refrigeration and HVAC services).¹² Attackers placed a piece of malware called Citadel on Fazio’s system to gather keystrokes, login credentials, and screenshots from Fazio users.¹³ The attackers then used the stolen login credentials from Fazio to access a vendor portal (or server) on Target’s network. The attackers escalated privileges on that server (i.e., took over the server) and gained access to Target’s internal network.

Once inside Target’s network, the attackers compromised an internal Windows file server. From this server, the attackers used malware named Trojan.POSRAM (a variant of BlackPOS) to extract customer data from POS terminals. BlackPOS was developed by a 17-year-old from St. Petersburg, Russia, and can be purchased from underground sites for about $2,000.¹⁴

Customer data was continuously sent from the POS terminals to an extraction server within Target’s network. It was then funneled out of Target’s network to drop servers in Russia, Brazil, and Miami. From there, the data was collected and sold on the black market.

## THE DAMAGE

For the attackers, the “damage” was substantial. It’s estimated that the attackers sold about 2 million credit cards for about $26.85 each for a total profit of $53.7 million.¹⁵ The prices for the remaining cards dropped due to the large supply available. The hackers could have chosen to hold back some of the stolen credit cards rather than flooding the market, but they had to sell them as quickly as possible because the stolen cards were being canceled and replaced by banks. The prices of stolen credit cards from Target, sold in batches of 100,000, dropped by 70 percent after 2 months.¹⁶ This was likely due to a reduction in credit card validity rates from nearly 100 percent to about 60 percent.

From the hackers’ point of view, the operation was a spectacular success. The payoff was substantial for just a few weeks of work. Unfortunately, payoffs like these encourage even more data breaches.

Target, on the other hand, incurred much greater losses than the hackers’ gains. It was forced to upgrade its payment terminals to support chip-and-PIN-enabled cards (to prevent cloning cards from stolen information), which cost more than $100 million. From 2015 through 2017 Target settled multiple lawsuits, including $67 million to Visa, $40 million to banks and credit unions, $19 million to Mastercard, $18 million to state governments, and $10 million to customers in a class action lawsuit. Adding to that $154 million in settlement payments, it also had to pay increased insurance premiums, pay legal fees, pay for consumer credit monitoring, and pay regulatory fines.¹⁷

Target also faced a loss of customer confidence and a drop in its revenues (a 46 percent loss for that quarter). In its annual report, Target estimated direct losses at $202 million, but analysts put the loss to Target as high as $450 million.¹⁸ The company lost CIO Beth Jacob, paid CEO Gregg Steinhafel $16 million to leave,¹⁹ and hired its first chief information security officer (CISO).²⁰

The data breach affected more than just Target. The amount of media coverage related to the Target data breach likely accelerated the shift from magnetic swipe cards to EMV-compliant smart cards that happened in 2015. This shift forced the replacement of 800 million payment cards and 14 million POS terminals at a cost of $7 billion.²¹

The good news is that the adoption of EMV-compliant smart cards will greatly reduce the $10 billion in credit card fraud that occurs each year. It will also likely reduce the amount of credit card theft by hackers because stolen credit card numbers are of little value without the physical card.

### Data Breaches
- A data breach happens when an unauthorized person views, alters, or steals secured data
- Billions of records stolen each year in thousands of data breaches costing organizations trillions of dollars
- The largest data breaches involve more than 100 million accounts per breach

### Why Do Data Breaches Happen?
- Stolen data is commonly used for credit card theft, identity theft, extortion, and industrial espionage
- Personally identifiable information (PII) is data that can be used to identify a person

### How Do Data Breaches Happen?
- Attacks are constantly evolving

### The Target Data Breach
- 98 million customer records stolen from point-of-sale system at Target retail stores
- Spear phishing is a targeted phishing attack aimed at a single individual
- Targeted HVAC vendor with custom malware to access Target's internal network
- Hackers sold data for $54 million profit
- Direct losses to Target were $202 million, total losses could be $450 million
