## Hands-on Projects
### Project 1
VeraCrypt® makes the process of securing your private files easy. Using VeraCrypt, you can create a mountable virtual drive from a file on your existing C: drive. The new virtual drive is fully encrypted. You can just drag-and-drop your files into this new drive. Once you are done using the files on the encrypted drive, you just unmount the drive with VeraCrypt.

After unmounting the drive, it will look like a normal (and big) file again. All your data is safely encrypted. If you want to access the files, you simply use VeraCrypt to mount the drive again. The ability to move files directly to another drive without having to enter a password for each individual file saves a great deal of time. VeraCrypt reduces the overall load on users. VeraCrypt is a great solution if you are working with a large number of files that need to be encrypted.

VeraCrypt can also encrypt your entire hard drive, a USB, or a partition. The best part about VeraCrypt is its price. It’s free.

- 1. Download VeraCrypt from www.veracrypt.fr/en/Downloads.html.
- 2. Click Download for the Windows version. (There are also versions for Mac OS and Linux.)
- 3. Click Save.
- 4. Select your C:\security folder.
- 5. If the program doesn’t automatically open, browse to your C:\security folder.
- 6. Right-click on VeraCrypt Setup 1.21.exe and select Run as administrator.
- 7. Click I Accept, Next, Next, Install, OK, No, and Finish.
- 8. Double-click the VeraCrypt icon on your desktop.
- 9. Click Tools and Volume Creation Wizard.
- 10. Click Next, Next, and Select File.
- 11. Browse to your C:\security folder.

- 12. Select any text file with your name showing. (You can create a new text file if one doesn’t exist. In this case, it’s RandyBoyle.txt.)
- 13. Click Save and Yes.
- 14. Take a screenshot.
- 15. Click Next.
- 16. Note the encryption algorithm used.
- 17. Click Next.
- 18. Enter 20 MB for the size of the container.
- 19. Click Next.
- 20. Enter a password that you can remember (in this case, “tiger1234” without quotes).
- 21. Click Next, Format, Yes, OK, and Exit. (The VeraCrypt Volume Creation Wizard will close, but the main VeraCrypt window should still be open.)
- 22. Click on the Q: drive. (You can select any available drive.)
- 23. Click Select File.
- 24. Browse to your C:\security folder.
- 25. Select the text file you chose earlier. (In this case, it was RandyBoyle.txt.)
- 26. Click Open.
- 27. Click Mount.
- 28. Enter the password you chose earlier.
- 29. Click OK.
- 30. Open Windows Explorer.
- 31. Drag-and-drop any file from your C:\security folder to the newly created drive (in this case, the Q: drive).
- 32. Take a screenshot.
- 33. Close Windows Explorer.
- 34. Back in VeraCrypt, click on the Q: drive.
- 35. Click Dismount.
- 36. Open Windows Explorer.

- 37. Browse to your C:\security folder.
- 38. Note the size of the text file you selected earlier. (In this case, RandyBoyle.txt is now 20 MB.)
- 39. Take a screenshot showing your new text file.

### Project 2
After people learn that all of the files they have deleted in the past could be recovered, they only want to know one thing—how to permanently delete those files. It’s not that they are trying to hide anything. They may just want to protect their privacy.

File shredders permanently delete files so they are unrecoverable. Some file shredders can permanently delete files that are still recoverable. In other words, they do this by “wiping” the free space on the hard disk. Free space may contain files that were not securely deleted.

Individuals and companies also need to make sure all confidential data are deleted from hard drives before they dispose of them. Recovering files from discarded hard drives is extremely easy to do. Eraser® will delete any files on your hard drive that were not securely deleted. It will not delete any existing data. It only wipes the free space.

- 1. Download Eraser from http://eraser.heidi.ie/.
- 2. Click Downloads.
- 3. Click on the latest stable build. (At the time of this writing, it was Eraser 6.0.10.)
- 4. Click Save.
- 5. Select your download folder.

- 6. If the program doesn’t automatically open, browse to your download folder.
- 7. Right-click the Eraser executable file you downloaded.
- 8. Select Run as administrator.
- 9. Click Next, Accept, Next, Typical, Install, and Finish.
- 10. Click Start, All Programs, and Eraser. (You can also click on the Eraser icon that was put on your desktop.)
- 11. Right-click the empty Eraser table and select New Task.
- 12. Click Add Data.
- 13. Select Unused disk space.
- 14. Select the drive you want to clean. (A small USB works faster than your hard drive.)
- 15. Click OK and OK.
- 16. Right-click the task labeled “Unused disk space.”
- 17. Select Run now.
- 18. Double-click the task labeled “Unused disk space” to see the progress of the wiping.
- 19. Take a screenshot while it is running. (If it takes too long, you can click Stop. If you selected your C: drive, it will take hours to complete.)
- 20. Open Windows Explorer (your file browser).
- 21. Browse to your downloads folder.
- 22. Right-click any file you want to delete. (You can select any of the prior downloads.)
- 23. Select Eraser and Erase.

## Case Study
### Losing Data Hurts
Losing data hurts. It can be stolen, destroyed, degraded, deleted, misplaced, overwritten, or fried. Data loss is invariably frustrating. In the case of corporate data, it can directly affect the bottom line. As one of this textbook’s authors (Boyle) was preparing a case on corporate data loss, an e-mail came through from Prasanna Ganesan, CTO at VUDU. VUDU is an online video content delivery service owned by Walmart and frequently used by the author to watch online movies. In the e-mail, Ganesan noted that hard drives and other items were stolen during a break-in on March 24, 2013. The information on the stolen hard drives included "names, email addresses, mailing addresses, account activity, dates of birth, and encrypted passwords, but NO full credit card numbers." Ganesan went on to suggest that all affected VUDU customers change their passwords because the stolen passwords, while encrypted, could be decrypted.

Sending an e-mail to your customers telling them you lost their confidential information would be a difficult thing to do. VUDU, and similarly responsible companies, should be applauded for their openness, responsiveness, and willingness to take responsibility for a large-scale data loss. Given the near-daily reports of large-scale data loss, it’s the companies that you don’t hear from that are worrisome.

VUDU responded to this data loss incident correctly. It notified its users quickly and was forthcoming with details. It also offered a year of free credit monitoring, beefed up its physical security, and began forcing users to have stronger passwords.

The interesting aspect of this data loss incident is that it was not electronic. It was a theft of the actual physical disks. Most corporate data loss incidents involve the data being extracted through an insecure web interface, exfiltrated by malware, or copied by a disgruntled insider. Savvy criminals are increasingly aware that there is spinning magnetic gold on corporate hard disks. They will get the data any way possible.

Most corporations collect large amounts of data. These data are usually inexorably tied to the organization’s competitive advantage. The loss of corporate data or the ability to reliably collect data can have dire consequences. For example, losing the technical plans for a novel medical device may result in millions in losses and eventual bankruptcy. On the other hand, losing customer data may result in a temporarily damaged reputation and a minor reduction in your customer base. Losses are dependent on how sensitive the data are.

The following are six key findings from KPMG’s Data Loss Barometer report. These findings provide insight into the threat environment surrounding corporate data losses.

- 1. **Hacking number one data loss threat**—Over the past 5 years, more than 1 billion people globally have been affected by data loss incidents. In the past 2 years, there has been a jump of 40 percent in the number of publicly disclosed data loss incidents. Over the past 5 years, 60 percent of all incidents reported were due to hacking.
- 2. **Health care sector shows significant improvement**—The health care sector, which previously struggled between 2010 and 2011 with the highest number of data loss incidents, has shown dramatic improvement in 2012. The percentage of data loss incidents that affected the health care sector has fallen from a high of 25 percent in 2010 to just 8 percent in 2012.
- 3. **Technology sector number one worst performing sector by number of people affected**—Over the past 5 years, the technology sector had fewer incidents than the top five worst-performing sectors (government, health care, education, financial services, and retail); however, the percentage of people affected by incidents in that industry remains the highest, accounting for 26 percent of the total number of people affected.
- 4. **Insurance sector number one at risk from social engineering and system/human error**—In the first half of 2012, the insurance sector appeared to be at greatest risk from social engineering attacks and system/human error incidents.
- 5. **First time in 5 years that insider threat has decreased and is at an all-time low**—Surprisingly, for the first time over the past 5 years, the threat from malicious insiders has dropped from an average of 25 percent of the total number of incidents in the previous years to an all-time low of 6.5 percent in 2012. Conversely, we see a dramatic rise of double the number of incidents from external sources in 2012 from 2010, accounting for 81 percent of the total number of incidents. This could be because the rise in hacking has taken people’s eyes off the insider threat—KPMG has not seen an improvement in controls to prevent or detect insiders in the period.
- 6. **Overall data loss incidents return to similar levels as 2008**—Following a fall in reported incidents in 2009–2010 when compared to 2008, the trend has reversed with a higher number of incidents reported in 2011 and with total incident numbers in 2012 almost returning to 2008 levels. This could be accounted for by a maturing regulatory environment where incidents are being identified and monitored more thoroughly but is also likely to be a result of the dramatic increase in the sophistication and variety of attacks we have seen in the past 18 months.
