
- *Confidentiality*—Confidentiality means that people cannot read sensitive information, either while it is on a computer or while it is traveling across a network.
- *Integrity*—Integrity means that attackers cannot change or destroy information, either while it is on a computer or while it is traveling across a network. Or, at least, if information is changed or destroyed, then the receiver can detect the change or restore destroyed data.
- *Availability*—Availability means that people who are authorized to use information are not prevented from doing so. Neither a computer attack nor a network attack will keep them away from the information they are authorized to access.

Many security specialists are unhappy with the simplistic CIA goal taxonomy because they feel that companies have many other security goals. However, the CIA goals are a good place to begin. Understanding incidents is a critical skill. In terms of the business process model, threats push the business process away from meeting one or more of its goals.

> When a threat succeeds in causing harm to a business, this is called an incident, breach, or compromise.

## COUNTERMEASURES

Naturally, security professionals try to stop threats. The methods they use to thwart attacks are called **countermeasures**, **safeguards**, **protections**, or **controls**. The goal of countermeasures is to keep business processes on track for meeting their business goals despite the presence of threats and actual compromises.

> Tools used to thwart attacks are called countermeasures, safeguards, or controls.

Countermeasures can be technical, human, or (most commonly) a mixture of the two. Typically, countermeasures are classified into three types:

- *Preventative*—Preventative countermeasures keep attacks from succeeding. Most controls are preventative controls.
- *Detective*—Detective countermeasures identify when a threat is attacking and especially when it is succeeding. Fast detection can minimize damage.
- *Corrective*—Corrective countermeasures get the business process back on track after a compromise. The faster the business process can get back on track, the more likely the business process will be to meet its goals.

## 1.2 Data Breaches

### 1.2 Describe the impact of data breaches.

A **data breach** happens when an unauthorized person views, alters, or steals secured data. Data breaches are particularly worrisome because of the amount of damage they can do to an organization. As mentioned earlier, more than 5 billion records were stolen in 2018.¹ Considering that there are only 7.7 billion people on the entire earth and only 4.2 billion of those people have access to the Internet at home, it’s likely that nearly everyone lost their records at least once in 2018.²

### COST OF DATA BREACHES

To give you an idea of how this affects organizations, Accenture’s 2019 *Cost of Cybercrime* report notes that, on average, a data breach in the United States costs an organization $13 million.³ The estimated cumulative global costs of data breaches between 2019 and 2023 will be $5.2 trillion. *Direct* costs of handling a data breach include paying for notification, detection, escalation, remediation, legal fees, and consultation.

Accenture also estimates that *indirect* costs related to loss of reputation, abnormal customer turnover, and increased customer acquisition activities averaged an additional $3.9 million per incident in the United States.

So, data breaches are costly. But what are the chances that your organization will be involved in a data breach? Based on actual responses from more than 2,200 organizations, the Ponemon Institute estimates that there is a 28 percent chance of experiencing a data breach over any given 24-month period.⁴ Take a minute to consider the costs and probabilities of a data breach at your organization. There is a one-in-four chance that your organization will lose records in the next year. Each of those records will cost on average $148.⁵ Is your organization financially, technically, and logistically prepared for such a breach?

## Why Do Data Breaches Happen?

More than 67 percent of data breaches come from hackers trying to make money. Hackers are primarily looking for **personally identifiable information (PII)**, or data that can be used to identify a person. This includes names, addresses, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, credit card numbers, health records, bank account numbers, PINs, and e-mail addresses.

Stolen information is commonly used for credit card fraud. Stolen credit card information is validated and then bundled and sold on the black market. The price of stolen credit cards can run from $9 to $26 per card depending on the type of account.⁷ Interestingly, if a large number of credit card accounts are stolen and offered for sale at one time, the per-card price can drop as low as $0.75! This is exactly what happened after the Target data breach.

Credit card fraud isn’t the only way criminals make money from data breaches. Stolen data is commonly used for identity theft, extortion, and industrial espionage. Any organization with valuable data may be a target. For example, a hacker group named Rex Mundi tried to extort €30,000 (about $40,500) from Domino’s Pizza in France and Belgium by threatening to release the company’s user account details (names, addresses, phone numbers, e-mails, passwords, and pizza preferences).⁸ It’s unknown whether Domino’s paid the hacker group.

Not all data breaches are the result of hackers in distant locales. Rogue internal employees can steal data even more easily than external hackers. In one stunning example, a 39-year-old technician working for the Korea Credit Bureau stole personal information for 104 million credit cards belonging to nearly 20 million people in South Korea.⁹ This single data breach affected 40 percent of all South Koreans. The employee, identified only as Mr. Park, had been hired to improve the security of client data. He attempted to sell the stolen information to executives at a phone marketing company.
