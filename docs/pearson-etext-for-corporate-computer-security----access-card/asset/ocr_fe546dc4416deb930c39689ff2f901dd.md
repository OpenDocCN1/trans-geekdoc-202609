# New from Black Hat

Hackers, security professionals, and government agents flock to Las Vegas each year to attend an important security conference: Black Hat. Black Hat caters to hackers, security professionals, corporations, and government entities.

Each year speakers make briefings on how things can be hacked. Presenters show exactly how to exploit weaknesses in hardware, software, protocols, or systems. One session may show you how to hack your smartphone, while another may show you how to empty the cash out of an ATM.

Presentations encourage companies to fix product vulnerabilities and serve as an educational forum for hackers, developers, manufacturers, and government agencies. The following are highlights from the 2017 Black Hat conference:

# Infected IoT Followers

Security researchers Masarah-Cynthia Paquet-Clouston and Olivier Bilodeau discussed a new way hackers are using botnets. After watching the behavior of the Linux/Moose botnet, they noticed that it wasn’t being used to attack firms. Rather, it was being used in a social media fraud scam.¹²

The malware would infect IoT devices and then use those devices to create and manage fake social media accounts. These IoT devices would be routers, cameras, toasters, doorbells, or any other IP-enabled device. Users would be completely unaware their new IoT device was part of a large botnet.

The hackers needed a large botnet to create and manage the millions of social media accounts because social media providers would block accounts if too many of them came from a single IP address. The large botnet of IoT devices would have enough IP address to spread out the fake accounts and not get blocked. The hackers could use the IoT devices to log into social media accounts, make posts, check inboxes, follow other users, and so on.

The researchers found that people looking to buy fake followers could buy them in bundles of 1,000 ($2.95) or 50,000 ($249). They noted that these people were often celebrities, bodybuilders, or web developers. One group that bought the most followers was political campaigns. Based on the size of the botnet, they estimated the hackers’ *monthly* income between $200,000 and $400,000.

# Smartlock Hacking

Security researcher Michael Huebler showed how to hack smartlocks at a high-end hotel in Europe.¹³ Hotels are moving

away from using keys or magnetic cards to lower costs and increase guest satisfaction. Smartlocks allow guests unlock the door to their room with their smartphone rather than swiping a magnetic card. Unfortunately, using a smartphone to unlock a smartlock creates additional problems because there are more points of failure. Now a hacker can focus on two smart devices or a Bluetooth connection.

Huebler analyzed Bluetooth traffic and found that the smartlock was vulnerable to a key-stealing attack. An exploit was then used to access the device and unlock the door. As long as the hacker was able to get close enough to the door to capture packets, the hacker could gain access to the room. Huebler and his team contacted the smartlock manufacturer and provided it with details about the attack. The manufacturer was able to create a patch and update *most* of the smartlocks. Unfortunately, some of the smartlocks weren’t able to be updated remotely. They would need to be updated by a person going door-to-door in each hotel using the locks. At the time of Huebler’s presentation, the smart-locks had not been updated and remained vulnerable.

The vulnerability in this particular smartlock isn’t unique. Researchers have found vulnerabilities in several other smartlocks. Thinking more broadly, Huebler’s presentation highlighted vulnerabilities showing up in a variety of IoT devices. Many IoT devices have been developed very quickly in order to gain market share in highly competitive markets. *Rapid* application development rarely leaves time for *secure* application development.

+   1. Why is social media fraud so damaging?
+   2. Why would social media providers like Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter be interested in mitigating social media fraud?
+   3. The market value of a social media company is largely based on the number of users on their platform. If the social media company is infected with bots, what happens to the value of the company? Why might investors be interested in the number of bots infecting social media companies?
+   4. Besides smartlocks, what other IoT devices might be vulnerable to a Bluetooth attack like the one described in this feature? Which devices might be of most concern?
+   5. How long should security researchers wait before disclosing a vulnerability to a manufacturer? How long should security researchers give the manufacturer to fix the vulnerability before they disclose it to the public? Why are these delays important?
