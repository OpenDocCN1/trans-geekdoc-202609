
# Hazard Analysis, Evaluation, and Direction

This section establishes risk and complications regarding the connections and differences between hazard analysis, appraisal, and management.

In Chapter 6, we presented recent research on assessing threats and vulnerabilities, including the Cloud Security Alliance (`CSA`) initiative, to examine the top threats against the cloud and gain better insight into how well Cloud Service Providers (`CSPs`) are prepared for them. The `CNIL` recently ran a privacy threat assessment for the cloud. `CNIL`’s work goes further by introducing steps to decrease risks to acceptable levels. Chapter 6 covered trust and risk models. At the same phase, we also present two models developed by `A4Cloud`, a European Framework Seven project.

The first is the **Cloud Adopted Risk Assessment Model** (`CARAM`), a qualitative design that adapts `ENISA` and `CNIL` frameworks for specific `CSP`–`CC` pairs based on controls employed by `CSPs` and resources the Cloud Customer (`CC`) intends to store or process in the cloud. The second model is the **Joint Risk and Trust Model** (`JRTM`), a quantitative model based on `CSP` operational information.

## Risk Definition and Uncertainty

A risk is defined as the impact of uncertainty on goals. If we are certain about the outcome of a procedure, there is no risk associated with it. Risks correlate not only with adverse outcomes (threats) but also favorable outcomes (opportunities). Missing an opportunity can be treated as a hazard. Therefore, uncertainty is the principal element in hazard analysis. Sources of doubt can be grouped into two broad categories: **epistemic** and **aleatory**.

### Epistemic Uncertainties
Epistemic uncertainties result from a deficiency of knowledge. As the cloud ecosystem and solutions grow, this category of doubts will decrease or move to the aleatory uncertainty domain.

### Aleatory Uncertainties
Aleatory is derived from the Latin term *Alea* ("rolling a die"). Aleatory doubts are based on the inherent randomness of the process or phenomenon under analysis. It implies that available information suffices for constructing probabilities or frequency distributions.

## Quantitative Hazard Analysis

When doubts can be treated as aleatory, a quantitative hazard analysis (Kaplan and Garrick 1981) can be completed. Three inquiries are answered:

*   The probability `pi` of all `si` (i.e., the likelihood that the situation is accomplished)
*   The result `xi` of `si`

The likelihood of a situation relies on:
*   Occurrence of vulnerabilities
*   Threats that may exploit vulnerabilities
*   Awareness of risks about vulnerabilities
*   Capabilities and willingness of threats to exploit vulnerabilities

A threat is fundamentally the product of risks, vulnerabilities, and the results of the exploitation of vulnerabilities by threats (i.e., the effects of danger).

## Qualitative Hazard Analysis

When doubts are mostly epistemic, a qualitative hazard analysis may be conducted. A qualitative scale is used for:
*   **Likelihood**: almost certain, probable, possible, unlikely, infrequent
*   **Impacts**: critical, necessary, moderate, small, insignificant

Note the use of *likelihood* rather than *probability* for qualitative analysis.

### Perceived vs. Absolute Risk
Risk perception for the same situation may differ between individuals or over time (perceived risk/relative risk). Absolute risk is the same for everyone but difficult to calculate. In this chapter, **relative risk** refers to the risk of a plan of action in contrast to another plan of action.

**Examples of relative risk:**
*   Utilizing the cloud vs. your own infrastructure and applications.
*   Getting services from one `CSP` vs. another `CSP`.

### Analysis Techniques
*   **Event Tree Analysis**: Inductive (bottom-up) method to assess ramifications of working or neglected systems given an event has occurred.
*   **Fault Tree Analysis**: Deductive (top-down) technique working from the upper-level undesired event to identify root causes for each branch.

## Risk Evaluation, Assessment, and Management

**Risk evaluation** is a systematic evaluation of a hazard scenario to comprehend its probability/likelihood and effects.
**Risk assessment** analyzes a risk situation (high, moderate, low) based on probability/likelihood and effects.
**Risk management** is the process of identifying, assessing, analyzing, communicating hazard situations, and mitigating them as necessary.

### Mitigation Strategies
Strategies can target causes or results of threat scenarios:
*   **Risk Acceptance**: Does not lessen likelihood or effect. Chosen when avoidance/limitation/transference costs are too high relative to impact.
*   **Risk Avoidance**: The activity subject to the threat is not undertaken.
*   **Risk Limitation**: Plans implemented to restrict causes or results.
*   **Risk Transference**: Threat moved to another party (e.g., insurance provider) at a cost.

The `CSA` list of top threats is a significant starting point. `CSA` conducted a poll among specialists and stakeholders, publishing results in *"The infamous nine: cloud computing top dangers in 2013"* (previous version 2010).
