At the time of writing, Google has a presence in more than 200 countries, with 29 regions, 88 zones, and 146 PoP or edge locations. As part of Google's strategy to expand, there are more regions announced every year.

Each region has at least two zones, and some have more than two zones to provide additional capacity and resilience. This is dictated by demand. As a rule of thumb, there are always two zones to provide fault tolerance and high availability. In order to address the resiliency and availability of your services, it is highly recommended to identify them, which is `<region>`, for example, us-central. Furthermore, a, b, or c is used to identify the zone, for example, us-east1-a. You can select any of these zones to deploy your cloud services, as compute instances.

Google Cloud service availability is also region-specific. You can see whether the service you want is available in the region of choice by navigating to `https://packt.link/ehHg8`.

We will now take a look at different types of resources that are either global, regional, or zonal in nature. As names suggest, global resources are global in nature, which means when configured inside a project, these resources can be accessed by any other resource in any zone as long as they are in the same project. VPC networks, firewall rules, routes, images, and snapshots are all examples of global resources.

Regional resources are only available in the region; for example, the subnets that you create are accessible only in the regions they are in and cannot cross a regional boundary. Some examples of regional resources include managed instance groups and cloud interconnects.

Zonal or per-zone resources are created in a zone and are available only within that zone. A compute instance that is created requires a zone to be specified where the resource will exist. This resource can access any other global or regional resources but cannot access any other per-zone resource, such as a disk.
