# Policy best practices

The following best practices apply to organizations with many Google Cloud users:

+   **Managing a large number of users:** When managing multiple user accounts with the same access, use Google groups. Each individual user account becomes a member of a group and the group is what inherited instead of individual user accounts.
* **Permissions granted at the organizational level:** Think about which principals have permissions at the organizational level. Only a few specific teams (such as security and network teams) should have access to this level of the resource hierarchy in most organizations.
* **Grant permissions at the folder level:** When granting permissions at the folder level, consider using layers of folders to match your organization's operation structure, with each parent/child folder having varying sets of grants that are aligned with business and operation demands. A folder, for example, could represent a department, while one of its children could represent resource or a group, and another child folder could represent a small team. All folders may manage projects related to their team's operational requirements. This method of using folders ensures correct isolation while adhering to policies inherited from the parent folder(s) and organization. When building and administering Google Cloud resources, this demands minimal policy maintenance.
* **Permissions provided at the project level:** When necessary, grant bindings at the project level to adhere to the principle of least privilege. For example, if a principal requires access to three of ten projects in the folder you should grant access to each of the projects separately; however, granting on the folder would grant the principal access to another seven projects they do not require.
* **Grant selectively:** You may also grant roles at the organizational or folder level using IAM Conditions.
* **Regularly review and audit IAM policies:** Conduct regular audits of IAM policies to identify any unnecessary or excessive permissions. Review the access granted to service accounts, and groups. Remove any outdated or unused permissions to minimize security risks.
* **Use IAM Conditions:** Leverage IAM Conditions to enforce additional security constraints based on contextual attributes as IP address, time of day, or device status. IAM Conditions provide granular control over access to resources and can enhance security for specific use cases.
* **Implement monitoring and logging:** Enable monitoring and logging for IAM-related activities to detect and respond to any suspicious or anomalous behavior. Monitor and analyze logs to identify any unauthorized access attempts, policy violations, or unusual patterns of activity.
* **Educate and train users:** Provide regular training and awareness programs to educate users about IAM policies, best practices, and the importance of security. Promote a culture of security within the organization and encourage users to follow secure practices when managing IAM.
