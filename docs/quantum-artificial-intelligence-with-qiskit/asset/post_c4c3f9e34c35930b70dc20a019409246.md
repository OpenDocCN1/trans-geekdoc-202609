### 15.4.4 Tree-Like Structures

We want to increase the probability of measuring the correct units representing the ones in the answer value and decrease the probability of measuring the zeros. For example, in a sparse code with $k$ nodes, $k$ measurements of different ones reconstruct the binary answer vector and we cannot use the idea of applying Tarbert amplification several times indicated before. Instead we can increase the probability of measuring one by the introduced tree-like structure [91]. The tree-like hierarchical associative approach is based on aggregating neighboring units [91]. The aggregation is a Boolean OR based transform for two or three neighboring weights of units resulting in a more dense memory, see Figure 15.17. It was shown by computer experiments that the aggregation between two and three is optimal [92]. The more dense memory is copied on top of the original memory. Depending on the number of units we can repeat the process in which we aggregate groups of two to three neighboring groups of equal units. We continue the process until we arrive in different groups of different units, the number of possible different aggregated memories is logarithmic, with $\log(n-1)$. Since in our example only four units are present we aggregate two results in a memory of four described by 2 identical units.

The query vector is composed of $\log(n-1)$ concatenated copies of the original

### CONCLUSION
The cost of the sleep phase and active phase are the same as one of conventional memory $O(n^2)$. We assume that we have enough time to prepare quantum memories in superposition until they are queried. Each of the quantum memories can be queried only once. We argue that the advantage to associative memories is present in the active phase the fast determination of information $O(\log n)$ is essential by the use of quantum memories in superposition compared to the cost of classical $O(n \sqrt{n})$ or $O(n^2)$.

## Amplitude Encoding

Amplitude encoding encodes a real or complex vector of length one into the amplitudes of a quantum state. We describe the top-down strategy step by step. Instead of representing the binary tree by multi-control gates, we can use simple rotation gates. The resulting circuits, however, require the same number of qubits as the operation. We cannot access the desired states but estimate the value between them.

### ENCODING EXAMPLE
Amplitude encoding encodes data into $\omega_i$ of a quantum state. For example, a normalized vector:
$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i |i\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{0.03} \\ \sqrt{0.07} \\ \sqrt{0.15} \\ \sqrt{0.05} \\ \sqrt{0.1} \\ \sqrt{0.3} \\ \sqrt{0.2} \\ \sqrt{0.1} \end{pmatrix}$$
with qiskit little ordering $|q_2q_1q_0\rangle$:
$$|\psi\rangle = \sqrt{0.03} \cdot |000\rangle + \sqrt{0.07} \cdot |001\rangle + \sqrt{0.15} \cdot |010\rangle + \sqrt{0.05} \cdot |011\rangle + \sqrt{0.1} \cdot |100\rangle + \sqrt{0.3} \cdot |101\rangle + \sqrt{0.2} \cdot |110\rangle + \sqrt{0.1} \cdot |111\rangle$$

### 16.2 TOP-DOWN DIVISION STRATEGY
We build a top-down binary tree that divides the probability of observing $|q_2$ on the first level, to the probability of observing $|q_2q_1$ on the second level, and finally the probability of observing $|q_2q_1q_0$ on the third level representing the required-position $|\psi|$. The binary tree is represented by multi-control rotation gates (see Figure 16.1) and requires $\log_2 n$ qubits to represent a vector of dimension $n$.

### 16.2.1 Level
The probability of observing $q_2 = 0$ is $\sqrt{0.3}$:
$$\sqrt{0.03} \cdot |000\rangle + \sqrt{0.07} \cdot |001\rangle + \sqrt{0.15} \cdot |010\rangle + \sqrt{0.05} \cdot |011\rangle$$
and probability of observing $q_2 = 1$ is $\sqrt{0.7}$. We use a parameterized RY gate:
$$R_Y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\frac{\theta}{2}) & -\sin(\frac{\theta}{2}) \\ \sin(\frac{\theta}{2}) & \cos(\frac{\theta}{2}) \end{pmatrix}$$
to perform rotation of one qubit along the y-axis by the rotation angle $\theta$ (in radiants):
$$\theta_0 = 1.98231 = 2 \cdot \arccos(\sqrt{0.3}) \quad (16.2)$$
with (see Figure 16.1):
$$RY_{\theta_0}|0\rangle = \sqrt{0.3} \cdot |0\rangle + \sqrt{0.7} \cdot |1\rangle \quad (16.3)$$
