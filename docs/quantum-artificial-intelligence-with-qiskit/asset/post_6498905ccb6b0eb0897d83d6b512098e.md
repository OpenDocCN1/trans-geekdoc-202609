## 17.1 QUANTUM KERNELS

For a quantum state $|\phi(x)\rangle$ and $|\phi(y)\rangle$, the inner product of two states is called a quantum kernel

$$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \langle\phi(\mathbf{x})|\phi(\mathbf{y})\rangle$$

with an inner product

$$(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^T \cdot \mathbf{y}.$$

The absolute value of the inner product can be estimated by the swap test [95].

## 17.2 QUANTUM KERNELS AND SWAP TEST

A quantum computer can estimate a quantum kernel and the estimate can be used by a kernel method on a classical computer [95]. This is because the exponential quantum advantage in evaluating inner products allows to estimate the quantum kernel maps directly in the higher-dimensional space. If the result is impossible to be simulated on a classical computer, then the kernel is classically intractable [94]. We can map

$$|\phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle \rightarrow |\mathbf{x}\rangle \otimes \cdots \otimes |\mathbf{x}\rangle = |\mathbf{x}\rangle^{\otimes m}$$

## 17.3 QUANTUM KERNELS AND INVERSION TEST

Quantum maps encode classical data into quantum data via a parametrized quantum circuit [40]. Instead of coding the classical feature vector $x$ of dimension $z$ by amplitudes, the feature vector is defined by $z$ parameters of the parametrized quantum circuit $U_{\phi(x)}$ with $z \geq m$

$$|\phi(x)\rangle = U_{\phi(x)}|0^{\otimes m} \quad (17.6)$$

with dimension of $\phi(x)$ being $2^m$. Parametrized quantum circuits based on superposition and entanglement are able to simulate and could lead to an advantage over classical machine learning approaches. The inversion test is based on the idea of estimating the fidelity (similarity) between two states [40]. For an input state $|0^{\otimes m}\rangle$ if we map it by parametrized quantum circuit $U_{\phi(x)}$ and un-compute it by $U_{\phi(x)}^{-1}$ the inverse if the parametrized quantum circuit $U_{\phi(x)}$ the probability of measuring the state $|0^{\otimes m}\rangle$ is one. If we represent the quantum circuit by a matrix $U_{\phi(x)}$, the inverse quantum circuit represented as a matrix corresponds to the conjugate transpose $U_{\phi(x)}^*$, also written as $U_{\phi(x)}^T$. If we parametrized quantum circuit $U$ by $x$ ($U_{\phi(x)}$) and the inverse of the parametrized quantum $U^1$ by $y$ ($U_{\phi(y)}^*$) and if $x$ and $y$ are similar, the probability of measuring $|0^{\otimes m}\rangle$ for the input $|0^{\otimes m}\rangle$ should be near 1,

$$U_{\phi(y)}^* U_{\phi(x)}|0^{\otimes m}\rangle \quad (17.7)$$

If $x$ and $y$ differ, the probability is smaller.
