
# HHL

The quantum algorithm for linear systems of equations is one of the main fundamental algorithms expected to provide a speedup over their classical counterparts. In honor of its inventors Aram Harrow, Avinatan Hassidim, and Seth Lloyd, it is called the HHL. It is going to be one of the most useful subroutines for any quantum machine learning algorithm because almost machine learning uses some form of a linear system of equations. For example, in support vector machines and quantum support vector machines, maximizing the objective function with the optimum values of the Lagrange multipliers are based on solving linear equations. We describe the HHL algorithm, give an example step by step using qiskit Hamiltonian, and indicate the constraints of the algorithm.

## 22.1 QUANTUM ALGORITHM FOR LINEAR SYSTEMS OF EQUATIONS

We see systems of equations in many real-life applications across a wide range of areas. For an invertible complex matrix $n \times n$ $A$ and a complex vector $\mathbf{b}$

$$A \cdot \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$$

we want to find $\mathbf{x}$. If $A^* = A$ (for real matrix $A^T = A$) then A can be represented by a linear spectral decomposition

$$A = \lambda_1 \cdot |u_1\rangle\langle u_1| + \lambda_2 \cdot |u_2\rangle\langle u_2| + \cdots + \lambda_n \cdot |u_n\rangle\langle u_n|$$

and

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\lambda_1} |u_1\rangle\langle u_1| + \frac{1}{\lambda_2} |u_2\rangle\langle u_2| + \cdots + \frac{1}{\lambda_n} |u_n\rangle\langle u_n|$$

It follows

$$A^{-1} \cdot |u_j\rangle = \frac{1}{\lambda_j} |u_j\rangle$$

and writing $|b\rangle$ as a linear combination of the eigenvectors of $A$

$$|b\rangle = \sum_{j} |u_j\rangle\langle u_j|b\rangle$$

leads to

$$A \cdot |b\rangle = \sum_{j} \lambda_j |u_j\rangle \langle u_j|b\rangle \quad (22.6)$$

and

$$|x\rangle = A^{-1} \cdot |b\rangle = \sum_{j} \lambda_j^{-1} |u_j\rangle \langle u_j|b\rangle \quad (22.7)$$

How can we estimate the eigenvalues effectively? When we know $e^{-tA}$ with a Hermitian $A$ is

$$U = e^{tA} = e^{t\lambda_1} |u_1\rangle \langle u_1| + e^{t\lambda_2} |u_2\rangle \langle u_2| + \cdots + e^{t\lambda_n} \cdot |u_n\rangle \langle u_n| \quad (22.8)$$

and

$$U^* = U^{-1} = e^{-tA} = e^{-t\lambda_1} |u_1\rangle \langle u_1| + e^{-t\lambda_2} |u_2\rangle \langle u_2| + \cdots + e^{-t\lambda_n} \cdot |u_n\rangle \langle u_n| \quad (22.9)$$

with

$$U \cdot U^* = I$$

Using Kitaev's phase algorithm, we could then estimate unknown eigenvalue $e^{2\pi i}$. If we apply $U$ to $|u_j\rangle$ we get

$$U \cdot |u_j\rangle = e^{2\pi i} |u_j\rangle = e^{i\lambda_j} |u_j\rangle \quad (22.10)$$

We do not need to perform the decomposition of eigenvector and eigenvalues of $A$ since this could lead to a circular problem.

### 2.2.1 Hamiltonian Simulation

The process of implementing a given Hamiltonian evolution on quantum computer is called Hamiltonian simulation.

$$|x(t)\rangle = e^{-itH} \cdot |x(0)\rangle = U_t \cdot |x(0)\rangle \quad (22.12)$$

The challenge is due to the fact that application of matrix exponentials is expensive. Finding reliable and accurate methods to compute the matrix exponential is difficult, and this is still a topic of current research.

### 2.2.1.1 Diagonalization

If the matrix $H$ is diagonal

$$H = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & a_2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & a_n \end{pmatrix} \quad (22.13)$$

then its exponential can be obtained by exponentiating each entry on the diagonal

$$e^H = \begin{pmatrix} e^{a_1} & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & e^{a_2} & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & e^{a_n} \end{pmatrix} \quad (22.14)$$

This result also allows one to exponentiate diagonalizable matrices

$$H = U \cdot D \cdot U^{-1} \quad (22.15)$$

and $D$ is diagonal, then

$$e^H = U \cdot e^D \cdot U^{-1} \quad (22.16)$$

### 2.2.1.2 Product Formulas

If the Hamiltonian $H$ can be written as

$$H = \sum_{k=1}^l H_k \quad (22.17)$$

and $H_k$ commute,

$$H_j \cdot H_k = H_k \cdot H_j, \quad \text{for all } j, k$$

then

$$e^{-itH} = e^{-itH_1} \cdot e^{-itH_2} \cdot \dots \cdot e^{-itH_l} \quad \text{for all } t. \quad (22.18)$$

General case: For general case of non-commuting $H_k$

$$e^{-itH} \neq e^{-itH_1} \cdot e^{-itH_2} \cdot \dots \cdot e^{-itH_l} \quad \text{for all } t. \quad (22.19)$$

For non-commuting $H_k$ the following asymptotic Suzuki-Trotter formula (Lie product formula) is true for real $t$.

$$e^{-itH} = \lim_{m \to \infty} e^{-itH_1/m} \cdot e^{-itH_2/m} \cdot \dots \cdot e^{-itH_l/m} \quad (22.20)$$

or we can write equivalently

$$e^{-itH} = e^{-itH_1} \cdot e^{-itH_2} \cdot \dots \cdot e^{-itH_l} + O(t^2) \quad (22.21)$$

For small $t$ the factorization is approximately valid,

$$U_t = e^{-itH_1} \approx e^{-itH_2} \cdot \dots \cdot e^{-itH_l}. \quad (22.22)$$

## 22.2.2 Kitaev's phase Estimation

After applying Kitaev's phase estimation algorithm to $U$ that was estimated by the Hamiltonian simulation for each value $j$, the values $\lambda_{kj}$ approximate the true value $\lambda_j$. For simplicity we assume

$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=0}^{T-1} \alpha_{kj} \cdot |\lambda_{kj}|u_j\rangle \approx \sum_{j=1}^{n} \beta_j \cdot |\tilde{\lambda}_j|u_j\rangle$$

## 22.2.3 Conditioned Rotation

We add an auxiliary state $|0\rangle$

$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} \beta_j \cdot |\tilde{\lambda}_j|u_j\rangle|0\rangle$$

and perform the conditioned rotation on the auxiliary state $|0\rangle$ by the operator $R$

$$R = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \alpha & -\sin \alpha \\ \sin \alpha & \cos \alpha \end{pmatrix}$$

with the relation

$$\alpha = \arccos\left(\frac{C}{\lambda}\right)$$

with $C$ being a constant of normalization. For each eigenvalue we have

$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} \beta_j \cdot |\tilde{\lambda}_j|u_j\rangle(R(\lambda_j^{-1})|0\rangle) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \beta_j \cdot |\tilde{\lambda}_j|u_j\rangle\left(\frac{C}{\lambda_j} + \sqrt{1-\frac{C^2}{\lambda_j^2}}\right)|0\rangle$$

## 22.2.4 Un-computation

We uncompute the phase estimation procedure with $F_m^*$ resulting in the state

$$|0\rangle\sum_{j=1}^{n} \beta_j \cdot |u_j\rangle\left(\frac{C}{\lambda_j} + \sqrt{1-\frac{C^2}{\lambda_j^2}}\right)|0\rangle$$

## 22.2.5 Measurement

By measuring the auxiliary qubit with the result 0, we get

$$|0\rangle\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left( 1 - \frac{C^2}{\lambda_j^2} \right) \cdot \beta_j |u_j\rangle = C|0\rangle \quad (22.29)$$

and with result 1

$$|0\rangle\sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{\beta_j}{\lambda_j} |u_j\rangle = A^{-1}(b) \approx C|x\rangle \quad (22.30)$$

## 22.2.6 Obtaining the Solution

Obtaining the required coefficient $x_i$ from $|x\rangle$ would require at least $n$ measurements, so the complexity of the algorithm would be $O(n)$ which prevents the cost on a classical computer for an approximate solution for a sparse matrix. We are interested in the global properties of $|x\rangle$ rather than the coefficients $x_i$. Many features describing the vector $|x\rangle$ can be efficiently extracted, for example we can well estimate if two different solutions are the same or not.

## 22.3 EXAMPLE

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1/3 \\ -1/3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. \quad (22.32)$$

and eigenvectors

$$|u_1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad |u_2\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \quad (22.33)$$

and eigenvalues

$$\lambda_1 = \frac{4}{3}, \quad \lambda_2 = \frac{2}{3}$$

It follows

$$U = e^{-i\frac{4}{3}\pi} \cdot |u_1\rangle\langle u_1| + e^{-i\frac{2}{3}\pi} \cdot |u_2\rangle\langle u_2| = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{4}{3}\pi} + \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{2}{3}\pi} & \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{4}{3}\pi} - \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{2}{3}\pi} \\ \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{4}{3}\pi} - \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{2}{3}\pi} & \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{4}{3}\pi} + \frac{1}{2}e^{-i\frac{2}{3}\pi} \end{pmatrix} \quad (22.34)$$

and

$$U^* = e^{i\frac{4}{3}\pi} \cdot |u_1\rangle\langle u_1| + e^{i\frac{2}{3}\pi} \cdot |u_2\rangle\langle u_2| = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{4}{3}\pi} + \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{2}{3}\pi} & \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{4}{3}\pi} - \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{2}{3}\pi} \\ \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{4}{3}\pi} - \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{2}{3}\pi} & \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{4}{3}\pi} + \frac{1}{2}e^{i\frac{2}{3}\pi} \end{pmatrix} \quad (22.35)$$
