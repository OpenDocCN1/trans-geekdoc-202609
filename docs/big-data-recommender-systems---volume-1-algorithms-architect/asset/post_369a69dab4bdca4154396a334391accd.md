Developing recommendations for LBSNs to provide a user with items that may be interesting for him/her (a venue or an event with a geographic location) has recently attracted research attention [4–6]. This application has become more useful and important when a user travels to an area far away from his/her hometown, where/she has little knowledge about the neighborhood. In this scenario, the spatial item recommendation has been proposed as recommendation for out-of-town users in LBSNs [7,8]. In this chapter, we focus on the effective and spatial item recommendation for users on LBSNs in both hometown and out-of-town scenarios by considering their historical data in LBSNs. As suggested in many working papers about LBSNs [9–12], geographical movement demonstrates significant temporal patterns on LBSNs and is also highly relevant to the location property. Therefore, the recommendation should be both time and location oriented.

Spatial item recommendation is highly challenging because of the following reasons:

(1) travel locality: with the rapid expansion of LBSNs, the number of spatial items visited by individual user is smaller compared to the total number of spatial items in LBSN, leading to sparse user-item matrix. Travel locality refers to the scenario that most users tend to check in near their living regions. According to the survey in [13], the check-in activities generated by users in non-home cities take only 47% of the ones generated in their cities. Travel locality aggregates the data sparsity problem in spatial recommendation in out-of-town scenarios (e.g., recommending spatial items to people from New York).

(2) Spatial dynamics of user behavior: when users travel in different geographical regions, their behavior patterns tend to change [14]. We have conducted a survey on Foursquare dataset. Specifically, with the application interfaces (APIs) published by Foursquare, we extract the top categories of check-in spatial items in different cities including Boston, Vegas and Gold Coast for a specific group of users. The number of users in the group is 3000 and each user in this group has checked in all all three cities. The percentage of check-in of each item category is that for the same group of users, they are more interested in casino (80.32%), nightlife (10.61%) and outlet (5.82%) when they are in Vegas, prefer beach (71.36%), surf (14.82%) and park (9.60%) when they are in Gold Coast.

(3) Temporal dynamics of user behavior: temporal influence also plays an important role in impacting users' daily activities in LBSNs [9–15]. For example, a user is more likely to go to a restaurant now than to a bar. An intuitive solution of temporal influence modeling is splitting time into slices at a predefined granularity (e.g., hourly or seasonal) and modeling the temporal preference of spatial items in each slice by mining the activities of the user. However, splitting the users' activities into multiple slices according to time makes the activity data sparser. Thus, modeling the temporal dynamics of user behavior is also challenging.

Recently, several methods have been developed to make spatial recommendations in both hometown and out-of-town scenarios. These existing approaches either not address all challenges or address challenges with ineffective strategies. For example, a CF-based method proposed recommendation for a target user based on the interests of both users who have visited common spatial items and friends from the user's social connections in LBSNs. Including spatial friends is supposed to handle travel locality when users travel to a distant place where the target user has limited check-in activities, by making recommendation according to activities social friends in the target region. However, according to the survey in [11], when a user travels to a place visited by his/her friends more than 100 km from hometown, the check-in probability at the target region is merely 7%. This means that a place far away from a user's hometown, his/her friends have few activities in LBSNs. Bao et al. model users' interests on category information of spatial items and consider both user personal interests and opinions of local experts. The opinions of local experts are expected to solve the travel locality problem. Nevertheless, the local experts are chosen from users who demonstrate same or similar interests with the target user by mining history activities. In this way, the local experts still have similar history activities with the target user, and this approach is unable to address spatial dynamics of user behavior.

In this chapter, we focus on efficient spatial item recommendation by dealing with the aforementioned challenges and propose ST-SAGE, an spatiotemporal model. Traditional mixture models [16] combine multiple factors by introducing additional latent variables as switches switches to control which factor is currently active. It is not only computationally expensive to learn switching variables for individual users to enable personalized recommendation but also difficult to learn these variables accurately when data is sparse, which is usually the case in most recommendation systems. Inspired by the SAGE model [17], we have designed our model in a way by adding the effect of all the facets in the log space to avoid the inference of the massive latent switching variables, with the aim of achieving improved robustness and predictive accuracy.

While modeling user-visiting behaviors, ST-STAGE takes into account users’ personal interests and dynamics of user behaviors caused by spatial-temporal drift. ST-STAGE learns a user’s preference as a topic-based vector by mining both the co-occurrence patterns of spatial items and their associated content information (e.g., comments and categories). One advantage of exploiting the content information of spatial items is to address the locality problem in out-of-town scenarios, where the content information associated with spatial items serves as the medium for transferring preference of a user mined from his/her hometown to the unfamiliar regions.

To adapt to temporal dynamics of user behaviors, ST-STAGE recognizes two roles of each individual in a specific region: local or tourist. Given a specific location and a specific time, ST-STAGE mines the visiting records from local users to learn temporal preferences and visiting records from tourists to learn temporal preferences. Users with the same role at location are more likely to share similar preferences and behavior patterns at a specific time. Thus, when we recommend spatial items to a target user $u$ at location $l$ and time $t$, we consider not only $u$s personal interests but also the temporal preferences of the crowd who have the same role as $u$. In this way, we leverage the crowd’s temporal preferences to overcome the sparsity of individuals’ activity data at out-of-town regions at a specific time. Specifically, given a user $u$, at time $t$ and his/her current location $l$, we first find a group of users sharing the same role with $u$ when they visited and then produce the time-aware recommendation based on their temporal preferences and the user’s personal interests.
