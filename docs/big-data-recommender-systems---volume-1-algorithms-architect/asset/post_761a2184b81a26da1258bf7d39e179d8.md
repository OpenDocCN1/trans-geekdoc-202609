In the MRF model considered, a vertex represents a preference $r_{ui}$ of user u on item i and each edge captures a relation between two preferences by the same user, similarly to the previously presented MRF-based models. This standard MRF model captures the local structure between item-item correlations. The ORF model captures item-item correlations with the point-wise ordinal distribution: $P(r_{ui}) = \Psi_u(r_{ui})Q(r_{ui}, i)$, where $\Psi_u(\cdot)$ is the potential function capturing the interaction among items $r$, and $r_u$ is the set of preferences from user u. The potential function can be decomposed into pairwise potentials: $\Psi_u(r_{ui}) = \exp(\sum_{r_{ij}\in r_u} w_{ij}f_{ij}(r_{ij}))$, where $f_{ij}(\cdot)$ is the correlation feature between items $i,j$ and $w_{ij}$ is a weight that controls the influence of each correlation relation to ordinal distribution. The feature function captures the item-item correlation in the form $f(r_{ij}) = g(r_{ij} - \bar{r}_j)$, where $g(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ and $\bar{r}_i, \bar{r}_j$ are the average ratings of $i$ and $j$, respectively. This function ensures that correlated items receive similar ratings by the same user after the grouping of each item. User-user correlations can be determined analogously and the bias with identity features for item-users can be addressed. The decomposition of the potential function in pairwise potentials allows proper scaling in bigdata environments and distributed operation.

The work in [23] provides more details on parameter estimation of the model based on a pseudo-likelihood approach, maximizing the regularized log likelihoods. Following the estimation of model parameters, rating prediction is straightforward, by identifying the rating with the greatest mass in local likelihood $r_{ui} = \argmax_{r_{ui}} P(r_{ui})$. The local likelihood in this case serves as a confidence measure. For predictions of scalar values, the expectation $r_{ui} = \sum_{r_{ui}} r_{ui}P(r_{ui})$ can be easily used. The authors in [23] compare the performance of the ORF approach with $K$-nearest neighbors and OMF approaches, verifying the effectiveness of their approach.

## 4.3.2.4 Markov random fields

As the second and higher order interactions among users and items are concerned the work in [24] is the first to address both via an MRF-based model approach. The preference relation is a kind of relative relation, and it is invariant to irrelevant factors, e.g., the user’s mind. It encodes user preference in the form of pairwise ordering between items, as $\pi_{ij}$ indicating the strength of user’s preference relation for the ordered item pair $(i,j)$. A higher $\pi_{ij}$ indicates a stronger preference for $i$ over $j$ if $\pi_{ij} \in ((2/3), 1]$, $j$ over $i$ if $\pi_{ij} \in [0, 1/3)$, or else $i,j$ are equal preference to u. The user-user preference can be defined as $P_{ui} = ((\sum_{j}\pi_{ij} > (2/3) - \sum_{j}\pi_{ji} < (1/3)))/|\Pi_u|$, where $\Pi_u$ is the set of user’s preference relation related to item i.

The task of preference-based systems is to serve as input and output top-$N$ recommendations. The goal is to estimate the value of each unknown $\tilde{\pi}_{ij} = \Pi_{unknown}$, such that $\hat{\pi}_{ij} = argmax_{\pi_{ij}\in[0,1]}(|\pi_{ij} - \tilde{\pi}_{ij}|)$. In practice, it is easier to estimate $\tilde{\pi}_{ij}$ by the difference between the two-user preferences, $\tilde{\pi}_{ij} = \phi(p_{u} - p_{v})$, where $\phi(\cdot)$ is a function that bounds the value into $[0,1]$ and ensures $\phi(0) = 0.5$, e.g., the inverse-logit function. The optimization problem becomes $(\hat{\pi}_{ij} = argmax_{\pi_{ij}}(|p_{u} - p_{v} - \tilde{\pi}_{ij}|))$.

The preference MRF uniquely combines preferences with local and global structure features, providing out item ranking. In the MRF method, each user $u$ has a graph $G_{u}$, where each vertex represents a preference (rating) $p_{ui}$ of user $u$ on item $i$, and each edge represents an item-item relation. An edge connects two preferences of the same user. The edges correspond to item preference $p_{ui}-p_{uj}$ and the edge between $p_{vi}-p_{vj}$ correspond to the same item-item correlation $\psi_{ij}$ item $i, j$. If $I_{u}$ denotes the joint set of items evaluated by user $u$ and $p_{u} = [p_{ui} | i\in I_{u}]$, then the MRF defines the distribution $P(p_{u}) = (1/Z_{u})\Psi(p_{u})$, where $Z_{u}$ is a normalization constant and $\Psi(p_{u}) = \prod_{i,j\in I_{u}} \psi_{ij}(p_{ui}, p_{uj})$. The potential $\psi_{ij}(p_{ui}, p_{uj}) = \exp[f_{ij}(p_{ui}, p_{uj})]$ captures the correlation between $i$ and $j$, $f_{ij}$ is the correlation function and $w_{ij}$ is a weight factor realizing the importance of each correlation feature (weights are estimated from real data). The unknown preference can be predicted by $\tilde{p}_{ui} = argmax_{p_{ui}\in[-1,1]} P(p_{ui}|p_{u})$,

In this form, the MRF captures the item-item correlations; however, it needs to include ordinal preferences depicting global structure, e.g., user-wise preferences, where $P(p_{u}) = \Psi_{u}(p_{u})\prod_{i} \psi_{ui}(p_{ui})$ captures the interactions among items evaluated by user $u$. The potentials can be further factorized as exponential weights of some of the features mentioned above. Feature design can take place like with ORFs, and the parameter estimation of the approach can be similarly based on the maximization of a regularized sum of log likelihoods, as explained in detail in [24]. The latter contains experimental analysis and comparison with relevant approaches, which can be used by the interested reader for comparison with the rest of graphical models presented in this section.

## 4.3.3 Information diffusion-aware recommendation approaches

Another aspect of socio-awareness that be exploited for improving the performance of RSs is information diffusion, or features of this process as it evolves over an SN of users. In general, one can observe growing interest by the research and industrial communities in integrating the sharing mechanism of information diffusion processes in recommender techniques for SN [27,28]. The main reason for this is twofold: information propagates through social connections and reaches targets. With respect to the latter, the models presented in this subsection attempt to tackle key problems of previous RSs, e.g., the long-tail effect (explained in more detail in the following), information overload and increase system efficiency in bigdata environments by combining the similarity-based predictions of traditional methods with the social-based predictions for the information propagation mechanisms.

Initially, provide some necessary fundamental concepts regarding information diffusion in SNs. Formally, information diffusion is defined as the process by which information is communicated through channels among the users of an SN [29]. The main factors influencing information diffusion are the network structure (density, centrality, clustering, connectivity) and the strength of influence (frequency of interactions, strength of influence) [27,28]. A significant aspect of SNA refers to the modeling of information diffusion, which can be coarsely classified as explanatory or predictive [30]. The goal of explanatory models is to infer the network over which information propagates, given the times users learn a piece of information. In contrast, in predictive models, the main concept is to predict the outcome of a specific diffusion process based on temporal or spatial characteristics [30]. Widely used graph-based models of information diffusion are the sender-oriented independent probabilistic model where node u at time t becomes active neighbor v at time t+1, with a probability p_uv. The process continues until no more activations occur. The receiver-oriented linear threshold model is the most popular of the threshold models used in studying diffusion. In networks, a threshold value or a set of old values are used to distinguish ranges of values where the behavior predicted by the model varies significantly [29]. Each edge uv of the network is affiliated with a weight w_uv, and each node v has a threshold t_v. In the case of RSs, both explanatory and predictive approaches are employed in order to strengthen their performance.

We segregate recently proposed approaches on information diffusion-aware RS into three categories according to the incorporation of the diffusion mechanism in recommender systems. Firstly, a noteworthy issue comes as a consequence of the power-law distribution of product sales in online commerce, as Amazon and Netflix [31], the long-tail problem: (hits)/(blockbusters), the common products (niche products) exceed in the aggregate market share of the former [31,32]. Therefore, it is essential for RS to provide suggestions of long-tail products. This serves not only retailers, who experience greater profit from otherwise unpopular items, but also individuals, who have access to more diverse products, which in turn increases their engagement to the online platforms. Until now, due to data sparsity, RS estimating users' influence (influence users of RSs) are modeled in the literature with temporal criteria as initial/early adopters [33] but can also be considered as those who exhibit high similarity to a great portion of users, having at the same high out-degree (in the SN) and influence propagates in the network may address the long-tail problem in RS, as follows: recommending long-tail items to the most influential users of the network leads to the wider adoption of the former through the diffusion mechanism. In [33], a collaborative innovation diffusion-aware recommendation mechanism is proposed to improve novel knowledge sharing (innovation diffusion) between the users of a corporate portal, where 80% of the total traffic comes from 2% of unique pages.
