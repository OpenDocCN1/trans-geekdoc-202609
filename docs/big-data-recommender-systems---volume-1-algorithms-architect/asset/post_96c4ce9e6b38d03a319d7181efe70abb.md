Such environments allow access to RSs at anytime and anywhere (ubiquity), while at the same time, measurements from built-in sensor devices are able to describe user's dynamically changing situation. Sensors are acting as contextual providing information referring to user's geolocation, motion and emotional conditions. For example, CARS are redefining the way shopping is performed through the support to individual customers in order to make more informed decisions about where to eat, shop, relax, etc. Since such sensors will be increasing dramatically in number, the anticipated environments where these approaches will need to operate are of big data systems.

There are various research efforts where recommendation algorithms utilize contextual data in support of personalized output. Research efforts presented in [42,43] are early attempts of CARS, to leverage tensor factorization techniques to enumerate latent features for users-context, in spaces that include more than the traditional two-dimensional-items model. In a similar manner, in systems presented in [44,45] user/device "location" is utilized as one of the most obvious cases for improving CARS recommendations. In work presented in [4,46], the utilization of sensors on smart phones allow the exploitation of contextual data (current user's location, time, weather conditions) in support of user behavior classification and the next activity recommendation. In [47], a comprehensive survey and analysis of the state-of-the-art on time-aware RSs (TARS) is presented. These approaches are exploiting temporal context and time in user modeling and recommendation strategies in order to improve overall recommendations performance. The review mainly focuses on reported results and conclusions about how to incorporate and exploit time information within the recommendation processes and provides a comprehensive classification of evaluation protocols for TARS. The authors in [48] present a survey of CARSs based on computational intelligence (CI) techniques. The approaches presented are CI techniques that not only improve recommendations accuracy also tackle issues such as sparsity, cold-start and scalability. The authors introduce a taxonomy of CI techniques used in CARS where the prevailing approaches are fuzzy sets, artificial neural networks, evolutionary computing, and artificial immune systems. In the CARS survey presented in [49], the authors are comparing the various approaches based on prefiltering, post-filtering and contextual modeling classification. The presented review aims to identify which method outperforms the others and under which circumstances, in an attempt to provide practical suggestions on how to select a good approach in order to improve the performance of a CARS.

As it indicated in [50], some of the main challenges that most of the modern CARS are that they are not generic and flexible enough to be utilized across different application domains. CARS are not able to be easily adapted to the specifics of each domain which results in a situation of several different frameworks following context modeling and management approaches that are hard to be integrated. In addition, a common weakness is anticipated in CARS [50] is that incorporated contexts are not enough for tailoring personalized services as users only few context data are utilized. In addition, existing frameworks and research efforts that are available in literature lack of real life evaluation as most of them follow an offline evaluation strategy and/or user studies. Although these evaluation approaches are effective during the design and development of a system, the expected results are often contradicting CARS is applied in real life application domains [51].

### 4.3.2 Context-aware recommendation systems in IoT ecosystem

In IoT ecosystems, modern embedded systems can exploit the advantages offered by context-aware and profile-driven recommendations. In fact, using data from IoT sensors as a context-aware computation and CARS are areas of increased research. It should be noted that the IoT architecture creates all the properties of a bigdata environment, name, volume, velocity and variety called the 4 Vs. Thus, data management and knowledge extraction requirements imposed by bigdata environments should be considered when designing CARS that will be utilized in IoT ecosystems. Most RS follow a request-response approach in which the recommendations are provided to the user upon their request. However, authors in [52] introduce a proactive RSS that are pushed to the user when the current situation seems appropriate, without explicit user request. The authors present a framework of CARS that recommends different types of items proactively under the IoT paradigm. A major part of the service is the context-aware management system which utilized a neural network that realizes the reasoning of the context to determine whether to recommend a response or not what type of items to recommend. The authors in [53] present a novel CARS which is based on a multi-agent system (MAS) solution for generating personalized recommendations on mobile devices with the use of contextual data acquired from an IoT environment. A server-side MAS architecture for bigdata processing is used in order to anticipate the data processing required for the personalization model preparation. Context-aware recommendations for very sparse data, based on different sensors and IoT information, are delivered with the usage of a factorization technique entitled CARS. In [54], the authors propose a mechanism for providing recommendations combining information from IoT ecosystems as well as Web Services and applications. The proposed algorithm considers qualitative attributes and semantic information of the venues (e.g., price and atmosphere), the profile and habits of the users for whom the recommendation is generated and the opinions of the users’ influencers. CARS that attempt to leverage on IoT frameworks have to face various challenges imposed by the existing weaknesses of the current IoT landscape. Given that IoT technology companies in many different IoT devices that cannot be easily integrated. Beside the data and platform interoperability challenge in IoT ecosystems, there is a lack of standards for authentication and authorization of IoT edge devices something that is particularly important when services are handling information about user’s behavior and personal preferences.

### 4.3.4 Context-aware recommendation systems and social networks

User text and SN data have recently attracted much attention as it has been proven to be a valuable combination of information for improving accuracy of RSs. However, understanding user text and emerging community structures through knowledge extraction approaches is often a complicated task as relevant collections of raw data are of unprecedented growth, noise, uncertainties and complexities. As it is already stated in Section 4.3.4.1, CF approaches aim to level similarities over the behavior of users who share common characteristics. One of the first approaches that enhanced CF RSs with context information is presented in [55]. The system was able to predict a user's preference in various contexts based on past experiences of other like-minded users who performed various actions in similar context. The system was able to automatically evaluate the influence of context on an activity, while adaptation of Pearson's correlation coefficient used to measure the correlation between two different contexts with respect to their user ratings.

The authors in [56] propose a CARS that operates processed SN information. Context is handled through the application of random decision trees, in order to partition the original user-item rating matrix and group ratings with similarities. Predictions of a user's missing preference for an item are measured through matrix factorization. SN information is incorporated through an additional social regularization term that allows to constrain difference between a user and his/her friend to take into higher consideration (with an increased weight) friends with similar tastes. Similarity between the user and his/her friends is measured by past rating patterns, i.e., characteristics of the items that both users have commonly rated. The PCC [57] is utilized for similarity evaluation as it has been proven to be more accurate than similarity-aware approaches. Similarity of $u_i$ and his/her $u_j$ is calculated as

$S(j,f) = \frac{\sum_{v \in V(i) \cap V(j)} (R_{i,v}-\hat{R}_v)(R_{j,v}-\hat{R}_v)}{\sqrt{\sum_{v \in V(i) \cap V(j)} (R_{i,v}-\hat{R}_v)^2} \sqrt{\sum_{v \in V(i) \cap V(j)} (R_{j,v}-\hat{R}_v)^2}} (4.2)$

where $v \in V(i) \cap V(j)$ is the set of items that $u_i$ and $u_j$ have commonly rated, and $R_i, R_j$ are the average ratings of $u_i$ and $u_j$, respectively. Similarity calculation is enhanced with contextual information through a context-aware version of PCC:

$S_c(j,f) = \frac{\sum_{v \in V(i) \cap V(j)} w_v(R_{i,v}-\hat{R}_v)(R_{j,v}-\hat{R}_v)}{\sqrt{\sum_{v \in V(i) \cap V(j)} w_v(R_{i,v}-\hat{R}_v)^2} \sqrt{\sum_{v \in V(i) \cap V(j)} w_v(R_{j,v}-\hat{R}_v)^2}}} (4.3)$

and the weight $w_v$, of each item $V_v$, is calculated based on

$w_v = \frac{N(pcc_v)}{\sum_{v' \in V(i) \cap V(j)} N(pcc_{v'})} (4.4)$

where the function $N(-)$ normalizes the given value to the range [0,1], and $pcc$, represents the PCC between $u_i$'s rating $R_v$ and $u_j$'s rating $R_v$ on the same item $V_v$.
