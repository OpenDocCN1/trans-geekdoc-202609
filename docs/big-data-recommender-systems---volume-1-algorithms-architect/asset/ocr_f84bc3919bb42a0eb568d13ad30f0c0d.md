# Efficient and socio-recommendation approaches

A RF X is called Gibbs RF if it satisfies $\mathbb{P}(X = x) = (1/Z)e^{-U(x)/T}$, where $Z := \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} e^{-U(x)/T}$ is the partition function and $T$ is called the temperature of the system. $U(x)$ is called the potential function and represents an "energy" metric of configuration x. However, a useful class of potential functions is one in which $U(x)$ is decomposed into a sum of cliques (maximally connected subgraph), $U(x) = \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \Phi_c(x)$, where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of cliques of nodes formed in the topology. Each clique potential depends only on the states of the cliques formed by the sites of the MRF. The clique-based representation of $U(x)$ is very useful in various applications of MRFs, e.g., in the modeling of malware propagation in computer/communication networks through direct neighbor interactions [25], process assembling considerably the spreading of rumors and marketing trends in an SN.

# 4.3.2.1 Pairwise recommendation MRFs

One of the approaches employing the concept of MRF for recommendations is the one presented in [21], where belief propagation (BP) approach is employed for computing the marginal probability distributions of a pairwise-MRF (PMRF) modeling the similarity associations between various items. Assuming $M$ users and $N$ items, the objective is to predict the numerical value (typically in the discrete range of 1-5) of the collection of variables $S_{ij} = \{r_{ij} : i \in L_j\}$, where $r_{ij}$ is the recommendation score of user $j$ for item $i$, $i$ is the set of items and $L_j$ denotes the set of rated items by user $z$. The employed PMRF consists of a hidden (item rated to be predicted) and an observed layer of sites (Figure 4.4). Similarity sites correspond to known local evidence, e.g., average rating from other users, using similarity relations for the user for whose rating is estimated: $y_i = \hat{y}_i + (\sum_{j \in \mathcal{K}_i} S_{zi}(r_{zi} - \hat{y}_j))/\sum_{j \in \mathcal{K}_i} S_{zi}$, where $\hat{y}_i$ denotes a set of $K_i$ users most similar to $z$ among users who rated item $i$, Figure 4.4. Similarity can be computed with any of the similarity mentioned before. Two items are connected if their similarity is above a predefined threshold, or if at least one is among the $K$ most similar items of the other, or both. $K$ is a fixed parameter.
