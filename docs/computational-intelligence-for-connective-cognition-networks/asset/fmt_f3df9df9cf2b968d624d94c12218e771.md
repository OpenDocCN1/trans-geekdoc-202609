
### 8.4.6 Sentiment-based feature

Sentiment analysis is found to be useful in identifying suspicious accounts from online social media by extracting the element of feelings, attitudes, and opinions [[45](#ch18.xhtml#r248)]. It is also found useful in fake news detection when combined with other extracted features [[26](#ch18.xhtml#r229)]. This happens because fake news often is biased toward one type of emotion. When people are experiencing positive or neutral emotions, they are more inclined to believe a claim to be “true,” yet when they are experiencing negative emotions, they may become more skeptical [[46](#ch18.xhtml#r249)].

Sentiment polarity is one of the important elements in sentiment analysis. It helps to determine how the stated sentiment is oriented. For example, it can help to identify if the text is expressed in negative, neutral sentiment, or positive from a statement. In this project, we extracted the polarity scores as one of the features using the sentiment analyzer from the natural language toolkit (`NLTK`) package [[47](#ch18.xhtml#r250)]. There are four different scores in this package which are positive, negative, neutral, and compound score. The polarity score of each score can be ranged from −1 to +1 and the add-up of positive, negative, and neutral scores will equal 1. The compound score is total of positive, negative, and neutral scores with the range of most extreme negative (−1) to most extreme positive (+1). The more positive the text is, the closer the compound score is to +1. We extract 8 sentiment-based features. [Table 8.5](#ch18.xhtml#t17) shows the details of the sentiment-based features.

**Table 8.5** Details of the sentiment-based features extracted[⏎](#ch18.xhtml#t17b)

| Feature’s Name | Data type | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `neg content` | N | Count of negative sentiment in content. |
| `neg title` | N | Count of negative sentiment in title. |
| `pos content` | N | Count of positive sentiment in content. |
| `pos title` | N | Count of positive sentiment in title. |
| `neu content` | N | Count of neutral sentiment in content. |
| `neu title` | N | Count of neutral sentiment in title. |
| `compound content` | N | The compound score of sentiment in content. |
| `compound title` | N | The compound score of sentiment in title. |

### 8.4.7 Named entity recognition (NER)-based features

NER is a procedure used to identify and classify the designated entities that are presented in a document. According to Shelar et al. [[48](#ch18.xhtml#r251)], the NER tool usually scans through the whole text document and categorizes the entity such as place, organizations, date, major people, etc. It allows for easy content discovery by categorizing the articles in defined hierarchies with the known relevant tags for each text document. With the help of the NER approach, we can identify the relationship between the entities and the truth of news or articles. For example, when an election happens, fake news tends to have major people’s names as headlines to attract the public. In this work, we are using the trained model “en_core_web_sm” (an English pipeline that includes vocabulary, syntax, and entities with training on written web text documents such as comments, news, and blogs) from the `spaCy` library. [Table 8.6](#ch18.xhtml#t18) shows the example of entities of the NER approach in the trained model. A study from [[3](#ch18.xhtml#r206)] has shown that most of the fake news is related to political news as the false information or political rumors on Twitter spiked during the 2012 and 2016 US presidential elections. This has motivated us to focus on the entities that are more relevant to political news such as organization (`ORG`), the major person (`PERSON`), geopolitical (`GPE`), and money (`MONEY`) while developing the fake news detection model. We extract eight NER features. [Table 8.7](#ch18.xhtml#t19) shows the details of all the NER-based features extracted.

### 8.4.8 Textual features

We have also used textual features in the `FNDetector` model. We use lemmatized title (with POS) and lemmatized content (with POS) (refer to [Table 8.8](#ch18.xhtml#t20)). However, because textual features cannot be directly fed into the classifiers, we used Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency (`TF-IDF`) (`RF` classifier) and tokenizer (`LSTM` classifier). Following are more details about `TF-IDF` and Tokenizer.

**TF-IDF**: According to Qaiser and Ali [[49](#ch18.xhtml#r252)], the term frequency (`TF`) is referring the occurrence of a particular term in a document. To calculate the inverse document frequency of a particular term, we can use the following formula:

```
TF = Occurrence of Words / Count of words of document
```
(8.1)

For example: if a 2000 words document contains the word “library” 5 times. The `TF` of the word “library” will be 5/2000, which is 0.0025. All the terms play an important role in a document. Inverse document frequency (`IDF`) aims to assign a weight to a particular term. The `IDF` algorithm assigns a more weightage to an infrequent term and a lower weightage to a frequent term in the document. To calculate the `IDF` of a particular term, we can use the following formula:

```
Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) = log_e(Total number of document / Number of documents with term)
```
(8.2)
