
Quantum states can possibly exhibit the interference phenomena in a similar way as the waves interfere, which leads to maxima and minima ([Kipnis 2012](#ch12.xhtml#r052)). Quantum interference can lead to the amplification of certain outcomes while cancelling the other analogous to constructive and destructive interference in wave theory. The principle of quantum interference is useful in quantum algorithms to enhance the probability of correct solutions as compared to the probability of incorrect solutions, which get diminished. This property can enhance the computational power and efficiency over classical technology.

### 2.2.5 Measurement

The last stage in quantum computation is measurement, thus ultimately collapsing the qubit from a superposition state into one of the definite states, 0 or 1. The process of measurement ultimately leads to a classical output from a quantum system by destroying the superposition state of qubits to a definite state. Correct outcome is typically guaranteed by the quantum algorithms but stochastic nature of the process can even lead to the non-sampling of the correct outcome. Thus, some classical technique is required after data processing to obtain the final correct output from raw results with quantum computer.

### 2.2.6 Quantum gates and circuits

The quantum versions of classical gates used to carry out the quantum computation are called quantum gates. Quantum gates operate on superpositions of states unlike classical gates and thus enabling quantum parallelism. Quantum gates operate on qubits through unitary operations to manipulate the state of qubits while preserving quantum properties like superposition, entanglement etc. Quantum gates are reversible and are able to perform reverse calculations and complex computations. These gates can be single-qubit as well as multi-qubits. Examples include single-qubit gates such as `Pauli-X` (analogous to the classical NOT gate), `Pauli-Y`, `Pauli-Z`, `Hadamard (H)` and `Phase (S)` gates; two-qubit gates, such as the `Controlled-NOT (CNOT)` gate, which flips the state of a qubit, thus enabling operations that are impossible with classical gates alone. A sequence of quantum gates can perform complex algorithms and thus forms a quantum circuit.

### 2.2.7 Quantum algorithms

Quantum circuits form the foundation for implementing quantum algorithms, which are then applied to one or more qubits to solve specific problems. Some of the examples of quantum algorithms are `Shor’s algorithm` for factorization of integers, `Grover’s algorithm` for searching databases, etc. Both these algorithms provide faster speedups in comparison to classical algorithms ([Shor 1999](#ch12.xhtml#r070); [Grover 1996](#ch12.xhtml#r045)).

## 2.3 Quantum-based networks paradigms: the next frontier in quantum communication

Although universal quantum computers or simulators are still in development, efforts are being made by researchers in linking these quantum computers to build quantum networks. Quantum based network paradigms are an emerging field at the intersection of quantum physics and information technology to bring a transformative impact in computation, communication and data transmission with unparalleled security and computational power. Each paradigm has a unique role, mechanism, applications, challenges and advantages. The successful implementation of these paradigms will play a crucial role in the future of communication networks and information processing at the global level. Quantum networks can be better understood by discussing its elements as follows:

*   Quantum networks are facilitated with quantum processors with minimum one qubit. A small quantum computer is known as a quantum processor which can perform quantum logic gates on qubits. The application is run on these quantum processors with one qubit known as end nodes. It might be possible to have quantum processors with more than one qubit as per the requirement of the application.
*   Communication lines are required for the transfer of qubits and telecom wires can be utilized for this purpose.
*   In quantum networks, qubits are delivered to the required processors with the help of optical switches in order to optimizing the communication process.
*   Fourth, long distance transmission of qubits is possible through quantum repeaters so as to avoid losses (Gu et al. 2024).

Key aspects and technologies in this domain include:

### 2.3.1 Quantum key distribution

QKD network is a typical example of a quantum network which does not require entangling the quantum nodes. Unlike classical cryptographic methods, QKD is based on information theoretical security utilizing fundamental laws of quantum mechanics and thus making it secure against any future advancement in computational power ([Xu et al. 2020](#ch12.xhtml#r076); [Zhang et al. 2018](#ch12.xhtml#r078)). In a QKD network, quantum channels (optical fibres or free space links) are used for the distribution of quantum keys between two parties. These keys are used to encrypt and decrypt messages transmitted through the communication channel. Any attempt at eavesdropping is detectable due to the disturbance of quantum states in the act of quantum measurement. [Bennett and Brassard (1984](#ch12.xhtml#r031)) proposed `BB84 protocol` which uses four polarization states of photons for encoding bits. Later on, more and more protocols were proposed and developed like `E91`, etc ([Zhang et al. 2024](#ch12.xhtml#r079)).

### 2.3.2 Quantum teleportation

It is a process in which the quantum state of a particle is allowed to be transmitted directly from one location to another, without physically transporting the particle itself with the help of classical communication ([Olmschenk et al. 2009](#ch12.xhtml#r064)). This quantum network uses entangled quantum nodes in which the qubits can be processed and transmitted directly. This paradigm creates a robust and versatile network offering incomparable security and computational power ([Devulapalli et al. 2024](#ch12.xhtml#r037)).

### 2.3.3 Cloud-based quantum computing

Cloud-based quantum computing provides access to quantum emulators and processors over the internet ([Soeparno and Perbangsa 2021](#ch12.xhtml#r071)). As a result, users can perform quantum computations instead of using local quantum hardware. This paradigm of quantum networking has broadened the access of quantum computing resources and thus speed up more research and advancements in this field ([Golec et al. 2024](#ch12.xhtml#r044)).

### 2.3.4 Entanglement-enabled communication

Entanglement-enabled communication makes use of qubits in entangled state to establish communication links with enhanced security. Any attempt at interference or eavesdropping is detectable as ensured by this network paradigm which in turn provides a robust foundation for secure data transmission ([Nötzel 2020](#ch12.xhtml#r063)).
