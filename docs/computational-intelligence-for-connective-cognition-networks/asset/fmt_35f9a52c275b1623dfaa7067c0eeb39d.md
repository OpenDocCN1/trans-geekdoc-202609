
# 9.3 Visual Deepfake detection methods

## 9.3.1 Stages used in Deepfake video detection studies

[Figure 9.5](#ch19.xhtml#f70) shows the overall steps used in the deepfake video detection studies. These steps are summarized below:

![Figure 9.5 Steps used in Deepfake detection.](html/images/9781032942346_C009_005.jpg)

**Figure 9.5** Steps used in Deepfake detection.

*   The Initial phase consists of the creation of a dataset by either collecting and organizing deepfake videos or using available datasets.
*   From the dataset, videos are preprocessed by extracting the video frames and cropping and resizing as needed is performed on the frames to prepare for improved feature selection.
*   Feature extraction is performed and useful features are selected to be used for detection.
*   The model to be used for detection is then either a newly created or a suitable available model.
*   The selected model is then trained on the dataset to learn key features for distinguishing real and fake videos.
*   Finally, model evaluation is done using various performance metrics to assess the performance of the model.

## 9.3.2 Classification of detection methods

*   **Biological/Physiological signal based methods**: use biological/physiological signals such as abnormalities in eye blinking, heart rate, skin color, etc., for detection of deepfakes.
*   **Statistical feature-based methods**: employ statistical measures such as `PRNU`, cross-correlation scores, etc., for forgery identification.
*   **Machine learning based methods**: work by defining features and the feature vector is fed to machine learning algorithms such as support vector machine, logistic regression, random forest, etc., to train the classifier for detection of deepfakes.
*   **Frequency domain feature-based methods**: are based on the identification of forgery clues in the frequency domain and help in the discovery of latent features which provide crucial clues for deepfake detection.
*   **Temporal domain feature-based methods**: use temporal inconsistencies in video frames for deepfake detection.
*   **Deep learning-based methods**: are widely used deepfake detection methods as they can extract complex latent patterns and learn features to provide high detection performance using deep learning models such as `CNN`, `RNN`, etc.
*   **Transformer-based methods**: use transformers such as `Vision Transformer (ViT)` [[45](#ch19.xhtml#r317)] for improved generalized deepfake detection by leveraging the self-attention mechanism to capture complex spatial and temporal dependencies within videos.

## 9.3.3 Early Deepfake detection methods

Early deepfake detection methods employed hand-crafted artefacts or `CNN`-based feature extraction methods to extract the visual features, which are different for manipulated and real images, and then train classifiers on the visual features to detect deepfake images.

### Biological signal based methods

Early hand-crafted artefact methods included physiological or biological signals-based deepfake detection methods that use physiological/biological signs such as eye blinking or heart rate as signs for classifying videos as either fake or real.

`Li et al.` [[46](#ch19.xhtml#r318)] used eye blinking abnormalities in fake videos to detect deepfake by using `Long term Recurrent CNN (LRCN)` model to capture eye states. Each frame is cropped to a rectangular shape around the eye area after face alignment and passed to the `LRCN` model to learn discriminative temporal features, predict the eye states, and calculate blink frequency. The model shows good performance in detecting deepfake videos on the evaluation of self-made datasets.

`Tackhyun et al.` [[47](#ch19.xhtml#r319)] proposed an integrity verification algorithm that uses an eye-blinking pattern to classify a video as fake or real. The authors established an initial `DB` containing natural values of various factors that affect the eye blinking pattern such as age, gender, time of the day, and the activity of the subject in the video and corresponding information like blink count, period, elapsed time, etc. The proposed algorithm is used to compare the blinking information from the database with that extracted from the video to classify the video as fake or real. The method is evaluated on a self-made dataset of deepfake videos and achieves an accuracy of little over 87%.

`FakeCatcher` [[48](#ch19.xhtml#r320)], proposed by `Aybars et al.`, is a biological signals-based deepfake detection model for fake portrait videos and uses color-based remote photoplethysmography (`rPPG` [[49](#ch19.xhtml#r321)]) to extract heart rate signals by observing subtle changes in the skin color. The `PPG` is a technique that can detect changes in skin color resulting from blood circulation. The model is evaluated using datasets `FF++`, `UADFV`, `FF`, and `Celeb-DF` in addition to their own dataset and achieving accuracies in the range 91%–96% for different datasets.

Similarly, `Hernandez-Ortega et al.` [[50](#ch19.xhtml#r322)] is also a biological signals-based model that uses a `convolutional attention network (CAN)` trained for heart rate estimation using `rPPG` to extract spatial and temporal information from video frames and analyze both the extracted information to detect fake videos. This model is evaluated on `Celeb-DF` and `DFDC preview` datasets and demonstrated `AUC` performance of 99.9% and 98.2% on the datasets, respectively.

However, biological/physiological signals-based methods are no longer efficient as the latest deepfake generation algorithms are able to reconstruct such signals with more efficiency and with good enough quality that such methods cannot efficiently detect abnormalities associated with biological or physiological signals.

### Early CNN-based methods

Early learning-based approaches used `CNN`-based methods such as [[51](#ch19.xhtml#r323)], which is based on the computation of co-occurrence matrix on the `RGB` channels of an image and used the computed vector as input to deep `CNN` model to detect deepfakes.

## 9.3.4 Statistical feature-based methods

Alongside these methods, statistics-based deepfake fingerprint identification methods mentioned in [[52](#ch19.xhtml#r324)] and [[53](#ch19.xhtml#r325)] used photo response nonuniformity (`PRNU`), noise pattern resulting by light-sensitive sensors of digital cameras for detection of deepfakes.

`Luca et al.` [[54](#ch19.xhtml#r326)] proposed deepfake detection approach that is based on an expectation and maximization algorithm to create a mathematical model to represent the local correlations between each pixel and its neighborhood and capture this relationship in the form of a vector called `convolutional trace (CT)` that characterizes the traces left by `GAN` process used for creating deepfake images. The method extracts `CT` and uses it as a feature vector as input to naïve classifier to discriminate between real and fake images. Experimentations were conducted on images generated by 10 different `GANs` which involved with and without face manipulation and using different classifiers such as `SVM`, `LDA`, `K-NN`, etc. The `Random forest` classifier provided the best classification result with an overall accuracy of over 98%, and when tested on deepfakes generated by `FACEAPP`, a test accuracy of 93% was achieved. However, accuracy decreases to as low as 56.96% on the application of different postprocessing techniques such as blurring, rotation, compression, etc.
