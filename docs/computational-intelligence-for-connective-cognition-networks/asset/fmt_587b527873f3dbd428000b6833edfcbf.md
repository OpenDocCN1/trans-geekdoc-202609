
Another research work introduces an initiative to incorporate generative AI by applying large language model into online higher education classes at large public university in the USA. The implemented LLM extracts the rich metadata from lecture videos, segment lecture into coherent units, extract key definitions, examples, and procedural knowledge of taught topics. Even, questions to test student understanding of the taught topic using LLM are also generated. This research paper outlines several research directions to explore the full potential of LLMs for online education [[13](#ch20.xhtml#r354)]. For quiz generation, EvalQuiz was introduced by Meißner et al. to automatically generate self-assessment quizzes in software engineering education using LLMs [[1](#ch20.xhtml#r342)]. Traditional quiz creation is time-consuming process that require expertise and manual review of questions. EvalQuiz aims to alleviate this burden by leveraging LLMs, specifically GPT-4, to automate quiz generation based on lecture materials. The process involves several stages: topic modeling and keyword extraction, prompt engineering and question generation, assembling and validation of quizzes, and evaluation. Lecture material is processed to extract relevant keywords and generate prompts for the LLM. The generated quizzes are then assembled and validated, ensuring compliance with specified formats. Finally, the quality of the quizzes is evaluated, considering factors like relevance, correctness, and originality [[1](#ch20.xhtml#r342)]. A personalized lecture summarization tool, HumSum, is developed by Kolagar et al. [[14](#ch20.xhtml#r355)] for humanities students. HumSum collects user preferences through a scenario-driven survey to guide the implementation of the tool. Utilizing Streamlit for the user interface and map reduce function OF Langchain with GPT-4 model of OpenAI enabled effective summarization without collecting personal user data [[14](#ch20.xhtml#r355)]. A survey published in a conference discusses the use of large language models specifically in the education domain [[15](#ch20.xhtml#r356)]. It delves into the various stakeholders involved in education, including teachers, students, parents, educational institutions, government, and society, highlighting their roles and interactions within the educational ecosystem. Several applications of LLM in education domain are briefed in the manuscript and future implications of LLM in education are discussed to address the challenges in building a landscape where smart education becomes more personalized, diverse, and digitally enhanced [[15](#ch20.xhtml#r356)]. Leiker and his group conducted experiments for the creation of course using an LLM under the guidance of prompt engineering and human oversight [[11](#ch20.xhtml#r352)]. They meticulously examine both the advantages and hurdles associated with employing LLMs in the asynchronous creation of courses for adult education, training, and upskilling. Despite the immense potential of LLMs in accessing and synthesizing extensive human knowledge, reservations persist regarding their accuracy and reliability. Consequently, the study advocates for a balanced approach, suggesting the integration of human oversight to ensure the quality and clarity of the generated output [[11](#ch20.xhtml#r352)]. Another research direction in this same domain is towards conversational tutoring system such as Ruffle&Riley [[16](#ch20.xhtml#r357)]. This system automates scripting and orchestration for learning-by-teaching interactions and LLM agents engage users in free-form conversations. A user study comparing Ruffle&Riley to simpler QA chatbots and reading activities have shown no significant difference in post-test scores, but users rated Ruffle&Riley higher in understanding, remembering, helpfulness, and coherence [[16](#ch20.xhtml#r357)]. Jauhiainen and Guerra research focuses on utilization of generative AI in primary school education. This theoretical study is done on 110 pupils of 8–14 years of age and studying in classes 4–6 in two different schools as participants in test lectures [[12](#ch20.xhtml#r353)]. The results of the study demonstrated the feasibility of utilizing ChatGPT-3.5 to personalize learning materials to meet the knowledge and learning skills of pupils with varying levels of knowledge. Importantly, a clear majority of pupils reported enjoying the generative AI-modified material, indicating the potential for generative AI to support motivated learning and skills development in school education [[12](#ch20.xhtml#r353)].

The studied research papers are usually used for theoretical subjects whereas lecture preparation material generation for professors using large language model for programming subject using a template-based prompting is still an unsolved and less explored issue.

## 10.3 Lecture preparation and generation procedure

The methodology involves using template-based prompt techniques [[17](#ch20.xhtml#r358)] for generation of lecture preparation material using ChatGPT-3.5 for two important computer science fundamental subjects – Data Structure & Algorithm (DSA) and Object-oriented programming System (OOPS). The seven types of queries for which lecture preparation material could be generated and used by a professor were explored. Then, these queries were given as an input to ChatGPT-3.5 along with a fixed template respective to the type of query defining the structure of the material generated. Further from this pool of queries, 50 queries were extracted, and lecture preparation materials were generated for them using zero-shot prompting [[18](#ch20.xhtml#r359)] and few-shot prompting [[19](#ch20.xhtml#r360)] on ChatGPT-3.5. The materials generated using each type of prompt technique, for their respective query, were scored and ranked based on the structure and content quality using Gemini Pro and rule-based evaluation, respectively, followed by human evaluation. The framework of the proposed methodology has been visualized in [Figure 10.1](#ch20.xhtml#f71).

The considered five types of queries are as follows:

*   **Single Topic Queries (ST):** Collection of 138 queries to provide lecture preparation material on a single topic such as `Arrays`, `Linked Lists`, `Stack` etc.
*   **Topic Combination Queries (TC):** Collection of 70 queries to give lecture material on combination of topics, for example, explain a topic say A for topic B or explain a topic A for topic B in topic C or discuss topic A in topic B or illustrate topic A to topic B i.e. `give lecture preparation material on implementing stacks using linked list`.
*   **Comparison Queries (CQ):** Collection of 11 queries to arrange lecture material on comparison of various algorithm/techniques/methodology like prime query. For example, `Provide lecture preparation material comparing various sorting techniques like selection sort, bubble sort or insertion sort`.
*   **Programming Question Queries (PQ):** Collection of 48 queries to provide lecture material on a programming problem. For instance, `Share lecture preparation material on the programming problem fractional`.
*   **OOPs and Complexity Queries (OCQ):** Collection of seven queries to provide lecture material on OOPs and Complexity related topics. For example, `Share lecture preparation material on inheritance`.
