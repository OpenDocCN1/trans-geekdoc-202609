
# Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC)

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) has been widely used as a performance measurement for evaluating medical diagnostic systems [[63](#ch18.xhtml#r266)]. According to Park et al. [[64](#ch18.xhtml#r267)], the ROC is represented as a graph that consists of two key components which are sensitivity (or true positive rate) and specificity (or false positive rate), and it can differentiate the signal from the noise. In a ROC graph, the sensitivity will be plotted on the *y*-axis and specificity on the *x*-axis, respectively. The ROC graph can clearly show the relationship between the false positive rate and the true positive rate. The extension of the ROC curve, area under the ROC curve (AUC) has been proved as a useful metric for summarizing the efficacy of the test [[65](#ch18.xhtml#r268)]. Mandrekar [[65](#ch18.xhtml#r268)] also mentioned that the value of AUC can be ranged from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates the test was perfectly inaccurate and 1 indicates that test is perfectly accurate. In general, when the AUC is greater than 0.5, there is a high chance that the classifier can differentiate the positive class value from the negative class value. However, there are some cases where the AUC value is equal to 0.5, and the ROC curve is presented as the diagonal line on the graph. In this case, the classifier will not have the ability to differentiate between the positive and negative classes.

## RQ1: What is the performance of the baseline classifier?

**Motivation:** We worked on building a fake news detection model using different types of features. It is significant to recognize how the fake news detection model performs without using any of the external feature extraction. Hence, in this RQ, we built our baseline classifier using the title and the content of news articles.

**Approach:** We used the textual data, i.e., the lemmatized title and content of the news articles to build the baseline classifiers. We then vectorize the dataset by using `TfidfVectorizer` before training the `RF` classifier model. For the `LSTM` classifier, we tokenize the textual data and insert it into an embedding layer before going to the `LSTM` layer. For this experiment, we use 100 n estimators for `RF` and 10 epochs for `LSTM`.

**Results:** [Figure 8.6](#ch18.xhtml#f50) and [Table 8.11](#ch18.xhtml#t23) show the results of this RQ. We obtained an accuracy value of 60.7% and 78.4% for `RF` and `LSTM` classifiers, respectively, and an F-measure of 79.2% and 41.1% for `RF` and `LSTM` classifiers, respectively (refer to Table 8.10). These results indicate that the baseline classifier gives very low accuracy and F-measure with respect to fake news detection, and hence, there is a need to improve these models. The baseline classifiers perform poorly for fake news detection tasks with `RF` and `LSTM` classifiers giving an accuracy of 60.7% and 78.4%, respectively. The performance of the `LSTM` classifier is much lower than the `RF` classifier both in terms of accuracy and F-measure.

## RQ 2: What is the performance of the proposed model, i.e., FNDetector, when contrasted to the baseline classifier?

**Motivation:** In this RQ, we investigated the performance of the proposed `FNDetector` model. To build this model, we combined different types of features. Additionally, we extracted features from both the title and content of news articles. This RQ will help in providing insights into the effectiveness of the `FNDetector` model.

**Approach:** To answer this RQ, we trained the `FNDetector` model using `RF` and `LSTM` classifiers to build two variants of the `FNDetector` model, i.e., `FNDetector[RF]` and `FNDetector[LSTM]`. We tested the performance of these two models on the test dataset and computed various metrics. We also performed a comparison of these two models with the baseline classifiers.

**Result:** [Table 8.12](#ch18.xhtml#t24) and [Figure 8.7](#ch18.xhtml#f51) show the prediction results of the `FNDetector[RF]` and `FNDetector[LSTM]` models. [Table 8.12](#ch18.xhtml#t24) shows that `FNDetector[RF]` gives an accuracy of 88.2% and an F-measure of 88.5%, whereas `FNDetector[LSTM]` gives an accuracy of 78.4% and an F-measure of 81.3%. [Figures 8.8](#ch18.xhtml#f52) and [8.10](#ch18.xhtml#f54) show the comparison of the performance of the baseline and `FNDetector` classifiers. [Figures 8.9](#ch18.xhtml#f53) and [8.10](#ch18.xhtml#f54) show that both `FNDetector[RF]` and `FNDetector[LSTM]` models outperform the baseline classifiers in both accuracy and F-measure. We compute the improvements and present the results in the form of bar graphs (refer to [Figures 8.10](#ch18.xhtml#f54) and [8.11](#ch18.xhtml#f55)). We notice an improvement of 9.8% (accuracy) and 9.3% (F-measure) using `FNDetector[RF]` model and an improvement of 17.7% (accuracy) and 40.2% (F-measure) using the `FNDetector[LSTM]` model. These results indicate that feature extraction is an important step in fake news model creation. From the result, we can conclude that feature extraction is useful in fake news model building for both random forest and `LSTM` classifiers. We obtained an improvement of 9.8% (`FNDetector[RF]`) and 17.7% (`FNDetector[LSTM]`) in terms of accuracy and an improvement of 9.3% (`FNDetector[RF]`) and 40.2% (`FNDetector[LSTM]`) in F-measure.
