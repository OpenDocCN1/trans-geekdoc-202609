# 2.4. Data Augmentation Strategies

As mentioned above, the performance of models depends on the quantity of data used to train the model. In some machine learning disciplines, it is possible to augment data by modifying training examples. For instance, when analyzing images, one can create a larger dataset by blurring or inserting elements into the data. Unfortunately, this augmentation is not possible with molecules. Every change to a molecule can drastically affect the molecular property in context. Multiple lines of research are aiming to address these limitations by generating additional data or looking for more effective ways of augmenting data. One direction that has already been shown to be highly effective is to suggest target training with data from related tasks (e.g., Netflix-style recommendation systems). Another method of line works is to use encoders of molecular structures to improve learned representations. Such approaches have been shown to be successful in computer vision and natural language processing to demonstrate similar benefits in the context of molecular property prediction.³⁴ However, the crucial dependence on training data means that activating a set of candidates requires a fixed training budget. These learning approaches provide a systematic approach to the exploration and optimization of their properties (encoding). By informing the collection of experimental data with diverse properties, researchers can hopefully strengthen the drug discovery process.

When an RNN is trained to generate molecules, it learns which parts of molecules belong to atoms or groups being connected. # 3. DE NOVO MOLECULAR DESIGN WITH GENERATIVE MODELS

Over the past few years, we have seen the emergence of a new number of approaches to novo molecular design that have been inspired by techniques used in areas such as computer vision and natural language processing. This field has gained significant interest in the machine learning community, lately of natural chemistry, to increase state-of-the-art performance. In instance, from 2017 on some data sets, the absolute gain reached over 20% (measured by the quality of generating molecules with the desired properties).

The first applications of generative models to novo molecular design used the Simplified Input Line Entry System (SMILES) representation of the molecular structure, which was transformed into a latent vector and further optimized to decode the desired property.³¹ The new optimized vector is then decoded into a SMILES string representing the output molecule. The key assumption in this approach is that representation in this space is smooth, in contrast to the discrete nature of molecular space. However, research has revealed that the constructed latent space is far from smooth: molecules with similar properties might not be similar to each other in the latent space. This part of the explanation can be bridged by the IMPACT of SMILES representation as a starting point—similar molecules may have very different SMILES strings. However, there are other contributing factors, such as property cliffs (chemicals with very similar structures but very different properties) and chemical constraints of the synthesis process. Recent work has significantly advanced the state of the art in novo generative models. Moving molecules representations have explored encoding and decoding algorithms that can effectively process molecular graphs. Unlike computer vision and natural language, the field is still far from having a standard architecture, and we expect to see new developments in this area.
