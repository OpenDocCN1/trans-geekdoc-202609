## 2.3. Uncertainty

When a learning model is used to predict the biological or activity properties of a molecule, it is important to understand the uncertainty in the prediction. While it seems intuitive that one can be more certain about predictions on molecules that are similar to those in the training set,¹⁶ the field has yet to agree reliable methods for assessing uncertainty. As the field moves to more complex molecules it becomes more difficult to quantify similarity and evaluate the uncertainty in machine learning models. A 2020 paper used five benchmark datasets to select several methods for quantifying uncertainty in neural network models. These authors explored the ability of the established strategies to estimate the error in prediction models.

- Ensemble methods, where network parameters are varied to create an ensemble of models and the variance as a measure of uncertainty.
- An approach has mean-variance estimation, where the network is trained to predict both the mean and the variance of the predicted for a particular molecule.
- Distance-based methods based on molecular fingerprints or latent space representations like those described in Section 3.2
- Union-based methods, which combine a neural network with a latent space representation to produce both an output and an associated uncertainty.

The authors concluded that none of the methods investigated were capable of consistently estimating uncertainty across the data sets. While there have been a number of published reports for estimating uncertainty,¹⁷ further study is necessary to create reliable methods that can be used in practice.

## 2.2. Data Requirements for Accurate Property Prediction

The accuracy of predictive molecular models depends on accurate data. Given a vast number of parameters, neural models require a sufficient amount of data to achieve an optimal molecular representation and ultimately the prediction set. While traditional molecular-fingerprint-based models can be trained on a few hundred molecules, that amount of data is insufficient for neural models. Thousands of molecules is required to start their training. Unfortunately, in many drug discovery scenarios, obtaining a large sample may not be feasible. A typical pharmaceutical optimization effort, which generates a few thousand, may provide sufficient data to train a neural model.

## 2.4. Data Augmentation Strategies

As mentioned above, the performance of models depends on the quantity of data used to train the model. In some machine learning disciplines, it is possible to augment data by modifying training examples. For instance, when analyzing images, one can create a larger dataset by blurring or inserting elements into the data. Unfortunately, this augmentation is not possible with molecules. Every change to a molecule can drastically affect the molecular property in context. Multiple lines of research are aiming to address these limitations by generating additional data or looking for more effective ways of augmenting data. One direction that has already been shown to be highly effective is to suggest target training with data from related tasks (e.g., Netflix-style recommendation systems). Another method of line works is to use encoders of molecular structures to improve learned representations. Such approaches have been shown to be successful in computer vision and natural language processing to demonstrate similar benefits in the context of molecular property prediction.³⁴ However, the crucial dependence on training data means that activating a set of candidates requires a fixed training budget. These learning approaches provide a systematic approach to the exploration and optimization of their properties (encoding). By informing the collection of experimental data with diverse properties, researchers can hopefully strengthen the drug discovery process.

## DEVO MOLECULAR DESIGN WITH GENERATIVE MODELS

Over the past few years, we have seen the emergence of a new number of approaches to novo molecular design that have been inspired by techniques used in areas such as computer vision and natural language processing. This field has gained significant interest in the machine learning community, lately of chemistry, to increase state-of-the-art performance. In instance, from 2017 on some data sets, the absolute gain reached over 20% (measured by the quality of generating molecules).

The first of these techniques, known as an autoencoder, utilizes complex tasks: to encode molecules in a continuous latent space that facilitates the prediction and optimization of their properties (encoding) and to learn an optimal representation of a molecular graph with improved properties (decoding).⁴⁰ This is illustrated in Figure 4.

The first applications of generative models to novo molecular design used the Simplified Line Entry System (SMILES) representation of the molecular structure, which was transformed into a latent vector and further optimized to the desired property.³¹ The new optimized vector is then decoded into a SMILES string representing the output molecule. The key assumption in this approach is that representation in this space is smooth, in contrast to the discrete nature of molecular space. However, research has revealed that the constructed latent space is far from smooth: molecules with similar properties might not be similar to each other in the latent space. This part of the explanation can be bridged by the impact of SMILES representation as a starting point—similar molecules may have very different SMILES strings. However, there are other contributing factors, such as property cliffs (chemicals with very similar structures but very different properties) and chemical constraints of the synthesis process. Recent work has significantly advanced the state of the art in novo generative models. Moving molecules representations have explored encoding and decoding algorithms that can effectively process molecular graphs.
