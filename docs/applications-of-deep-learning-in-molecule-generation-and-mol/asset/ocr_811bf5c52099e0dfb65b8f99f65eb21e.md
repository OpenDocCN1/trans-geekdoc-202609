# 2. MOLECULAR PROPERTY PREDICTION

The generation and evaluation of new molecules based on data are critical tasks in the design of new medicines. In the early days of drug discovery, iteration were largely manual and intuition-driven. Scientists would test a set of compounds, examine the data produced, and use an understanding of the underlying science to synthesize molecules. The structural-activity relationships (QSAR) in the 1990s created a framework for associating compounds with chemical properties from physical or biological responses. These quantitative models formed the basis of many virtual screening strategies.

The promise of virtual screening has moved decades beyond infancy. machine learning (ML) methods for predicting molecular properties ranging from biotoxicity to distributive and physical properties. If successful, these methods could transform the process by reducing the time and expense associated with experimental screening while broadening the chemical space. Current experimental screening approaches can potentially access millions of molecules, virtual screening can enable the evaluation of billions of molecules in a short time. The ML algorithms developed using virtual screening can capture complex chemical structure and the nature of molecules. The assumption is that when methods of chemical analysis used with large amounts of data in the form of measured properties, these algorithms can systematically capture properties that may be too subtle for humans to grasp.

Over the past years, the field of molecule and property prediction has seen a revolution, which has been driven by deep learning. Advances in computer hardware and software have made natural language processing now applicable to molecular properties including bioactivity, ADME, and interactions with targets such as QSAR. Rather than relying on sets of expert-defined models, deep learning has enabled researchers to develop models that can be trained directly on large datasets. With the depth and complexity of the neural networks employed, these models have outperformed traditional QSAR approaches. This has also transformed the field of de novo generation. Rather than expanding existing molecules or using a set of rules to link existing generative models, deep learning generates molecules based on learned features of existing molecules.

# 2.1. Neural Networks for Property Prediction

Advancements in neural models opened a possibility to handle this state of the era. One of the key departments of these models is how molecules relate to properties represented within the machine learning algorithms. Traditional models use pre-computed features such as fingerprints. The role of the ML algorithm was to learn how to weigh these features to deliver accurate attributes. Clearly, the success of these models depends on the quality of the training data, which is critical for a predictive modeling task. In contrast, neural models learn their own experience representations from the data, mapping a molecular structure into a continuous vector space using various network architectures.

While the field is advancing, it still faces a number of significant challenges in current research efforts. Although researchers are developing heuristics, we specifically lack a fundamental understanding of molecules required to develop a learned representation and the scope of chemical space covered by a model. As models become more powerful, we need to develop guidelines for the evaluation of these “discoveries” and comparing them with more traditional human-driven methods.
