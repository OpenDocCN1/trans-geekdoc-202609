In my early years, I faced steep learning curves and my managers were very busy people. They usually couldn't provide help at a moment of notice, so I asked them to suggest how I could get help. Answers came, of course, but had one thing that was collecting all the blockers, or where I was stuck, and thinking through them. I found that for some, I could break them down into subproblems and then tackle the overall problem. For other problems, there was information that I needed to move forward. This helped my list of blockers and it made me more independent. As people freed up to help (typically after the financial markets closed), we had things to work through. The second big thing that was developing an internal network of people in similar roles. There was a good chance that someone near my level could offer to help.

The current environment is for crowdsourcing your questions. Instant message boards like Slack or Teams are conducive to posing questions to groups of people and getting answers in a timely fashion. If you have a coding question, there is a great chance that web resources like Stack Overflow will have an answer. In the other direction, I encourage you to share your knowledge and help others when you can.

Beyond getting help for yourself and staying productive on projects, you should also think about what you want to do and to love your work. This might include challenging projects, headcount, a promotion, or a raise. Whatever the result may be, be just that you really want it, that you are ready for it, and you can document why you deserve it. Some of these things could come with more responsibility, or different responsibilities. For example, changing from individual contributor (IC) to manager will require a different set of skills. ICS need to produce work, while managers need to motivate and coach others to produce the work. Not every IC will enjoy being a manager? ## 2.6 Assume Positive Intent

It is very hard to know what people are thinking at any given time. Making this more challenging is remote work and communication channels such as email and instant messaging. We can lose vital information including body language, inflection, and tone. At the same time, many data science roles involve working with people under challenging conditions that include time-pressure, uncertainty, and changing requirements. All of these poses difficulties as we try to maintain positive relationships with coworkers.

As director of an online master's program in data science, I watched many of my students struggle to balance a demanding degree, family responsibilities, and a full-time job. Several projects involved group work, and it was difficult for teammates to work at a constant pace. This was the first time I heard mention of the term *positive intent*, which suggested that students assume others are trying their best and mean no harm, particularly in ambiguous circumstances. One might imagine, it can be very easy to interpret a Slack message negatively (especially when it's missing the right emoji). Moreover, once things take a negative turn, situations can escalate quickly. The advice was very powerful, assuming positive intent brushes off things that may mean nothing. It means giving the benefit of the doubt to the other person, and can induce empathy.

In *Conversations* [5], the authors suggest that we invent a story to explain behavior. When things don't align with ours, the story is often negative. We assume a manager has a low opinion of our capabilities, or that a colleague doesn't like us. The tragedy is that the damaging story may not be correct. As an example, I had an excellent intern who was very driven and results-oriented. I'll call her Christine. As the summer was ending, a colleague mentioned that Christine working for my team and that she was leaving since she wasn't offered the option to return - some of her friends working for larger firms had received offers - she was feeling increasingly discouraged. She made up a story to explain what was happening, and this story included the message that her work was not valued. Of course, this was far from the truth. The truth was that we worked at a startup which was long on work and process - our timing simply didn't align. I was very grateful to her in this instance, as it provided me the opportunity to eradicate the negative story. We made Christine an offer, and she still happily works for the company.

My story was about an intern not asking for a full-time position. This specific scenario may not apply to you, but a larger scenario that may apply during your career is a pay raise or promotion is in order. A sad but true fact is that many companies are not thinking about how to give more money to their employees, even when they highly value their efforts. This is another example where assuming positive intent and having a conversation, even when uncomfortable, is preferable to inventing a negative story. In the space between conversations, we build up stories and feelings to explain differences between reality and expectations. Asking for things, including honest conversations, will help with alignment.

## 2.7 Help Others

I want to briefly make the case for helping others through knowledge sharing and displays of empathy. Earlier, I mentioned that you will need help from others, so it's only fair that I ask that you help others whenever possible. Helping others is not only good karma, but it will increase your satisfaction and value to the organization. Ultimately we feel good when we can be helpful, and it promotes a message of respect and care for our teammates.

There are many ways to be helpful. If you see someone struggling and you can help them, please go ahead and help before they ask. Some organizations groups of employees with specialized expertise helping on a common goal. At UVA, there was a desire to scale the number of online programs. A team of leaders from each university would regularly convene to share best practices. Such groups provide several benefits, including opportunities for networking and accelerated learning.

I have worked in all kinds of organizations, from startups to massive enterprises, from competitive places to cooperative. One of course is entitled to their opinion, but I find that cooperative organizations promote stability, support, and well-being. You might ask why people choose not to help others. There can be two common factors that I have found are from leadership and a scarcity mindset.

People generally respond to incentives. Many salespeople carry quota, as a certain amount of revenue they need to bring in each quarter. The quota is a dangling carrot that leads to planned behaviors. If a team of scientists is a dangling carrot to mentor, then you may find that the ones that this role will respond to the incentive. When leadership rewards workers for their values, and the workers agree with these values, they tend to propagate them [8].

Now let's think about a scarcity mindset and an abundance mindset. If you have a great deal of resource (e.g., money, opportunity, knowledge), then you might feel to donate money or train a junior employee. If you worry that you don't have enough for yourself or your family then you will be reluctant to give it away. When on Wall Street, I met several people who were afraid of colleagues aiming to take their job. As a result, they selectively helped, as they feared that indiscriminately helping people would make them replaceable. In certain situations, this seems understandable. In general, however, playing defense is a strategy based in anxiety, and anxiety is draining. I encourage playing offense: learn as much as you can and focus on your growth. Learning will yield you an abundance of knowledge, and sharing this knowledge will make teammates better and deliver value to the organization.
