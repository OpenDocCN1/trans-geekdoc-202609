# 9.21 One-Sample Test of the Mean

Next, we will conduct a specific test on the population mean $\mu$. The hypothesis test will look like this:

```
$H_0 : \mu = \mu_0$
$H_A : \mu 
eq \mu_0$
```

Given the alternative hypothesis, this is a two-tailed test where we must consider the parameter taking a value less than $\mu_0$ or greater than $\mu_0$. We compute the p-value, we calculate the probability of a test statistic taking its or more extreme in direction. For example, if the test $t=-1$, the p-value is the probability that $t < -1$ or $t > 1$ which is true. $P(|t| > 1)$.

The sample mean $ar{X}$ is used to estimate $\mu$. As its distribution will depend on the sample size $n$, we denote this random variable as $X_n$. The central limit theorem makes this statement about the sampling distribution of the mean:

As $n \to \infty$,

```
$\sqrt{n}\frac{X_n - \mu}{\sigma}\to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$
```

Before standardization, this says that as $n$ increases, $X_n$ is normally distributed with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. The quantity $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ is called the *standard error (SE)* of the sample mean. In general, the SE of a statistic is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution. As the sample size increases, the standard error decreases, with $X_n$ approaching the population mean $\mu$.

One complication is that we generally won't know the population standard deviation $\sigma$. In such a case, we can replace it with the sample standard deviation $s$. Now let's look at the quantity

```
$\frac{ar{X}_n - \mu}{s}$
```
