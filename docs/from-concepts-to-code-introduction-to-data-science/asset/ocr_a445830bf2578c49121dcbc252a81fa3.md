Increasing the sample size will reduce this sampling error, but including more data is not always possible. We will therefore need to account for the variability of the estimate when making a decision.

The estimate will follow a distribution called the sampling distribution, and for large samples (n ≥30) this will increasingly resemble a normal distribution by the central limit theorem. In data science, one can expect the samples to nearly surpass 30 by a large margin. After standardizing the estimate, it will follow a normal distribution, and probabilities can be computed with software (the modern approach) or tables (the earlier approach).

It will be of interest to compute a probability that this statistic or something—“What is the probability of observing this value or more extreme than $H_0$ is true?” The "more extreme" statement is determined from $H_A$. This probability is called the p-value. When the p-value is "sufficiently small," we reject $H_0$; otherwise we fail to reject $H_0$. The benefit of this approach is that it is driven by the data. We will discuss the choice of threshold for making the decision next, but let's first consider a small example where

```
$H_0: θ ≥0
nH_A: θ <0$
```

and the statistic is -0.25. The probability of a statistic of -0.25 or less assuming that $	heta ≥0$? Intuitively, if this probability (or p-value) is very large, then means $H_0$ is likely true. However, if the p-value is very small, then there is evidence to reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_A$.

There are two possible errors that can be made with this approach. If we reject the null when it is true, it is called a Type I error. If we fail to reject the null when it is false, it is called a Type II error. Since uncertainty cannot be eliminated, the strategy is to accept some probability of making a Type I error the significance level, denoted $\alpha$. The most common value for $\alpha$ is 0.05, which amounts to error in 1 out of 20 decisions. A less stringent value of 0.10 (1 out of 10) and a more stringent value of 0.01 (1 out of 10) is also common. These numbers are not magical and should be taken with a grain of salt when results are borderline. A two-way table of decision versus (or truth) is shown below. For the decision to reject $H_0$ (accepting $H_A$) when $H_A$ is true results in a correct decision.

| decision/actual | $H_0$ | $H_A$ |
|---|---|---|
| $H_0$ | Type I error | Type II error |
| $H_A$ | correct | correct |
