## 2 Communicating Effectively and Earning Trust

Once you move beyond data science for yourself as a hobby, you'll need to communicate your work with others. Data scientists have the potential to be transformative and highly visible, and it is not uncommon for them to present work to leadership, board members, venture capitalists, and customers. Effective communication is so essential that if you don't master it, no paying customer or employer will enlist your technical services for long. That is why this chapter appears before the technical topics. A side effect of learning communication skills is better relationship building, which is associated with greater happiness and fulfillment [5].

One of my interviews at a healthcare IT firm was with a seasoned clinician I'll call Janice. She was apprehensive about my ability to come in and lead a team of data scientists to develop their electronic health record system. By my surprise, she had worked with many scientists before who had developed neural networks to predict health outcomes. Digging deeper, it turned out that Janice had been burned by data science before, and the pain radiated through the organization. This is very common, unfortunately, as data science outcomes can be highly uncertain, and the field has overwhelmed. To win over Janice, you'll need to earn and maintain trust. You will need to show that you are competent and responsible, and that you respect and value people.

### 2.1 Master Yourself

Before you win over the hearts and minds of others, you need to convince yourself that you're doing the right things for the right reasons. If you're taking an introductory data science course and plan to go no further in the field, then you have a compelling reason that doesn't require a lot of deep digging: doing this to meet a requirement. My hope, however, is that you fall in love with data science and make it a longer-term endeavor. In this case, having a “why” and revisiting and reinforcing it will be invaluable [6]. When we have a burning reason for doing something, it propels us forward through adversity, and it gives others an entry point to admire our work. This is how great leaders can influence others. As a bonus, when an interviewer says “Tell about yourself,” your “why” will do the important work of telling your unique, important story.

In addition to a “why,” you will need a strong belief in yourself. You may worry about the computer science requirements of the field, public speaking, or some other type of data science. From the start, keep an open mind, stay positive, keep a list of topics for exploration, and gradually chip away at the challenges. Even for accomplished professionals, feelings of self-doubt not deserving a “seat at the table” are very common. The phenomenon is so prevalent it has a name: imposter syndrome. A meta-analysis in the Journal of General Internal Medicine shows imposter syndrome prevalence rates from 9% to 82% over 62 studies and 14,161 participants [7].

Having some wins in your areas can help build confidence. For the areas that need improvement, plan a plan for moving forward. You may be able to get coaching from a mentor or more senior colleague. It is perfectly to be honest about things you don’t know and ask for help. Finally, a mentor can help greatly. In the highest ranks of large organizations use mentors to share ideas, identify weaknesses, and identify opportunities for growth.

Let’s imagine that you’ve been working in data science for a few years and you feel confident doing things like coding, preparing, and building machine learning models. While these functions may not change, the underly tools and techniques will constantly change. I mention this because I want you to be prepared for this certainty. Data science is a field that constantly evolves, as is driven by technology and changing customer demands. If you can accept the challenge of learning new things, and the idea that you’re never done, this will keep you relevant and engaged. For support take part in activities that you enjoy, joining data science community, attending conferences, or reading blogs. Finally, here is one of my favorite quotes from Salk, the developer of one successful polio vaccines:

> There is hope in imagination, and in those who make dreams a reality.

### 2.2 Technical Competence

When we review the work of others, we tend to look for logical fallacies. Methodology issues early in a presentation can quickly eradicate the interest of the audience. "Wait... this dataset is missing a key group of individuals. In that case, including them in this analysis." When sharing our work, it is ideal to avoid making errors, and to anticipate tough questions by the work. An effective strategy that Amazon uses when developing a new product is to write a PR/FAQ, which is a combined press release and question/answer section thinks around corners and gets ahead of reader questions and objectives.

In my first job at Merrill Lynch, my manager had an uncanny knack for looking at numbers and finding errors. He didn’t need to see the code or details. A great scientist needs to be a thoughtful guide. Like any technical field, data science uses abstractions, complex ideas, and jargon. Data and models have limitations that may render your analysis useless. To earn trust, make everything clear to your audience. Statistics and limitations import upon. Explain why the approach is used. If we discuss a metric, it will most often be return on investment (ROI), which reflects expected revenue and capital investment. Are you summarizing customer satisfaction with a scale of 1-10? If possible, test the assumptions and impact, and try things in different ways.

### 2.3 Know Your Audience

As an advisor and consultant, I work with customers to conceptualize, plan, and implement applications of AI and machine learning. This includes working with their leadership to prioritize initiatives, plan a detailed proof of concept (POC), help with implementation, and report out the findings. From project to finish, the audiences will change and I need to be thoughtful on how to lead discussions. As a technical person, I need to avoid my temptation to talk about deep networks with a CEO, for example. I need to "know my audience," so let's get to know them a bit. My meetings with the leadership team may include the CEO, the Chief Officer, and the VP engineering. In these discussions, the focus will be at a strategic level: it is not about fighting fires to solve a small, immediate problem. We talk about things that can have a large impact on the business, such as creating a new product. The questions are focused on what should be done, and not how it should be done. Of course, machine learning is part of the how.
