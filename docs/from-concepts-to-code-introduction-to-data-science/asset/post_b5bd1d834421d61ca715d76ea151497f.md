## 8.5 Matrix Algebra

### Matrix, Vector, and Scalar

A $matrix$ **A** is a rectangular array of numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns written

$$\begin{matrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & a_{13} & \dots & x_{1n} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & a_{23} & \dots & x_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ a_{m1} & a_{m2} & a_{m3} & \dots & a_{mn} \end{matrix}$$

Matrices (plural of matrix) will be useful when we organize our data for calculations. They also provide a compact notation for solving systems of equations, which we encounter in the upcoming regression modeling.

### Matrix Algebra

The number of rows $m$ and columns $n$ of a matrix comprise the dimensions of the matrix: $m \times n$. The dimensions are an extremely important attribute of a matrix, as they define which operations are possible.

Here is a $2 \times 3$ matrix:

```
X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & 0 \\ 2 & 12 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
```

A matrix with one row is called a *row vector*, while a matrix with one column is called a column vector. A vector with only one element is a *scalar*.

Here is a row vector of length 3 and a column vector of length 4, respectively:

```
\mathbf{r}^T = [1 \ 5 \ 0] \quad \mathbf{c} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 7 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
```

### Matrix Addition and Subtraction

Two matrices may be added or subtracted if their dimensions match. When adding or subtracting matrices **A** and **B**, their corresponding elements are added or subtracted in an element-wise fashion like this:

```
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & 0 \\ 2 & 12 & -1 \\ 4 & 12 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 6 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
```

```
\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & 0 \\ 2 & 12 & -1 \\ 4 & 12 & 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 6 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
n= \begin{bmatrix} 1+1 & 5+4 & 0+0 \\ 2+(-1) & 12+0 & -1+6 \\ 4+0 & 12+1 & 3+0 \end{bmatrix}
```

```
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 9 & 0 \\ 1 & 12 & 5 \\ 4 & 13 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
```

### Matrix Multiplication

For $m \times n$ matrix **E** and $n \times p$ matrix **F**, two products **EF** and **FE** may be formed, and are generally different. The dimensions of **E** and **F** determine if the product is valid, and if so, the dimensions of the product. A short mnemonic can be used: if $X$ is $a \times b$ and **Y** is $c \times d$, then **XY** will be $(a \times b) \times (c \times d)$. The inner dimensions must match for the product to be valid. The outer dimensions will be $a \times p$.

For the case of **E** and **F**, the calculation looks like $(m \times n) \times (n \times p)$. The inner dimensions are both $n$, and so the product is valid. The outer dimensions will have the same number of rows as the left matrix and the same number of columns as the right matrix.

Returning to our numerical example, **C** can be formed as follows: For each element of **C**, written $c_{ij}$, row $i$ from **A** and column $j$ from **B**, compute the *inner product* of these two vectors as follows:

```
$c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_{ik}b_{kj}$
```

For this example,

$c_{11} = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_{1k}b_{k1} = (a_{11} \times b_{11}) + (a_{12} \times b_{21}) + (a_{13} \times b_{31}) = (1 \times 1) + (5 \times (-1)) + (0 \times 0) = -4$

Computing the products for the remaining elements produces

**AB =** $\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 30 & 6 \\ -10 & 72 & 12 \\ 4 & 12 & 3 & 0 & -8 & 19 \end{bmatrix}$

Next, let's look at an example. We create the matrices **A** and **B** as NumPy arrays. The operator @ supports matrix multiplication. Note that the operator * is element-wise and not matrix multiplication. It returns a nicer output.

```
A = np.array([[1,5,0], [2,12,-1], [4,12,3]])
B = np.array([[1,4,0], [-1,0,6], [0,1,0]])

print('A: \n', A)
print('B: \n', B)
print('AB: \n', A@B)
```

OUTPUT:

```
A:
[[ 1  5  0]
 [ 2 12 -1]
 [ 4 12  3]]

B:
[[ 1  4  0]
 [-1  0  6]
 [ 0  1  0]]

AB:
[[ -4 30  6]
 [-10 72 12]
 [ -4 -8 19]]
```

Let's define another matrix D with dimensions that do not support multiplication with A. Then we can see how Python handles such a case.

```
D = np.array([[1,4,0],[-1,0,6]])

print('D: \n', D)
print('A dimensions:', A.shape)
print('D dimensions:', D.shape)
print('AD: \n', A@D)
```

OUTPUT:

```
D:
[[ 1  4  0]
 [-1  0  6]]

A dimensions: (3, 3)
D dimensions: (2, 3)

ValueError: matmul input has a mismatch in its core dimension 0, with gufunc signature (n,k),(k,m)->(n,m) (size 2 is different from 3)
```

The shape attribute retrieves the dimensions of a matrix. Since A has 3 columns while the product AD will be invalid, Python throws a ValueError indicating a dimension mismatch calling the matmul method (the @ operator is shorthand for this method). Operations with invalid dimensions are common source of bugs involving matrix algebra. Checking the dimensions of each matrix is highly recommended.

### Transpose

The transpose of matrix A is a matrix denoted A^T and A.T is obtained by taking the rows (or columns) of A to be the columns (or rows) of A^T. Here is an example:

```
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 5 & 0 \\ 2 & 12 & -1 \\ 4 & 12 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
```

```
A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 5 & 12 & 12 \\ 0 & -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
```

For example, the first row of A, [1, 5, 0], became the first column of A^T. Note that [1, 5, 0] is a row vector, and taking its transpose made it a column vector. Taking the transpose of a column vector makes it a row vector.

When a matrix M is equal to its transpose M^T, it is said to be symmetric. An example is

```
M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 12 & 12 \\ 4 & 12 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
```

Symmetric matrices can reduce the amount of storage, due to repeated values.

### Diagonal

The diagonal of an n n matrix includes 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere. That is, they begin at the top left and proceed to the bottom right.

### Identity

The identity matrix I_n is a square n n matrix with 1s on the diagonal. The subscript is sometimes suppressed. Multiplying an appropriately sized matrix by I_n (on the left or right) will reproduce M.

### Summary

This serves the same role as the number 1 in multiplication. Here is a $3 \times 3$ example:

```
$I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
```

### Zero Vector and Matrix

The *zero vector* and *zero matrix* contain all zeros. Here are examples of each:

```
\mathbf{0}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
```

```
\mathbf{0} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
```

### Inverse

The inverse $M^{-1}$ of a square matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is a matrix such that $MM^{-1} = M^{-1}M = \mathbf{I}$. The inverse is unique, and it serves the same role as arithmetic. Finding the inverse of a matrix is efficiently computed (e.g., Python has a function `numpy.linalg.inv()`), it involves solving a system of linear equations. Gaussian elimination* is one popular method for inverting a matrix. A matrix with an inverse is called an *invertible matrix*; it is otherwise called a *non-invertible matrix*. For further details, an excellent text is [29].

## 8.6 Chapter Summary

We started this chapter with an introduction to set theory. Sets, collections of objects, are everywhere in data science, from sets of products to sets of recommendations. It is important to compare and combine sets; operations the inclusion, union, and intersection were discussed. Sets are also essential blocks of functions and probability.

A function is a mapping that assigns each element of a set and assigns an element as output. In data science, we use functions to transform input data to (hopefully) more useful output. We might also perform a sequence of transformations, which is an example of function composition. The inverse function of a function serves to undo the function. We discussed checking if a curve is a function (each input must map to only one output), and if a function has an inverse (it must be one-to-one and onto).
