

# Energy Harvesting and Batteryless Sensing Systems

## Overview

Information has become one of the most important factors in the modern economy, playing a key role in numerous fields such as healthcare, infrastructure, and supply chains. Whenever information from the physical domain is required, sensing systems must be employed to collect relevant data in a scalable and economical manner. Designing these systems for long-term deployment is a daunting challenge because traditional battery-powered devices have limitations in size, cost, reliability, and maintenance. Large energy storage elements can provide a stable power supply, potentially enabling long system lifetimes even if the system's power requirements are highly variable. However, current trends point toward deploying billions of interconnected embedded systems that sense data from their surroundings and will be integrated into the Internet of Things. In many IoT use cases, it is desired that these sensing devices "disappear" both physically and mentally, and require almost no maintenance, which drives the use of mobile wireless sensor nodes. Energy harvesting is widely recognized to play an increasingly important role in providing sufficient energy for such resource-constrained devices. However, despite decreasing costs, few IoT products adopt energy-harvesting-based solutions. This is partly attributed to the mismatch between the power density and timeliness of energy production and consumer demand.

## Batteryless Sensing Systems

A new class of batteryless sensing systems has recently emerged, offering a sustainable long-term solution for providing sufficient operating power to the anticipated vast number of IoT devices. These opportunistic energy systems operate only when the environment provides sufficient operating energy; otherwise, they consume zero energy. This paper focuses on the design challenges of efficient execution for batteryless sensing applications, particularly those powered by light. The work presented here perfectly complements the detailed mathematical models of indoor photovoltaics discussed in Volume 3, Section 3.6.

## Design Optimization Criteria

Starting from the study of energy constraints imposed by indoor environments, the main optimization criteria for batteryless platforms are introduced. Based on this formalized approach, different application scenarios for wearable and statically placed sensors are presented. These applications and their hardware considerations are discussed in detail, along with the software optimizations required to execute them reliably and efficiently in batteryless systems.

## Environmental Energy Dynamics Analysis

If system operation depends on energy extracted from the surroundings, understanding the dynamics of environmental conditions becomes crucial. In the worst case, environmental conditions are non-deterministic and highly variable. Therefore, systems relying on energy harvesting need to tolerate [[261]](#backmatter-001.xhtml#b_9783110785944-010_ref_261) or adapt to [[11]](#backmatter-001.xhtml#b_9783110785944-010_ref_011) variable harvesting conditions for robust operation. Data from spatiotemporally variable environments and the energy extractable through harvesting are invaluable for the sizing, calibration, and testing of such systems. Outdoor solar harvesting irradiance data provided by weather stations worldwide is abundant, often dating back decades. In contrast, indoor solar harvesting data is only sparsely available, but its importance is increasingly prominent for many IoT applications targeting deployment environments such as building automation and assisted living.

## Indoor Energy Harvesting Dataset

We discuss an extensive indoor energy harvesting dataset, first proposed in [[632]](#backmatter-001.xhtml#b_9783110785944-010_ref_632), which addresses the lack of long-term indoor solar harvesting traces. While other works have performed illuminance measurements in indoor environments [[267]](#backmatter-001.xhtml#b_9783110785944-010_ref_267), this work jointly monitors the energy extracted from the solar panel, the energy stored in the capacitor, and the environmental conditions. Power measurements realized using a real harvesting system, combined with rich environmental sensor data, provide diverse opportunities for analysis and evaluation, including power estimation, energy harvesting source modeling, and harvesting system efficiency analysis. A key insight from this dataset is the quantitative understanding of the energy variability of indoor photovoltaic harvesters over a period of more than two years. The same indoor solar cell can generate energy ranging from 0 to potentially hundreds of joules in a single day, depending on geographic location, building design, and many other variables.

## Opportunistic Energy Operation Paradigm

Following the batteryless sensing paradigm, this potentially large energy flow should be consumed immediately as it is harvested. The main reason for this opportunistic energy operation is that the alternative (storing energy for future use) requires expensive rechargeable energy storage devices. For example, a 500 mAh lithium-ion polymer battery may cost several dollars even in bulk, whereas a 47 µF ceramic surface-mount capacitor costs only $0.10. Minimizing energy provisioning will make the IoT economically more viable, environmentally friendlier, and potentially more energy-efficient. When battery-powered devices keep the application circuitry powered for years, a significant portion of the stored energy is spent on *sleep* modes. Even if this is a highly optimized low-power mode, most commercially available microcontrollers can only reduce power consumption to a few µW at best, assuming all other peripherals can be turned off. Therefore, these few µW can accumulate to thousands of joules over years, energy that is not used for the actual application but merely to keep the system powered on. Keeping the system on is crucial for many sensing applications, but not all. Energy-driven sensors will consume harvested energy by running the application as quickly as possible. During nights without energy, the batteryless sensor cannot support an energy-saving *sleep* mode but shuts down completely, consuming zero watts until the environment can supply energy again. Consequently, batteryless systems spend only a minuscule amount of energy maintaining the *sleep* state; the vast majority of energy is actually used to execute the application.
