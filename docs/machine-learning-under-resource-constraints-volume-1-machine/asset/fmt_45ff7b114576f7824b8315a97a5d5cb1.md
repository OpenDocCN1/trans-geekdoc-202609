
### (4.36)

$$
\begin{matrix}
{\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{2}W \cdot \left( {(1)}_{n \times n} - Y \right)} \\
{\text{subject to}\quad Y \succcurlyeq 0} \\
{\quad y_{ii} = 1\quad\forall i \in \{ 1,\ldots,n \}}
\end{matrix}
\qquad (4.36)
$$

Here `W` is defined as the weight matrix of `G` and `(1)_{n \times n}` is the matrix that contains 1 in each component. By Lemma 17 we can obtain an optimal set of vectors `v[i]` with a Cholesky Decomposition in time 𝒪(`n`³).

Now, we show how to modify our formulations to get an approximation algorithm that results in a cut with a performance guarantee of 0.79607 for MAX-DICUT. We start again with the formulation of MAX-DICUT as IQP:

### (4.37)

$$
\begin{matrix}
{\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{i < j}w_{ij}\left( {1 + x_{0}x_{i} - x_{0}x_{j} - x_{i}x_{j}} \right)} \\
{\text{subject to}\quad x_{i} \in \{ -1,1 \}\quad\forall i \in \{ 1,\ldots,n \}}
\end{matrix}
\qquad (4.37)
$$

Here, we introduce an additional variable `x[0]` that marks which side lies in `S`. More precisely, if `x[0]` is equal to −1 all other nodes `i` with `x[i]` = −1 should be assigned to `S` and to `T` otherwise. The term `(1 + x[0]x[i] - x[0]x[j] - x[i]x[j])` evaluates to 4 if `x[i]` is assigned to `S` and 0 otherwise. That is why we have to normalize with the value $\frac{1}{4}$. Similar to the undirected MAX-CUT, we relax the variables in Equation 4.37 so that they are `n` dimensional vectors.

### (4.38)

$$
\begin{matrix}
{\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{i < j}w_{ij}\left( {1 + v_{0}v_{i} - v_{0}v_{j} - v_{i}v_{j}} \right)} \\
{\text{subject to}\quad |v_{i}| \in S_{n}\quad\forall i \in \{ 1,\ldots,n \}}
\end{matrix}
\qquad (4.38)
$$

For its formulation as a SDP we use the matrices `X ∈ ℝ^{(n+1)×(n+1)}` and `Y`, `Z ∈ ℝ^{n×n}` that are defined as follows:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix}
y_{00} & y_{01} & \ldots & y_{0n} \\
y_{10} & y_{11} & \ldots & y_{1n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
y_{n0} & y_{n1} & \ldots & y_{nn}
\end{pmatrix}
\quad
Y = \begin{pmatrix}
y_{11} & y_{11} & \ldots & y_{1n} \\
y_{21} & y_{22} & \ldots & y_{2n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
y_{n1} & y_{n2} & \ldots & y_{nn}
\end{pmatrix}
\quad
Z = \begin{pmatrix}
y_{10} & y_{10} & \ldots & y_{10} \\
y_{20} & y_{20} & \ldots & y_{20} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
y_{n0} & y_{n0} & \ldots & y_{n0}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Then MAX-DICUT can be formulated as SDP as follows:

### (4.39)

$$
\begin{matrix}
{\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{4}W \cdot \left( {(1)}_{n \times n} + Z - Z^{T} - Y \right)} \\
{\text{subject to}\quad X \succcurlyeq 0} \\
{\quad y_{ii} = 1\quad\forall i \in \{ 0,\ldots,n \}}
\end{matrix}
\qquad (4.39)
$$

When we compute a solution for Equation 4.39, we choose a random vector `r` uniformly distributed over `S[n]`. Since `v[0]` marks the side for the partition `S`, we assign all nodes `i` where `v[i]` and `v[0]` lie on the same side to `S`. More precisely, we set `S = {i | sgn(v[i] · r) = sgn(v[0] · r)}` and `T = {i | sgn(v[i] · r) ≠ sgn(v[0] · r)}`.

By analyzing the algorithm similarly to the undirected MAX-CUT, we find that our algorithm has a performance guarantee of 0.79607. The following theorem summarizes our results.

**Theorem 18.** The described algorithm calculates a cut with a performance guarantee of 0.79607 in polynomial time.

### 4.4.3 Framework

In this section we introduce a parallel framework that computes a MAX-DICUT with high quality in shared memory. First, we give an overview of the whole framework before we introduce each step individually.

Our approach is to partition an input graph `G` into `k` subgraphs `G[i]` of roughly equal size so that the dependency between the subgraphs is minimized i.e. the edge-weights between the subgraphs are minimized. Then on each computed subgraph, we run in parallel a sequential MAX-DICUT algorithm to compute several local cuts. In a final step, we have to merge the locally computed cuts. We achieve this by defining a new graph on which MAX-DICUT is solved where each node represents a partition computed by the local MAX-DICUT algorithms.

#### 4.4.3.1 Graph Partitioning

The first step of our framework is to partition the input graph `G = (V, E, w)` into `k` subgraphs so that we can run a MAX-DICUT algorithm on each subgraph independently. Our goal is to include as much edge information as possible into each subgraph to improve the quality of the computed MAX-DICUT. We achieve this by maximizing the sum of the edge-weights in each subgraph or, vice versa, by minimizing the sum of the edge-weights between the subgraphs. That is to say, we want to minimize Σ`[i,j∈{1,...,k}] E[ij]` where `E[ij]` is the sum of the edge-weights between subgraph `G[i]` and `G[j]`. To compute a well balanced graph partitioning in shared memory, there already exist several approaches. In our framework we use the graph partitioner KaHIP [[13]](#backmatter-001.xhtml#b_9783110785944-010_ref_013), which partitions `G` into the subgraphs `G[i] = (V[i], E[i], w)`, `i ∈ {1, ..., k}` so that each subgraph has roughly equal size, i.e. `|V_i| ≤ (1 + ϵ)⌈|V|/k⌉`. We also use a partitioning algorithm that naively divides either the nodes or edges into `k` chunks of equal size. We could also use Metis, as in [Section 4.3](#9783110785937_013188.xhtml#b_9783110785944-004_s_003). However, KaHIP outperforms Metis and is better suited for our application. In [Figure 4.16](#9783110785937_013188.xhtml#b_9783110785944-004_fig_016) we see an exemplary input graph that is partitioned into 4 subgraphs.
