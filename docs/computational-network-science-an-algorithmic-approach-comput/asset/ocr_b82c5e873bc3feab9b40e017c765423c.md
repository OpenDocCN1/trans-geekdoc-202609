All individuals are considered to have an equal probability of contact (i.e., infection), denoted by $eta$. At each time $t$, each person may infect $eta \times N$ others with equal probability. The fraction of contacts between an infected individual and a susceptible is $S(t)/N(t)$. Therefore, the new infection rate is computed by $\beta \times N[S(t)/N(t)] \times I(t) = \beta \times S(t) \times I(t)$. The population leaving the susceptible camp is equal to the number entering the infected camp. Meanwhile, a number of individuals equal to the fraction of infected individuals are leaving this class per unit time and entering the removed class (i.e., the law of mass action). Let $\gamma$ denote the rate of infection and recovery is much faster than the timescale of births and deaths overlapped in the model. Therefore, the rate of infection change is $dR(t)/d(t) = \beta \times S(t) \times I(t) - \gamma \times I(t)$ and the rate of recovery is $dR(t)/d(t) = \gamma \times I(t)$.

There have many variations to the SIS model, including SIR and models that account for births and deaths in mathematical epidemiology (Brauer et al., 2008) with models available from Vynlicky and White (2010). Another good source of discussion of these models is Jackson (2008).

Whether epidem grown or not, a community is a group of closely related and connected entities with similar attributes; it can also be a part of a large network with groups of communities. Entities in community can interact with other communities. A good community will interact less with the entities of an outside community and more with those of the inside community.
