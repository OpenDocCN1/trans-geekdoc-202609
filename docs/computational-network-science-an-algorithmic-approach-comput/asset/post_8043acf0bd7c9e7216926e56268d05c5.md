For every node $v$ that is not active at $t-1$, find all nodes $u$ that may influence $v$ (i.e., incoming edges to $v$) and execute *activation attempt*, which is performed by a Bernoulli trial using probability $p$. If $u$'s activation attempt succeeds, add $v$ to $S_t$, that is, node $u$ influences (i.e., activates) $v$ at time $t$. If there are multiple nodes that may activate $v$, the result is similar irrespective of which one activates $v$. Since activation attempts of nodes are independent, the model is an independent, cascade model. When no more activation is possible, the network stays the same, and the set of activated nodes becomes the *final* $S$. At some point, no new activations are possible. All nodes are either activated or not. We call this *saturation*. An interesting property of ICM is that the capacity for diffusion is the same for all seed set nodes known as the *submodularity principle*. Another property of ICM is that the set state is invariant to the seed size known as the *monotonicity principle*.

*A voter model* (Clifford and Sudbury, 1973) uses an undirected graph, where each node possesses a binary state 0 or 1. Iteratively, at each step, a node $v$ at time $t$ selects a random neighbor $u$ to mimic it at $t-1$. To state more formally, states of node $\vec{x}_i$ probability that it is in state 1 (as opposed to being in state 0) is denoted by $\bar{x}_i(t)$, and $\bar{x}_i(0)$ is the state of node $i$ at $t=0$. In a Markov random field model, each node of an undirected graph is a random variable $X_i$. According to the Markov property, $X_i$ is independent of nodes that are not its neighbors, $\tilde{N}_v$, but do depend on its neighbors of $v$, $N_v$. Let $C(\tilde{N}_v)$ be a possible configuration of nodes in $\tilde{N}_v$ and $S_0$ be the set used. Then Equation 7.3 holds (Domingos and Richardson, 2001):

## 7.2 SOCIAL LEARNING

DeGroot assumes a society of $n$ agents, where individuals possess an initial ($t=0$) opinion on a singular subject, represented by a vector of probabilities $p(0) = (p_1(0), ..., p_n(0))$, where $p(t)$ denotes the probabilistic belief of agent $i$ at time $t$. Let $T$ be a matrix of interactions among agents, where $T_{ij}$ is the amount of weight (i.e., trust) agent $i$ places on $j$'s opinion at time $t$ in forming $i$'s opinion at $t+1$. We assume that agent $i$'s influence (i.e., trust) is limited to the sum of its peers. Therefore, the model renders to be stochastic. Probabilities are updated using Equations 7.4–7.6 with the Markov chain property:

```
$p(t) = T \times p(t-1) \quad (7.4)$
```

```
$p(t) = T_i \times p(0) \quad (7.5)$
```

```
$\forall i, p_i(\infty) = \lim_{t\to\infty} p_i(t) \quad (7.6)$
```

A network $N$ and a matrix $T$ reach consensus if $\forall i, j \in N, p_i(\infty) = p_j(\infty)$. Such a matrix $T$ is a steady-state Markov chain. A society is said to be wise when the most influential influence vanishes in the society (Jackson, 2008). Further discussions of convergence and examples are found in Jackson (2008).

## Power in Exchange Networks

Each link between a pair of nodes that provides an opportunity for a transaction between the pair produces a value that can be shared between the pair. This is the premise in exchange networks (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). Dating and mating networks can be represented with exchange networks (Blau, 1964). An exchange requires at least two individuals and can be tangible or intangible entities (i.e., goods and services) with imbalances of utility for participants (Turner, 1998). Blau promoted the idea that social choices are social actions that are counterparts of physical, speech, and epistemological actions (Blau, 1964). In the social exchange theory, exchanges yield value (i.e., worth) that is rewards—costs of the exchange. If the value is positive, the exchange is a desirable relationship; otherwise, exchange and the corresponding relationship are worthless.

In search of desirable exchanges, a few individuals may lack partners (e.g., remain bachelors) while others may have plethora of potential neighbors from whom to choose. Economic networks as buyers and sellers of goods and services are well represented with exchange networks (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). Sellers and buyers could create network nodes and links would model transactions among. Sellers who have exclusive buyers have power over the extent of their exclusivity. Sellers who are exclusive providers of scarce commodities have power over the extent of their exclusivity and scarcity of their goods (i.e., monopoly in economic terms). When buyers are scarce, they have higher power over the extent of their scarcity (i.e., monopsony in economic terms). In more general terms, dependence among agents derived from their relative position in the network gives rise to their structural power. There is a reciprocal relationship between potential power and dependence among agents (Emerson, 1962). Dependence, exclusion, and saturation are reported underlying determinants of structural power (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010).
