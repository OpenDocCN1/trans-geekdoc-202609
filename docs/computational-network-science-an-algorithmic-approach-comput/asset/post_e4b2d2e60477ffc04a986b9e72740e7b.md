# CHAPTER 11
Network Organizations

Networks provide platforms for establishing and sustaining relationships among individuals (i.e., agents). The most common form of relationships is between pairs of agents (i.e., dyadic relationships). Other relationships can be between agents and their allegiance to groups. A specific type of a group is a network organization (NO), which is common in many forms. Business organizations (e.g., IBM) have been using their own internal versions of internal social networks for far longer than those available to the general public (e.g., Facebook), which have routinely supported informal, long- and short-duration, communities of interest. Civic, volunteer, and myriad associations are other examples of NO. An individual may have various relationships with an NO including passive membership, active participation, and key player in the operation.

Electronic institutions (E-institutions) have been proposed to guide normative patterns of organizations (Sierra and Dignum, 2001). Our suggestion of using social capital for reasoning about resources in Chapter 9 is complementary to E-institutions, in which they can be argued to guide resource allocation in NO. However, our social capital perspective here is more on local decision making than as global (i.e., systemwide) supported by E-institutions.

An NO may provide access to forms of intangible, status bearing capital (e.g., an elite member of a neighborhood watch association), and, as such, it would be said that the NO possesses social capital by its capacity to dispatch capital. This is the only exception in the entire NO and specific agents possess social capital. This chapter does not further consider this type of NO social capital and other varieties of social capital not stemming directly from NO as those derived from ethnographic-, gender-, and class-based capitals of agents (Parks-Yancy et al., 2006). Social capital and institutions overlap in managing resources but this comparison is outside our present scope of interest (Lin, 2001).

# Computational Network Science: An Algorithmic Approach

In contrast to the stylized relationships of traditional networks, an alternative for the network is to consider dynamic and fluid relationships. A group might gather under loose connections such as business label with no physical presence. Since there is absence of tangible places and resources to identify the group, this type of dynamic network is often called a virtual organization (Dziuzla-Rucinska et al., 1994; Potter and Bazard, 2002). Norman et al. (2003) provide strategies for formation of virtual organizations. Another type of dynamic network is when a group with complementary expertise and interests may form a rather temporary and impromptu network to work on a short-duration goal, which we call networked organizations. Typically, these organizations lag coordination and control schemes before they start. Postmortem analyses reveal serendipitous, implicit, and emergent coordination protocols. Examples of such situation-based networked organizations are commonly found in the law enforcement cases where disparate law and crime professionals collaborate as needed on solving crimes. A recent synthesis of ideas for emergent networked theory that is described in Westaby (2012). Westaby puts forward social psychological explanations for how individuals in a social network are motivated to pursue goals. He develops qualitatively metrics that account for social forces that propel or prohibit progress goals.

Westaby contrasts role-based organizational charts with sociograms of social networks, where actual communication does not follow a long organizational chart. This is contrasted with dynamic network charts, where metas illustrate informal grouping of individuals who positively or negatively influence goal pursuit and achievement. Goal striver, goal preventer, system supporter, system resistor, system reactor, interactants, and observer are several metas by Westaby. Network motivation as social forces that promote goals is a function of strivers and supporters. The opposite of network motivation is the social force of network resistance as a function of goal preventers and supportive resistors. Network affirmation and de-affirmation are social forces that approve or disapprove network goals, however without active participation. The theory considers various goal conflicts. Westaby introduces activation levels and performance measures in order to determine overall network effectiveness measures. Westaby’s system of visualizing metaroles overlaid over a NO is very enlightening and aids in analysis of dynamic processes operating inside a NO. Webster's characterization of NOs is certainly rich and articulates many interesting features of NOs. It can be implemented in a computational decision aid. However, determining measures of social forces cannot be automated and remains subjective, which must be subjectively entered. This draw-backs its wider applicability and proliferation of his theory.

# 11.1 CONCLUSION

NOs are emerging groups that use the Internet including social media to facilitate collaboration. This has provided serendipitous and emergent interaction among individuals that exceeds traditional organizational framework. This is a nascent aura that requires attention. We envision this technology to transform information society into cohesive wholes.
