## 9.1 NETWORK EFFECTS

In an economic network, there are direct and indirect effects between an individual's choices on others' and vice versa in what economists call *externality*. For example, a producer's firm profit and a consumer's utility are proportional to the numbers of producers and consumers using the same technology. A famous model of externality is telecommunication subscription (Rohlich, 1974). Let potential subscribers be indexed by $x$, where $0 \leq x \leq 1$. Consumers indexed by low values of $x$ value the subscription highly, whereas consumers indexed by $x$ close to 1 place a low valuation on this service; $p$ denotes the subscription fee, and $q^x$ is the expected number of subscribers. The expected utility of a potential subscriber indexed by $x \in [0, 1]$ is computed with Equation 9.1.

$\alpha > 0$ measures the intensity of network effects. Higher values of $\alpha$ indicate that consumers place a higher value on the ability to communicate with the $q^x$ subscribers. In contrast, $\alpha = 0$ implies there are no network effects. The parameter $\beta > 0$ captures the degree of consumers' heterogeneity with respect to consumers' benefit from this service.

```
$U(x) = \begin{cases} (1-\beta x)\alpha q^x - p, & \text{if he or she subscribes} \\ 0, & \text{if he or she does not subscribe} \end{cases} \quad (9.1)$
```

Externalities exist in limited settings. Consider the two-player game shown in Figure 9.2, where there are four strategies of adopting and avoiding changes.

| | Adopt | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| I | Adopt $\alpha,\alpha$ | Avoid $\gamma,\delta$ |
| | Avoid $\delta,\gamma$ | $\beta,\beta$ |

Fig. 9.2. An economic decision game payoff matrix for adopting a new technology. If $\alpha > \delta$ and $\beta > \gamma$, each player earns a high payoff if the player adopts; therefore, network externalities exist.

The game has two Nash equilibria of (adopt, adopt) and (avoid, avoid). If $\alpha > \beta$, the outcome (adopt, adopt) Pareto dominates the outcome (avoid, avoid). This is known as *excess inertia* (Farrell and Saloner, 1985). If $\alpha < \beta$, the outcome (avoid, avoid) Pareto dominates the outcome (adopt, adopt) and is called *excess momentum*. However, neither is a subgame perfect equilibrium with sequential decisions. A similar analysis is possible with competition in prices (i.e., Bertrand game) or in quantities (i.e., Cournot game) (Jackson, 2008). In two-sided markets, demand and supply spillovers create externalities for buyers and sellers (Rysman, 2009). When customers are identified with components, the externality is *considered direct*. However, in most economic networks, the externality is only *indirect*. Further information is available from NYU online site maintained by Dr. Nicholas Economides.

## 9.2 CONCLUSION

Whether with friends, colleagues, or strangers, economic networks are common exchange networks in our daily lives. There are clear applications of economic networks in advertising space, for example, selling Google ad space (Chiang, 2012). There are also potential applications for policy analysis. In order to understand range and scope of impacts for a new or changed policy, network effects need to be explored.

Network Capital

Networks are storehouses for a variety of capital that have value and worth. In economic terms, capital is money (Marx, 1967). Physical capitals are tools and resources (Economy and Levi, 2014). These might be common tools or raw materials such as oil and gas, which are commonly accessible to a group. Cultural capital consists of language, customs, and lifestyle choices (Beemyn, 2014). Whereas diversity promotes minority, social conventions promote conformity. Political capital is largely a measure of power to persuade others (Burgmann, 2014). Democracy and human rights serve as example areas, where social forces promote them from the West to the rest of the world. Social capital (SC) is an intangible capital to manage and change relationships such as interpersonal trust made famous by the works of Pierre Bourdieu, James Coleman, and Robert Putnam (Field, 2014). There are two major perspectives on SC in networks. In the macroscopic perspective, SC for the entire network is considered. In this view, individuals do not incrementally add to the system or withdraw units of SC. Instead, the areas of interest are the system principles such as norms and conventions that provide resources for the overall social welfare.

Physical goods and services are often scattered unevenly in networks. In contrast, the microscopic perspective explores how individuals gain access to capital by their positions. Nodes may take action to do one of the following: gain access to capital (e.g., gasoline), exert influence over nodes (e.g., energy), have power over capital (e.g., tuna), and have control over flow (e.g., nuclear materials).
