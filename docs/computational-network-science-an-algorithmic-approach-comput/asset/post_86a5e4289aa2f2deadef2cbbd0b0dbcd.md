Identification of capital is crucial for legitimacy of SC. It is also crucial to note that SC is meaningful only for agents whose reality result in capital benefits. Our present model assumes a finite number of single-unit capitals (i.e., resources) placed at the subset of nodes within a network.

Gaining and controlling access to resources is a predominant form of reasoning about resources. Manipulating marginal access to resources (i.e., externalities) is another common form of reasoning about resources. SC in the network is the degree to which an agent is able to handle capital in the network by social means. For our use, handling resources is considered in either one of two forms of access and activation. Access to resources is the measure of potential to reach and use resources, and that is the primary focus here. Activation is the choice of resources and types of efforts in approaching them (Hurlbert et al., 2000). The term bonding SC is meaningful only in the context of an individual, who may exploit his her social membership with a bonding measure in the network for gaining access to a form of capital available. Bridging is another common property of a network, and an individual who is exposed to a bridging feature gains access to capital that might be in the remote parts of the network and be said to be bridging SC. To say “SC exists”, we must identify the capital and beneficiaries. In fact, we consider a generalization of FSC, where F is a network feature. Examples of network features are agent clustering coefficient, bonding, bridging, and leadership. Online organizations provide a network in which agents experience bonding and bridging SC (Ellison et al., 2007). On the one hand, SC may reflect merits of a node’s current position in the network with respect to relative locations of capital that the node values in the network. If a node A’s good neighbor R has a valuable resource, A will possess a simple form of location SC and will be motivated to maintain strong ties with R in order to have access to R’s resource. The farther away A is from R, A experiences diminished location SC. Another form of location SC is when a node is an intermediary node (i.e., a bottleneck) between some resources and a set of nodes who desire those resources. Various location SCs are exemplified by geographical locations of countries relative to natural or political resources around the world. Another perspective on SC is in the form of social manipulation ability to mobilize or constrain resources, which location is less pertinent. Nodes that possess resources themselves or are near resource sources may enact policies that give away or hoard (i.e., share or deny) resources to others. SC for the owners of capital is their manipulation capacity over access to their resources. For nonowners of capital, SC in this context is their ability to successfully deal with capital owners to provide them with access to the resources. Setting and changing exchange policies among nodes of the network is a type of social manipulation over resources. Trade policies among countries exemplify this type of resource manipulation. There are a few main elements of the SC, which have a proportional relationship to one another.

Topologically speaking, high bonding rates provide more opportunities for interaction and growth of SC. However, network structure itself is inadequate for determination of SC. We must examine the contents of interaction and dispositions that create social flows that attract or repel individuals. At the level of a single link, the nature of social flow in the link leads to accumulation of SC. Social flows can be benevolent and positive or negative and lack of benevolence. Positive social flow is an interaction that embodies friendship and sharing congruent positions on topics of interest. Whereas positive flow leads to net gain in SC, negative flow leads to loss of SC. Apart from social flow, dyadic ties harbor or promote trust (Fukuyama, 1995). Trust supports SC, whereas distrust erodes SC. If the topic of interaction between the pair is of mutual interest, that link positively contributes to SC. In sum, flow, trust, and link are link attributes that are proportional determinants for social influence.

Definition (link influence): Social influence in a link is measured by (a) accumulation of positive values of social flow, (b) trust, and (c) abundance of communication over a common topic. For simplicity, we suggest treating all three attributes equally.

In the following, consider N agents and M distinct resources. Let there be a node i, who has a direct link to a node R, who owns a resource. When the influence between pair of agents is high, nodes' neighboring resource owners count on their influence to gain access to resources. When i's distance d to the nearest alternate resource is small, it is more willing to share than when distances are far. In fact, the willingness to share can be modeled as proportional to inverse of square of geodesic distance, that is, 1/d.

When a pair of nodes has a prior history of sharing resources, the probability of sharing resources between them in the future is correspondingly. When a resource owner has access to alternate resources, he or she is more willing to share his or her own resource. Resource scarcity plays a big role in alternate resources. Expected reciprocation by other resource owners can be modeled by 1/(M-1). We capture these factors in a resource sharing policy defined next.

# **Definition (resource sharing policy with a neighbor):** R's willingness to share its resource with i depends on the following attributes:

- (a) Influence in the link between i and R must be larger than a predefined threshold.
- (b) Distance between R and the nearest alternate resource holder is small.
- (c) There is a prior balance of resource sharing between i and R.
- (d) R has a nontrivial expected reciprocation of resources from other resource owners.

A direct consequence of owning a resource is dependence that others have on that. According to Emerson (1962), dependence is the opposite of power. Therefore, the resource owner has social power over others. An agent's interest in hoarding a resource is the complement of the agent's willingness to share the resource.

Next, we consider a policy for remote access to a resource. Let there be a node i, which does not have a direct link to a node R that owns a resource. When the sum of influence between R and all the links along all shortest paths to reach i is high, R would be willing to share its resource with i. When a pair of nodes has a prior history of sharing resources, the probability of sharing resources between them in the future is proportionally large. When R’s geodesic distance d to the nearest alternate resource is small, it is more willing to share when distances are large. This is similar to the neighbor case. When R has access to alternate resources, the agent might have high expected reciprocation and be more willing to share its own resource. This is similar to the neighbor case.

# **Definition (resource owner sharing policy with a remote node):** R’s willingness to share its resource with a remote node depends on the following four attributes:

- (a) Influence along all shortest paths between i and R must be larger than a predefined threshold.
- (b) Distance between R and the nearest alternate resource holder is small.
- (c) There is a prior balance of resource sharing between i and R.
- (d) R has nontrivial expected reciprocation of resources from other resource owners.

The opposite of sharing is denying resources (i.e., hoarding), which we present as a complementary concept to sharing. Next, few policies provide a few common resource denial policies.

# **Definition (hoarding policy with a neighbor):** R’s degree of hoarding is the complement of R’s willingness to share its resource.

# **Definition (prohibition hoarding policy with a non-neighbor):** R refuses to share its resource with a remote node i if there exists a weak influence link between i and R.

# **Definition (exclusivity hoarding policy):** R refuses to share its resource with any node i if R knows that i has access to a peer resource owner.

# **Definition (competitive hoarding policy):** R refuses to share its resource with a peer resource owner.

In order for a node to access a resource, it must interact with resource owners, captured in the next definition.

# **Definition (resource access):** Resource access for node i with respect to resource R from resource holder R is the willingness of R to share the resource with i.
