A desirable objective for exchanges is called *stability*, the production of preferred outcomes that augment values of nodes engaged in exchange. If there are incentives for changes in exchange partner in order to increase value, there is *instability*. An exchange network is stable if and only if there are no instabilities (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). Game theoretic, strategic bargaining has been used to optimize network stability (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010).

Whereas structural power empowers agents with capacities to influence others, it is the agent choices, that is, agent *strategic actions*, that manifest social power. Agents can capture positive exchanges by dispensing or withholding rewards, wielding power strategies in their exchange networks (Russell, 1938; Molm, 1990).

Individuals often join groups to increase their power. This is captured with the concept of *coalitions*, for example, economic blocks that may be regional or transnational. For the basics of coalition theory, refer to cooperative game theory in Chapter 3. For a few good examples, the reader may consult Bonacic and Liu (2012). There is much that remains to be explored and mathematical models that are of benefit for network science (Simpson et al., 2014).

## 8.1 CONCLUSION

*Power* has been of interest to network scientists and social scientists. We referred to structural and strategic notions of power with historical roots (Russell, 1938). There is a strong connection between power and influence, even though there are scarce formal models for qualitative and quantitative analyses (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). An intuitive direction for progress appears to be capture inequalities of resource distribution and access to them in the network. Those who are close to resources may regulate resource access to others in the network. Those who are remote from resources might wish to engage in strategic negotiation or have brokers engaged in such buffering.

## Economic Networks

Buyers, sellers, and traders are individuals with interests in goods and services. These individuals often form nodes of network called markets, which are a special type of an economic network. An economic network is a type of exchange network where the links are used for exchange of commodities and services. These exchanges are direct and indirect economic effects for individuals. Interfacing buyers and sellers as matching markets are presented in Chapters 10 and 11 of Easley and Kleinberg (2010).

Payoff (i.e., utility) for node $j$ is defined as $v_j - p_j$ where $v_{ij}$ is the value derived by agent acquiring $i$'s goods and $p_i$ is the price that is paid for the goods. For a set of prices, $P = \{p_1, p_2, ...\}$, each buyer wants to maximize his or her payoff. If there is a tie among sellers, a random seller is selected. Buyer rationality dictates that buyers purchase only if their payoff is a positive value. A buyer places a value on each item and the valuation profile of a buyer is the list of the values that the buyer assigns to items that are on sale. A buyer's seller is a set of sellers that provides a positive value. Market clearing is achieved when buyers and sellers are perfectly matched so that every item can be sold. There are a few illustrative examples in Chapter 10 of Easley and Kleinberg (2010). There are also a couple of positive results. First, for every set of buyer valuations (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). Second, for every set of market clearing prices, any resultant perfect matching for buyers yields a maximal valuation for items bought from sellers. Sometimes attributed to Jenő Egervári, there is a procedure for constructing market clearing prices. This procedure is famously known as Gale-Shapley algorithm shown in Figure 9.1. A constricted set is a set in which edges from this set to the other side of the bipartite graph “constrict” the formation of a perfect matching. Further examples and details are available from Easley and Kleinberg (2010) and will not be duplicated here.

In more complex markets, there are intermediaries called *traders* that facilitate an interface between buyers and sellers. The role of traders in a market for agricultural goods between local producers and consumers is illustrated here in Chapter 11 of Easley and Klein (2010) and we summarize here. The flow of goods from sellers to buyers is determined by a game in which traders set prices, and then sellers react to these prices. Each trader $t$ *offers* a bid price to seller to whom she is connected, denoted by $b_{ti}$. This bid price is an offer by $t$ to *buy* $i$'s copy of the good at a value of $b_{ti}$. Each trader $t$ *offers* an ask price to buyer $j$ to whom she is connected. This ask price is denoted by $a_{tj}$, and is an offer by $t$ to *sell* the copy of the good to buyer $j$ at a value of $a_{tj}$. Each trader posts bid prices to the sellers to whom she is connected, and ask prices to the buyers to whom she is connected. Once traders announce prices, each seller and buyer chooses at most one trader to deal with. Each seller sells his or her copy of the good to the trader she selects, or keeps his or her copy of the good if he chooses not to sell it. Each buyer purchases a copy of the good from the trader he selects, or receives no copy of the good if he does not select a trader. This determines a flow of goods from sellers, through traders, to buyers. There are incentives for a trader not to produce bid and ask prices that cause more buyers than sellers to accept his or her offers. There are also incentives for a trader not to be caught in the reverse, with more sellers than buyers accepting his or her offers. A trader's payoff is the profit he makes from all of his or her transactions. It is the sum of the sum of the bid prices of his or her accepted offers to sellers, minus the sum of the bid prices of his or her accepted offers to sellers.
