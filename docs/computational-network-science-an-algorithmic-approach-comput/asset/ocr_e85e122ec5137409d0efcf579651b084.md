A desirable objective for exchanges is called *stability*, the production of preferred outcomes that augment values of nodes engaged in exchange. If there are incentives for changes in exchange partner in order to increase value, there is *instability*. An exchange network is stable if and only if there are no instabilities (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). Game theoretic, strategic bargaining has been used to optimize network stability (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010).

Whereas structural power empowers agents with capacities to influence others, it is the agent choices, that is, agent *strategic actions*, that manifest social power. Agents can capture positive exchanges by dispensing or withholding rewards, wielding power strategies in their exchange networks (Russell, 1938; Molm, 1990).

Individuals often join groups to increase their power. This is captured with the concept of *coalitions*, for example, economic blocks that may be regional or transnational. For the basics of coalition theory, refer to cooperative game theory in Chapter 3. For a few good examples, the reader may consult Bonacic and Liu (2012). There is much that remains to be explored and mathematical models that are of benefit for network science (Simpson et al., 2014).

# 8.1 CONCLUSION

*Power* has been of interest to network scientists and social scientists. We referred to structural and strategic notions of power with historical roots (Russell, 1938). There is a strong connection between power and influence, even though there are scarce formal models for qualitative and quantitative analyses (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). An intuitive direction for progress appears to be capture inequalities of resource distribution and access to them in the network. Those who are close to resources may regulate resource access to others in the network. Those who are remote from resources might wish to engage in strategic negotiation or have brokers engaged in such buffering.
