
Definition (a node's resource access measure): SC for node i with respect to resource R is the sum of resource access to all resources in the network. This includes neighbors who are resource owners and remote resource owners.

An obvious observation is that a node that is well connected with many nearby resource owners has a higher SC than a node that has fewer and more distant resource owners. It is possible for nodes to be rich or poor in SC given their position in the network relative to resource owner nodes. Given the policies for sharing and hoarding, it is possible to build differential relations and make certain nodes beneficiaries of SC. It is also possible to withhold resources from certain nodes, thereby ostracizing them by lack of SC.

Next, we focus on determination of SC in a given network as stated in the following definition.

## Definition (a node's SC measure): SC for the network with respect to resource R is the sum of SC for all nodes in the network.

SC is generated in the links through dynamics of interaction inside the links. Therefore, SC for a network linearly scales by summing SC for all links in the network. Increased links are proportional to increased SC stated in Observation 2. The effects of topology are overlooked in the network perspective but are considered in the egocentric in the next definition. All bridged communities contribute to accumulation of the over-instrumental purpose of SC (Lin, 2001). In general networks and from an egocentric perspective, bridging is said to contribute to SC (Smith, 2008). We return to this when we discuss our egocentric view of SC.

## Observation 1: SC in the network is proportional to network bonding measure.

Network bonding leads to increased density and closure in the network, which is believed to increase resource access (Lin, 2001).

## Observation 2: A network topology that is a clique produces the highest SC.

Observation 2 is obvious since the binding rate is proportional to the number of links. According to Observation 1, the more the number of links in the network, that is, the more interconnections a network has, the more opportunities it has for accumulation of SC. If a network has the most links (i.e., a clique), it supports the highest SC. Next we define an ego-centric perspective for SC.

**Definition:** SC for a node (i.e., egocentric for an individual) is the sum of SC derived from its links with direct neighbors plus the sum of SC of all its neighbors.

This egocentric definition of SC deliberately mirrors the Bonachich power index (Bonachich, 1987). This coincidence helps us exploit the topological position of nodes. A node that is well positioned by having a high power index (i.e., high Bonachich centrality value) similarly possesses high SC.

**Observation 3:** Nodes in a network with higher power possess SC.

**Observation 4:** Nodes in a network with bridge roles possess SC.

We consider SC to be a scalar value that can be either accumulated and consumed verbatim or used as credit. In a network, SC might be used to trade for or exchange with others in the form of delegation of tasks. Bartering with SC can be limited to a pair of nodes through an immediate link between them. Alternatively, a node might barter anonymously with another node with which there may not be a direct link.

If the network is very large and resources are scattered widely, it may be ineffective to engage in direct interactions with resource owners due to large distances to resource owner nodes. It would be beneficial to find nodes that interface on their behalf. On the other hand, some resource owners may wish to make their resources available far beyond their immediate surroundings. It would be useful if the resource owner could have an interface agent in order to expose availability of the resource to remote parts of the network. This is discussed in the following section.

### 10.1.1 Intermediaries

Some agents may play intermediary roles to facilitate resource access among other agents. The simplest intermediary role is when a resource owner agent delegates access to its resource to a broker agent, whom we dub the coordinator. To keep things simple, we assume that each resource owner is identified with a single coordinator in the network and that the coordinator possesses the highest SC in the network with respect to the resource owner whose it is coordinating. All access policies of the resource owner are handled by the coordinator.

Another type of intermediary is when a number of agents with low SC themselves are identified with a representative broker whose task is to secure access to a specific resource on their behalf. Between agents and their representative must be high SC. The representative must strive to build strong SC to targeted resource owners in order to have the capacity to deliver resource access to its constituents. The representative agent must continually review resource policies of resource owners and, when applicable, policies of coordinators. In very large networks, once coordinators and representative broker agents are identified, majority of interactions are (a) between agents and representatives, and (b) between coordinators and resource owners. The next section offers a rudimentary high-level outline for resource access using SC. Details are omitted for brevity and many variations are planned for future work.

### 10.2 CONCLUSION

We have outlined a variety of capital available on a network. Nodes may desire to access, influence, exert power, and control capital. This is a largely unexplored topic and our exposition is a call to arms for building useful models. We sketched a model of SC to facilitate access to physical resources for nodes that lack them. Repeated social interaction among agents leads to development of SC, which is used as a measure of access to resources. We discussed how locations of agents relative to loci of agents give them different levels of SC and resource access. We then outlined a number of policies that can be used to regulate resource access. A resource owner may relinquish access to its resource to be controlled by an intermediary, coordinator agent.
