Power in Exchange Networks

Each link between a pair of nodes that provides an opportunity for a transaction between the pair produces a value that can be shared between the pair. This is the premise in exchange networks (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). Dating and mating networks can be represented with exchange networks (Blau, 1964). An exchange requires at least two individuals and can be tangible or intangible entities (i.e., goods and services) with imbalances of utility for participants (Turner, 1998). Blau promoted the idea that social choices are social actions that are counterparts of physical, speech, and epistemological actions (Blau, 1964). In the social exchange theory, exchanges yield value (i.e., worth) that is rewards—costs of the exchange. If the value is positive, the exchange is a desirable relationship; otherwise, exchange and the corresponding relationship are worthless.

In search of desirable exchanges, a few individuals may lack partners (e.g., remain bachelors) while others may plethora of potential neighbors from whom to choose. Economic networks as buyers and sellers of goods and services are well represented with exchange networks (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010). Sellers and buyers could create network nodes and links would model transactions among. Sellers who have exclusive buyers have power over the extent of their exclusivity. Sellers who are exclusive providers of scarce commodities have power over the extent of their exclusivity and scarcity of their goods (i.e., monopoly in economic terms). When buyers are scarce, they have higher power over the extent of their scarcity (i.e., monopsony in economic terms). In more general terms, dependence among agents derived from their relative position in the network gives rise to their structural power. There is a reciprocal relationship between potential power and dependence among agents (Emerson, 1962). Dependence, exclusion, and saturation are reported underlying determinants of structural power (Easley and Kleinberg, 2010).
