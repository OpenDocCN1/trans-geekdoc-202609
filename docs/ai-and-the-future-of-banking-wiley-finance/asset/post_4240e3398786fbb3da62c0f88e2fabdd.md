
Fitch Learning identifies what they describe as ‘Mission Critical’ attributes of a future leader. These are categorised into two elements: critical skills, and critical capabilities. Critical skills to be identified and developed are:

- Visioneering
- Strategic
- Ethical
- Purposefulness
- Analytical
- Pragmatism
- Agility
- Communication

Critical capabilities to be attained through structured learning are:

- The ability to inspire
- The ability to challenge
- The ability to activate

Beyond this, Fitch set out what they describe as a ‘Roadmap for Development’ over a 6-year period for the individual. It has three key phases:

- 1. Gaining tactical ability, developed over the first period of 0–24 months
- 2. Gaining strategic ability, developed over an interim period of 24–48 months
- 3. Gaining game-changing ability, developed over the ‘final’ period of 48–72 months

This recognises that even when a future leader has completed that 72-month (6-year) programme, the journey is not completed because there will always be a need for continuous review, especially to avoid complacency.

For those who might be concerned about this length of time, others have suggested that to attain mastery, it is necessary to study it for 10,000 hours, the equivalent of 10 years (referenced earlier). Some have pointed to the fact, however, that these views were based on a 1993 paper written by Karl Ericsson, who was a professor at the University of Colorado, called ‘The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance’, which dealt specifically with learning the violin. Others still remind us that it is the quality of the learning that is important, not the quantity.22

All training is about preparing the individual for the future, but in some cases the training is backward-looking rather than adequately forward-thinking. As a Chinese proverb suggests, ‘One should build the well before you are thirsty’. It should also be recognised that the concept of an AI-infused leader is not a destination but rather part of a journey. As AI and analytics increasingly affect industries and professions, so too will the way that our interface with AI continues to evolve.

Perhaps the challenge will be not how to prepare for AI-infused leadership but rather how individuals give direction to the use of AI and robotic systems instead of allowing the technology to direct us as humans. Humans are stakeholders in the future and therefore not only do we have a right and obligation to get involved in the debate but also a duty. These societal changes are too important to be left to technologists alone.

## ‘Digital Future of Banking Requires a New Leadership Model’

In his article of this name Jim Marous of The Financial Brand also makes the argument for needing to change our understanding of leadership in the banking industry to reflect changes in technology, new forms of competition and the changing nature of the customer base.²³

He suggests that not only do leaders need to be able to ‘articulate the vision’ but also importantly make certain that adequate and timely resources are made available to ensure that the vision can be realised. He references the results of a five-year study between MIT Sloan and Deloitte that consider whether traditional models of leadership are sufficient, whether an entirely new model of leadership is needed to respond to the digital age or whether (as the report concludes) the future requires a blend of the old and the new.²⁴

In an era of disruption, Marous concludes that future leaders must ‘embrace change, take risks, and disrupt themselves’. As for the leaders themselves, a survey of over 3000 by MIT/Sloan identified three key issues:²⁵

- 1. The single most important attribute leaders needed to have was that of a ‘transformative vision’ (22%). By ‘transformative’, respondents to the survey believed that leaders should have ‘the ability to anticipate markets and trends, make savvy business decisions and solve tough problems in turbulent times’.
- 2. The next highest was having a ‘forward-looking vision’ (20%), made more difficult by the need to manage business based on multiple possible scenarios they describe as ‘embracing ambidexterity’. In practical terms this means doing something differently with each hand simultaneously. In business terms, it refers to operating two business models at the same time with all the organisational tension that creates.²⁶
- 3. The third most important skill was identified as being ‘technologically savvy’ (18%) by having an understanding of the technological landscape. The 2019 report identified that ‘only a quarter of all board members fell within that category, and that digital literacy didn’t seem to exist above middle management level’.

MIT/Sloan identified four key activities for leaders in banking to enable them to become digitally aware:

- 1. Self-directed learning, at conferences or from digitally aware mentors, or both
- 2. Becoming ‘digital tourists’ by meeting with peers from more technologically advanced businesses
- 3. Having digital strategy ‘retreats’ that focus specifically on technology
- 4. Recruiting new leaders with strong digital skills

It’s important to recognise that this is not a one-off programme of activities but rather needs to be continuous to reflect the continuous evolution of technology in the current climate.

MIT/Sloan also sought to draw out the differences between what they described as traditional organisations when compared to digital ones, identifying the top differences as:

- The pace of the business
- The cultural mind set
- Flexibility, higher collaboration and transparency
- Higher levels of productivity
- Greater access to tools and relevant data
- Continuous connectivity

## Zen and Leadership in Banking

Ambidexterity in leadership requires a combination of ‘enhancing optimization and exploration simultaneously’ and also being able to embrace failure. The concept, which applies to managerial contradictions, was first introduced by the academic Robert Duncan in 1976 and since then it has surfaced in different areas:

- 1. Strategic management where it represents the problem of alignment versus adaptability
- 2. Operations management where it represents flexibility versus efficiency
- 3. Innovation management where it represents the challenge of radical versus incremental.

To do so, say MIT/Sloan authors, requires future leaders to have ‘emotional fortitude’. This is described as being a combination of tenacity, the ability to cope with multiple roadblocks and also the willingness to ‘let go of treasured projects’ if they are not providing appropriate results. Others might simply call this mental toughness.

They also draw out links with Zen Buddhism that allow leaders to identify possibilities when they don’t have all the answers. It’s tempting to expand on that particular element and wonder whether future leaders will need to have ‘Zen-like’ qualities in their approach to business. This isn’t a unique idea, insofar as there have been previous efforts to stretch what is in effect a religious ethic into business self-help (i.e. Zen and the Art of Work) and even into motorcycle maintenance.

Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance27 was first published in 1974 and has sold over 5 million copies. It sought to compare and contrast what the writer Pirsig described as the rational and the romantic. In the context of motorcycle maintenance, the ‘rational’ viewpoint was to understand the workings of the machine and to fully maintain it. On the other hand, the ‘romantic’ approach was simply to run the machine into the ground and then pay for a mechanic to repair it. The story suggests that the optimum situation is a combination of the two. From this co-existence of knowledge and being ‘in the moment’, it becomes possible to gain insights that are not normally possible and may not be rationally explained.
