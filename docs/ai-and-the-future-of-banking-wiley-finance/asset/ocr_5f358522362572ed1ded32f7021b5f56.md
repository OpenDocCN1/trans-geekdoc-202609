We should not overlook the issues of scale here. The larger banks, including Asian and Chinese banks, have customer bases measured in many millions of customers. In 2005 the largest Chinese bank, Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC), had 2.5m corporate customers and over 150m private customers.¹⁰ The bank (at the time of writing) now reports 567 million personal customers.¹¹

Albeit that there are greater controls around social media in some countries, this number of customers will not necessarily generate a directly proportionately huge amount of social media data. However, the potential remains for the creation of unstructured data relating to a particular individual banking business at an unprecedented scale. By comparison, in what might be described as the more local European theatre, the largest of the banks, HSBC, serves a ‘mere’ 39m customers globally, with this number being split into 66 countries and territories.

# Relationship Pricing

Relationship pricing is generally meant to represent a ‘framework’ where pricing is based on a customer’s overall purchasing activity, rather than on a product by product basis. It allows banks to set the price of a product or service based on the amount of overall business done with the bank by that client (the relationship) and as a result, uses customer-centric parameters to determine the price. It’s also an approach that banks use to provide a single point of contact for a service and to improve the loyalty of its customers.

With the majority of these services comprising loans, relationship pricing is principally the ability to ‘handcraft’ the terms of a loan for individual customers, usually commercial or higher-value customers. Increasingly the ability to quickly create such a bespoke offering, without undue referral, is viewed as a critical success factor.

In a personal banking situation, it is also often possible for a customer who has just taken a mortgage to negotiate the reduction or removal of fees for their current account.

Where the opening of new accounts is a KPI for bank sales executives, this can lead to doubtful practices. In the US, Wells Fargo adopted an aggressive (and punitive) programme for new accounts to be opened by relationship bankers between 2011 to 2016, only to subsequently discover that these new accounts had not been opened with the agreement of the customers involved. The total number of new accounts was estimated at 3.5m, and is a prime example of problematic KPIs and performance measures driving bad behaviours.¹²

Calculating a ‘relationship price’ requires the bank also to understand what is the ‘full opportunity cost’ of the relationship – that is, the potential benefits that might accrue in the event of the customer moving some or all their accounts over to the lending organisation. In effect, this is a cross-selling approach with attractive pricing intended to lure the customer into an increasing and deeper relationship. However, in the US the Bank Holding Company Act Amendments of 1970 prevent the banks from ‘contingent pricing’: making the cost of one service or product contingent on another.

It also requires them to take into account the degree of risk – ‘risk-based pricing’ – associated with the loan, such as the impact of collateral and guarantees.¹³
