
Social media not only exists to improve consumer sentiment and brand image, but also it is a key communication medium for the customer base. That means that the content is not only snappy but readily understandable on a small screen. This goes beyond ‘flat’ images and web shots but also to the use of short videos, and social media channels such as Instagram and Facebook. As we specifically consider the banking of the future, then it needs to be recognised that Millennials are likely to use social media as one of the primary channels, if not the main one.

For banks to effectively use social media, they need to be able to:

- Manage large amounts of data, and effectively use analytics to create actionable insight.
- Deal with this at speed, so that any insights generated are relevant.
- Understand the context of text, either relative to the individual (which requires a high degree of granularity) or to the peer group as a whole.
- Be able to communicate this effectively using multiple social media platforms.

There’s an array of tools typically used in social media analytics, including:

- Natural language processing, which enables computers to process and analyse large amounts of human-generated content
- Sentiment analysis, sometimes known as opinion mining, which allows computers to ‘identify, extract, quantify, and study affective states and subjective information’⁸
- Data mining, which is the process of discovering patterns in large data sets
- Social network analysis, which is the process of identifying social structures within the text, such as friendships or affinity groups
- Social media mining, which is the process of obtaining data from social media sites for the purpose of obtaining insights

The market offers a large number of software tools to enable these functions to be undertaken. The ultimate choice of vendor might depend on the available budget and the importance of the outcome required.

In banking specifically, sentiment analytics are also used to gain insight into trade movements in financial markets, sourcing organisations such as Bloomberg News, Facebook and Twitter, as well as PR releases from companies. Such insights not only provide better insight and help improve economic forecasting but can also be built into economic models to improve accuracy. Experts such as Cuemacro⁹ remind us that companies, including banks, have much to gain by looking at internal unstructured data such as internal emails and reports from business partners, as well as from publicly available content.

In the wider context, effective use of social media analytics by banks allows them to:

- Understand and influence consumer sentiment
- Contribute to more timely and relevant offerings
- Improve brand image and brand equity
- Provide a catalyst for more effective social interaction with the customer – for example, around complaints management

We should not overlook the issues of scale here. The larger banks, including Asian and Chinese banks, have customer bases measured in many millions of customers. In 2005 the largest Chinese bank, Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC), had 2.5m corporate customers and over 150m private customers.¹⁰ The bank (at the time of writing) now reports 567 million personal customers.¹¹

Albeit that there are greater controls around social media in some countries, this number of customers will not necessarily generate a directly proportionately huge amount of social media data. However, the potential remains for the creation of unstructured data relating to a particular individual banking business at an unprecedented scale. By comparison, in what might be described as the more local European theatre, the largest of the banks, HSBC, serves a ‘mere’ 39m customers globally, with this number being split into 66 countries and territories.

## Relationship Pricing

Relationship pricing is generally meant to represent a ‘framework’ where pricing is based on a customer’s overall purchasing activity, rather than on a product by product basis. It allows banks to set the price of a product or service based on the amount of overall business done with the bank by that client (the relationship) and as a result, uses customer-centric parameters to determine the price. It’s also an approach that banks use to provide a single point of contact for a service and to improve the loyalty of its customers.

With the majority of these services comprising loans, relationship pricing is principally the ability to ‘handcraft’ the terms of a loan for individual customers, usually commercial or higher-value customers. Increasingly the ability to quickly create such a bespoke offering, without undue referral, is viewed as a critical success factor.

In a personal banking situation, it is also often possible for a customer who has just taken a mortgage to negotiate the reduction or removal of fees for their current account.

Where the opening of new accounts is a KPI for bank sales executives, this can lead to doubtful practices. In the US, Wells Fargo adopted an aggressive (and punitive) programme for new accounts to be opened by relationship bankers between 2011 to 2016, only to subsequently discover that these new accounts had not been opened with the agreement of the customers involved. The total number of new accounts was estimated at 3.5m, and is a prime example of problematic KPIs and performance measures driving bad behaviours.¹²

Calculating a ‘relationship price’ requires the bank also to understand what is the ‘full opportunity cost’ of the relationship – that is, the potential benefits that might accrue in the event of the customer moving some or all their accounts over to the lending organisation. In effect, this is a cross-selling approach with attractive pricing intended to lure the customer into an increasing and deeper relationship. However, in the US the Bank Holding Company Act Amendments of 1970 prevent the banks from ‘contingent pricing’: making the cost of one service or product contingent on another.

It also requires them to take into account the degree of risk – ‘risk-based pricing’ – associated with the loan, such as the impact of collateral and guarantees.¹³

In terms of analytics, experts such as McKinsey recognise that a digital bank has a much greater chance of implementing such processes compared to a traditional ‘analogue bank’. Elsewhere, companies such as Zafin explain that an analytically based approach can help provide the necessary insights to allow more effective relationship pricing. Zafin’s approach is to use what they describe as a ‘Customer Relationship Score’, which uses algorithms to understand a customer’s behaviour, and what they call the customer’s ‘stickiness’. Their Chief Analytics Officer, Suman Singh, describes it as allowing the bank ‘to look systematically at customer experience from a strategic perspective of value and opportunity, and morph its interaction model to maximise customer satisfaction.’

Cutting through the jargon, it’s about running a FICO credit-scoring type of approach which aligns to the risk appetite of the lending bank, with the key components of the scoring system comprising:

- Current product ownership
- Payment preferences
- Product usage behaviour
- Customer interaction experience

The scoring model allows the bank to segment customers as being:

- Best in class
- Strong relationship
- Moderate relationship
- Weak relationship

Based on the Customer Relationship Score (CRS), it allows the bank not only to identify what products it may choose to offer, but also at what price.¹⁴

The idea of altering the price (aka ‘pricing elasticity’) is not new, and is one of a series of capabilities that sees price flex depend not only on supply and demand but also on the customer’s propensity to buy. The idea recognises that a relatively small change in price can have a large impact on demand. (A product is deemed to be ‘inelastic’ if a large change in price does not impact on demand.)

Pricing elasticity, or as it is sometimes known, ‘pricing optimisation’ is a common practice in other industries such as hotels and transport – for example, in the cost of flights. On the other hand, the approach has been criticised especially in the area of property insurance, where the cost is based not on the physical risk but on the customer’s behaviour. In 2015 the Florida Insurance Commissioners expressed concern that pricing optimisation was ‘discriminatory’.¹⁵

How the customer responds to pricing elasticity is thought to depend on several factors:

- The competitive environment, and the pricing of equivalent peer products and services
- The essential nature of the product, and if there are any ‘substitutes’
- The habitual nature of the purchase (for example, the purchase of alcohol)
- The loyalty of the customer base
- The impact of effective advertising
