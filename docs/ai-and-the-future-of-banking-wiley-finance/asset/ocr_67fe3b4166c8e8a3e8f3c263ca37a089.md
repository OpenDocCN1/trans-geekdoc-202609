Does a new concept of brand need some sort of flexibility within an agreed set of tramlines or parameters? A flexible approach to branding must be more than a willingness to change a logo or product package.

In the context of the Future of Banking and the impact of advanced analytics and AI, what might branding in the future entail? The impact of data, analytics and AI is considered to be disruptive but perhaps this will also lead to disruption in the brand proposition.

According to brand managers Fast Company, the future of branding will be that of ‘debranding’. They suggest that the smoothest branding, called ‘native branding’, was mainly to hide the commercial intention, aimed at creating a product familiarity and giving the brand the impression of reliability and being indispensable. Backlashes against branding – which often occur following overt product placement, for example – have increasingly led to subtler approaches, which they call ‘camouflage branding’. This is, in effect, subliminal messaging. Consumers, they say, are ‘are more likely to follow a happy, undemanding brand instead of bonding with real people and real-world problems. A brand will never ask you for help. It won’t confront you with difficulties or opposing views.’

‘Debranding’ effectively represents a backlash against consumerism. It is about the experience rather than the product, about people and about places rather than purchasing. It is said to be the ideal application for the networked age. ‘The brand that screams the loudest no longer commands the most attention; the one that offers something genuinely useful does’.

It’s a callout for quality, not quantity, for value rather than price. ‘The only information on packaging will be features such as origin, the intentions of the maker, the production process, and the environmental impact.’ Ultimately the argument is that the consumer no longer needs brands but quality, and that they will be able to find this quality more easily in a networked society.²⁰
