Elsewhere, other organisations are pointing to similar concerns. In their Cyber Readiness Report 2019 mentioned earlier, insurer Hiscox report a sharp increase in the number and cost of cyberattacks. In their study of more than 5,400 organisations across seven countries, more than three out of five firms (61%) reported one or more attacks in 2018, despite there being no significant reduction in their capability and readiness to respond. From a representative sample of businesses in the US, UK, France, Germany, Spain and the Netherlands, only 10% were scored high enough by Hiscox to rank as ‘experts’.

Amongst the key findings are:

+   - There was an increase in intensity and frequency of attacks: 61% reported in the 2019 report, compared to 45% in the 2018 report.
+   - More small- and medium-sized businesses (over 50 employees) are being attacked, reporting an increase in incidents from 33% to 47%. Amongst medium-sized firms (50–249 employees), the percentage of attacks between 2017 and 2018 has increased from 36% to 63%.
+   - Cyber losses have increased. The average cost of a cyberattack has increased from $229k in 2017, to $369k in 2018, an increase of 61%. For large firms (250+ employees), the average cost for cyber-related losses in 2018 was reported at $700k, compared to $162k in 2017.

The adoption of the GDPR regime has improved cyber security and people’s awareness of the problem, but there’s still progress to be made. A survey of British businesses by the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport has also found that:

+   - 74% of UK companies regard cyber security as a senior management issue.
+   - 73% of UK companies have yet to establish a formal policy on the matter.

The department also identified that in 2017, 43% of UK businesses and 20% of UK charities suffered a cyberattack or data breach.24 According to Ciaran Martin, CEO of the UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), ‘People often start out scared of cyber security as an issue. They think it’s too technical and an unmanageable risk’. As a result, the NCSC have created a Board Toolkit25 to help directors understand the dynamics of cyber risk management, setting out five key questions that they should ask their information security officers:

+   1. How do we defend against attacks?
+   2. How do we control the use of privileged IT accounts?
+   3. How do we keep our systems up to date?
+   4. How do we ensure that our partners and suppliers protect the data we share with them?
+   5. What authentication methods are being used to control access to data?
