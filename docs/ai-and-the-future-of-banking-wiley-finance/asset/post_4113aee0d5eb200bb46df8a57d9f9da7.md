## INTRODUCTION
Arguably there has never been a greater time to innovate. According to Ark Investments, ‘the global economy is undergoing the largest technological transformation in history’. The impact of innovation platforms such as AI, robotics and Blockchain (as well as energy storage and genome sequencing) is estimated to dwarf the historic impact even of steam, the telephone, automobile, the internet and electricity.

For individuals and organisations, this is the ‘Gold Rush’ of the modern age. Like the old prospectors of the Yukon, the secrets rest not only in where to dig for gold but how to sell and distribute it. As with the Gold Rush, there are profits to be made not only by the diggers themselves – many of whom failed and were reduced to austerity – but also by the intermediaries who rode on the wave of opportunity through the success of others. These intermediaries were key ‘enablers’ to the process, much in the same way that innovation hubs are offering intermediated and added-value services to start-ups. The size of valuations of companies such as WeWork as US $16.0 billion in May 2018, a US $6.0 billion increase in 9 months for what is, in effect, office rental space for some is startling but is, nevertheless, an indication as to how some place huge value on innovation.²

Innovation is a subject others have covered in the past and in its broadest sense it’s just about doing something new. ‘New’ may refer to something entirely new or just new to the sector. In fact, many innovations occur as a result of a transfer of ideas from one industry to another or from one geography to another. Innovation need not comprise a ‘eureka’ moment but may simply involve a logical consideration of an approach or technology operating successfully in one sector and, with an enquiring mind, wondering how it might apply to a different one.

Many organisations have a desire to be ‘first’ and bring new innovative ideas to the table, but there are many advantages to being second, subject to issues of intellectual property ownership. Second mover advantage occurs when an organisation looks at the ‘first mover’, in effect the original innovator, and not only improves on that innovation but also exercises or implements the innovation more effectively.

Typical advantages of being a ‘second mover’³ include these instances:
- The first mover has already established a market or opportunity.
- The development of the product is likely to be more robust.
- From analysis of the customer base, it’s possible to go to market more quickly and profitably.
- The supply chain can be optimised, unless the ‘first mover’ has obtained unique options through some form of agreement.

Examples of second mover advantage include names such as Amazon, Apple, Pampers and Facebook. In a period of innovation there may be a real temptation to wait, replicate and build on the success of others. There is a key decision to be made: Should one try to innovate as a first mover, or replicate as a second mover but with superior implementation?
