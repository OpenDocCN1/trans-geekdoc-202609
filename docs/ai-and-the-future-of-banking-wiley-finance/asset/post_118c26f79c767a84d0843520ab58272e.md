## The Conversational Bank

Conversational banking is about customers mainly removing human interaction from banking conversations, whether that relates to checking a balance, querying a transaction or developing a budget. ‘The concept is one of managing your financial life through voice or text’, says Keith Armstrong, co-founder of Abe, a chatbot that currently works through Slack (a cloud-based workflow), or SMS messaging to monitor income and expenses or to set a budget.34,35

It’s an interesting approach, using humans as the default or place of last resort in terms of consumer requirement. It seems to establish some form of hierarchy yet at the same time recognises that some of the queries are too complex for automated systems to cope with. It also reflects an evolution from mobile banking.

In their 2017 report ‘Ready to Talk’, Accenture refer to conversational banking as being a natural evolution that reflects the fact that many devices today and presumably tomorrow are capable of bi-directional voice interaction and refer to examples such as the Garanti Bank Mobile Assistant and Capital One’s connectivity through Amazon’s Alexa device.

Any move towards conversational banking requires a different set of technical skills with less dependence on mobile app developers, and greater dependence on language and semantic experts interlocked with advanced analytical capabilities.36

## The Collaborative Bank

Mergers and acquisitions in the banking sector have become familiar in many parts of the world, both to satisfy the demands of scale efficiencies and also to provide a platform for revenue growth where there are complementary territories or products (a term sometimes described as ‘inorganic growth’). Another advantage is that they reduce competition and M&A activity effectively removes one of the competitors. It’s described as a ‘horizontal merger’ because the merging entities are usually involved in the same kinds of commercial or business activities.

In many countries global banks are extending their operations with regional banks in those areas, helping the global players increase their dominance. M&A activity is controlled or ‘regulated’ by the ‘apex’ leading financial institution in the country, for example, the Reserve Bank of India.37

There can also be a downside to M&A activity. Job losses may occur, which may be economically or politically unacceptable, and there may also be major challenges in integrating legacy technologies. On the other hand, general deregulation and market liberalisation remain as market factors in the structural changes in the marketplace. There is a fourth model to consider, that of a more collaborative banking system. (See Figure 8.19.) In many cases improved vertical collaboration along the supply chain is a precursor to acquisition activities as banks acquire new technological capabilities to ensure differentiation. The advent of Open Banking is suggested as also providing an eventual catalyst for greater collaboration.

Although the term ‘collaboration’ is often used along the supply chain between the bank as client and a vendor (which may be a technology company but could quite easily be a distributor), the prospect of ‘horizontal collaboration’ between banks themselves should not be ruled out. Whilst operational optimisation is a factor, the customer experience should not be overlooked. For many retail consumers, the banking branch network provides a useful social function where local charities and communities can make cash deposits. Also, in areas where social deprivation or generational issues prevents (or slows down) a move towards a cashless society, the traditional bank has an important role to play. In 2018 the total number of bank branches in the UK had fallen to 7,207, from 11,365 in 2007.

## TRANSFORMATION OF THE INVESTMENT BANK

In their very good and comprehensive report ‘Killing the I Bank: The Disruption of Investment Banking’,³⁹ authors CB Insights recognise that investment banks are also going through a disruptive transformation, which they describe as being ‘eroded by technology and regulations’.

They view the catalyst for change as being due to:

- The increased complexity of financial instruments being used
- A knock-on effect of the collapse of the largest of the banks, Lehman and Bear Stearns
- The financial impact that followed the collapses

US regulations subsequently forced investment banks to trade with their own capital and to keep that capital at hand. Consequently, investment banks suffered reduced profits, leading to rationalisation and cost cutting. Beyond this, new technologies are increasingly automating traditional processes that historically were relationship-driven, such as those that automatically match companies to buyers.

CB Insights identify what they consider to be five key disruptive factors (see Figure 8.20):

1. **Disruption of the IPO:** Top companies seeking to raise capital are no longer going via the traditional investment bank route, which connects them with investors, helps determine prices and creates legal frameworks. Rather, they are dealing directly with venture capitalists and seeking alternative solutions such as ‘direct public offerings’ (DPO). The companies that follow the traditional route by using investment banks are using these alternatives to negotiate lower fees, leading to lost revenue on the part of the bank. Goldman Sachs, for example, identified that about half of the 127 IPO steps could be done by computers, reducing labour costs, increasing speed and maintaining profitability.
2. **Disruption of M&A:** With M&A activity accounting for ‘about 34% of bank revenue’ according to Dealogic, the emergence of new specialist ‘boutique’ banks and the impact of technology is affecting not only the topline figures but also fee levels. New technologies are allowing companies to undertake ‘DIY M&A’ where buyers are algorithmically matched with sellers, which avoids the research and relationship-driven activities normally associated with investment banks.
3. **Disruption of Asset Management:** There is a gradual move away from investment bankers to specialist asset managers. This is as a result of increased regulation and the ability of the specialists to provide greater returns at lower fees. As a result, larger investment houses are refocussing their activities on wealth management. There is more widespread use of robo-advisors and greater automation will inevitably become more commonplace.

Goldman Sachs, for example, have launched their digital advisor for savings, known as ‘Marcus’, which actively focusses on the millennial market. Notwithstanding, a recent 2019 survey by GlobalData says, ‘Only 5% of UK millennial investors use robo-advice services, as human expertise is still preferred over AI technology.’⁴⁰ That’s not to say that this level of interest and engagement is consistent in all geographies, but it does give an indication. The argument is that ‘traditional wealth managers have experienced multiple recessions and periods of market volatility, proving they are able to weather a storm and with a potential market downturn on the horizon, [but] robo-advisors may not be ready to tackle the risks to client investments that come with it.’

Sergel Woldemichael, Wealth Management analyst at GlobalData, suggests, ‘Pigeon-holing the next generation into a digital chamber is the wrong way to view millennials’ and calls for a hybrid model. In other words, this ‘hybrid’ is a human face with a robotic ‘back office’.
4. **The Disruption of Equities Research:** When MiFID II banned the ‘bundling of research and trade execution’, requiring them to be priced and sold separately, this rendered this traditional function within investment banks redundant.
