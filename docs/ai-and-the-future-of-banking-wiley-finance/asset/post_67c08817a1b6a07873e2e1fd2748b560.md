
Another key element is that of risk assessment, typically to help individuals or organisations understand their propensity for risk and ensure that suitable investments are made that align to that assessment. Risk attitude is often considered to be linked more to behavioural attributes than to financial status. AI is seen as a better method of measuring this by incorporating societal, behavioural and attitudinal factors, and it may be that many of the same metrics used in 360-degree customer assessment (Chapter 4) might also be reused. There are also broader issues to consider from other sectors when evaluating risk. The judicial system has previously attempted to use algorithms to predict the likelihood of offenders becoming repeat offenders but apparently with limited success, so there needs to be a degree of prudence in using analytics to assess the risk appetite of individuals.

Finally, in considering risk assessment, banks should recognise that an individual’s risk appetite can change over time, and this might influence their investment appetite. Currently risk assessment seems relatively static as a process, yet human behaviours and attitudes are dynamic in nature. The future of wealth management may well be a much more dynamic model than currently exists.

## THE BIOMETRIC MORAL ARGUMENT

At face value, it seems that biometrics must have a critical part to play in the banking industry. Although this book is about AI and banking, access to the banking system is a critical component. In other words, the future of AI in banking will be biometrically infused.

Potential applications for the sector include:²⁹

- Branch banking where visiting customers can be authenticated at the counter by iris, fingerprint or palm scanners, or a combination of any or all of these
- ATM authentication through fingerprint scanners
- Multifactor biometrics, namely voice and location, for example, for internet banking and more substantial transactions
- Use of biometrics such as voice or facial recognition for mobile banking

Some European corporate organisations already use biometrics to allow their employees to gain access to the premises. To what degree might we as individuals have different personal ‘rules’ relative to our access to the workplace, compared to access to our personal bank account?

The challenge overall then is that of how quickly this might be taken on board by the consumer. There are market maturity issues to contend with. Biometrics experts M2Sys reported:

> Many banks worldwide are already using biometrics with their banking systems to authenticate employees and customers and among all banks utilizing biometrics, 52% are located in Asia.

> Japan has more than an estimated 15 million customers using biometric authentication for banking transactions.

Banks in Mexico, South America, Africa, and the Middle East are also moving towards the use of biometric identification technology because of its popularity with consumers, and its ability to offer more security than traditional personal identification numbers (PINs) and passwords.

Elsewhere the Chinese biometric market has experienced double-digit growth between 2014 and 2019. Part of the reason is government intervention and security issues, but also a general trend towards increased usage. In 2014, almost a decade ago, more than 20 commercial banks, 130 regional commercial banks and 200-plus foreign banks were using biometrics. The rate of acceptance in that particular region seems in many ways to be linked to a more engaged, ubiquitous use of the technology, including:

- Government intervention where biodata is required to be added to passports and national ID cards for quick and easy identification
- Cross-border travel where the weight of numbers especially at national holidays forces the use of new innovative technologies
- Security and identification at gaming festivals
- Coal mining to keep track of workers in this volatile and at times risky working environment
- Healthcare, especially for patient safety but also to provide supply-chain verification in issues of blood supply, for example³⁰

With increased progress in this area principally in Asian and growth markets, what is preventing Western and North American customers from moving to a more biometrically oriented approach to identification?

- Perhaps we think our identities are fundamentally private, and that biometrics steps over the line between the data that we, as individuals, are prepared to share and have stored.
- Possibly it just feels too Orwellian and from the realms of science fiction.

There are, however, very practical issues to contend with. In order to comply with Europe’s Payment Services Directive 2 (PSD2), banks in the EU need to provide multi-factor identification. There is a need for a second factor, but banks are still increasingly looking to traditional methods such as passwords, security questions and card readers. The use of a biometric solution alone will not suffice, but could form part of the identification equation.

This may be principally a generational issue. What will it be that starts to compel consumers to be prepared to engage with biometrics, and why have the other markets moved so quickly into the biometric space? It’s likely that government intervention is one dominant driver, but there may simply be stronger direction at a macro level towards the use of this type of advanced technology. This may be driven in part by the sheer weight of numbers in certain markets and also the diversity of the population, but also the increasing acceptance of new technologies in that region and their ability to rapidly implement new approaches.

## CONCLUSION

The banking sector is increasingly data-driven and for that reason is especially likely to suffer disruption as a result of technology, robotics and AI. Over 75% of banking functions can be automated, say experts, and a single robot (whatever that is) can replace 8 humans.

The indicators are that robotic systems can operate on a 24/7 basis, are relatively low maintenance, are more flexible to change, and, some might argue, constitute a better investment than people training. The potential, therefore, is not only to transform the banking industry but to radically reform it.

There are, however, additional risks or societal impacts that may emerge:31

- Rogue algorithms, which can create distortion
- Unconscious bias emerging in those who build the systems
- Contagion where systems respond to meet market conditions, which might prove to be incorrect
- Cyber attack made especially worse by interconnectivity between systems

In their useful report ‘How artificial intelligence is transforming the financial ecosystem: The new physics of financial services’,32 Deloitte, together with the World Economic Forum, aimed to cut through the *sensationalism* of the AI debate, suggesting nine key findings, including:

- Back-office services could become profit centres, not cost centres, as shared services become increasingly common.
- Customer loyalty will be transformed by added-value services provided by financial organisations, as banks move away from commoditised offerings.
- AI will increasingly automate the finances of customers, and enhance self-service.
- Collaborative solutions will emerge that will pool data and information, and include ‘safety’ in the financial services sector.
- Strategic partnerships will increase in importance but ‘be fraught with strategic and operational risk’.
- Regulations related to data privacy will start to shape what banks and financial services can offer.

This book was never intended to give a complete understanding of how AI works, nor does it seek to report on all the different types of elements and applications that might contribute to some form of advanced analytical system or machine learning in the banking sector. Rather, it is intended to give some insight into the depth of capabilities that have evolved to date and are presently evolving, and the potential implications.

Beyond this, it’s important to recognise the tangential impacts of these technological changes on the way we will live. Whilst the business case for biometrics in banking might be clear, the wider impact of biometrics on society is less so. So too is the case with the use of AI.
