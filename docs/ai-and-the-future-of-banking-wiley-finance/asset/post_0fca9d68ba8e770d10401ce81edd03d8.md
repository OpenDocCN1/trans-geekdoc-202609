
In 2009, Mossiman, Dussault³ and others identified the issues attaching to the Office of Finance:

- A lack of information to regulate what has happened and what will happen
- The relevance, visibility and credibility of financial information
- The need for balance between short and long term, detailed focus and the big picture
- Finding a path between top down vision and bottom up circumstances

They recognised the challenge of multiple decision makers within the organisation, often holding budgets and more importantly carrying accountability for delivery; the balance between cash flow versus working capital; decisions regarding capex versus opex; and the management of the treasury. Not much of that world has moved on over the past ten years.

In the same way that the IT department has needed to change, and perhaps in some aspects has been slightly marginalised in favour of the line of business decision-maker, the role of the Office of Finance is also going through change, especially as more advanced analytical methods become both available and more affordable. This change will continue to happen as AI systems increasingly become embedded into the organisation, automating many of the traditionally routine tasks through a process known as RPA (Robotic Process Automation). Financial decisions are increasingly being placed in the hands of the line of business, as systems replace centralised control. Big data has brought with it ‘big empowerment’, and also what we might describe as ‘big accountability’. At an operational level, the Office of Finance inevitably needs to justify their decision to withhold funding or restrict budgets, for example, more than ever before.

The rules of engagement have also changed as well, especially around the use of analytic capabilities. Line of business executives and staff with specialist needs are increasingly able to identify tools that meet their particular operational needs. Technology vendors are making their tools free of charge (‘freemium’) in an effort to get engagement. Self-service identification of analytic tooling is dangerously becoming the norm and it’s important that the adoption of these ‘tools’ is managed carefully.

The expression ‘dangerous’ is not used lightly, in that multiple tactical purchases potentially lead to a disjointed outcome. The banking organisation is broad and complex, and requires standardisation to ensure alignment, although new ‘tools’ are emerging that help ‘glue’ disparate systems together. Independent decision making and choice of technology can lead to tooling for specific needs but this may be contrary to the greater good of the organisation. Fragmented purchasing cuts across everything that procurement hopes to achieve – standardisation, price leverage, management of upgrades, integration of tooling and strategic procurement rather than a series of tactical relationships that may be more difficult to manage and maintain.

## Performance Management and Integrated Decision Making

One of the key tenets of analytics is that there is a *single version of the truth*. What this means in technological terms is that all information placed before end users is derived from the same data. Gone are the days, in theory at least, of disagreement around the numbers (albeit there may still be scope for a degree of interpretation). It is sometimes in these grey ‘interpretative’ areas that disputes occur, but nevertheless it is a step forward to think in terms of all information being reliable because it comes from the same source.

To do this, reliable forms of measurement must also be used with an understanding that the same data will be used not only to manage the business, but also to meet regulatory requirements.

It’s also helpful to bear in mind that different industries and similarly different parts of the financial services sector are at varying levels of maturity, with banking, for example, having a greater level of maturity than insurance. Departments responsible for asset and liability management have extremely high levels of analytical insight. Analytical tools are used in what is a niche area and whilst some of these may not be obviously transferable across the enterprise, a culture of data-driven decision making may well be.

Movement of individuals across departments and also industries is likely in time to have a levelling effect. Whilst some industries and departments are more advanced than others, there will nevertheless be some degree of knowledge and capability transfer as well as some cultural resonance.

Retail bankers might reasonably take a lead from the Consumer and Retail Goods (CRG) sector. Invariably consumer retailers have their finger on the pulse in terms of what is selling and what is not, and what level of discount needs to be offered to encourage the customer. Customer behaviour management is as much a financial issue as it is a marketing and psychological issue and to do this effectively requires absolute and indisputable agreement about issues such as revenue, profitability and growth, and how they are linked to product, channel and reputation.

Bankers would be well advised to look outside their peer groups to find a benchmark of progress. It is clear that in terms of customer analytics, retailer and telecom companies are far ahead of bankers in terms of their analysis of buying behaviours but that gap is rapidly closing. With highly analytical companies such as Amazon entering the banking space, it’s critical that traditional banks look and learn – and do it very quickly.⁴

> *A true anecdote: A room full of bankers and insurers listened avidly to a High Street retailer describing how, through loyalty cards, they understood the customers’ buying behaviour with absolute granularity and could not only pitch an attractive offer, but anticipate where and when that offer should be made. The audience were especially interested in one specific question, ‘What is the banking equivalent to loyalty cards?’*

## The Key Elements of Banking Performance Management

The banking business performance management process comprises a series of six key tenets, typically:

- 1. **Understanding the Business Drivers:** That is, the need to understand the key business and performance drivers. A business driver is ‘a resource, process or condition that is vital for the continued success and growth of a business.’⁵

Effectively managing the key business and performance drivers translates into effectively managing performance. Studying past performance can help banks understand the key reasons for success and from this, to allow key performance indicators to be prepared. In banking specifically, some of the key drivers are customer assets and liabilities, and their corresponding interest margins and profitability. It’s then possible to use techniques such as matched-funds transfer pricing to drive growth in particular areas. (Matched-fund transfer is an approach to understand how specific funds are adding to the profitability of the bank, and can assist in product development, branch optimisation and judging the efficiency of processes.)⁶

- 2. **Use of Rolling Forecasts:** A rolling forecast is a financial model which, unlike static budgeting models, manages the performance of a business over a continuous period. It operates on an add/drop process where months are added/dropped as the year progresses, and has the main benefit of helping businesses operating in a fluid and sometimes volatile business environment. For example, moving from a traditional balance sheet approach to a rolling forecast can help banks swiftly and effectively understand the impact of a change in central bank rates, and what effect this might have on the business plan.

- 3. **Reporting and Visualisation:** Effective use of dashboarding and visualisation as a method of communicating data-driven information and insights, together with drilldown capability, helps users rapidly understand what is happening at a product, branch and distribution level. The integration of bi-directional location analytics also helps banks (and others) understand what is happening with a geographical perspective.

- 4. **Democratisation of Information:** This expression implies that unambiguous ‘trusted’ information is shared across the enterprise, with appropriate permissions and levels of authority, so that business users have timely access to relevant information. In this way, quicker and better decisions can be made.

- 5. **Planning tools:** The most effective planning tools and processes enable effective linkage between departments, lines of business and the enterprise. This eliminates independently initiated manual operations, and helps provide integration between development and execution of strategy, business planning and, ultimately, business execution. Relative to banking, in particular, calculations such as consolidation of business units, transfer pricing between business units, calculation of funds transfer pricing and interest margins can be both automated and driver-based. This eliminates manual errors and time-consuming reconciliation processes.

Effective planning tools also include workflow management to aid the planning administrator in managing deadlines, especially where there is a high-participation planning process.

- 6. **Effective Analytical Tools:** These analytical processes and tools help explain variance against plans (such as Variance Analysis, aka ‘ANOVA’); tools that test ‘what if’ hypotheses and ‘recover’ scenarios; and others like predictive analytics – all of which ultimately improve decision making.⁷
