
In other words, it’s a process that helps remove duplicate data, creates standardisation and creates business rules that prevent ‘rogue data’ from entering the system. As a technological process or function it is also evolving.

There are considered to be three ‘ages’ of MDM:

1. First-Generation MDM focused on data quality and governance. This allowed businesses to consolidate information from customers, products or financial data from multiple systems, and was especially useful where there had been mergers and acquisitions.
2. Second-Generation MDM reflected the evolution of systems by placing greater emphasis on the customer, and especially relating to omnichannel strategies by providing real-time visibility into multiple channel activity.
3. Third-Generation MDM reflects the introduction of automation and new systems, with users increasingly pressing for exchange of information across multiple ecosystems. In doing so, they are demanding greater flexibility, data virtualisation, the ability to retrieve data without requiring technical information about the type of data (such as how it is formatted) and data syndication (information from third parties, such as consumer product or goods manufacturers).

According to Forrester’s report,⁹ the two major initiatives in third-generation MDM are:

1. **Prioritising agility over compliance**: Whilst recognising that compliance, including data protection, is a crucial component, companies also want to provide a personalised experience to the customer in an agile manner that reduces the ‘time to value’ (TTV). ‘Time to value’ is a metric usually defined as the amount of time it takes for a customer to realise value from a product or service.
2. **Forming the cornerstone of digital change**: MDM is increasingly being recognised as what is described as ‘the centrepiece of flexibility’, allowing companies to gain deeper insight into customers, products and channels.

In terms of the AI agenda, companies such as Informatica, who are identified as a leader in Forrester’s report, aim to use AI to ‘simplify MDM integration, provide matching, discover relationships or automate rules-based data quality’.

## The Hierarchy of Analytics

Analytics, or the analysis of data, is generally recognised as the key by which data insights are obtained. Put another way, analytics unlocks the ‘value’ of the data.

There is a recognised hierarchy of analytics (see Figure 3.6):

- **Descriptive Analytics** serves simply to report on what has happened or what is happening. In banking, this might relate to the performance of a bank branch, a product or a service for a given date or period. Descriptive analytics is also often known by the expressions ‘business intelligence’ or ‘management information’.

**FIGURE 3.6** Hierarchy of Analytical Capability

- **Predictive Analytics** seeks to make predictions on the balance of probabilities, that is, what is likely to happen next. An example of this is the projection of sales and branch performance, enabling the bank to take a view as to what corrective campaign action might be needed.
- **Prescriptive Analytics** not only anticipates what will happen next but what should be done about it, usually through some form of rules-based system. It is called ‘prescriptive analytics’ on the basis that it ‘prescribes’ (or suggests) a course of action. One example of this might be the activities happening within a contact or service centre. Commonly also known as ‘next best action’, which might be better expressed as ‘best next action’, it provides the contact centre agent with, for example, insight to help position the best next proposed offering to help to close a deal.

Predictive analytics and prescriptive analytics are probabilistic in nature, in that there is no absolute certainty as to the predicted outcome, but rather the algorithms anticipate the most likely outcome based on the information available. It follows that the more data there is, and the better the data, then the likelihood of accurate prediction and more effective prescription will occur.

Statisticians will be aware of the notion of the ‘false positive’. There are different meanings for this term dependent on the industry application. Generally, it’s taken to mean that of an incorrect outcome (or analysis) based on information that results in a ‘positive result’ when in fact a negative result should have been more correct. It’s a statistical anomaly and is often seen in filtering out junk mail, or in fraud analysis where an innocent transaction is identified as being ‘doubtful’.

In all these cases there is an increasing quality of visualisation either in the form of dashboards, advanced graphics or some type of graphical mapping. Such visualisations are increasingly important as a tool to help users understand the complexities of greater data, and to allow quicker decisions to be made. However, judgments based on the appearance of a dashboard are no substitute for the power of an analysis sitting ‘underneath’ the dashboard.

Overall, it is important to emphasise that analytics is not the destination but rather what is done with the analytics, and that analysis should be viewed in that spirit. Analysis provides a means to an end, by changing processes and practices, and in doing so can create a combination of hard and/or soft benefits. These benefits may comprise:

- Higher levels of customer satisfaction leading to higher levels of retention or cross selling/upselling
- Greater operational efficiencies
- Better risk management
- More effective investment policies

Insight provided from analytics should not only feed in to the development of best practices but also to manual and automatic decisioning, and ultimately to strategic and operational judgments. Overall the analytical process should not sit in isolation to the wider business but rather must be an integral part of the organisation, one we might describe as an ‘analytical enterprise’.

## 'Next-Generation' Cognitive Analytics

'Next-generation' analytics is increasingly likely to be ‘cognitive’ in nature, not only by providing probabilistic insight based on some degree of machine learning but also with a more natural human interface (as opposed to requiring machine coding). Cognitive analytics is a stepping stone towards ‘full-blown’ AI. However, ‘cognitive’ is not ‘artificial intelligence’ or ‘AI’ out of the mold of HAL in Kubrick’s 2001: A Space Odyssey but rather represents a different relationship between the computer and the user.

There’s already a great deal of confusion relative to the use of the terms ‘cognitive computing’ and ‘artificial intelligence’. It’s important not to be too churlish about these definitions. After all, the man and woman in the street often describe the ability of a system that makes predictions such as choice of music or viewing as a form of AI.

Purists argue that they are different; generalists say that they are more or less the same. The concept of ‘cognition’ implies that the computing platform in some way replicates the operation of the human brain by combining different technical capabilities such as language processing, visual recognition, sophisticated algorithms and speech. The net outcome of this combination of capabilities is that the system ultimately ‘assists’ human decision making, rather than replaces it. By comparison, true ‘AI’ completely does away with the need for human intervention and for that reason sits at the very peak of the ‘hierarchy of analytics’.

Cognitive analytics will have a major part to play in the banking challenges of skill shortages and the so-called demographic explosion.

Forms of cognitive computing are already being used in healthcare and asset management, in the context of ‘wealth advisors’, and it is only a matter of time before it finds its way into mainstream retail banking activities.
