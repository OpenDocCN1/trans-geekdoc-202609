
- 5. Time remaining until maturity of the option
- 6. Risk-free interest rate

Statisticians will also be concerned about the concept of ‘fat tails’ – that is, the situation in a ‘bell curve’ where there is a ‘skew’ in the normal statistical distribution. Also known as ‘heavy tails’, the phenomenon is also seen in physics and earth sciences as well as in economics. Black-Scholes is based on a normal distribution, but if there is a probabilistic skew, this can lead to distortions in the prediction. Therefore, fat tails are viewed to create higher levels of risk in terms of the accuracy of the prediction. As to when these fat tails occur, it’s suggested that at times of financial catastrophe such as the ‘bursting’ of the dot.com bubble, the data is ‘not well behaved’.³²

Because of this ‘bad behaviour’, statisticians such as Nassim Taleb wrote about the difficulty of prediction in his book *The Black Swan* (described as one of the 12 most influential books since World War II). His argument is that risk is very difficult to predict, and suggests that there is no real point in investing in what is a ‘medium risk’ – conservative investors should invest 80%–90% in extremely safe instruments, with the balance in high risk. Alternatively, high-risk investors should invest 80%–90% in high risk, and the balance in a potential fallback. He describes this as being ‘hyper conservative’ or ‘hyper aggressive’.

In the second edition of *The Black Swan*, Taleb writes, ‘One of the most common (but useless) comments I hear is that some solutions can come from “robust statistics”. I wonder how using these techniques can create information where there is none’.³³

It’s a daunting reaction to the idea that, using AI and machine learning, we can increasingly automate the investment process. Notwithstanding, UBS are already investigating ways to eliminate bias from the performance of portfolio managers and to automate some processes.³⁴

## Derivative Markets

Specifically in derivative markets, most of the ‘market making’ is done by dealers who undertake a series of ‘swaps’ either of interest rates or currency, which together with ‘hedging’ creates an effective and profitable portfolio.³⁵

It’s suggested that much of this can be done intuitively, but in a complex portfolio this is much too difficult, and the dealer will use a series of portfolio management techniques which are known as ‘Greek options’, or ‘Greeks’. These are a series of options, or predictions, which ‘capture the sensitivities of an option value to market parameters such as time, interest rate and volatility’. They are called ‘Greeks’ because the sensitivities of an option to various market parameters are labelled with letters from the Greek alphabet, such as delta, gamma and vega.³⁶

Beyond this complexity, investment managers also use a technique called ‘Value at Risk’, or VaR, which put simply is an assessment of the degree of confidence in a worst-case scenario. It’s a complex area and when pushed to the limits is known as ‘stress testing’. In a complex portfolio, ‘stress testing’ is said to reveal ‘risk holes’, or excessively risky exposures to an ‘implied volatility’ – that is, a mathematical assessment of volatility based on the characteristics of the exposure such as the ‘spot price’ (current market price) and interest rates, for example.³⁷

These are enormously complex areas, and are sufficient to have whole books written on the subject rather than a passing part of a chapter. The definitive book in this area is possibly *Investment Analytics: Revolutionizing Professional Investment with Artificial Intelligence, Big Data, and Cloud Computing*³⁸ by Bernard Lee, but there is no shortage of other reading matter. In his book *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*, Marcos Lopez de Prado postulates, ‘what if economists started to consider non-linear regression’, and how ‘to deal with 100 variables, instead of 3’.³⁹

Lopez de Prado also refers to an ‘industrial chain’ to the process, comprising:

- Data sources and data platforms
- Feature analysis: by researchers or automatically generated
- Strategy development: using manual model selection or through automated machine learning
- Backtesting: both sequential and randomised
- Deployment of outcome, in an automatic deployment way
- Portfolio oversight: both in real time (live) or post audit

Of these it is suggested that it is only the first (*data sources and platforms*) that cannot be automated – that is to say, cannot be the subject of AI-infusion.

This point is reinforced by Yves Hipschich, who rather novelly introduces the concept of AI in finance under the moniker ‘finqince’ (sic).⁴⁰ He also suggests the role of AI in trading principally comprises two key elements:

- 1. Prediction engines, which embed statistical methods and technical rules in an operating environment of machine and deep learning
- 2. Decision rules, which set the transactional framework for the system, comprising entry rules, stop loss levels and position sizing for example

## CONCLUSION

In this chapter, different types of the use of analytics has been considered but, in the nature of the breadth and depth of the subject, there are areas that command deeper investigation.

The increasing push for hyper-customer centricity at scale can only lend itself to increased automation, robotics and AI. Understanding the customer is only one part of the equation, with the most important part (for the bank) being what to do next. With retention becoming a bigger issue with open banking, knowing what the customer wants, or will do next, becomes especially important.

Elsewhere, in trading, for example, the challenge (or opportunity) for better cross-asset trading optimisation and portfolio management requires deep insight, which is fundamentally analytical in nature, and ultimately gives high levels of insight into markets and pricing, and informs trading strategies. One interesting element of the discussion is whether that ‘deep insight’ need be ‘human orientated’ or will in time be replaced even in part by robotic solutions or AI. Others suggest that the market is just too complex to be ‘analysed’ accurately.

Specifically considering wealth management, for example, the opportunity for the use of better, more advanced analytics and even robo-advisors to help optimise portfolios is highly probable. This involves the optimisation of portfolios, including alerting, greater insights into customer acquisition and ultimately the use of enhanced financial advisor solutions to add yet further value to the customer value proposition.

Increasingly complex issues of risk and compliance seem to demand greater automation. This area especially reflects the growing convergence between data, analytics, compliance and supervision. In looking at this from an angle of operational effectiveness, banks need to think about the degree that the process can be automated, which is a large step on the road to the use of AI.

There are some esoteric issues to consider with respect to the use of analytics in these very deep and complex areas, especially as we consider the increased use of automation in investment banking, for example. It requires some imagination but let’s consider that, with machine learning and various AI applications, the important algorithms have evolved to a point that any improvement only gives marginal (if any) benefit. As a result, investment managers might see no competitive advantage in the algorithms used, whether they are built in-house or purchased off the shelf. In fact, there might be no incentive to even try to build these capabilities internally as the cost of upgrade and maintenance will become too prohibitive. We enter into a world of ‘investment software as a service’.

Speed of transactions has already been recognised as a competitive differentiator, with transactions undertaken in micro-seconds. Perhaps with time speed will cease to be, or at worst become less of a differentiator. All investors will refer to and analyse the same sources of information: Bloomberg News, Twitter or whatever their future iterations will look like or be named. Larger institutions will have the advantage of being able to look inside the enterprise, with internal data perhaps giving some additional insight (although it’s not obviously apparent what that internal additional ‘valuable’ info might be).

So where then will the competitive element rest? Surely one of the key differentiating factors will be new forms and sources of data, probably from different and previously unconsidered sectors. Maybe it will be data that is deliberately sought, through new or different devices, or in different formats.

These new data might comprise:

- Internal data to the organisation itself, in which case larger organisations appear fundamentally to be at a competitive advantage
- New sources of data, data that already exists but hasn’t yet been ‘mined’
- Historically unstructured data, which has now, somehow, been sorted
- New types of data that haven’t yet been collected but were it to be so, it would add to the mix and provide deeper insight
