This all starts to sound like gaming technology and certainly there is increasing cross-over of techniques.¹⁰

For practitioners there are already many data visualisation apps accessible on the market. Many of those available, such as Shiny, provide tutorials. The foundational technical requirement in Shiny is the ability to use R as a coding language. (R is a programming language and free software mainly used for statistical computing and graphics, and supported by the R Foundation for Statistical Learning. It's an extension of the S programming language, which itself was based on FORTRAN in the 1970s–'80s, and on which this author ‘cut his teeth’).¹¹

Other meaningful visualisation products are available, such as Visio and Microsoft Power BI, to name but two companies in the field. There's a tremendous variation in capability and cost. According to one reviewer:

> At the higher end are tools that can change visualizations on the fly in the same way that outputs from sophisticated algorithms change after repeated direct querying of real-time data (i.e., streaming data) and across multiple data sources.

> The tools occupying the middle of the spectrum do not represent real-time data but still produce visualizations from advanced analytics outputs.¹²

One of the most important capabilities is often thought to be bi-directional visualisation. What this means is that it is possible through integrated technologies to create effective visualisations from the data and also to click into the visualisation to reveal the data set that it is formed from.

# Voice Recognition and Voice Assistants

Voice recognition is a process by which systems and software are used to ‘identify, distinguish and authenticate’ the voice of an individual speaker.¹³ It’s also known as speech or speaker recognition. It evaluates what are the ‘speech biometrics’ of an individual including speed, intonation and accent. (See Figure 5.13.)

Speech as a topic is a complex thing and is represented by the study of linguistics. It also includes the study of phonemes, or small pieces of language. The word phoneme itself is recognizable from its association with ‘phonetics’ and derives from the ancient Greek φώνημα (phōnēma), ‘sound made, utterance, thing spoken, speech and language’.¹⁴
