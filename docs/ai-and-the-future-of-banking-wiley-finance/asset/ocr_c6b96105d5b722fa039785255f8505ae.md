Fitch Learning identify what they describe as ‘Mission Critical’ attributes of a future leader. These are categorised into two elements: critical skills, and critical capabilities. Critical skills to be identified and developed are:

+   - Visioneering
+   - Strategic
+   - Ethical
+   - Purposefulness
+   - Analytical
+   - Pragmatism
+   - Agility
+   - Communication

Critical capabilities to be attained through structured learning are:

+   - The ability to inspire
+   - The ability to challenge
+   - The ability to activate

Beyond this, Fitch set out what they describe as a ‘Roadmap for Development’ over a 6-year period for the individual. It has three key phases:

+   1. Gaining tactical ability, developed over the first period of 0–24 months
+   2. Gaining strategic ability, developed over an interim period of 24–48 months
+   3. Gaining game-changing ability, developed over the ‘final’ period of 48–72 months

This recognises that even when a future leader has completed that 72-month (6-year) programme, the journey is not completed because there will always be a need for continuous review, especially to avoid complacency.

For those who might be concerned about this length of time, others have suggested that to attain mastery, it is necessary to study it for 10,000 hours, the equivalent of 10 years (referenced earlier). Some have pointed to the fact, however, that these views were based on a 1993 paper written by Karl Ericsson, who was a professor at the University of Colorado, called ‘The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance’, which dealt specifically with learning the violin. Others still remind us that it is the quality of the learning that is important, not the quantity.22

All training is about preparing the individual for the future, but in some cases the training is backward-looking rather than adequately forward-thinking. As a Chinese proverb suggests, ‘One should build the well before you are thirsty’. It should also be recognised that the concept of an AI-infused leader is not a destination but rather part of a journey. As AI and analytics increasingly affect industries and professions, so too will the way that our interface with AI continues to evolve.

Perhaps the challenge will be not how to prepare for AI-infused leadership but rather how individuals give direction to the use of AI and robotic systems instead of allowing the technology to direct us as humans.
