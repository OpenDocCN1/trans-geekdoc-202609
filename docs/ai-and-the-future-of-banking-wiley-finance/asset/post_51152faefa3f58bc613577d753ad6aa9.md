## Branding Expectations of Generation Z

This later group, known as Generation Z, are defined by having been born between 1996 and 2010 and are sometimes called the ‘Toddler to Teen’ army. As a collection it’s said that they haven’t developed sufficiently to be able to be characterised with certainty but inevitably they’ll share not only some of the characteristics of their parents but also of later peer groups. In his report ‘Get Ready for Generation Z’,^10 writer Ryan Scott suggests they have four key distinguishing factors:

1. Ingrained technology, with short attention span
2. Greater desire for privacy and nervousness about leaving a digital footprint
3. Greater cultural diversity
4. Pragmatism, sensitivity and risk-averse behaviors

Beyond these there may be other cultural and societal undertones that will increasingly impact on the way that Gen Zers both work and spend their free time, such as multiculturalism, new cultural currencies and even disruptive practices. Even so, according to marketers Vision Critical, some key characteristics are emerging, even at this relatively early stage:^11

1. Their media consumption habits differ from previous generations, and even from Millennials.
2. They prefer cool products over cool experiences.
3. They are typified by entrepreneurial and tech-savvy behaviours.
4. They respond to edginess and edgy campaigns.
5. They want to co-create culture—and they do.

## Branding Expectations of Generation X

Although it is tempting to discount the Gen X grouping as we are considering branding in the context of banking of the future, and arguably Gen X are ‘yesterday’s generation’, this customer segment will still also remain key stakeholders at least for the next couple of decades and should not be overlooked. (For this reason, this chapter has covered Gen Y, Gen Z and Gen X in that particular order of perceived importance relative to the topic, rather than alphabetically.)

Gen X by definition is the population segment following the ‘baby-boomers’ (those born mid-1940s to about 1964) and who were born between the mid-1960s and early 1980s. Whilst it might have been convenient to have dealt with these three segments sequentially, Gen Y and Gen Z have more in common through their digital heritage and influences than Gen X, whose greatest influences arguably were political, societal and economic. Gen X are said to be a group that grew up at a time when society was more focussed on adults than on children, when divorce rates were rising and at a time that coincided with the sexual revolution.

Much has already been written on this topic mainly because social scientists have had plenty of time to consider the causes and effects of this phenomenon. The interest here, though, is how this group specifically respond to branding and marketing, and especially their relationship with banking and the financial sectors. How they choose to ‘digest’ branding and marketing information is also interesting, with some overlap between distribution channels by demographic segment. (See Figure 6.14.)

Gen X are described¹² as being:

- ‘Independent, resourceful, and self-sufficient’ and disdain being micro-managed
- ‘Technically adept, with the ability to absorb the use of new technologies’
- More flexible, especially around the workplace
- Value the work/life balance

Marketers such as Maye Create suggest that Gen X aren’t as conservative as their baby boomer parents but less liberal than their Gen Y children.

## BRANDING AND CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE INTERLOCKED

The financial market is increasingly reflecting the newer generation of spenders. ‘Young Money’, established in 2011, was identified as the ‘first British blog to help young people understand personal finance’. Their mission as stated appears both clear and attractive to the generation it targets.

‘All young people’, its website states, ‘should be financially knowledgeable, confident and in control of their futures. This is essential for individual, economic and social progress. This means promoting real choice and opportunity and moving away from a system that drives apathy, unfair sacrifice and learned helplessness’.

‘We deserve’, it goes on to say:

- ‘A truthful, fair and informed view of the financial sector
- Policies and regulations which genuinely serve OUR (i.e. their) interests
- Representation at all levels so that we can properly shape our financial futures’

Founder Iona Bain has been described as the UK’s ‘go-to voice’ on young people’s money issues. She was ‘Money Blogger of the Year’ at the Santander Financial Journalism Award in 2016, and in 2018 was named by the Association for Independent Professionals and the Self-Employed (IPSE) as ‘Freelancer of the Year’.¹⁵

Having control over personal finances is certainly a topic that is attracting investors. Whilst the topic of Fintech and start-ups is covered more generally elsewhere, one specific company is worth mentioning at this stage: Revolut.

Revolut started up on the Level 39 financial incubator in London and has already attained the status of ‘unicorn’, that is, having a start-up value of more than US $1 billion (£740m). Although not a bank when it started, it offers itself as an alternative to banks, including app-based current accounts; as well as other services, it provides budgeting services that ‘show you exactly where your money is going each month, and even let you set up monthly budgets for things like restaurants and groceries.’¹⁶
