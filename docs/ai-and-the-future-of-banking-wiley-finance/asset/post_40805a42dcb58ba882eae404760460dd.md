
Even a decade ago, contact centres were already using early forms of analytics to measure performance, especially in terms of agent productivity. These productivity tools remain in place but there is more focus on qualitative measurement of the call, rather than simply quantities, or numbers, of calls taken.

Beyond agent productivity, key metrics in the contact centre may include:

- Customer retention rates
- Satisfaction scoring such as NPS (Net Promoter Scoring)
- Product/relationship progression

Contact centres still remain a major expenditure for banks, and poor service there can be a key contributor to dissatisfaction and customer churn. Contact centres represent as much as 10% of all customer interactions and account for 30% of ‘moment of truth’ experiences. As many as 70% of inbound calls are said to be avoidable and could be handled by better online digital solutions. They are a prime candidate for automation/augmented assistance.28

Effective use of analytics allows banks to predict the most likely course of action that a customer is likely to take, and to recommend the ‘next best action’ to ensure not only retention, but also cross-selling or upselling. To allow this to operate effectively requires the bank to have a 360-degree view of the customer (covered earlier), to incorporate predictive models, and include rules-based systems to direct the query to the right solution or product.

Increasingly the mood in the short term is for ‘augmented automation’, that is, ‘attended’ RPA (or ‘robotic process automation’), where the solution rests at the employee’s workplace/workstation and outputs are determined by specific events, actions or commands by the employee as part of an agreed workflow. Think of it in terms of a robotic call-centre advisor. In effect it is a form of ‘soft robotics’ where the main decisions are being made automatically, but the information is communicated to the consumer through human intervention. Being critical, one has to ask exactly what value does the human add to this process other than empathy and a kindly voice perhaps?

The next step beyond ‘attended RPA’ is that of ‘unattended RPA’, which is close to an AI solution where actions are triggered by the autonomous robots themselves with little or no human intervention. In reality, most unattended RPA is presently used in back-office operations such as claims and invoice processing. At face value, there’s not much between the technologies of ‘attended’ and ‘unattended’ RPA. With the increasingly common process of voice recognition, it is only a matter of time before contact centres become fully automated.

## Sales, Compensation and Commission Management

Banks and their employees are under considerable pressure to maximise revenue, so the process of compensation management is intended to retain and reward the most successful staff with appropriate compensation methods, taking into account ethical performance, which includes customer service and satisfaction. Many banks still operate using a combination of spreadsheets, outdated legacy systems, emails and variations of commission and compensation planning across multiple departments.

The impact of this is to create difficulty in the management of the audit process, compliance with regulations and proof that compensation processes are not putting the bank at risk.

Effective compensation management requires:

- Alignment of compensation to sales and service
- Visibility and auditability
- Ability to be effectively tracked
- Effective management reporting system in place

An interesting issue arises. Traditionally the salesperson has used technology to identify the most promising ‘leads’ and to follow them up. The ability of technology to identify those leads with greater certainty now places a different onus on the ‘human’ salesperson. If the analytics seems to demonstrate that the prospect of closing a sale is so strong from a probability point of view, then the onus shifts to the salesperson to justify why a sales contact hasn’t been concluded.

Taken to its ultimate, analytics and AI have the potential to match both the aspiration of the buyer with the service/product provided by the seller, and to automatically bring them together. A sort of commercial dating agency perhaps? What then really is the human contribution to the sales role? Of course, it will always be argued that ultimately ‘selling’ is a people-to-people thing, but maybe we should be less complacent.

## Analytics in Portfolio Management

A useful but perhaps slightly unusual beginning to this section is that of a short historical review of what is described as ‘financial mathematics’. The starting point is with a botanist, Robert Brown (1773–1858), who observed the motion of pollen suspended in water, a phenomenon he called Brownian motion. Louis Bachelier (1870–1946), considered the founding father of financial mathematics, is believed to have been the first to have applied mathematical models to the analysis of financial markets, and proposed in his thesis ‘Theory of Speculation’ that stock markets evolve according to Brownian motion. Thorvald Thiele, a Danish astronomer (1838–1910) proposed a mathematical theory explaining Brownian motion, which Albert Einstein expanded on in 1905 to allow predictions.

Brownian motion is explained as being a stochastic process in statistics, that is, a mathematical object (in this case, the stock value) defined as a series of random variables. As well as in finance, the process is used in biology, ecology, chemistry and computer science (amongst others). Even at this basic level, it’s a complex statistical calculation. Notwithstanding, other experts such as Wiener and Robert Merton continued to push the financial boundaries to what is now ‘masters level’ mathematics.

Taking matters to the next stage, we then stumble across what is commonly known as the Black–Scholes (or Black–Scholes–Merton) formula, which was created by three scientists to calculate the theoretical price of one type of financial security. Their approach was that stock either rises or falls with the same degree of ‘predictable unpredictability’.31

When the formula was first introduced, it was used for pricing of stocks when an explicit formula wasn’t available. Nowadays, it is much easier to use, and there are online analytical tools that do the ‘heavy lifting’ in terms of the calculations for the user. Before it came into use, investors would find it difficult to assess whether an ‘option contract’ was reasonably priced and if so, did it represent good value? They could only speculate in terms of the price and value of ‘options’, and this was seen as being, on the whole, too risky. As a result of Black–Scholes, the trading price of an option can now be calculated mathematically so that there is only one accurate price for an option. With the formula, the trader or investor can determine if the market price is higher or lower than its theoretical value, and decide to invest (or ‘hedge’) accordingly.

It calculates the value using six factors:

- 1. Whether the option is a ‘call’ or a ‘put’, which is a description of the contract that places the emphasis on either the buyer (a put option) or the seller (a call option)
- 2. Current stock price
- 3. Strike price: the price at which the option can be exercised
- 4. Volatility of the underlying security, which is considered to be the most important of the factors
