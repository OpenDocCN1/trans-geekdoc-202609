
## Fraud Detection

This section will specifically consider fraud and financial crime (and we will specifically deal briefly with fraudulent investment trading later in the chapter). Fraud is a major operational risk for banks. Typical fraud might comprise:

- Identity fraud
- Anti-money laundering (AML)
- ATM fraud

Key issues for bankers include:

- The need to manage fraud detection at scale
- The ability to investigate using limited resources
- The need to maintain effective customer service, and not to treat all unusual transactions as being potentially fraudulent
- The need to manage internal and external compliance issues (such as trade monitoring)

## Regulatory Compliance

With such focus by banks on compliance, the use of advanced analytics and AI in this critical area is an inevitable area of focus. Known as Regtech, this falls broadly into the area of operational risk (which comprises a failure to comply with regulations, as well as other issues of compliance such as internal requirements).

The particular challenge for global banks is that of complying with diverse local requirements even if there are similar underlying requirements such as customer interaction and capital management.

In the area of customer management for example, the use of AI-infused recording and analysis can be used to monitor the nature and quality of advice given during a customer conversation, and ultimately to improve it. With the consent of the customer, AI can be used to break down a conversation into relevant key parts, such as whether the discussion was specifically financial advice or just general ‘chitchat’. From this, the financial advice element can be interrogated to establish whether the information given was within the accepted parameters. The AI element is a key component of how insight can be obtained, and a process by which the system constantly increases an understanding both of the nature and quality of interactions, and also the context of the conversation.

At central bank level, AI is increasingly likely to play a part especially in the management of capital adequacy, in particular in the area of stress testing. The volatility of the economic climate lends itself to greater automation. The likelihood is that as banks enter the next decade, they will enter more into fully digitised processes. The impact of this is that they will move, probably quite quickly, from paper to fully digitised formats, which lend themselves more readily to modern approaches such as machine learning, natural language and ultimately to AI.

## Risk Management and AI

The principles concerned here are:

- Management of counterparty risk
- Management of credit risk
- Management of compliance

With specific reference to the use of risk management, the issue is one of whether AI can provide some sort of automation, or whether it is just another element of the risk management ‘toolbox’. Even relatively robust solutions currently require human talent from data scientists and industry analysts. Beyond this, the data in itself may not be sufficient even when coupled with appropriate algorithms. Ultimately there must be the potential for AI to:

- Combine different volatility models (a process called ensemble learning)
- Choose and combine between different backtesting models, where strategies or models are applied to historical data
- Make forecast volatility models learn from errors (reinforcement learning)
- Create early warning systems for potential crises (neural networks)

Regtech startups, and even those companies that are more established, offer to provide software and capabilities that identify and assess risks, and provide a series of control measures to optimise risk management comprising.^21

- Modelling of risk mapping and risk matrices (by company, activities)
- Modelling of control plans
- Incident and recommendation management
- Escalation processes

It’s a complex area. In 2019 NatWest Bank announced a collaboration with Microsoft and AI specialist DreamQuark to develop what they called ‘Next Generation Complex Analytics (NGCA)’.^22 Working together, they aim to try to simulate the behaviours of different environments and sectors, and in doing so to create some sort of meaningful pattern that helps predict the future. By this, they mean predicting future outcomes in terms of risks and opportunities across industries, geographies and consumer segments. The intention is to do this through a combination of simulations and machine learning.

Accurate prediction will in itself bring greater certainty, but what does this mean in real terms? Isn’t the whole ‘game’ that of managing uncertainty, about spreading bets and about taking a bit of a risk? Can we ever really expect to operate in a risk-free environment, and if so, then what are the consequences? We have to take a step back. In the foreseeable future, it’s impossible to cope without the problem of human intervention, and humans might prove to be the weakest link in the process. The weakest link is when emotion and irrationality happen, which is something that automated systems are unlikely to allow – unless they are specifically programmed in.

## OPERATIONAL EFFICIENCY

The need for operational efficiency arises because of the need for processes and procedures to operate in the most cost-effective way whilst still maintaining the quality of the service or product offered. The aim is to optimise the way that tasks are done, reducing or removing entirely those elements of the process that either add unnecessary cost, do not add value or both.

In banking there are naturally many types of systems and processes, and the following section provides insight into just a few of them. Whilst considered under the heading of operational efficiency, there are overlaps with issues of customer centricity and of risk management. One example is in the case of sales management, which aims to optimise the sales process by focusing on the customer’s particular needs, and without falling foul of the highly regulated issue of mis-selling.

A key element of optimising operational efficiency is in understanding the actual cost of a process or system. Effective financial performance management is a critical component of this exercise (covered earlier) but with technology increasingly being a feature in process costing, the need to understand the IT cost that ‘attaches’ to that process is a key enabler to understanding.

## IT Cost Transparency

To have a clear idea on operational costings, and how to optimise them, banks are normally keen to understand how IT costs are allocated to the various parts of the business. The key components of transparency in the cost of banking technology comprise:

- A ‘catalogue-based menu’ of costing allocation, which means the provision of a single, consistent source of services and agreed functions, which simplifies product management, service charging and assurance.
- Consumption measurement, which can be based on either agreed hourly or functional rates, and is payable upon usage.
- Technology chargeback and internal invoicing, which comprises internal cost allocation mechanisms.
- Driver-based planning, which is an approach to management that identifies the key ‘drivers’ of a business, and ‘creates a series of business plans that mathematically model how those things most necessary for the organisation’s success would be affected by different variables.’²⁴

## Branch Performance Management

Perhaps the first question to be asked is whether the branch has a foreseeable future, especially in the world of advanced analytics and AI. As a starting point, it’s important to understand what makes a branch effective. First, we need to think about how we measure the success or profitability of a branch, and how this in itself is measured. We call this ‘branch performance management’. Branch performance management takes into account the use of data and analytics to measure current and past performance, and help in the planning process. Effective branch performance management takes into account the mix of customers who hold accounts at the branch, their product type and how to upsell and cross-sell to ensure maximum profitability.

Beyond this, it allows a bank to understand whether a particular branch is meeting its key objectives, which are normally sales or service oriented, and to what degree these are aligned to the wider strategy of the bank. This normally is related to growth and customer retention targets but there may be other ‘softer’ measures such as customer satisfaction.
