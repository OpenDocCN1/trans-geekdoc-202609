### INTRODUCTION

What drives the demand for financial services, and why is ‘demand’ important? A 2019 report from the UK-based CBI/PwC Financial Services Survey reported that demand and sentiment for financial services had fallen for the first time since 2013, rounding off three full years of flat or flagging optimism. Volumes of business amongst banks, building societies and specialist lenders were at their lowest since 2013 affecting profits. Rain Newton-Smith, CBI Chief Economist, said:²

> A combination of macroeconomic and Brexit uncertainty, regulatory compliance and global market volatility are taking a toll on the UK’s financial services sector. The persistent weakness in optimism and the deterioration in expectations sound a warning for the outlook.

It’s clear the sector is grappling with a number of other challenges too, from using data to improve customer experiences, to new entrants to the sector. However, with new risks and demands come opportunities.

Technologists and banking leaders already accept that the banking industry is likely to be transformed not only by technology such as AI, but also by changing customer needs and expectations. It should probably be accepted that such transformation is a ‘given’ but how will it manifest itself? Should consumers expect the demise of the High Street banking presence or will this reinvent itself in some way? Is a High Street presence needed and, if so, why?

An AI-infused bank or banking system is not one that concentrates solely on the use of advanced analytics, machine learning and more, but rather one that is prepared to reinvent itself in all its channels and transactions, with AI being both the catalyst and the glue that holds it all together. ### BRANCH MAKEOVER

In this increasingly technologically driven environment, customers are unlikely to be won over by better seating and a new coat of paint in the front office area.

In his article ‘A bright idea: Seven elements of the ultimate branch format’,³ David Kerstein created a picture of a branch in the bank of the future:

The ‘ideal branch’ of the future will be fully automated with robots or holograms serving as branch staff. The interior walls will reconfigure at the push of a button to create meeting rooms of different sizes and shapes with chairs popping out of the floor to fit any configuration.

Branch signage along with interior finishes (wall colours, floor coverings, artwork, etc.) will be all digital. They will change remotely if the bank merges with a competitor.

Customers will self-identify through retina scans or facial recognition. The facilities will be fully paperless, green and LEED-certified.

He describes seven key elements of a future branch setup:

1. Transaction zone where the ‘business will be done’
2. A self-service zone
3. Private meeting spaces
4. Customer meeting room, which is more like a customer’s living room than a bank office
5. Demonstration area
6. Marketing displays
7. Branding area

In summary he describes the branch of the future as:

- Open and private
- Offering high-tech solutions and highly personalised services
- Warm and inviting
- Leveraging technology to enable flexibility

All banks need to consider how the traditional branch transforms itself into a new, welcoming space. Adrenaline Agency considered this in their blog ‘Best practices in refreshing, transitioning and reforming space’, suggesting that there are three key implementation phases:

- Refresh
- Transition
- Reform

Within each of these three phases there are two key elements:

- **Visual**: Principally this relates to branding, internal and external signage. Improving visual elements such as lighting and colour can have a dramatic impact on how a space ‘feels’.
- **Operational**: This comprises the construction of the space and the equipment contained there. It starts to address the functionality of the space by removing areas that don’t work well and creating what they describe as ‘new zones of experience’.

Through these three phases of refresh/transition/reform, it’s possible to initially understand how a relatively low-cost refresh programme might simply improve the visual impact. The topic of addressing functionality falls into the transition category, perhaps by having new desks or pedestals. More radically, reform requires the bank to completely reimagine the space from top to bottom. The challenge is that banks may not want to go through the costly exercise of reforming or ‘reimagining’ every single branch. It becomes important that banks are able to prioritise which branches should be improved and this requires the use of analytics to effectively measure branch performance (as has been covered earlier in this book). To that extent, analytics becomes a critical tool to establish not only performance but branch potential and investment rationale.

An alternative approach might be that of combining banking with other services such as retail or, as suggested below, with the providers of coffee. ### The Emergence of the Café Bank

Millennials have adopted a new way of working. Once called the Flat White Society after the preferred coffee on order, new start-ups around the world increasingly centre their business activities around the coffee shop, an informal setting where the internet is free. Coffee shops also seem to be part of the devil’s bargain because they seem not to worry about time spent by visitors, nor do they change their internet access codes on a daily basis. As start-ups become more successful, they often evolve from the café to the innovation hub, where expertise is consolidated, mentoring is readily available and support services are within reach.

But let’s focus on the coffee shops and their role in the future of banking. The idea of banking coffee shops isn’t new but it’s an emerging trend. Already in local bank branches customers are often offered coffee whilst waiting to see an ‘advisor’, but this may be more of a courtesy than any attempt to replicate the café bank model. Some might argue that café banks are a new form of distribution channel. In the same way that there has been a crossover between vehicle ownership, leasing and financial services, the café banks seem to represent a crossover among retail, recreation and financial services, which places emphasis on co-location.
