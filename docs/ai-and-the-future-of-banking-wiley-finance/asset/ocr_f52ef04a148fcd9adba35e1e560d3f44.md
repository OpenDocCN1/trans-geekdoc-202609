To have absolute confidence, and win the hearts and minds of their customers, and with ‘trust’ increasingly becoming a critical success factor, banks need to effectively manage (directly or indirectly) their supply chains, including their technology. This is a more proactive rather than reactive role. It isn’t enough to ask suppliers to provide ‘evidence’ of security compliance but rather that the banks must play a much more dynamic role in the proactive management of data throughout the supply chain. The use of so-called ‘white hat hackers’ who hack in an ethical way might also be employed by firms to identify areas of vulnerability.

Additionally, banks must recognise that whilst technology is a problem in cyber security, many ‘leaks’ are of a relatively simple nature. These leaks can involve employees who are either careless and reuse passwords; who are disgruntled and deliberately steal information; or who are gullible enough to open unsolicited emails. Sophisticated criminals are increasingly focusing on social engineering by finding enough about an individual on social media to be able to send a communication that looks credible enough to open.

But where will the burden of that operational delivery in the supply chain rest in practical terms? Perhaps with the supply chain management professional whose main criteria, historically and maybe arguably, has been in getting the best deal or structuring the right commercial relationship between the parties. For that traditional supplier manager, data protection and cyber security management may be no more than a ‘hygiene factor’ and part of a ‘box-ticking exercise’. But perhaps, looking forward, it could be the most important criteria of them all.

One problem, however, is that smaller businesses along most supply chains are often considered poor relations. To what degree should banks – and other larger organisations – provide a certain level of free advice when it comes to cyber security, especially given the large costs involved in compliance? If the cyber gaps need to be effectively plugged, banks may need to take a leading role in that process. Some sort of ‘utopian society’ could be created in commerce where the larger organisations, such as banks, help the smaller ones, in the interests of both. Already there’s much free information available online and many vendors provide free access to conferences, but perhaps there’s a better way of exchanging cyber safety knowledge for the benefit of all?

It’s impossible to disregard the potential general weakness of supply chains. If the idea is that at least one version of the ‘Future of Banking’ is that of a traditional business model becoming ‘disaggregated’, that is, that future banking becomes in effect part of some sort of collection of supply chain components, then there’s significant pressure on banks (and the other ‘actors’ in that operating model) to get their cyber risk management house in order. It could be a critical success factor in the future of the banking industry.
