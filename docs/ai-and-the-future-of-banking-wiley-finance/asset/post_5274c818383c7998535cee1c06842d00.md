## Voice Recognition and Voice Assistants

Voice recognition is a process by which systems and software are used to ‘identify, distinguish and authenticate’ the voice of an individual speaker.¹³ It’s also known as speech or speaker recognition. It evaluates what are the ‘speech biometrics’ of an individual including speed, intonation and accent. (See Figure 5.13.)

Speech as a topic is a complex thing and is represented by the study of linguistics. It also includes the study of phonemes, or small pieces of language. The word phoneme itself is recognizable from its association with ‘phonetics’ and derives from the ancient Greek φώνημα (phōnēma), ‘sound made, utterance, thing spoken, speech and language’.¹⁴

In terms of how the computer analyses these, there are four broad approaches, although these can be further broken down into more granular analysis, as per Figure 5.13:

- 1. **Simple pattern matching**: This is where each word is recognised in its entirety so that the system recognises the pattern of speech. This is a very basic approach and one that is familiar in the use of call centres, where the automated phone system invites us to say our ‘selected service’ or to read out our bank account details. It usually only comprises the ability to recognise ten numbers and the computation is relatively not complex.
- 2. **Pattern and feature analysis**. This is where each word is broken into bits and each bit (such as a vowel) is recognised. In this process, the speech (in its analogue form) is digitised, converted into a graphical form and into acoustic frames each lasting 1/25th or 1/50th of a second. The output is then compared to a phonetic dictionary and the system identifies what has probably been said. Many of these systems have a form of correction process so that there is a degree of continuous learning by the system.
- 3. **Language or statistical modelling**. This is where a knowledge of language, grammar and the probability of words being used helps to speed up accuracy. It uses statistical analysis to anticipate the sequence of certain words or phrases, for example that two nouns rarely are used side by side in language but rather are most likely to be preceded by a noun or adjective. Modern speech recognition systems not only do this but also take into account the possibility of gaps between words. One of the leaders in this space was a Russian mathematician called Andrey Markov, whose system of analysis is known as the ‘Hidden Markov Model’ (HMM), ‘hidden’ because the system of analysis sits in the background of the system.
- 4. Artificial neural networks. This is where AI-type systems can reliably recognise patterns and word sounds. Whilst HMM has been the backbone of speech recognition, the use of systems which mimic the human brain through internal ‘connections’ has also allowed the recognition of speech patterns. These are known as ‘Artificial Neural Networks’ (ANN) and are sometimes used together with HMM. It’s easy to understand the complexity of the challenge.15

Today’s personal systems make speech or voice recognition more commonplace and most are already familiar with systems such as Siri, Alexa or Cortana, for example. Most personal devices already have these capabilities built into the system, so rather than typing a question into a search engine, the only action required is to speak into the device. Admittedly, there are often issues with background noise but it is possible to incorporate filters into the recording process, eliminating ambient sound much in the same way that top-end headphones eliminate aircraft noise. Perhaps the biggest barrier is the user themselves and their reluctance to ‘talk’ to a system. With time, that attitude will change.

When we consider the Bank of the Future, we should imagine that screens and keyboards will become increasingly obsolete and that our interaction with the banking system will be mainly voice-driven. Even if we improve the accuracy of recognition, it’s likely that users will still remain cautious when it comes to financial matters. How quickly will it be before payments are made to a third party or a transfer of funds based on a voice command only? How will that instruction be tracked? Where will the governance around ‘mistaken verbal instruction’ rest?

On the other hand, can’t an intelligent system query an ‘odd’ or seemingly incorrect instruction? And if the cost benefit of using voice recognition or indeed any other system outweighs the risk, can’t some pricing be built in for the risk, and if so why should consumers feel constrained? Can’t fraud analysis advance at the same pace as biometric analysis?

If AI is being properly considered in the context of banking, initially in retail banking but extending to wealth management, then the increasing importance of more effective voice recognition capabilities should not be ruled out.

## Visual and Facial Recognition

Face recognition systems are those that can identify or verify an individual from their digital image. In general, they work by comparing selected facial features of the individual and comparing them to a database. It’s described as a ‘biometric AI application’, biometrics being the technical term for any measurement of the body. Biometric identification is the process also used in fingerprint or eye iris recognition systems.

With its origins in security in the mid-'60s, one of the particular challenges was not only the ability to match facial elements to the database but also to compensate for issues such as ‘head rotation and tilt, lighting intensity and angle, facial expression, aging, etc.,’ according to Woody Bledsoe, who was one of the early pioneers. Technology has significantly moved on since then and both Facebook and Google, for example, now provide tags and photo apps that help users organise their photos by automatically identifying family and friends.

Facebook became especially competent in this area by being able to analyse the content of 1.5 billion users, but some argued that this is a breach of trust, especially in what appears to be a largely unregulated area. In 2014 they issued a paper on a facial recognition program called Deepface, which claims to have 97.35% accuracy. According to Cognitec Systems,¹⁶ one of the leading vendors:

> Essentially what is being looked at is a landscape of the face. Facial recognition software takes various measurements of each face and turns these into a string of numbers. Then it’s just a matter of comparing one string of numbers with another. The higher the similarity score, the more likely it is that you’re looking at the same person.

The ‘Biometric Information Privacy Act’ may offer some remedies and protections to consumers. BIPA, as it is also known, was passed by Illinois State in 2008 and (currently) remains the only law addressing regulation in this area, although there are a number of laws pending across several US states. It also permits injured parties to seek remedies in the court for misuse of biometric data.

Facial recognition is a ubiquitous piece of technology with uses in almost every sector of industry or even in the community:¹⁷

- In security where facial recognition is used; for example, in casinos, railway stations and airports.
- In gaming with Microsoft’s Xbox having the ability for users to access their profile using face recognition technology.
- In advertising; for example, Digital Astra beers ads in Germany can detect the age and gender of passers-by and tune the digital billboard accordingly.
- In retail it is used to count people and genders waiting in queues and to help in reorganising store layouts as well as to recognise shoplifters.
- In social or religious settings; for example, in 2015 a company called Churchix even launched an app allowing churches to discover who was attending.

A survey of US consumers by First Insight found that 75% said that they would not use a store that used that sort of technology, but interestingly the figure dropped to 55% if the store offered good discounts. If that’s the case, then the customer reaction to facial recognition seems to mirror that of the telematics industry, where users are prepared to ‘opt in’ for location tracking and ‘push notifications’ from coffee shops, for example, if they get some benefit such as a discounted drink.
