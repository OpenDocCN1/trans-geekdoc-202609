The concept of Open Banking arises principally from a lengthy 700-page report from the CMA into the retail banking market. In 2016 it recognised the dominance of the major banks both in terms of personal accounts (PCAs) and the structure of banking for SMEs. Part 1 of the report considers market conditions in detail, while Part 2 introduces a series of remedies to make PCA and SME banking markets work better for customers.

What was described as a ‘remedy package’ in effect comprises foundational measures of standardisation, service quality information, customer prompts and three key pillars:

- 1. Current account switching measures, including better governance of switching processes and customer access to transactions
- 2. PCA overdraft measures; alerting, maximum charges
- 3. Additional banking for small businesses; including SME switching tools and loan rate transparency

Clause 168 of the report states:

> > We are requiring the largest retail banks in both GB and NI to develop and adopt an API banking standard so as to share information to a specified timetable and we are requiring it to be an open standard so as to enable it to be widely accessible.

This will enable intermediaries to access information about bank services, prices and service quality. Customers who are satisfied about privacy and security safeguards, and are willing to give consent, will be able to share their own transaction data with trusted intermediaries, which can then offer advice tailored to the individual customer.

The overall aim was for it to be easier for customers to identify the best products for their needs, but there are some interesting challenges. The burden almost seems to be on the consumer to be the prime mover as far as any change of bank is concerned. The consumer has to assume that the provider of the APIs being used is meeting minimum standards and has been approved. Regardless of whether the vetting process is adequate, cynical consumers will be wary of previous poorly delivered, government-initiated IT schemes such as the failed NHS patient record scheme described as the ‘biggest IT failure ever seen,’ said to have cost £10 billion and ultimately abandoned.⁴

Some experts believe that the concept of Open Banking (OB) is most probably a slow burner. It is highly political in nature, and major banks inevitably are reluctant to encourage processes that could lead to loss of customers and revenue. Others suggest that whilst OB may not be a destination in itself, it may nevertheless provide some sort of a catalyst for new collaborations and partnerships. EY’s report suggests, ‘81 per cent of UK fintech firms surveyed are getting ready for Open Banking, while 29 per cent of respondents said they were already fully prepared’.⁵

## Interlock between AI and Open Banking

Whilst it is increasingly accepted that the future of Open Banking depends on the successful implementation of apps and APIs to provide a friction-free transfer process, this implies that customers readily want to change their banking provider. This is not always the case; many consumers suffer from issues of inertia or lack of confidence when contemplating changing their bank, insurer, utilities services or other providers.

Effective use of advanced analytics and, ultimately, AI will become key weapons in the battle between banks to identify who are the most desirable customers to retain or to attract, and who they might readily pass over in terms of retention or acquisition. Incumbent banking providers will have greater visibility in terms of those profitable – or future profitable – customers.

There is a strong feeling that PSD2 will bring a greater amount of information to the table that will allow banks to create better services and products for customers. One major challenge will be that of gaining insight from what could be a deluge of data, and making sense of it to such a degree that it becomes operationally useful. Advanced analytics and AI may well prove to be the key that unlocks that safe.

## Blockchain: Setting the Stage

There is a Blockchain revolution ongoing at the moment, and the banking and financial services sector is part of that revolution. Blockchain is often described as a ‘distributed ledger’ or set of accounting records (see Figure 9.21), which follow the process of:

- 1. Someone initiates a transaction.
- 2. The transaction is broadcast to a set of networked P2P computers known as nodes.
- 3. A validation process takes place using complex algorithms.
- 4. A transaction takes place that may comprise *cryptocurrency* or ‘stable coins’, documents, contracts or other records.
- 5. Once the transaction is completed the transaction forms part of a ledger, and in effect creates a new ‘block’ or component of the data. It becomes part of the ‘block chain’ and as such it is unalterable and permanent.
- 6. The transaction is completed.

In this context, *cryptocurrency* is a currency that has no intrinsic value. It cannot be redeemed for gold (although can be bought and sold), has no physical presence (existing only on the network), its supply is not determined by a central bank and the network that it is operating through is decentralised. The concept of the shared or distributed ledger means that the data isn’t held in one place but is hosted by (potentially millions) of computers simultaneously and as a result is accessible to anyone with the internet and an appropriate password.

It’s described as having three key pillars:

- 1. **Decentralisation**: The traditional approach is that information is centralised such as in the banking system. The only way that you can get to your money is through the central system. It’s suggested that a centralised approach has flaws; the main

## Interlock between Blockchain and AI

Experts such as Francesco Corea identify a number of opportunities where Blockchain and AI meet. In his 2017 article ‘The convergence of AI and Blockchain: What’s the deal?’⁶ he points to several possible uses for AI:

- Management of Blockchain energy consumption: Blockchain is already recognised as being very energy-hungry, and he suggests that effective use of AI may help in managing that energy requirement.
- Helping with the problem of scalability: When (if) Blockchain is fully utilised, the issue of scaling is likely to become problematic and AI may help in some way by ‘pruning’ saved data, and ensuring only critical data is retained. This may avoid tying up unnecessary storage and ultimately may help create new blockchain ‘models’.
- Improving security: Although one of the key selling points of Blockchain is security, the effective use of AI may further reinforce that capability.
- Improving privacy: Data security and privacy regulations will continue to mature, and increasingly apply themselves to the Blockchain.
- Improving efficiency: The ultimate scale of the network will bring issues of network optimisation, and AI may prove to be a tool in improving this, working together with energy consumption reduction.
- Talent management: Current thinking regarding the use of Blockchain is that creating a ‘distributed ledger’ is mainly a manual operation, but Corea does not rule out the possibility of automating this using AI in some way.

Conversely Corea also suggests that the use of Blockchain may also help in the adoption of AI by:

- Improving trust in data systems
- Improving AI effectiveness, because a more secure data sharing model will lead to more data being provided and thus more able to be analysed
- Lowering market barriers to entry as AI becomes more accessible to smaller players by being ‘built onto blockchains’
- Increasing ‘artificial trust’ in direct machine-to-machine interactions
