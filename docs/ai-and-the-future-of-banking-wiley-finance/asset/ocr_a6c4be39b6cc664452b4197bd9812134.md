The emerging trend is that of moving from ‘big data’ to ‘fast data’, which is the assimilation of information and its analysis in real or near-real time. In a banking context, speed of information helps banks and other organisations provide a more customised and more relevant offer.

Variety: Data comes to the user from many sources and in many forms – structured, semi-structured and unstructured. Semi-structured data presents particular problems because it is seldom consistent. Structured is in the form of established and organised data warehouses. Unstructured data (for example, plain text or voice) has no structure whatsoever but some might reasonably argue that there is a ‘golden thread’ tying both structured and unstructured information, and that is the ‘thread’ of location. After all, it’s suggested, isn’t everyone and everything somewhere?

Currently, a significant amount of data is unstructured, perhaps as much as 80%. It’s suggested that the winners of the future will be those organisations which can obtain insight and therefore extract value from the unstructured information. In a banking context this might comprise data, for example, which is based on social media and which helps establish consumer sentiment and brand equity.

Veracity: This is normally taken to mean the reliability of the data. Not all data is equally reliable because it comes from different sources. One key measure of veracity is the ‘signal to noise’ ratio, which is an expression for the usefulness of information compared to false or irrelevant data. (The expression has its origin in the quality of a radio signal, for example, compared to the background noise.)

In a banking context this may relate to the amount of ‘spam’ or off-topic posts on a social media site where a banker is looking for insight into customers’ reactions to a new media campaign. Spam may also include information automatically created in large volumes by ‘webots’, also known as ‘WebRobots’, ‘WWW robots’ or just simply ‘bots’. Typically a bot performs repetitive tasks that could be done by a human but at much higher volumes.

As organisations become obsessed with data governance and integrity there is a risk that any data that is less than perfect is not reliable. This is not necessarily true. One major UK bank, for example, gives a weighting to the veracity, or ‘truthfulness’, of the data. It allows them to use imperfect information in their decisions. The reality is that even in our daily personal life, decisions we take are often based on the best information available, even if it is not perfect, and subsequent actions are taken accordingly with different levels of confidence.

Value: the final characteristic and one not widely commentated on is that of the value of the data. This can be measured in different ways, such as the value to the user of the data in terms of giving deeper insight to a certain issue, or the cost of acquiring key data to gain better information about, for example, the creditworthiness of a customer. The challenge for many banks – and for other organisations – is how to ‘monetarise’ the data they presently have and can collect in the future.
