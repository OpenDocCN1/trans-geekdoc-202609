And if that wasn’t sufficient, consider that moving on from analogue to digital thinking isn’t good enough either, and the name of the game isn’t about digitalising existing processes and ideas but rather it’s an entirely new and different ball game. What does that new ‘ballgame’ look like? Thinking about it starts to open the door to new styles of leadership, a form that might be described as being either ‘augmented’ by technology or ‘AI-infused’.

‘Augmented leadership’ indicates that the style of leadership is principally augmented by technology. When we start to look for examples, one temptation is to look at some modern leaders whose period of office is infused by and augmented by the use of social media.

The message appears to be that, increasingly, leadership and technology cannot be separated.

In business, effective operational leaders need to be absolutely engaged with appropriate technologies. This need not necessarily extend to being active users but they (or their team) must have complete awareness of the value (and risks) of these new capabilities. Normally such leaders aren’t interested, or don’t have the time to be interested in the ‘numbers’. They need quick visualisations that enable them to understand at a glance where the issues and pressure points are. Increased emphasis on visualisations by business intelligence companies that converge analytics with location intelligence opens the door for senior executives to be able to understand and to digest heavyweight analytical interpretations.

Effective visualisations are a key extension of the digital environment as complex information becomes more easily digested. What might an AI-infused environment with equivalent complexity represent for our business leaders? Will they be threatened, confused or disbelieving? Or enabled, encouraged and empowered? After all, if the truth is ‘in the data’, then how can any leader not want to use that data and insight to make effective decisions?

Increasingly, data integrity and realistic insight will gain in importance. Few leaders will be prepared to ‘buck the trend’ and make decisions that are not supported by the output of analytical insight. Those who back their own intuition rather than the data might find themselves vulnerable in terms of corporate governance.

Stakeholders who are also dependent on their ‘institutional leaders’ acting on the data are likely to be extremely critical were those leaders to reject insight from high-powered analytical interrogation.

## Analytically Infused Leadership

Apart from issues of governance as considered above, what are the likely characteristics of an analytically infused leader? Before we answer that question, it’s important to take a step backwards. If we assume that current styles of leadership are inadequate and that overnight transformation isn’t likely, then there’s likely to be an intermediate step. This is a form of transition leadership where decision makers are no longer relying on intuition but also on data, appropriately analysed. Some might suggest that as business exists in a period of change, all recent leadership is ‘transitional’ in that it constantly manages technological advances and its impact on business models, customers and the workforce.

These new transitional leaders have the opportunity not only to make rational decisions based on the trusted information put before them but also to be able to defend their decisions if called to do so. Leaders have always been dependent on information put before them. Sometimes those decisions have been subject to uncertainty as a result of the poor quality of the data or of ambiguity of interpretation.

In an analytically driven world where there is confidence in the data and there is a ‘single version of the truth’, there’s little or no scope for disagreement about the information put before decision makers. The main issue is that of the most appropriate action that needs to be taken.

At this stage it has to be assumed that the decision process is not ready to be automated, but this remains a possibility. An agile banking operation might allow the automation of new product generation, sunsetting of existing products or investment strategy, for example.

In most cases, analysis can provide suggestions or recommendations as to the most appropriate action. Therefore the role of the leader is almost relegated to one of endorsement, advocacy, stakeholder management or communication. The situation is not dissimilar to that of a robotic wealth advisor, which, having considered all the options including the customer’s risk profile and appetite, makes recommendations as to the most appropriate portfolio. Beyond this, the investment manager then uses advanced analytics to help him or her make decisions as to where that money is best placed. What then is the real ‘value’ of the human who interfaces with the robotic wealth advisor, or the investment manager who uses advisory systems to decide on investment strategies?

Perhaps it’s no more than a human issue. Humans tend to like to deal with humans. Humans trust humans (mainly) but not yet machines. Do we trust bankers more than machines, or vice versa? Having said that, we appear to trust our Sat-Nav system to negotiate the quickest way home, and avoid the traffic jams. The Sat-Nav may have a human voice (female more often than male as we respond to females better, apparently) but that’s just a communication mechanism and one often exploited by automated contact centres. In robotic wealth management decisions, would customers respond better to an authoritarian male voice or an empathic female voice? And, gender equality issues aside, would women customers respond better to a robotic wealth manager with a female or a male voice?

Human leaders need help to make decisions, which increasingly comes in the form of effective visualisations. The complexity and variety of data is such that humans just can’t compute or digest the amount of information that may be internal to the organisation, external, contextual, structured and unstructured. Systems have begun to incorporate effective visualisation to help to make sense of it all. Going forward as the extent of data develops and grows, it’s extremely probable that intelligence systems will operate as a sort of filter. As a result, data or information that is irrelevant to decision-making will be excluded and what is left will be displayed through effective visualisations. One particular challenge is that of the operation of the filter mechanism and whether this is of sufficient sensitivity and awareness to understand what is important and what is not.

Beyond this, it will be possible to model the future in a form of war games approach by creating various scenarios, understanding the consequences and developing strategies (and mitigations) accordingly. Instead of an interview process, the future may well see prospective leaders ‘tested’ in a model-oriented form of gamification.

The ability for humans to make decisions based on incomplete information already exists. We intuitively figure out what’s relevant and what isn’t. Subconsciously the options are considered, the likely outcomes evaluated, and then decisions made based on the optimum position. Think of it as the equivalent of ‘war games’ for finance. As analytics and ‘gaming theory’ converge, business leaders will become more not only the figurehead of businesses but might also become experts – or gain ‘mastery’ – in the financial services equivalent of Minecraft.

> *Minecraft, for the uninitiated, is a 2011-invented video game of a 3-D created procedural world, in which the player must gain resources to build the world and to maintain health. There are different ‘modes’ for the player – survival, creative, adventurous and spectator. Beyond this is a ‘hardcore’ mode, but where the player has only one life.*

As considered in Chapter 6 when brand and marketing were reviewed to reflect the attributes of new ‘digital natives’ of Gen Z, a moment should also be spared to think about the impact that this new generation will have on how banking and financial services strategies are created and implemented. Some members of Gen Z will become the leaders of banking and financial services, and in doing so will bring their ‘gaming’ approach to the commercial table. That’s not a bad thing. After all, if a decision could be accurately modelled before being made, surely that would help? Intelligent systems ultimately might not only model the options but also carry out the evaluation.

## A New Approach to Leadership: ‘Trust but Verify’

If the approach is taken of intelligent systems potentially modelling the options, carrying out the evaluation and ultimately potentially implementing the optimum solution (however this is measured), confidence in decision-making starts to move away from individual decision-making regardless of seniority but to the data and the model.

At what point might an individual stand up and say, ‘I hear (or see) what the model is telling us but it doesn’t reconcile with my own experience’? The mantra needs to be ‘Trust but Verify’. In their report of the same name, ‘Trust but Verify’, SiliconAngle describe a process they call ‘financial aid levering’.

This is where algorithms are used to identify students who ‘nearly’ have enough money for further education and then direct funds towards them to enable them to successfully complete their courses. (In doing so, they also identified ‘machine learning’ as being the hot topic that had led to a surge in interest in machine learning courses.)
