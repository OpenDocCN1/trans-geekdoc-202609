
## Cyber Risk Management Apps

There is an increasing focus on the use of Fintech to address cyber risk issues. The Willis Towers Watson Quarterly InsurTech briefing of Q4, 2018¹⁷ particularly focused on the management of cyber risk, referring to:

- ‘Cyence Risk Analytics’ from Guidewire, which models the economic effect of cybercrime
- ‘Corax’, which builds analytics tools to support the design of new cyber insurance products
- ‘Paladin’, which creates cyber-risk detection
- ‘Zeguro’, which builds tools to reduce cyber risk
- ‘Refirm Labs’, which creates methods to vet and validate hardware security

From an insurance point of view, the cyber problem is increasingly being described as ‘Silent Cyber’, referring to insurance liabilities and obligations arising from cyber-related losses. These ‘silent’ losses stem from traditional property and liability insurance policies that were not specifically designed to cover cyber risk but where such losses aren’t specifically excluded, nor are they priced for by underwriters. It’s becoming more topical as an issue. In 2017 the NotPetya cyberattack was to become one of the costliest in history, attacking a range of businesses, from shipping companies to law firms, and demanding a bitcoin ransom. The attack was thought to have cost shipping giant Merck and also FedEx over US$300 million each.¹⁸,¹⁹

In mid-2017 the UK-based Prudential Regulatory Authority (PRA) said that it ‘expects firms to be able to identify, quantify and manage cyber insurance underwriting risk’. But it’s a challenge. As John Merchant, director of customer solutions at Guidewire Cyence Risk Analytics, puts it, ‘Five years old is ancient in terms of cyber’.²⁰

## Broader Cyber Issues for Banking

Whilst cyber security is a worry at a personal level, there are broader issues that need to be considered. In part, these are increasingly leading to a new category of cybercrime insurance, which in many cases has yet to be tested in the courts of law. According to global insurers AXA, there are at least four key areas that need to be addressed.²¹

- **Infiltration of industrial systems**: That is, the ability of cybercriminals to infiltrate industrial controls (In 2014, cybercriminals increased the speed of machinery in a German steel mill until it burst into flames.)
- **Ransom risk**: The concept of ‘cyber extortion’, where a system is infiltrated and a ransom is demanded, usually payable through some form of crypto-currency
- **Reputational risk**: Infiltration leading to loss of brand trust and the public losing faith in an organisation they have trusted with their personal information
- **Professional liability risk**: Where organisations (such as lawyers) that are less concerned with data loss realise that they are holding sensitive information, the loss of which would create insights that are both actionable and potentially detrimental to the owners

## SAFEGUARDING THE BANK OF THE FUTURE: NEW CYBER SECURITY THREATS

In considering the future of banking in an AI-context, the future of cyber threat also needs to be addressed. In his report ‘Future cyber threats and challenges: Are you ready for what’s coming?’22 information technology expert Michael Nadeau challenges the suggestion that the world is changing and the internet is changing as a result. Rather, he suggests, the internet is changing and the world is changing as a result. He looks through the other end of the telescope. If his thinking is extended, it’s possible to recognise that as the internet changes and evolves, then banking will also change as a result.

He also refers to a group called ‘The Internet Society’ (ISOC), founded in 1992 and still going strong. Their 2017 report ‘Paths to Our Digital Future’ reinforces their view that ‘the future of the internet is ours to shape for the next generation. Humanity must be at the centre of tomorrow’s Internet’.

Elsewhere Nadeau also writes about the ‘State of Cybercrime 2017: Security Events Decline, but not the Impact’. In it, he refers to the US State of Cybercrime Survey, conducted annually. Of the 510 respondents of these major respondees, the average IT security budget is US $11 million, yet the majority of respondents (61%) saw cyber security as an IT issue rather than one of corporate governance.

Beyond this, Symantec’s 2019 report23 also records multiple cyber intrusions:

- 1. ‘Formjacking’ attacks skyrocketed, with an average of 4,800 websites compromised each month. (Formjacking is an approach where hackers insert a small piece of code into the checkout page of an online supplier and from this are able to steal credit card details of the purchaser.)
- 2. A reduction in ‘cryptojacking’, which comprises the secret use of your device to ‘mine’ or access crypto currency.
- 3. ‘Ransomware’ criminals who are shifting targets from consumers to enterprises, where infections rose 12%.
- 4. More than 70 million records were stolen from poorly configured third-party supply chains, put down to rapid cloud adoption. Supply chains remain a soft target, with attacks ballooning by 78%. This especially presents a challenge to the concept of the extended universal bank, considered earlier.
- 5. Internet of Things (IoT) and devices were a key entry point for targeted attacks, and that most IoT devices are vulnerable. The same issues arise as in item 4 above, in terms of the extended universal bank trying to add value but in doing so adding vulnerability.

Items 4 and 5 are particularly important as society increasingly considers itself as being ‘connected’ (at least at a technological level). Symantec suggests that the weakest links are both in the supply chain and in the devices that will be more readily used. The concept of the ‘Connected Car’ sits alongside the ‘Connected Home’ and the ‘Connected Factory’ where the IoT indicates that associated devices are the weakest link in the chain. Symantec’s view of where commercial and technological vulnerability lies is potentially worrying. As banks extend into other value-add services by outsourcing, they may be improving the customer experience but at the same time increasing cyber risk. Banks must effectively manage the cyber risk along their entire supply chain.

Elsewhere, other organisations are pointing to similar concerns. In their Cyber Readiness Report 2019 mentioned earlier, insurer Hiscox reports a sharp increase in the number and cost of cyberattacks. In their study of more than 5,400 organisations across seven countries, more than three out of five firms (61%) reported one or more attacks in 2018, despite there being no significant reduction in their capability and readiness to respond. From a representative sample of businesses in the US, UK, France, Germany, Spain and the Netherlands, only 10% were scored high enough by Hiscox to rank as ‘experts’.

Amongst the key findings are:

- There was an increase in intensity and frequency of attacks: 61% reported in the 2019 report, compared to 45% in the 2018 report.
- More small- and medium-sized businesses (over 50 employees) are being attacked, reporting an increase in incidents from 33% to 47%. Amongst medium-sized firms (50–249 employees), the percentage of attacks between 2017 and 2018 has increased from 36% to 63%.
- Cyber losses have increased. The average cost of a cyberattack has increased from $229k in 2017, to $369k in 2018, an increase of 61%. For large firms (250+ employees), the average cost for cyber-related losses in 2018 was reported at $700k, compared to $162k in 2017.

The adoption of the GDPR regime has improved cyber security and people’s awareness of the problem, but there’s still progress to be made. A survey of British businesses by the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport has also found that:

- 74% of UK companies regard cyber security as a senior management issue.
- 73% of UK companies have yet to establish a formal policy on the matter.

The department also identified that in 2017, 43% of UK businesses and 20% of UK charities suffered a cyberattack or data breach.24 According to Ciaran Martin, CEO of the UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), ‘People often start out scared of cyber security as an issue. They think it’s too technical and an unmanageable risk’. As a result, the NCSC have created a Board Toolkit25 to help directors understand the dynamics of cyber risk management, setting out five key questions that they should ask their information security officers:

- 1. How do we defend against attacks?
- 2. How do we control the use of privileged IT accounts?
- 3. How do we keep our systems up to date?
- 4. How do we ensure that our partners and suppliers protect the data we share with them?
- 5. What authentication methods are being used to control access to data?
