# The Collaborative Bank

Mergers and acquisitions in the banking sector have become familiar in many parts of the world, both to satisfy the demands of scale efficiencies and also to provide a platform for revenue growth where there are complementary territories or products (a term sometimes described as ‘inorganic growth’). Another advantage is that they reduce competition and M&A activity effectively removes one of the competitors. It’s described as a ‘horizontal merger’ because the merging entities are usually involved in the same kinds of commercial or business activities.

In many countries global banks are extending their operations with regional banks in those areas, helping the global players increase their dominance. M&A activity is controlled or ‘regulated’ by the ‘apex’ leading financial institution in the country, for example, the Reserve Bank of India.37

There can also be a downside to M&A activity. Job losses may occur, which may be economically or politically unacceptable, and there may also be major challenges in integrating legacy technologies. On the other hand, general deregulation and market liberalisation remain as market factors in the structural changes in the marketplace. There is a fourth model to consider, that of a more collaborative banking system. (See Figure 8.19.) In many cases improved vertical collaboration along the supply chain is a precursor to acquisition activities as banks acquire new technological capabilities to ensure differentiation. The advent of Open Banking is suggested as also providing an eventual catalyst for greater collaboration.

Although the term ‘collaboration’ is often used along the supply chain between the bank as client and a vendor (which may be a technology company but could quite easily be a distributor), the prospect of ‘horizontal collaboration’ between banks themselves should not be ruled out. Whilst operational optimisation is a factor, the customer experience should not be overlooked. For many retail consumers, the banking branch network provides a useful social function where local charities and communities can make cash deposits. Also, in areas where social deprivation or generational issues prevents (or slows down) a move towards a cashless society, the traditional bank has an important role to play. In 2018 the total number of bank branches in the UK had fallen to 7,207, from 11,365 in 2007.
