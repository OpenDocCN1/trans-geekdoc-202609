## INTRODUCTION

We think we know all about branding and brand awareness. After all, we live with it every day, are surrounded by it and often make our buying decisions based on it. But what is it really other than (some might say) just another tool in the back pocket of marketing professionals? According to the American Marketing Association:^2

> A brand is a name, term, design, symbol, or any other feature that identifies one seller’s good or service as distinct from those of other sellers.

It’s the image that people have in mind when considering the physical nature of the good or service, and their emotional engagement with it. In their book *Marketing Management*, Kotler and Keller say, ‘Branding is endowing products and services with the power of a brand’, which seems fairly straightforward, and there’s no shortage of information about the topic given its importance.^3

Branding is achieved in multiple ways, such as:

- Advertising
- Communication strategy
- Product and packaging design
- Customer experience
- Sponsoring and partnerships
- Visual identity of the brand (logo, website and colours).

But why does brand resonate so strongly with us, how does it create such a differentiating effect, why is it such a key component to loyalty and why will it be even more important going forward? As we understand these aspects more, what will be the impact of the digital age and the AI-era?

In his paper ‘The Importance of Brand Awareness in Consumers Buying Decisions and Perceived Risk Assessment’^4 Associate Professor Olividiou Moisescu carried out research into the relationship between retail banks and consumers, and his findings reinforce the importance of brand awareness and recognition in the decisioning process.

Moisescu equates branding in the eye of the consumer with issues of risk, as familiarity with the brand and its characteristics provides confidence in the purchasing decision. Brand awareness falls into two categories: ‘unaided’, which relates to brand recall, and ‘aided’, which relates to brand recognition. Both of these are important relative to the purchasing decision, but some might argue that these are techniques more suited to commodity purchases rather than financial services.

Marketing for financial services is increasingly considered to be a specialist area requiring in-depth knowledge not only about products and services but also about consumer sentiment and what is most likely to resonate with current and future customers. Brand positioning for many financial services seems to be relatively intangible when considered against the bank itself. Financial services often use brand identities associated with sports, animals or both, and then aim to reinforce messages of innovation, teamwork and trustworthiness. In a more sophisticated world of AI and imagery being presented on a small mobile screen, almost certainly new brand techniques will need to start to emerge such as sound, voices and other sensory attributes. AI methods will in time start to customise how an individual consumer is best attracted by a particular element of branding.

As the concept of universal or full-service banks develop, the challenge for marketers will be that of ensuring the brand message is communicated by all elements of the banking ecosystem in a consistent way. The weakest link of the supply ecosystem will become the weakest link of the banking universe. More effective governance of the supply chain will become increasingly important, and the role of the supply chain manager will be obliged to reflect this.

## BRAND VALUE AND EQUITY IN BANKING

Brand isn’t just an exercise in attracting customers but has a strong and relatively clear financial value as well. In *Managing Brand Equity: Capitalizing on the Value of a Brand Name*,⁵ David Aaker – who is an American organisational theorist, consultant and professor emeritus at the University of California – considered the concept of ‘brand equity’ or ‘brand value’, describing it as a ‘complex combination of brand awareness, perceived quality, brand loyalty and brand associations’.

Brand value is measured in three key ways:

- Cost-based brand valuation
- Market-based brand valuation
- Income-approach brand valuation

Experts also recognise the importance of value and how to financially assess it, typically understanding the significance of:

- Value derived out of customer perception, social media, and so on, as a relative measure against an unbranded/common brand
- Value derived in terms of growth in market share and revenue through perceived brand equity over a time period

The essence is, therefore, that brand isn’t just a form of ‘magnet’ that attracts new customers and encourages existing ones to be loyal. Both of these, incidentally, are important elements as the concept of Open Banking gains in traction, and brand value becomes a vehicle in terms of customer magnetism. Rather, it recognises that in banking marketplaces where mergers and acquisitions are likely to feature, either between banks or other business (such as technology companies for example), then brand is important in terms of the market valuation of a potential ‘target’.

Studies by companies such as Brand Finance, a leading brand valuation consultancy, seek to identify the world’s biggest banks by their brand value.^6^ They calculate value through three contributing factors:

- Enterprise value, which they define as ‘The value of the entire enterprise, made up of multiple branded businesses’
- Branded business value, which they define as ‘The value of a single branded business operating under the subject brand’
- Brand contribution, which they define as ‘The overall uplift in shareholder value that the business derives from owning the brand rather than operating a generic brand’

Brand Finance differentiate between ‘brand value’ and ‘brand strength’. The latter is defined by ‘the efficacy (or value) of a brand’s performance on intangible measures, relative to its competitors’, measured by marketing investment, stakeholder equity and the impact of those on business performance.

In their 2018 report, Brand Finance also draw specific attention to non-finance competitors in the broader financial services sector such as Apple, Facebook, Google and Amazon. They make the point that these tech giants have either already created consumer-facing financial services or are expected to do so soon. As such they ‘represent new challenges to traditional banking services, as they are more aligned to modern customer expectations around instant multi-channel and multiplatform services’.

## Millennial and Gen Y Brand Expectations from Bank Brands

In considering the importance of brand in the future, it’s important to give more attention to the nature of the customers whom current and future brands will be addressing, and to get inside their minds.

As a level-set we describe Millennials (sometimes known as Gen Y, following Gen X and preceding Gen Z) as being that group born from the 1980s through to the mid-1990s and reaching adulthood in the early twenty-first century. Although there is considerable divergence by location, economic and social conditions, they are generally characterised by their increased use of and familiarity with technology. Known as ‘digital natives’, they have been brought up with technology as opposed to those who have been introduced to the topic in later life (and who are known as ‘digital immigrants’). Both expressions were first coined in 1996 in the ‘Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace,’8, which was in effect a rebuttal that organised government did not have a mandate to manage the laws of cyberspace (aka the internet) as the internet was developing its own rules or ‘social contract’.
