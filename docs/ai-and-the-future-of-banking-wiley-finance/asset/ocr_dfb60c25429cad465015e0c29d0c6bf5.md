Nowadays it is such a hot subject on the agenda that it may even have reached the point of becoming ‘hype’. Some cynics suggest that the topic has mainly been driven along by those set to benefit, mainly technology companies and vendors. Others view it as the panacea to all ills in the world, and a methodology by which services (and products) can be customised, friction can be removed from processes, and risk can be mitigated.

AI seems to have a polarising effect, with participants in the debate either believing in it or being sceptical. AI sits at the extreme end of a maturity curve of analytics comprising:

+   (a) Descriptive analytics
+   (b) Predictive analytics
+   (c) Prescriptive analytics
+   (d) Cognitive analytics
+   (e) Artificial intelligence

Cognitive analytics is a system which has the ability to interact with the user, such as in the situation of a wealth manager for example, and which helps the human advisor give the right information to their end customer. It has by engineering the ability to learn, usually through some sort of closed loop mechanism. In many cases it’s problematic to try and segment the different types of analytics too rigidly. In fact, some are already referring to AI as ‘assisted intelligence’, which is a form of cognitive analytics. Cognitive analytics usually has some element of predictive analytics embedded within it, and AI has machine learning capabilities. Beyond this, both have the ability to use algorithms to examine large volumes of unstructured data and to find patterns which extend not only to text data but also to voice and other unstructured forms.

In addition, both cognitive analytics and AI have ‘machine learning’ capability which enables the system to learn without being explicitly programmed. This occurs at three different levels:

+   1. Supervised learning where the system is given data and a required outcome and is then helped by a teacher to compute a set of rules
+   2. Unsupervised learning where the system is tasked to find patterns in unlabelled data
+   3. Reinforcement learning where the system learns in a dynamic environment, such as in the playing of games.

The element of learning is a key attribute both in cognitive analytics and AI. It accepts in principle that there will be exponential growth in the use of AI to a point where there is an intersection between man and machine, a concept called ‘singularity’. It is at that stage where AI first matches and then surpasses human intelligence. The jury is still out as to when that may happen but it is suggested by AI experts such as Ray Kurzweil that by 2045 ‘there will be no distinction between man and machine’. He bases his projection on Moore’s Law, expressed over 50 years ago, that the number of transistors doubles every two years, and that self-learning machines will double their speed every two years.
