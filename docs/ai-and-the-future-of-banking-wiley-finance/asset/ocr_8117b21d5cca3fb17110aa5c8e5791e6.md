# CONCLUSION

In this chapter the issue of banking imperatives has been considered both in terms of those which currently exist and those which will be needed in the future. Imperatives have been operationally ‘devolved’ to what might be considered as business ‘objectives’, but these in themselves might be becoming increasingly irrelevant in favour of a more dynamic method of both personal and business measurement of progress.

This in itself throws up some interesting organisational challenges about how personal contribution is measured in a new AI-infused age. Many ‘Personal Development Plans’ are objective-based, yet in a more volatile business world traditional ‘objective-based’ measures are becoming less relevant and important. Skills and knowledge are being replaced by agility and flexibility and this places new pressures on banks to effectively measure progress in a different way at a corporate, divisional and ultimately individual level. We may be reaching a point where the concept of ‘performance management’ needs to be reinvented.

It’s not just a challenge for banking. As roles and responsibilities increasingly change through the uses of automation and AI, operational functions within the enterprise are also being both reinvented and redefined. Although they may not have yet realised it, the HR professional is being pushed more and more to the front line of change, which few are prepared for. There will be an inevitable spinoff in terms of performance management, competence-based pay, employment law and the like.

It’s not just a matter of applying today’s banking imperatives and thinking about tomorrow’s but of recognising the implication of these changes both on organisations and individuals. The revolution towards Banking 4.0 will affect all the stakeholders, both back office and front office. There is no hiding place. The new mantra will become ‘competences, not objectives’.

At a strategic level, the notion that banks might need to extend their traditional ecosystems into other areas also opens the door to convergence of imperatives. At the end of the day, banks, retailers, telecoms providers and healthcare providers are dealing with the same end user, the ‘consumer’, albeit in different guises. In a truly customer-centric environment, the customer’s needs should come first but it’s more complex than that. Effective use of AI could help create some new form of ‘balanced scorecard’ for the next millennium, one that not only reflects customer needs but also risk and operational effectiveness.
