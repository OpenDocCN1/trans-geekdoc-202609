The process is that it ranks consumers according to their creditworthiness, sets the price of the proposed loan and allocates the capital from investors.

Despite the level of investment, margins are said to be relatively low because Zopa in effect acts as the middleman. A fee is paid both by the lender and the consumer when the loan is set up. There are other peer-to-peer lenders. Funding Circle, the market’s biggest player and recently subject to an IPO in 2019, was the first to use a peer-to-peer lending platform for businesses rather than consumers. Since then it has facilitated £5.0 billion in loans to 50,000 small and medium-sized businesses.16 It operates in the UK, the US, Germany, and the Netherlands. Lender funds are auto-allocated, with an average rate of return for lenders of 6.5% (2017), which is well in excess of High Street saving rates. Their backers include the leading firms behind Facebook, Twitter, Skype and Betfair. Investors include local councils, universities and the British Government. ‘Funding Circle has secured an extra £40m from the Government to lend to small businesses, providing fresh capital at a time when business borrowing is facing a squeeze’ (Telegraph, January 2017).

There seems to be an element of gamesmanship in the lending process. According to one user of the bank, in the capacity of a lender of money:

> Although I’ve lost money, the average interest rate has been good. The trick is to lend the smallest amount you can to the most borrowers, so when investing, take your time allocating funds.17

Other peer-to-peer lenders include Ratesetter, Lending Club, Upstart and Prosper Marketplace. The disappointing outcome of the Funding Circle IPO in 2019 may well have put off some of the other P2P companies from adopting a similar IPO strategy, at least for the moment, but analysts suggest that this may have had more to do with market nerves than the business model itself.

P2Ps are attracting the attention of global regulators with the UK regulator FSA having created a framework that:

- Provides additional protection for all the customers
- Promotes healthy competition within the P2P industry
- Ensures that the platforms provide correct information and have clearly defined procedures for handling funds from the clients
- Guarantees that the companies deal with customers appropriately during financial difficulties18

In terms of analytics, the process may also lend itself to increased automation as more granular forms of auto-allocation could allow more accurate matching of lenders and borrowers, effectively cutting out the bank as ‘middleman’. Compared to traditional systems, P2P is said to be quicker, frictionless and more user friendly. Ethically it appears to play well to the disruptive ‘sharing economy’ that is developing amongst Millennial consumers. It also creates yet another benchmark by which the Bank of the Future must compete to remain relevant.

## PAYMENT PROCESSES BECOME MORE CUSTOMER CENTRIC

With electronic payments increasingly taking the place of cash, there’s plenty of interest in the topic of e-payments and m-payments. It’s a focus area in its own right, with the payments community comprising banks, service providers, consultants and fintech (of which there are said to be 25,000 start-ups focussing on this area).

According to Mark Buitenhek, Global Head of Transaction Services at ING, ‘Payments are no longer a bank-only monopoly and we know it.’¹⁹

ING carried out a survey of 15,000 mobile device users discovering:

- 41% already use their device for mobile banking.
- 58% shop online using their mobile devices.
- 34% said they would be a repeat customer if the retailer offers one-click service (rising to 55% if there are other benefits as well).

Speed and convenience are viewed as being the top reasons for use but amongst those that do not use mobile payments:

- 42% said that they don’t trust apps that aren’t from banks. (84% said they trust an app from a bank).
- Only 5% trust an app from a social media provider.

In their 2019 report ‘Payment World Explodes’, researchers SMA (Strategy Meets Action) identify the complex world of payments, both inbound and outbound, and make the point customer expectation is what is driving change, not vice versa.

## FIVE SCENARIOS FOR THE ‘BANK OF THE FUTURE’

It’s both challenging and risky to set out a single view of the Bank of the Future, so a number of different scenarios are offered. One of these may prevail but perhaps the final outcome will be some sort of hybrid. Earlier the idea of ambidexterity of strategy was considered and it’s feasible that two or more scenarios might co-exist within the same organisation.

In this case, a single major assumption has been made and that is one of AI infusing the end result. The Bank of the Future will be an ‘AI-enabled’ organisation. Maybe the bank itself will not be designed by humans but by the technology itself in some form of ‘self-learning’ evolutionary process. Advanced analytical technology has already recently gone through a sea change, transforming itself from being a ‘knowledge-based system’ (that is, a system which is capable of making decisions based on the information it is storing and then applying context and an interface engine), into a ‘machine-learning’ capability with the ability to partly or fully automate using RPA (robotic process automation) techniques.

Put another way, knowledge-based systems work according to rules created by humans, whereas with a machine learning system it is the machine itself that creates the rules (or algorithms). The problem, according to an MIT report, is fundamentally one of scale, with so much data that so many rules are required and there is a need to manually create codes. The solution, apparently, might be to allow the machines to sort out the rules themselves.²²
