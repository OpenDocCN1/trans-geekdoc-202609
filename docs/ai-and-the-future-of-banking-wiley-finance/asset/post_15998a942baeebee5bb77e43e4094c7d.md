
For completeness, other project management approaches are listed here:

**V-Model:** ‘V’ is for verification and validation, which is like a waterfall implementation but each part of the plan is verified and tested before moving onto the next part of the plan.¹³

**Incremental Model:** In effect, this is a series of mini-waterfalls or modules, with each module going through requirement, design, implementation and testing phases.¹⁴

**RAD Model:** The Rapid Application Development model is one where each module is built in parallel to an agreed timeframe and then bundled together into a working prototype; useful for projects that are clear and need delivery within 3–6 months.¹⁵

**Spiral Model:** Like an incremental model but with more emphasis placed on risk management. Each phase of the project – such as planning, risk analysis, engineering and evaluation – is measured against an agreed baseline of risk. This process is especially useful where risk management is important, such as in large and mission-critical projects.¹⁶

Overall, the argument being adopted is that in such a technological world, traditional approaches to development by financial services businesses are no longer adequate. They need instead to mirror the approaches of technology companies, especially those such as Apple and Amazon, which are increasingly moving into the financial services sector.

An additional important factor is that financial services companies looking to transform will not be able to do so by maintaining a traditional, conservative approach because they won't be able to recruit or retain appropriate talent. Another interesting insight from the Futurion survey included the fact that even in complex organisations the most successful innovators ‘acknowledge, embrace, and thus master their complexity’. They use essential tools such as analytic insights (to measure progress), effective stakeholder management, compliance management and high-quality communication to achieve their goals.

## The Use of ‘Design Thinking’

All too often innovators create new capabilities and then look for an industry where it has the best ‘stickability’. Mentors are often used in incubators not only to help with development of the product or solution but also to help with the go-to-market. Where an innovation can hitch itself to a business problem, then this helps with the fundraising process and in due course with the success of the innovation.

Whilst the domain of the innovators seems to be occupied by baggy trousers and T-shirts, there is an increasing band of ‘mature’ entrepreneurs with seasoned experience who approach the need for change by understanding the key industry issues or problems. Recognition of the business imperative of any industry become critical. Whilst most mature innovators and entrepreneurs may know this either intuitively or through experience, those with less experience may need to either do their homework or rely on a supportive mentor.

This is not in principle very different to the approach of ‘Design Thinking’, which is a ‘mindset and toolbox’ approach that helps an organisation focus on:

- Understanding the problem
- Building the ‘right’ thing
- Building the thing ‘right’

## Finding Capital to Innovate: One Example

For many banks, the pressure is on to get beyond their normal walls of business and have the ability to compete with newer, more flexible competitors. Unless specific funds have been put aside for development, there is the inevitable challenge of where funding might be found. Despite recognising that in many cases it becomes a ‘mission critical’ exercise to transform, at the same time there are often budgetary challenges.

This particular example is included to flag up the potential for looking more carefully into an existing business to identify where development funding might be obtained. In this situation, the use case was that of developing an omnichannel solution across online and social media environments, with funding obtained through better management of their telecom costs.

By way of background to this innovation example, omnichannel banking is described as a ‘seamless banking experience’ that a customer enjoys however he or she interacts with the bank, whether mobile, retail or over the internet. (See Figure 10.26.) It reflects the desire of consumers that the bank ‘speaks with one voice’. According to Global and Finance Banking site, by adopting an effective omnichannel strategy, Wells Fargo bank in the US have reported an ‘increase in purchase rate by 1.9 times, increase in customer retention by 20ppt and increased cross-selling per house-hold by 6 products among others.’17

With such apparent benefits it would seem that investment in omnichannel is critical for banks, but a large proportion of IT investment is still directed towards the maintenance of their legacy systems. As a result, banks are increasingly looking at ways to free up capital and one approach is that of Telecom Expense Management (TEM). According to Dave Locke, CTO of World Wide Technology, ‘Telecom Expense Management (TEM) is a popular way to tap into this source of capital’. It allows banks to map their existing communications expenditure arising from multiple networks between branches, back and front office, and even to ATMs and to optimise the telecom arrangements. According to Locke this can reduce telecom costs by up to 40%, which can be directed into other digital initiatives.18

## FINTECH FOR BANKING

It’s impossible to consider the future of banking, especially in an AI era, without considering the impact of Fintech. The word is a portmanteau of ‘Financial Technology’. Originally focussing on back-end systems, in more recent times there has been increased focus on customer- (or consumer-)facing solutions. Fintech has expanded to include technology solutions for ‘financial literacy, advice and education, as well as streamlining of wealth management, lending and borrowing, retail banking, fundraising, money transfers/payments, investment management and more’. (See Figure 10.27.)

It’s big business, with Fintech start-ups receiving $17.4 billion in funding in 2016 alone according to Forbes.19 Start-ups aspire to become ‘unicorns’, an expression coined in 2013 by venture capitalist Aileen Lee, alluding to the rarity and mythical quality of these beasts. In 2017 these ‘Fintech unicorns’ were valued at over $80 billion. North America produces most of the Fintech start-ups followed closely by Asia.
