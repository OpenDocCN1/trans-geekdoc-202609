The tagline of Starling Bank, one of the UK challengers, is ‘Clever banking apps . . . putting themselves at the heart of their customer’s financial ecosystem’. ‘Banking, but better’ is another of their smooth (and assertive) marketing lines, which sets the challenge for traditionalists. The company was only started in 2014 as a digital, mobile-only bank and has attracted significant investment funding. It’s very innovative, with leading Open Banking capabilities and open APIs, which allow customers to share their data with other financial product providers.⁸

Often the measure of success in an app is whether it is ‘clunky’ – that is, whether there is a choppiness in its usage, which can be due to systematic errors in its development. It’s commonly thought that this clunkiness is often due either to the speed of development or as a result of failure to bring in ‘functional testers’ early enough in the process, by which time the problem becomes both complex and usually costly to resolve. Alternatively, it is possible to bring in third-party testers, including companies like TestIO, which adopt a crowd testing approach to quality assurance and control.⁹

As we consider Banking of the Future, we should also recognise that poor apps are highly likely to influence consumer sentiment. In an environment of social media, poor app performance can result in criticism spreading both quickly and widely. For an industry mainly based on trust, bearing in mind our changing understanding of the meaning of money as explained earlier, there’s a perceived correlation between whether an app is trustworthy and therefore whether the bank itself is trustworthy. One might argue that if a bank (or any organisation) can’t be trusted to look after its technology, then how can it be expected to look after the customer’s financial assets?

The other side of the coin may well be that of ease of use of apps. An ability to quickly set up a new account with an overdraft limit could potentially lead to an epidemic of debt, especially amongst younger people whose circumstances force them to spend unbudgeted money. The necessity of paying bills and the temptations of living in a consumer society make it all too easy to rack up a debt without due caution. They may also suffer from undue peer group pressure. One example might be that of being invited to a friend’s wedding, perhaps in some remote and glamorous location, which tempts them to turn ‘a duty’ into an expensive holiday A similar problem arises with the ease of taking out a new credit card and then passing on the debt balance from one card to another.
