## Branding and Human-Centred Design

Brand is often associated with the quality of customer experience, and may be either a positive or a negative association. As ‘customer experience’ seems to be one of the buzzword expressions, it might be helpful to see what at least one banking group was asking of its experts in that field.

A recently (and almost certainly now-filled) job vacancy advertised for a position in what they described as a ‘group roadmap and business design’ team. The task required the individual to:

- Challenge and shape existing thinking and align portfolios across the group.
- Identify how developing technology could change the way the company interacts with customers and colleagues and influence future business models and architecture.
- Define what the overall strategy means in practice and develop frameworks to allow Value Streams, Enterprise Enablers and Major Core Programmes to achieve strategic business and architectural objectives.
- Support the mobilisation of key elements of the portfolio.
- Coordinate across Business Design, IT Architecture and Human-Centred Design (HCD).

‘Human-centred design’ is an approach to problem solving that develops solutions to problems by taking the human perspective in all parts of the decision-making process. Its international standard, ISO 9241-210:2010(E), states:

> Human-centred design is an approach to interactive systems development that aims to make systems usable and useful by focusing on the users, their needs and requirements, and by applying human factors/ergonomics, usability knowledge, and techniques. This approach enhances effectiveness and efficiency, improves human well-being, user satisfaction, accessibility and sustainability; and counteracts possible adverse effects of use on human health, safety and performance.

Applicants were also required to be:

- Comfortable working in an ambiguous and rapidly changing environment, managing a diverse set of priorities
- Highly aware of the overall strategic landscape with customer focus, diplomacy and strong stakeholder management ability
- Experienced in shaping and managing complex portfolios, with a proven record
- ‘Fascinated’ by the way technology is being used, and understand the challenges of the banking industry
- Very knowledgeable about banking products, processes and portfolios¹⁷

It’s an example in isolation but one that hopefully reinforces the interface between the service offering of this particular (un-named) bank, the consumer (or ‘human’) experience, and the use of technology. Usefully, the vacancy advertisement breaks down the traditional silo mentality between the different departments and especially places the use of developing technology as being a key enabler.

## To Brand or to Debrand?

Tom Peters’ book Re-Imagine!¹⁸ is thought by some to be a ‘Marmite’ publication. (Marmite is a British yeast-based spread of the Australian Vegemite school of appeal, with the slogan ‘Either you love it, or hate it’). First published in 2004, his book falls into that category for reviewers not only for its rather radical layout but also for its provocations, one of which is that of ‘branding’.

He reminds us that branding is an ‘emotional connection point’, a ‘powerful connecting experience’ and that it is within the grasp of organisations to define their own brand ‘based on what they feel deeply about’. His frustration is that we tend to see branding as an external image rather than being a reflection on who (or what) the business really is, or what its personality, values and identity are.

Some basic questions are asked of companies:

- Who are you?
- Why are you here?
- How are you unique?
- How can you make a dramatic difference?
- Who cares (do you care?)

Branding and leadership are described as ‘Siamese twins’ with the inspiring leader setting the template for the ‘brand promise’. This in itself creates a challenge, especially as the flag waving, ‘follow me’ sort of leadership, as we traditionally know it, is increasingly being recognised as an unsuitable model for this new AI-infused future. After all, it might be argued, which of our leaders has experience of this new era of data, analytics and AI? Perhaps the leadership of the future must inevitably come from Gen Z rather than Gen X or Gen Y, in some form of ‘leap frogging’ of generations?

Others describe leadership as a function of power, rather than a role or position, and believe that the leadership role can move from person to person, dependent on the nature of the circumstances.¹⁹

If the leadership model is to change, then perhaps also is our understanding of brand. If branding is the way that organisations think, feel and behave, then this must in some way be embedded into its DNA in a sustainable sort of way. In the context of the Future of Banking and the impact of advanced analytics and AI, what might branding in the future entail? The impact of data, analytics and AI is considered to be disruptive but perhaps this will also lead to disruption in the brand proposition.

According to brand managers at Fast Company, the future of branding will be that of ‘debranding’. They suggest that the smoothest branding, called ‘native branding’, was mainly to hide the commercial intention, aimed at creating a product familiarity and giving the brand the impression of reliability and being indispensable. Backlashes against branding – which often occur following overt product placement, for example – have increasingly led to subtler approaches, which they call ‘camouflage branding’. This is, in effect, subliminal messaging. Consumers, they say, are more likely to follow a happy, undemanding brand instead of bonding with real people and real-world problems. A brand will never ask you for help. It won’t confront you with difficulties or opposing views.’

‘Debranding’ effectively represents a backlash against consumerism. It is about the experience rather than the product, about people and about places rather than purchasing. It is said to be the ideal application for the networked age. ‘The brand that screams the loudest no longer commands the most attention; the one that offers something genuinely useful does’.

It’s a callout for quality, not quantity, for value rather than price. ‘The only information on packaging will be features such as origin, the intentions of the maker, the production process, and the environmental impact.’ Ultimately the argument is that the consumer no longer needs brands but quality, and that they will be able to find this quality more easily in a networked society.²⁰

Foresight Alliance take a different angle on branding,²¹ suggesting that by 2030:

- ‘AI Branding’ as a concept will emerge where intelligent customised brands actively seek out their customers in digital micro-segmentation. As a result, advertising will be transformed into a distribution channel for communication.
