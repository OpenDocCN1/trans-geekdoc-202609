
- 4. Personal or physical power where we might be physically or mentally intimidated by an individual or group.
- 5. Personal self-awareness which is a sort of ‘Zen’ approach to whether an individual exerts or subsumes to other powerful influence, such as externally (bullying) or internally (depression)
- 6. Power of love where there is (irrational) emotional influence.

Focusing more specifically on leaders, they need to:

- **1. Understand the key drivers of good and bad decisions, and the difference between decisions and outcomes.**
  It might seem obvious to us what is a good decision or not, but it’s often more complex than that. *Psychology Today* defines a good decision as ‘one that is made deliberately and thoughtfully, considers and includes all relevant factors, is consistent with the individual’s philosophy and values, and can be explained clearly to significant others’.

  They suggest that a ‘good decision’ is different to a good outcome, as ‘the outcome of any decision can be affected by factors which the decision maker cannot control.’⁷

- **2. Be able to create the right environment for effective decision-making, which is more than just the right physical environment.**
  This also includes having access to the right information (on which the decision is being made), the right tools (De Bono’s ‘Thinking Hats’ approach, for example), and having the right people involved in the process.⁸

- **3. Understand the key barriers to effective decision-making.**
  This might include psychology (the science of behaviour and mind including conscious and unconscious reaction); sociology (the study of social relationships and interactions); anthropology (the study of human behaviour including what the ‘norms’ are, which extends to culture and the use of language); political science (which deals with governance, political power and distribution of resources); and economics (the study of the production, consumption and transfer of wealth).

- **4. Understand behaviour decision-making science.**
  This includes the science of judgement and decision-making which extends to ‘cognitive science’, rational thinking, and decision-making tendencies (such as underweighting or overweighting of probabilities).

It all sounds a pretty daunting set of skills and competences to attain and maintain. Some leaders have the ability to do many of these things intuitively, but others require formal and continuous training. One key question to address is to what degree these skills will be supplemented or even replaced by robotics or AI systems.

Most seem to agree that it’s unlikely that leadership will be replaced by robotics. They argue that:

- There are too many variables, and machines don’t know the difference between the important ones and the less important ones.
- Humans still have the ability to take a broad view of not only what is happening but what will happen and how this will have an impact in the future.
- Human executives have developed the ability to make informed decisions based on limited information, and it is unlikely that this will be readily replicated in a data-driven business environment.

In the article ‘Can Artificial Intelligence Replace Executive Decision Making?’⁹ the author suggests that ‘there will be a mix of good, bad, and ugly’ as executives are relieved of the mundane tasks but may find challenges from ‘automated virtual workers’ whose dominant purpose may be to deliver data-driven and therefore logical decisions. ‘Ugly’ in this context represents the possible failure of an executive of the future to be able to deliver any added value to the decision-making process.

‘Mastery’ seems to be a rather different proposition. In his 2008 book, as mentioned earlier, Malcolm Gladwell looked at people he described as ‘outliers’, that is, those who are extraordinarily proficient in a skill or subject. He suggests that by practising 90 minutes per day for 10 years (in other words, having 10,000 hours of study in a particular area), one in effect reaches the ‘tipping point’ for ‘greatness’.¹⁰

Others suggest that 10,000 hours isn’t a magic number. Tim Ferriss says, ‘I believe you can become world class in only 6 months’, a concept he describes as ‘meta learning’¹¹ and emphasises quality of learning over quantity. (The idea of becoming an expert in any subject and achieving ‘mastery’ in 6 months seems much more attractive than in 10 years.)

The mood is one of more focused learning programmes, which comprise:

- 1. Feedback loops for continuous assessment to identify weaknesses and areas for improvement
- 2. Deliberate practice of skill subsets where individuals knowingly go beyond their current abilities
- 3. Becoming a teacher on the premise that if you can’t explain it to others then you don’t understand it yourself

## LEADERSHIP IN AN AI-INFUSED AGE

So what’s the implication in an AI-infused age? Firstly, let’s think about different styles of leadership. Styles of leadership seem to align strongly to personality type, and one company, Chron, suggests that there are seven leadership ‘types’:¹²

- The autocratic leader adopts a command and control approach, giving orders and expecting prompt execution. Company ownership is often an issue. In this case the leader pushes his or her employees hard but there is sometimes a price to be paid by the leader in terms of employee loyalty and commitment.
- ‘Laissez-faire leadership’ (from the French for ‘do as you will or choose’) is very ‘hands off’, unstructured and dependent on the employee to be self-motivated and with the right sense of self-imposed direction.
- Transformative leadership is often viewed as being inspirational, where the leader has a big vision they can effectively communicate; it is both inspirational and charismatic.
- Hands-on and participation leadership is very collaborative in style and application but is sometimes criticised for not being adequately decisive.
- Transactional leadership is driven by process and environment, and is particularly ‘results oriented’, such as in a contact centre environment.
- Bureaucratic leadership is driven by discipline, rules and regulations. In such a situation, leaders find it hard to inspire or motivate, yet must try to get employee engagement and enthusiasm in a very constrained operating environment.
- In situational leadership, the style changes to meet the situation and can range from a collaborative approach to one that is overtly directive.

As we reflect from time to time on our own personal careers, it’s usually possible to categorise some of our organisational hierarchy into one or more of the above types. Perhaps we flatter some of them as being ‘transactional leaders’ as opposed to ‘managers’. After all, what actually is a ‘manager’? The Oxford English Dictionary (OED) simply describes a manager as ‘A person responsible for controlling or administering an organisation or group of staff’, perhaps without having (or needing to have) the apparently critical capabilities of vision, charisma and the rest.

Does a manager become a ‘transactional’ or ‘bureaucratic’ leader, if ever? At the end of the day, perhaps we are just playing with words.

Is leadership (in all its forms), mastery, management and the rest simply a set of semantics? If so, where does that leave the idea of ‘thought leaders’ and ‘futurists’? The OED describes a ‘thought leader’ as ‘one whose views on a subject are taken to be authoritative and influential’,¹³ and defines a ‘futurist’ as ‘a person who studies the future and makes predictions about it based on current trends’.¹⁴

There seems to be a great deal of ambiguity and doublespeak, so it’s important to understand:

- What is the character of the people who open the door to our future?
- Who are our leaders who will translate that vision of the future into practical applications?
- Who are the masters of their subjects who can guide us forward without undue reliance on technology and jargon?
- Who are those leaders (but could equally be called managers) who will operationally implement and deliver these transformations, to budget and to time?

It’s possible to flirt with, review and revisit existing leadership and management theory to our heart’s content. There’s no shortage of information written on management and leadership theory and no shortage of courses that time and money can be spent on. The really big issue is to what degree (if at all) current management and leadership theory is relevant for the new age of AI. Almost all the management theory currently in practice was valid for a pre-AI era but we have to ask ourselves whether it is still valid not only for today, but especially for tomorrow.
