
In the financial services sector, MasterCard are already experimenting with systems that validate payment based on taking a selfie. According to commentator TechCrunch, biometrics for payment authentication are also gaining attention following the launch of the ‘Apple Pay’ fingerprint-powered system for iPhones and the Apple Watch. This innovation isn’t confined to Apple; Android devices are also increasingly including fingerprint readers that align to payments systems such as Android Pay and Samsung Pay.18

## Thumbprint Recognition

The idea of fingerprints being used for identification is longstanding. There’s evidence of them being used as a form of identification as far back as in Babylonian days of the second millennium BCE, when they were used as imprints into clay to validate contracts.19 By 265 BCE, they were also being used by the Chinese as a form of contractual identity, and fingerprints (with handprints and footprints) were taken as ‘scene of crime evidence’ even at that time.20

By then the fact that they were unique had yet to be established. This wasn’t recognised until the nineteenth century when fingerprint records started to be kept, perhaps the earliest version of biometric data storage. In the US, the FBI manage the ‘Integrated Automated Fingerprint Identification System’, which apparently holds data on 51m criminal subject records and 1.5m civil (non-criminal) records. The US-VISIT programme, which captures fingerprint data on those entering the country, also holds records on over 5 million non-US visitors.

Finger and thumbprints have inspired an entire scientific field known as dactyloscopy. Prints are unique because of the special characteristics of what are called ‘fingerprint ridges’, which not only include patterns such as arches, loops and whorls but also much smaller features experts call minutiae. In essence, a fingerprint or thumbprint is a record of the ‘friction ridges’ or ‘epidermal ridges’ – that is, the raised parts of the finger. These ridges not only provide grip but convey information to sensory nerves beneath the skin. Fingerprints are difficult to alter and are enduring, meaning that they don’t change during the lifespan of the user even if there have been efforts by criminals from time to time to remove personal fingerprints using ‘superglue’.

Automated scanners aim to measure not only the location of the friction ridge but also the depth between the peaks and the valleys of the ridge. This information can be distorted by applying pressure to the fingers. For this reason the idea of ‘contactless scanning’ of fingerprints has been introduced through which 3D information about the fingerprint can be obtained. This area has been the subject of criticism. Simple scanning devices can be conned using gel moulds, and in 2013 a group of hackers bypassed the thumbprint validation system of Apple’s iPhone 5S Touch ID fingerprint sensor (which Apple have now seemingly corrected).21

Nevertheless, it’s an area that continues to receive attention. Modern techniques capture a digital image of the finger, which is processed into a biometric template, which is then stored and matched. These techniques can be optical, capacitive (that is, managing electrical resistance across micro points of the skin), thermal, ultrasonic, or even by considering the ‘microelectromechanical’ component of the skin, which considers such complex combinations as electrostatic charges, magnetic elements and surface fluid mechanics.

It’s clearly an exceptionally complex field and, in the relevance to banking, the use of finger and thumbprints is already being considered as a replacement to signatures. More importantly, as the banking industry becomes increasingly digital, consumers have the growing personal challenge of managing (and recalling) multiple passwords. The ability of a consumer to access information using biometric information becomes all the more attractive, although the flip side is that a potential vulnerability starts to emerge: if one site is hacked using faked biometrics, then perhaps the entire digital ecosystem of the consumer is placed at risk.22,23

Biometrics are an inevitable part of the Bank of the Future. For example, Windows Hello aims to stretch biometric information into other device authentication, ‘With Windows Hello,’ suggests Microsoft’s marketing, ‘you’ll be able to just show your face, or touch your finger, to new devices running Windows 10 and be immediately recognized’.24

## Palm Vein Recognition

Palm vein recognition is a form of biometric identification that takes into account the layout of the veins below the skin in the palm of the hand and the amount of oxygen-carrying protein in the blood (haemoglobin). By using near-infrared scanning of the veins, it’s possible to create a map of the veins that provides a unique identifier of the individual, especially as the way that the blood is pumped through the hands (and the rest of the body) is unique to the individual.

This is also known as ‘vascular biometrics’ or ‘vascular technology’, and developers point to unique advantages:

- The contactless nature of the recognition process reducing health and safety issues, especially where identification was required in a public place
- 100% accuracy in terms of identification
- The resilience of the system to skin surface problems, such as dryness, wetness or roughness
- The need to have the user’s consent to provide that physical scan of the hand, unlike with facial recognition, which can be done remotely without permission, for example
- Reduced fraud potential as the consumer needs to be physically present

It’s an interesting area. One downside may be that it would be possible to scan a person who was unconscious, perhaps undergoing medical treatment or worse and obtain access to their accounts.25

## WEALTH MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS AND APPS

Countless companies have websites and apps for wealth management with real-time updates of financial news, tracking of stocks and interactive highly visual graphs and tables. Growing expectations from digital natives require all too frequently that their stock portfolios can be managed anytime, anyplace and anywhere.

There’s increased interest in the use of robo-advisors and social trading – that is, sharing of trading actions and ideas that allow investors to follow one another’s actions.

Normally, traders might want to use approaches such as ‘technical analysis’ or ‘fundamental analysis’ looking at financial performance, leadership and dividend history to make investment decisions, but peer activity may be another interesting and relevant consideration. Following the crowd is a well-recognised but sometimes risky approach.

In FOREX (Foreign Exchange) trading, broader economic indicators such as GDP and employment rates might also be key factors, as might public and social media. The use of wealth management systems means that inexperienced traders (and some experienced ones as well) can let more experienced investors make decisions and follow them. What value does the inexperienced trader bring in these circumstances?

Social media or social trading may increasingly play a part in wealth management. Social trading is similar to ‘copy trading’ and ‘mirror trading’. Copy trading connects the portfolio of the user to that of the expert, and can be done automatically. Mirror trading is a method used in FOREX trading similar to copy trading.26

There is no shortage of advice available for those who wish to use automated wealth management sites. Suggested benefits of these systems, especially social trading, include:

- Greater accessibility
- Ease of usage
- Lower costs
- Lower minimum investment
- Reduction in learning curve of usage
- Ability to learn to trade in a risk-free sandbox

There is concern that whilst wealth apps and robo-advisors might have benefit in a bull market, traditional wealth and investment managers suggest that they specifically add value in a bear market where there is considerable uncertainty and where customers need more ‘hand holding’, or where a customer has particular needs such as tax management. Increasingly there is a move towards investment managers having ‘multi-distribution channels.’27

One particular area of interest in the topic of robotic control is that of chatbot wealth advisors. These are typically available through social media and mobile devices and, with permission, can delve into a customer’s financial situation and make recommendations. If the customer is happy, then investments are made on their behalf. To do this accurately, the chatbot needs access to the customer’s account, usually on a read-only basis so that it cannot gain direct access to any transaction authorities. Principally these are e-wallets, usually with a banking backer, but not normally covered by financial compensation schemes (i.e. in the UK).

One system, called Plum, is linked through the user’s Facebook site and can make recommendations regarding savings based on the customer’s mood as indicated through the Facebook app. It even gives a ‘happy’ emoji to the customer to congratulate them for making a saving. Rather than pay interest, it puts the money aside into a peer-to-peer lending provider (‘RateSetter’), which usually offers a higher rate of return than traditional banking, but with higher risk.28
