The point of convergence is unclear. Bryan Moynihan, chairman and chief executive of Bank of America, is considered to have changed that organisation designated the number two US bank into ‘a technology company that happens to be a bank’.³

Moynihan might well be called a ‘digital banker’, meaning someone who not only understands the issues of both banking and technology but someone who can operationalise these capabilities and start to build strategies around them. It is not an easy journey. Although some suggest that the bank of the future will be a combination of both sets of skills in a converged team (and they are probably right in the short term), the true future of banking rests with a new profession of hybrid banking and tech/ analytical skills.

It isn’t likely to be an easy journey for those who will be successful. It was suggested that as much as 80% of routine functions would be automated, and that there would be many fewer people in the banking workforce. Those who remain will require exceptional competences and capabilities, but on the up side their rewards are likely to be phenomenal. What does it require for a banker to understand AI and machine learning?

A recent accelerated learning AI course⁴ by a London-based Level 39 start-up Thalesians is aimed at post-grad-, Master’s- and PhD-level applicants, and suggests as a minimum requirement not only an understanding but the ability to apply the skills outlined in Table 2.

With a focus on finance, economics and engineering, Thalesians specifically focus on *time series data*, which they describe as sequences of ‘timestamped updates, arriving in chronological order, on the state of a particular process evolving over time’. They claim the creation of a new science built on the foundation of quantitative finance, algorithmic trading, high-frequency trading and markets microstructure, machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), deep reinforcement learning (DRL), AI, reactive programming, and big data, which they call *neocybernetics*.
