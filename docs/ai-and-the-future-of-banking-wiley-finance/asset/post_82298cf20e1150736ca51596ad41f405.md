Banks have transformed themselves from being financial intermediaries to becoming the creators (and sometimes destroyers) of money. Beyond that, through their corporate desire to be innovative, they’ve also become the consumers of money as well, especially from public funds during the various bailouts. But enough has already been written about the problems that led to the collapse of Lehman’s and RBS and it’s time to move on, in every sense.

We cannot understand banking and especially the future of banking without thinking about the concept of money and also its future. There’s no need to go over old ground other than to reflect on the fact that many previous books (perhaps all of them) were written in a predigital age when AI was simply a science fiction notion. Only by understanding what we really mean by both the terms ‘money’ and ‘banking’, even in the current age, can we throw some light on what an analytically and AI-infused future might look like.

Perhaps the name of the game is not one of goal setting and reaching objectives but rather a combination of:

- Vision setting, which provides stakeholders with some form of mission statement
- Development of competences both at business and personal level
- Organisational rethinking to allow participants to be more agile and responsive to the current times

This sort of relatively radical approach in isolation might be fine if businesses are operating in a vacuum or perhaps for a start-up that needs to think on its feet. But will be of cold comfort to more established enterprises such as banks, which have a duty not only to customers but also to stockholders, and who have the shadow of regulation above them.

There is still a need for banks to have strategic objectives, but the nature of these strategic directives is necessarily changing, not only as a result of the digital age but of the impact of advanced technologies.¹

In their 2018 article ‘Six Digital Growth Strategies for Banks’,² McKinsey suggest six opportunities for what they describe as ‘navigating the chaos’. These are:

- 1. Expanding beyond their core business of banking into non-core activities such as home services, healthcare, mobile devices and facilities management
- 2. Creation of a financial supermarket; in other words, a form of cost comparison approach
- 3. Extending the value chain such as the provision of advice beyond that solely connected with core banking services
- 4. Monetising the data or, in other words, using analytics to extract value from data they are already holding within their enterprise
- 5. Becoming a financial factory by renting out their back-office functions to other smaller organisations or start-ups, and in doing so, turning a cost centre into a profit centre
- 6. Becoming what McKinsey call a ‘digital attacker’, by employing a digital go-to-market approach or new innovative business models, in order to attain market share

They suggest four key action points:

- 1. Identification of which strategy best aligns to current resources and market opportunities
- 2. The need to prioritise rather than pursue all these opportunities simultaneously
- 3. The need to understand what organisational structures and governance is needed to achieve any of these objectives
- 4. The need to understand what capabilities are needed to deliver on these objectives

Beyond this, McKinsey also suggest a sense of urgency. For banks, they say, this means to attack the market rather than defend it, but this is arguably easier said than done.
