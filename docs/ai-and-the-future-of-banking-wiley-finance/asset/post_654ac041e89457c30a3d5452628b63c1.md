There are tradeoffs to be made with respect to agility versus operational efficiency and cost reduction. For example, smaller banks might have to be prepared to outsource their back-office services to a larger (and more established) competitor, technology company or perhaps to a ‘pure’ outsourcing provider to ensure that they have the dynamism to compete in the digital age. These decisions are important not only in terms of operational cost but also in relation to the customer experience. With a great deal of outsourcing being offshore, language and dialect are often operational issues. In many cases, xenophobia aside, banking customers still tend to deal with human contacts at the other end of the line which they can easily understand. Perhaps ultimately chat bots will remove that unintentional bias.

## STRATEGY AND IMPERATIVES

### Strategy

The expression ‘strategy’ (from the Greek στρατηγία, strategia, which means ‘art of troop leader; office of general, command, generalship’) started off as a military term, comprising a plan to achieve goals under conditions of uncertainty by using several subsets of skills or ‘tactics’. The word ‘strategy’ in business first came into usage in the 1960s but the reference to planning during conditions of uncertainty seems perfectly fitted to this current age.³

In fulfilling their obligations to investors, banks typically are relatively transparent with respect to their strategic objectives. The information is usually provided in investor reports and updates as well as in newsfeeds.

For technology vendors, for example, the need to understand the strategic objective of a bank (or any organisation) is critical as it allows alignment of the vendor’s technology (what they are trying to sell) with the bank’s strategic intent. If there is no alignment, then the chance of a successful sale is reduced. This approach is normally called ‘strategic selling’, and it is a well-known approach to complex (‘non-tactical’) selling. Strategic selling allows the vendor to engage with the customer at an earlier stage, usually at a higher level, and usually with a greater chance of success.⁴

Start-up ‘Fintech’ companies – which we will consider more specifically later on – would be well advised to understand this approach, if only to understand how their newly created capabilities fit into the wider picture of their prospective client rather than hoping that the start-up’s new technology can find a place in the organisation.

Banks would do well to better understand what is happening to High Street retailers whose traditional retail processes are being replaced by on-line shopping. Retail premises are increasingly being used for ‘showrooming’, that is, locations where potential customers look at and try goods before buying them on-line, usually at a cheaper price.

As a result, retailers are increasingly creating what they describe as a ‘shopping experience’. For example, the Mercedes ‘Me’ store in Sanlitun, Beijing, aims to provide ‘an integrated and multi-sensory customer experience journey, encompassing elements of hospitality (food & beverage), the arts (entertainment) and lifestyle (premium retail).’ In addition, the second floor of this two-storey building provides an upmarket bar and restaurant, both contributing to Mercedes brand positioning themselves as ‘lifestyle partners.’⁸

Similar Mercedes Me stores are opening in Hamburg, Munich, Milan and Tokyo and are usually found in locations of other luxury labels such as Prada and Gucci.⁹ Mercedes aim to stretch their brand as far as practically possible, including shoes, clothing and backpacks. The topic of branding is critical to the Mercedes offering, and we will look at this more specifically in Chapter 6.

Banks may need to figure out a way of showrooming their financial services. In this context, therefore, what should be our ‘banking experience’, especially when many still associate their past experiences with distrust, complexity and uncertainty?

An ‘experience’ is usually described as a combination of physical, mental and emotional triggers, some of which are conscious and others unconscious. We even describe a spiritual engagement as a religious experience. Customer experience (more commonly abbreviated to CX) is defined as the sum of the interactions that a customer has during the entire customer journey.¹⁰

According to customer experts Ameyo, there are four key steps to improve customer experience in banking:

- 1. Plan, by having a set of defined goals and targets, all appropriately segmented.
- 2. Strategise, by having a customer experience strategy to achieve those targets.
- 3. Improve the experience by designing and delivering experiences.
- 4. Measure, using a data-driven approach to continually refine the experience.
