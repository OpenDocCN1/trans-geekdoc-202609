But isn’t the idea of client servicing, with its rather static reporting, now ‘old hat’? What might the future look like in this sector? Invariably, it will also be affected by the digital transformation of the banking industry, rather than run parallel to or to bypass it. Investment and asset managers are already using advanced technology to anticipate changes in the stock market, so why shouldn’t these same technologies also apply to the client services sector? The essence of future client servicing is likely to be one of proactivity rather than reactivity.

But there is a potential danger especially as these capabilities are increasingly available on mobile devices. Asset managers constantly advise that the essence of successful asset management is to take a longer view and not to apply knee-jerk reactions. How easy will it be to resist such temptations to change or adjust the portfolio, if the predictive indicators forecast bad news on the horizon? Would such quick responses to sell, for example, become some form of self-fulfilling prophecy? As we increasingly automate the process, using advanced tools with in-built rules engines, how might those robotic fund managers show restraint, if restraint is needed? And if any in-programmed systematic self-restraint results in major financial losses, where might the burden of responsibility for those losses actually lie? It’s as complex a conundrum as that of the self-driving, or autonomous, car-making algorithmic decisions in real time about an impending accident, and what might comprise the least-damaging or injurious outcome. Increased automation in multiple business areas opens new issues of responsibility and legality.

Finally, in this subsection, we should also take a moment to reflect on the financial disparity that the banking industry is also trying to address. On the one hand is this growing number of ‘rich’, but on the other hand is that part of the global population at best where there has been no income growth, and at worst who are below the poverty line, and whose situation is dire. Because of their own difficult conditions, many have migrated from rural locations to the city, with the issue of urbanisation creating not only new problems, but adding to existing ones. They are the so-called ‘underbanked’ and perhaps for the banks provide new opportunities, which will be considered in Chapter 9.
