## 5. The Disruption of Sales and Trading

In 2014 the Volcker Rule, which was passed as part of the Dodd–Frank regulation, banned investment banks from making bets with their own capital, also known as ‘prop trading’. The rationale behind this was that if the bank was making bets with its own capital, it might take higher risks, and in doing so place the whole bank and the customers’ money at risk. As a result, investment banks can now make money from trading only by charging a commission on each executed trade. Trading revenues for investment banks has plummeted. Collectively JP Morgan, Citigroup, Bank of America, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley suffered a fall of 30% of trading revenue over a decade.

Because of this, the mood again is towards the use of automation, including blockchain. Goldman Sachs’ automated trading platform, Marquee, was launched in 2018 with a view to automate processes, reduce staff, reduce cost and improve profitability.

So what does the future look like for investment banks? Like all organisations, they are taking a good hard look at what is profitable and what is not. Effective use of financial analytics becomes a critical success factor in terms of making those important decisions. Key options include:

The key issue is that of the ability both of the big investment banks and perhaps of the industry as a whole to transform itself. There is a temptation to think that in the banking industry it is usually the retail side of ‘the house’ that will change but this is unlikely to be the case. As with all industries and professions, the advent of advanced analytics and AI will lead to new roles and business models emerging. It will no longer be sufficient to digitise existing business models and hope for the best but rather that the investment banking industry must prepare itself for turbulence of the first order.

One particular area of interest is in sales, with banks such as BNP investing heavily in machine learning research. Another aspect has been that of trying to explain to clients how the algorithms work as they are principally ‘black boxes’ with hidden algorithms and are complex by virtue of the number of inputs. Elsewhere, regulators are understanding how with much greater granularity of data it is increasingly possible to discover who might be the beneficiaries of a transaction, and in doing so, uncover more market abuse. ## CONCLUSION

The Bank of the Future will not only embrace AI and other related applications such as chatbots and advice bots but will also involve physical transformation. Whilst retaining some semblance of brand identity, future retail banks may look more like lounges, cafés or even shops. In the same way that high-end retailers have reinvented themselves by replacing shops with showrooms, allowing purchasers to look, touch and then buy online, banks will follow a similar route, although the approach will need to be different due to the intangible nature of banking products and services. After all, as considered in the prologue of this book, what banks are likely to do in the future is mainly to manage financial data and digital assets.

Whilst a High Street presence may still have some brand reinforcement value, the increased use of robotics and virtual services undermines the need for this. In any event, who is really certain what the High Street of the future will look like anyway? Because of this, some possible scenarios are provided. Any or all of these might happen, perhaps some form of hybrid, perhaps something completely different. Do not rule out talking ATMs.

Banks will increasingly extend their ecosystem and ‘universality’, and correspondingly extend their brand footprint. The ability to do this not only will allow bidirectional data flow but will also demand improved controls, governance and customer sensitivity.

Investment banks will also become increasingly automated as the shift towards self-service and robotic advice becomes commonplace. There will still be a role for the human advisor, but one that works more in conjunction with expert systems. Human advisors will need to be clear about the value they bring to the equation. It could be a difficult conversation.
