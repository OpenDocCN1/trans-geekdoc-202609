CB Insights identify what they consider to be five key disruptive factors (see Figure 8.20):

+   1. **Disruption of the IPO:** Top companies seeking to raise capital are no longer going via the traditional investment bank route, which connects them with investors, helps determine prices and creates legal frameworks. Rather, they are dealing directly with venture capitalists and seeking alternative solutions such as ‘direct public offerings’ (DPO). The companies that follow the traditional route by using investment banks are using these alternatives to negotiate lower fees, leading to lost revenue on the part of the bank. Goldman Sachs, for example, identified that about half of the 127 IPO steps could be done by computers, reducing labour costs, increasing speed and maintaining profitability.
+   2. **Disruption of M&A:** With M&A activity accounting for ‘about 34% of bank revenue’ according to Dealogic, the emergence of new specialist ‘boutique’ banks and the impact of technology is affecting not only the topline figures but also fee levels. New technologies are allowing companies to undertake ‘DIY M&A’ where buyers are algorithmically matched with sellers, which avoids the research and relationship-driven activities normally associated with investment banks.
+   3. **Disruption of Asset Management:** There is a gradual move away from investment bankers to specialist asset managers. This is as a result of increased regulation and the ability of the specialists to provide greater returns at lower fees. As a result, larger investment houses are refocussing their activities on wealth management. There is more widespread use of robo-advisors and greater automation will inevitably become more commonplace.

+   Goldman Sachs, for example, have launched their digital advisor for savings, known as ‘Marcus’, which actively focusses on the millennial market. Notwithstanding, a recent 2019 survey by GlobalData says, ‘Only 5% of UK millennial investors use robo-advice services, as human expertise is still preferred over AI technology.’⁴⁰ That’s not to say that this level of interest and engagement is consistent in all geographies, but it does give an indication. The argument is that ‘traditional wealth managers have experienced multiple recessions and periods of market volatility, proving they are able to weather a storm and with a potential market downturn on the horizon, [but] robo-advisors may not be ready to tackle the risks to client investments that come with it.’

+   4. **The Disruption of Equities Research:** When MiFID II banned the ‘bundling of research and trade execution’, requiring them to be priced and sold separately, this rendered this traditional function within investment banks redundant.
