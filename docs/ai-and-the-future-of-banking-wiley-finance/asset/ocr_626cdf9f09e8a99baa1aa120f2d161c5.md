Modern speech recognition systems not only do this but also take into account the possibility of gaps between words. One of the leaders in this space was a Russian mathematician called Andrey Marcov, whose system of analysis is known as the ‘Hidden Marcov Model’ (HMM), ‘hidden’ because the system of analysis sits in the background of the system.

4. Artificial neural networks. This is where AI-type systems can reliably recognise patterns and word sounds. Whilst HMM has been the backbone of speech recognition, the use of systems which mimic the human brain through internal ‘connections’ has also allowed the recognition of speech patterns. These are known as ‘Artificial Neural Networks’ (ANN) and are sometimes used together with HMM. It’s easy to understand the complexity of the challenge.15

Today’s personal systems make speech or voice recognition more commonplace and most are already familiar with systems such as Siri, Alexa or Cortana, for example. Most personal devices already have these capabilities built into the system, so rather than typing a question into a search engine, the only action required is to speak into the device. Admittedly, there are often issues with background noise but it is possible to incorporate filters into the recording process, eliminating ambient sound much in the same way that top-end headphones eliminate aircraft noise. Perhaps the biggest barrier is the user themselves and their reluctance to ‘talk’ to a system. With time, that attitude will change.

When we consider the Bank of the Future, we should imagine that screens and keyboards will become increasingly obsolete and that our interaction with the banking system will be mainly voice-driven. Even if we improve the accuracy of recognition, it’s likely that users will still remain cautious when it comes to financial matters. How quickly will it be before payments are made to a third party or a transfer of funds based on a voice command only? How will that instruction be tracked? Where will the governance around ‘mistaken verbal instruction’ rest?

On the other hand, can’t an intelligent system query an ‘odd’ or seemingly incorrect instruction? And if the cost benefit of using voice recognition or indeed any other system outweighs the risk, can’t some pricing be built in for the risk, and if so why should consumers feel constrained? Can’t fraud analysis advance at the same pace as biometric analysis?

If AI is being properly considered in the context of banking, initially in retail banking but extending to wealth management, then the increasing importance of more effective voice recognition capabilities should not be ruled out.
