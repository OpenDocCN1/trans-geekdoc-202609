Coupled with this is likely to be the emergence of contextual analytics. Banking organisations will become better at knowing and optimising their own performance but unless consideration is given to what is happening outside their own organisation – for example, amongst their competitors – then these viewpoints are being made in a vacuum. The American scientist Alan Kay expressed it succinctly: ‘Context is worth 80 IQ points.’¹⁰

In the cold light of day, two key objectives need to be adopted by bankers:

+   1. First, outperform direct competitors.
+   2. Second, achieve the strategic objectives of the organisation.

To do one and not the other is a job only partly completed. Often (but not always) these two key objectives go hand in hand. As some banks focus all the more on softer measures of success – for example, engagement with the community or responding to social needs – success may increasingly also be measured by consumer sentiment and brand recognition as well as by profitability.

# Extracting Value from Data

Data in either its structured or unstructured forms does not naturally flow into analytic outcomes, which usually take the form of reports, predictions or recommended actions, but is dependent on intermediate processes. These exist between the data and the analytics, and might generally be known as the ‘plumbing’ of the systems. This essential process allows all organisations to extract value or ‘monetise’ the data. (See Figure 3.7.)

How this is done in practice is a matter for technical experts, but in simple terms the raw data needs to be captured, then brought into the system where it is filtered, cleansed and usually stored. Massive volumes of data lend themselves to complex sorting systems or ‘landing zones’, most of which have their own language and jargon. Often a ‘datamart’ or ‘staging layer’ is created to ensure that an analytical outcome can be created relatively quickly. The process by which data is moved through the system is referred to as ETL, or ‘extract, transfer, load’.

There are other alternatives, such as ‘data warehouse appliances’, which provide a parallel processing approach and create a modular, scalable, easy-to-manage database system. These high-speed solutions allow very rapid computing power by providing an alternative to traditional linear processing, and often come with prebundled analytical and geospatial capabilities. In effect, this is a ‘plug and play’ approach to data and analytics. This serves as a reminder that, in the same way as was experienced with the internet in the early days, both organisations and individuals are increasingly pressing for computing power in the form of analytics to be provided ‘at speed’.
