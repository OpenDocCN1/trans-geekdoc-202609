(d) External: How to capture the vast amount of external data that might refer to the customer’s location, workplace and use of devices (for example) and to recognise and reflect in an offer what is particularly relevant to the customer.

# Data Reservoir

From this assortment of data, it becomes possible to create a single repository or ‘reservoir’ of relevant information, and in doing so, create a 360-degree view of the customer. Without effective data management and tooling, data collection by itself is irrelevant.

The various different types of data – structured, unstructured and from different sources – require a variety of technological tools to extract the information from the sources. The intention isn’t to comment on or to recommend these, but these data management tools might include, for example, the management of:

+   (a) Continual data feeds, to ensure that offers are both timely and relevant
+   (b) Content management, which effectively captures and sorts data
+   (c) Text analytics, which makes sense of free text and voice translations from contact centre interactions and web contact
+   (d) Device data analysis, such as locational details from smartphones
+   (e) Customer identification, which tracks single customer identification across multiple apps

All of these processes need to be enveloped around an appropriate culture of governance and security, especially as the bank is potentially vulnerable to infiltration as it metaphorically opens its doors to external sources of data.

# Creating Insight

In the previous chapter, we reminded ourselves that neither data nor analytical processes were the destination but rather it was what was done with this analysis that makes the difference. Fundamentally, the aim of this exercise is to establish with an increased degree of probability what is the customer’s propensity to buy a product or service. This insight will become increasingly important as banks offer Open Banking and de facto, facilitate increased mobility of customers between banking organisations. (See Chapter 9.) Better insight becomes critical, therefore, to allow banks to be able to identify which customers are most likely to leave so, in a way that is similar to the insurance problem of customer churn, appropriate attention can be given to matters of retention.

Additionally, as banks consider other business models such as ‘universal banking’ and ‘full-service banking’, which are in effect brand extensions, they will also need to gauge the propensity of their existing customers to purchase a broader financial (and even possibly non-financial) product set.
