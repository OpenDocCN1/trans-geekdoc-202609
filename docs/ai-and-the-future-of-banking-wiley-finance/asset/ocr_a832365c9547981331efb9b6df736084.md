Statisticians will also be concerned about the concept of ‘fat tails’ – that is, the situation in a ‘bell curve’ where there is a ‘skew’ in the normal statistical distribution. Also known as ‘heavy tails’, the phenomenon is also seen in physics and earth sciences as well as in economics. Black-Scholes is based on a normal distribution, but if there is a probabilistic skew, this can lead to distortions in the prediction. Therefore, fat tails are viewed to create higher levels of risk in terms of the accuracy of the prediction. As to when these fat tails occur, it’s suggested that at times of financial catastrophe such as the ‘bursting’ of the dot.com bubble, the data is ‘not well behaved’.³²

Because of this ‘bad behaviour’, statisticians such as Nassim Taleb wrote about the difficulty of prediction in his book *The Black Swan* (described as one of the 12 most influential books since World War II). His argument is that risk is very difficult to predict, and suggests that there is no real point in investing in what is a ‘medium risk’ – conservative investors should invest 80%–90% in extremely safe instruments, with the balance in high risk. Alternatively, high-risk investors should invest 80%–90% in high risk, and the balance in a potential fallback. He describes this as being ‘hyper conservative’ or ‘hyper aggressive’.

In the second edition of *The Black Swan*, Taleb writes, ‘One of the most common (but useless) comments I hear is that some solutions can come from “robust statistics”. I wonder how using these techniques can create information where there is none’.³³

It’s a daunting reaction to the idea that, using AI and machine learning, we can increasingly automate the investment process. Notwithstanding, UBS are already investigating ways to eliminate bias from the performance of portfolio managers and to automate some processes.³⁴

# Derivative Markets

Specifically in derivative markets, most of the ‘market making’ is done by dealers who undertake a series of ‘swaps’ either of interest rates or currency, which together with ‘hedging’ creates an effective and profitable portfolio.³⁵

It’s suggested that much of this can be done intuitively, but in a complex portfolio this is much too difficult, and the dealer will use a series of portfolio management techniques which are known as ‘Greek options’, or ‘Greeks’. These are a series of options, or predictions, which ‘capture the sensitivities of an option value to market parameters such as time, interest rate and volatility’. They are called ‘Greeks’ because the sensitivities of an option to various market parameters are labelled with letters from the Greek alphabet, such as delta, gamma and vega.³⁶

Beyond this complexity, investment managers also use a technique called ‘Value at Risk’, or VaR, which put simply is an assessment of the degree of confidence in a worst-case scenario. It’s a complex area and when pushed to the limits is known as ‘stress testing’.
