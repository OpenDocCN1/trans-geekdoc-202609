
## New Cyber Roles, New Cyber Responsibilities

According to Poppy Gustafsson, one of the Darktrace co-founders, their technology system emulates the human immune system, which ‘has an innate sense of self’ and by virtue of that can ‘identify anomalies such as bacteria and viruses.’ Their technology is claimed to replicate that approach, monitoring the ‘pattern of life’ or normal behaviour of every device on the network and identifying when the pattern changes at which point the safeguards respond ‘autonomously’.^29^

For banks and other major institutions to be able to respond effectively to the cyber threat it becomes critical for them to be able to call on capabilities that are broad, deep and usually of a global nature. To be able to provide comprehensive protection, experts need to be able to call on commercial solutions that provide know-how in areas such as identity and access management, data security, application security, network security and endpoint security.

Optimum solutions include pervasive encryption, security intelligence, analytics, cognitive and artificial intelligence, and forensic tooling. Experts need to be able not only to defend against cyber intrusion but also to process information on IT security events, vulnerabilities and provide detailed insight about potential threats to the virtual organisation, which include customers and the supply chain. Cyber and risk experts increasingly need to design and implement layered approaches to the ways an organisation’s data and infrastructure are managed. This includes controls and policy development to defend against cyber security attacks and threats on networks, end-user devices, servers, applications, data and cloud.

Beyond this, there are issues of culture. Cyber security is a matter for everyone. Internal risk experts must also ensure adequate training and education, not only for their own organisation but for the wider ‘virtual organisation’ in order to foster a culture of security awareness and responsibility.

At the outset of the book, the meaning of money was considered in the context of banking, which is a complicated industry with many functional roles, often wrapped in a matrix of overlapping (and sometimes duplicated) management and authority. From the outside it’s sometimes almost impossible to understand the complexity of the matrix and which career routes are most likely to allow an individual to climb the ladder of success. Whilst cybercrime experts are unlikely to be entirely replaced by AI systems, the future must invariably bring systematic support in some fashion to that particular skill set.

The challenges of cyber security continue to take the banking industry into new levels of complexity and difficulty. In addition to technology roles considered earlier, effective cyber security in the future may depend on two pivotal roles:

- **The Chief Security Officer:** The CSO will increasingly become a prominent position. The security of both the real and virtual enterprise will rest with them, in their hands. They will not only need to cope with current threats but also to anticipate future ones. It’ll be a tough job, requiring insight, imagination and technical expertise, and some of the best CSOs may come from the ‘hacking community’ itself.

- **Supply Chain Managers:** Their responsibility will not be simply to create and manage relationships within the bank’s ecosystem, extending the brand and the extent of customer services, but to do so without compromising the IT security of the bank’s own operations. Proactivity rather than reactivity will become the norm. Supply chain managers who have been traditionally trained in issues such as negotiation and contract management will find themselves needing to improve their IT capability a notch or two, or ten.

New ‘hybrid’ roles also emerging will comprise some form of blend between traditional supplier management and IT/cyber security management. New systems will need to be developed to help with these in this new landscape of responsibilities. There will be a need to supplement these new functions with, for example, AI-based systems to assist with issues of operational risk, including cybercrime management and fraud detection. ## AI FRAUD DETECTION IN BANKING

A significant element of risk management in banking relates to the matter of fraud, for which advanced analytics and AI potentially have solutions.

### Advanced Analytics in Fraud Detection

Generally, banks and other financial institutions already use both predictive and prescriptive analytics running pretrained algorithms to score transactions on their propensity to be fraudulent in nature. Beyond this, closed-loop analysis helps improve the algorithm through machine learning. The process by which this is done is that banking data experts or data scientists need to run a large volume of legitimate or fraudulent transactions through the system, and the machine learning model begins to be able to recognise which are fraudulent or not.

Typically, the triggers that might be involved could relate to products that the account owner would never buy or locations where an account owner was unlikely to shop. The system is ‘trained’ to recognise these inconsistencies and to either flag them as being suspicious or not allow the transaction to proceed. As more transactions are passed through the system, then it becomes ‘smarter’.

Banks are increasingly able to use these capabilities regardless of channel such as through mobile apps, ATMs or recurring payments such as a crowdfunding website. For example, crowdfunding site Patreon uses US tech company Stripe to process their payments. Their analytical process recognises ‘transactional fingerprints’ in payments or processes and identifies when anomalies occur. Location analytics is also playing a more significant role in recognising fraud, especially if the account holder allows the bank to access the user’s smartphone or mobile device, and to identify any personal data contained therein.

This appears to be an interesting trade-off. If the customer is prepared to share their personal information from their own device, then the banks are more able to prevent fraud and to safeguard the individual’s digital money. This seems to be a win-win that avoids problems such as forged identities, stolen identities and fraudulent transactions, but there is a downside, which is that of the individual trusting the bank to safeguard their personal information.

Fraud management and detection is a tempting marketplace for technology vendors. Like insurance fraud management, the return on investment for banks is both obvious and striking, with some companies claiming the ability to discover 30–50% more fraud with accuracy rates of up to 90%. Others claim a reduction in false positives of up to 80%, that is, an analytical result that wrongly (embarrassingly) indicates a particular customer behaviour is fraudulent. The aim must be to increase the accuracy rate from 90% to, say, 99% or higher, which will require even more advanced analytics, machine learning and AI. It is foreseeable that banks and software providers will be able to detect current types of fraud with 100% success. At that point detection devices either prove to be a deterrent for criminals or a stimulus for more sophisticated fraud, especially in a ‘new world’ of facial and voice recognition.

The probable reality is that as commerce moves closer towards an age of entirely digital cash, the amount of information that is or could be held about us creates a form of ‘money DNA’. The way that money is spent, on what, on whom and where, might create a set of unique and distinctive characteristics.

### AI and Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection is about the recognition of inconsistencies or inaccuracies in behaviour or payments. Carrying this out with any degree of accuracy requires the creation of a set of ‘baseline normalities’ against which current behaviours can be tested and analysed. There are at least four issues with this approach:

- Correctly establishing the baseline
- Ensuring that the baseline is adequately representative
- The management of ‘normal’ deviations, alternatively, is any ‘deviation’ unacceptable?
- The impact of anomaly detection on the ‘customer experience’

The expression ‘AI’ is often misused in place of ‘advanced analytics’ but that aside, there is a need to recognise not only the issues relating to fraud but also the multi-channel, multi-device, volatile nature of the commerce marketplace. The ability of advanced systems not only to take an enterprise-wide view but also relate empathically to the individual starts to become some form of a ‘Holy Grail’.^30^

When it came to light that a bank may have inadvertently enabled thousands of money-laundering transactions to pass across its platform as a result of a system error, SAS expert Colin Bristow reinforced the argument for the use of AI solutions in the payment process. ‘Anti-money laundering efforts need to be highly accurate and extremely fast – much faster and more accurate than a human could be’, says Bristow. ‘As finance increasingly goes digital, fraud is getting harder and harder to spot. Businesses must use advanced technologies like AI to stay on top of the rising tide – or fall prey to ever-more advanced fraudsters.’^31^

### Fraudulent Use of Data

One tempting question is that of understanding who is looking at whose data. It may not be fraudulent, especially if the information is already in the public domain, but may be being misused, as in the case of the Cambridge Analytica data scandal where personal data of millions of people was taken from Facebook and used without their consent for political purposes.^32^

The new Google Alphabet capability starts to answer that question, reports *Forbes*. “All the information is quickly indexed and organized,” they report, “then customers carry out searches on the data, like ‘Are any of my computers sending data to “X”?’ From there, cybersecurity investigators can start asking more and more questions, such as: ‘What kinds of information is “X” taking, when and how?’”^33^
