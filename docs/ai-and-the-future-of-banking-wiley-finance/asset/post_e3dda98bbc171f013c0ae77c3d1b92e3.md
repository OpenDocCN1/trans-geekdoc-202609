
- The product lifecycle, which is a function of the number of substitutes available and the peer behaviour.<sup>16</sup>

In banking, which clearly is increasingly commoditised with plenty of competition, it seems clear that the key issues here are brand (through advertising) and loyalty. At least one of these attributes – brand – can be manipulated to some degree through effective advertising and marketing. The other, loyalty, is more difficult to manage: banks should be careful not to antagonise ‘loyal customers’ by not reflecting that loyalty where, for example, they offer vastly preferential rates to new customers. Loyalty is difficult to win, but easy to lose. The impact of Open Banking, when it is fully implemented, may prove to be a major factor in the importance of price elasticity in the banking sector.

For over a decade, specialist banks, wealth managers and investment managers have been using analytics to provide better reporting and analysis so as to advise their clients of the changing value of their portfolio, using capabilities such as analysis of fund and asset management performance, across asset classes and geographies, and creating highly visual reporting dashboards with drill-down capabilities.

But isn’t the idea of client servicing, with its rather static reporting, now ‘old hat’? What might the future look like in this sector? Invariably, it will also be affected by the digital transformation of the banking industry, rather than run parallel to or to bypass it. Investment and asset managers are already using advanced technology to anticipate changes in the stock market, so why shouldn’t these same technologies also apply to the client services sector? The essence of future client servicing is likely to be one of proactivity rather than reactivity.

But there is a potential danger especially as these capabilities are increasingly available on mobile devices. Asset managers constantly advise that the essence of successful asset management is to take a longer view and not to apply knee-jerk reactions. How easy will it be to resist such temptations to change or adjust the portfolio, if the predictive indicators forecast bad news on the horizon? ## RISK MANAGEMENT

At the heart of banking, the effective use of analytics allows those in charge to have an understanding of their risk across risk classes. The topic of risk in itself provides sufficient content as to consume full-time and part-time courses, and invites the student to consider items such as:

- **Operational risk.** This relates to risk originating from failed process, software, individual behaviour and the impact of external events.
- **Credit risk**, both at a retail and wholesale/counterparty risk level. In terms of credit risk, effective analytics allows banks to ensure that ‘originating banks’, that is, banks where the credit line originates, are in line with planned diversification and strategies. This takes into account:
  - The characteristics of the originating bank and also the volumes involved
  - The ‘back-end’ performance, which takes into account the characteristics of the portfolio, previous losses and any delinquencies
  - Management of capital and reporting, according to Basel II, Basel III and subsequent iterations.
- **Market risk:** ‘Market risk is described as the risk of financial loss resulting from movements in market prices.’ The European Banking Authority (EBA) defines market risk as the risk of losses on-and-off the balance sheet that occur as a result of ‘adverse movements in market prices.’<sup>18</sup>
- **Investment and liquidity risk:** Liquidity is the capability of an entity to pay its debts without suffering catastrophic losses. So, liquidity risk is fundamentally about the quality and attractiveness of an investment, and whether it can be bought or sold quickly enough to prevent or minimise a loss. Expanding this, ‘Liquidity risk’ per se is the measure by which an entity – at either a corporate, organisational or institutional level – can meet its short-term debt obligations.<sup>19</sup>

The most effective training courses cover issues such as international considerations, reflecting the needs of worldwide markets, and look at techniques used to identify, reduce and manage specific aspects of risk.

### Risk Scenario Analytics

In very general terms, in business, the concept of ‘scenarios’ requires the business not only to consider the outcome of a series of events but also the development paths leading to that outcome. Effective scenario analysis does not rely on historical developments but rather considers the individual turning points and events that might contribute to the possible outcome. These possible outcomes can be optimistic, pessimistic or relatively improbable.

One should not, of course, rule out the concept of unexpected consequences, which can also be grouped into three categories:<sup>20</sup>

- *Unexpected benefit*, which gives a positive unexpected outcome, like winning the lottery
- *Unexpected drawback*, which gives an adverse result in addition to the expected one (The example suggested is that of irrigation systems providing vital water for agriculture but resulting in waterborne diseases such as cholera.)
- *Perverse result*, sometimes known as ‘backfire’, which gives a result entirely contrary to what might be expected, and where an intended solution only makes things worse.

In financial services, a bank might also use this approach to anticipate changes in the economy and how this might impact their strategy, or to gain a better understanding of likely returns in periods of rapid, moderate or slow growth.
