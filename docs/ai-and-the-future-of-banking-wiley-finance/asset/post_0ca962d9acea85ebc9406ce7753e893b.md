
These are very major and long-term decisions. As the banking industry looks to the future, with advanced analytics, AI, Blockchain and open banking to name just a few of the transformational technologies, senior executives are faced with making the right choices, against a background of an uncertain future. (We will consider some issues around implementation later on in Chapter 10.)

It is easy to get bogged down in terminology and jargon. Readers should try to avoid becoming distracted or confused by many unfamiliar expressions. The concept of Blockchain and the ‘distributed ledger’, for example, is challenging especially to those who have a ‘financial head’ rather than a technical head. It may be sufficient for individuals simply to become aware of what they do *not* know, and as a result have an open mind about technology and change. Some may view this as a catalyst, a personal challenge or perhaps a call to action in order to find out about new elements of their own industry and other associated industries.

Managers should encourage their direct reports to become more familiar with technology as part of their annual personal development planning, even if it means those individuals stepping outside their personal comfort zones. It’s also incumbent on the managers and ‘captains of industry’ to keep ahead of the times.

## The Importance of Location Analytics

Everything and everyone is somewhere. When we contemplate the challenges of data, analytics, customers and successful outcomes, it’s critical that the topic of location analytics is taken into account. The topic of location is critical when we consider:

- Where each customer lives and works, and their buying behaviour in each location
- How they physically get from one place to the other (car or public transport, for example), which might influence the customer’s views on how this is funded, and the role of the banks as ‘enablers’ of that process. In other words, how might a bank assist in the funding of a ticket loan or a car?
- The issues around where the customer lives and the demographic considerations in terms of house buying, retail behaviour and local trends (which may in turn influence localised campaign management, for example).

Traditionally, location analytics companies have tended not to focus on banks, instead favouring insurance and retail, but increasingly the mood is changing. One of the largest companies, ESRI, make this point: ‘Everything in banking is hyperlocal’.¹³

Referring specifically to bank mergers and acquisitions, the ability to understand the branch locations of two (merged) companies, overlaid by a new, now-shared customer base, allows banks to optimise the physical banking network, improving customer service yet at the same time reducing cost.

The ability of a bank to identify where the greatest concentration of high-net-worth customers are may also influence decisions regarding the best place to locate or to relocate a branch, and what type of services to provide within that physical banking environment. It tends to be the case that high-value customers prefer personal service and branches may need to be physically located closer to those with higher disposable income, and most probably with adequate parking. This seems to imply that, in the future, there will still be branch networks of sorts for that particular HNW demographic.

Similarly, the concept of a café bank recognises the need for co-location of banking and coffee providers, and especially targets participants of the so-called ‘flat white economy’, named after that group whose preferred (daytime) beverage is flat white coffee. Perhaps banking services in some form or another will be physically placed where the public are likely to have a need for financial advice.

There are other, equally important uses for location analytics going forward. At a more ‘defensive’ level relative to either natural disaster or human conflict such as weather, crime, terrorism or cyber infiltration, effective use of location analytics can help a bank plan where best to place its critical physical infrastructure to mitigate risks.

Even if we anticipate a future AI-infused world heavily influenced by remote devices and ‘smart devices’, the role of location still plays a critical part. Futurists speculate on a world where advertising images are personalised and communicated to us as we walk past some form of physical or virtual billboard. Major banking corporations will continue to extend their brand and boost their profitability by taking every opportunity to understand the location of their existing customers (for cross sell/upsell opportunities), and of potential future customers (for customer acquisition). This will be more important as Open Banking becomes more operational.

The story of location analytics as a profession and industry is in itself transformational. A decade ago, the role of the GIS (geographic information systems) analyst was very much a backroom position, but as ‘location’ has become ever more critical, that role has propelled itself forward. Even so, with such a critical capability, the reality is that salaries in the GIS profession are still relatively poor. Based on Feb 2019 figures, the average salary for a GIS analyst is $26.26 per hour in the United States, whereas the average salary for banking is $40.00 (ranging, incidentally, from $26,150 to $324,651 annually).14,15

Perhaps the reason for that disparity is that GIS analysts are technically and analytically minded rather than commercially minded. Going forward, we are increasingly likely to see a convergence of technologies such as analytics, AI, machine learning and others, but with expertise more aligned to industry-specific requirements.

Talent acquisition and management will also remain important across all elements of analytics and AI. Organisations will need to plan and implement a strategy for ensuring that not only new entrants but also existing participants are increasingly ‘tech savvy’, and that the historic demarcation between the ‘line of business’ and the ‘IT department’ is broken down. This will inevitably lead to new roles, new challenges around talent management and the development of new competences.

## CONCLUSION

The banking industry has no shortage of data. Some elements of this complex industry are almost entirely reliant on it, and roles have emerged (and continue to evolve) based on data. This chapter aimed to set out a hierarchy or migration curve in respect of analytical maturity.

Some organisations discuss AI as if it is the imminent future for them, but yet struggle to come to terms with even the basic issues of reporting, and having a ‘single version of the truth’. Managing the basics effectively remains a key component of success, if only to be able to measure progress accurately.

For many, technology is some sort of ‘black art’ to be implemented by those who have their own jargon and language. Those technologists might equally be confused by the deep nuances and complexities of the banking industry. Beyond this, the generic expression ‘banking industry’ often fails to convey the detailed complexities of each sector.

The future will inevitably reflect a convergence of banking and technical knowledge but the skills of both industries will probably mean that there might never be such a simple definition of a role as a ‘banking analyst’ in its broadest form. Analysts will become specialists in the areas of retail, commercial and investment banking (amongst others). There are likely to be some transferable skills but the real experts are those with both industry knowledge and the ability to apply particular technical applications. Ultimately they are likely to be able to command a premium in the employment salary market.

The perennial challenge remains. Is it easier for a technologist to become a banker, or for a banker to become a technologist? It’s a hypothetical question because the ability of one profession to fully migrate to another is unlikely, perhaps even impossible, but over the passage of time there will be a convergence of skills. Those learning about banking in general terms will need to learn about technology; those learning about technology may choose to learn about banking as a specialty. In the early stages both will deal with the topic only at a relatively high level. Specialisation will ultimately be a critical success factor at a personal level, but having a general understanding will also be important.

Ultimately each must face the challenge of whether the role that they have trained for will be replaced by automated systems, machine learning and AI. They should not be put off from engaging with either industry and should be aware of the changes in each. If AI in banking is an express train rumbling down the track, then it’s important that those who engage in the topic are passengers on that train rather than observers standing on the platform. AI in banking is anything but a spectator sport.
