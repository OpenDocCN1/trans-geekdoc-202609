This is different to ‘horizontal technical platforms’ (such as a single AI provider operating across multiple functions of the same business, for example), but rather anticipates an increasing convergence of different industry sectors under an AI-infused ecosystem and the consequences of this. In a mature AI environment, it is entirely feasible that multiple ‘industry imperatives’ might exist simultaneously without conflict, all to the benefit of the end user.

Industry-specific marketing, whilst valuable, also often fails to recognise customer synergies across their entire range of experiences. Similarly, technology-specific marketing fails to recognise the particular needs of distinct sectors. Throw into the equation the challenges of local market maturity and it’s possible to identify with the challenges faced with global banking players who seek to create a single global, ‘one size fits all’ strategy.

The most effective international companies need to operate a global template, locally executed, but beyond this also need to think of how AI infusion affects their go-to-market proposition. In other words, AI will affect everyone in the organisation, including the global marketing team.

# FUTURE IMPERATIVES IN BANKING

In considering three non-banking industries (retail, telecoms, healthcare), some key trends start to emerge.

# Greater Customer Centricity

This might be a ‘given’ because it features in today’s banking imperatives as described above, but banks increasingly need to think of customer centricity at an entirely new level. Retailers such as Mercedes and Apple are pushing the limits of what they call the ‘customer experience’ and are, for example, reinventing the purchase and ownership of a prestige car into a ‘lifestyle experience’.

Banks need to understand the needs and expectations of their customers and to be able to implement new products and services in a quickly and timely manner. To do this will almost certainly result in increased complexity, which, when added to issues of globalisation and developing technology, will present more difficult challenges of implementation.

Whilst the answer may in part rest in the increased use of ‘apps’ and mobile technology, the customer experience is invariably negatively affected by clunky, slow apps, which add inconvenience, rather than adding value.

What might a ‘lifestyle experience’ in banking look like? As property insurers and healthcare providers move away from a reactive claims model to one of proactivity and wellness management, for example, what is the banking equivalent to this?
