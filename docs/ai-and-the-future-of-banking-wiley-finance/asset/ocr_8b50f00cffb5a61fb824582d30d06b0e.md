MIT/Sloan also sought to draw out the differences between what they described as traditional organisations when compared to digital ones, identifying the top differences as:

+   - The pace of the business
+   - The cultural mind set
+   - Flexibility, higher collaboration and transparency
+   - Higher levels of productivity
+   - Greater access to tools and relevant data
+   - Continuous connectivity

# Zen and Leadership in Banking

Ambidexterity in leadership requires a combination of ‘enhancing optimization and exploration simultaneously’ and also being able to embrace failure. The concept, which applies to managerial contradictions, was first introduced by the academic Robert Duncan in 1976 and since then it has surfaced in different areas:

+   1. Strategic management where it represents the problem of alignment versus adaptability
+   2. Operations management where it represents flexibility versus efficiency
+   3. Innovation management where it represents the challenge of radical versus incremental.

To do so, say MIT/Sloan authors, requires future leaders to have ‘emotional fortitude’. This is described as being a combination of tenacity, the ability to cope with multiple roadblocks and also the willingness to ‘let go of treasured projects’ if they are not providing appropriate results. Others might simply call this mental toughness.

They also draw out links with Zen Buddhism that allow leaders to identify possibilities when they don’t have all the answers. It’s tempting to expand on that particular element and wonder whether future leaders will need to have ‘Zen-like’ qualities in their approach to business. This isn’t a unique idea, insofar as there have been previous efforts to stretch what is in effect a religious ethic into business self-help (i.e. Zen and the Art of Work) and even into motorcycle maintenance.

Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance27 was first published in 1974 and has sold over 5 million copies. It sought to compare and contrast what the writer Perzig described as the rational and the romantic. In the context of motorcycle maintenance, the ‘rational’ viewpoint was to understand the workings of the machine and to fully maintain it. On the other hand, the ‘romantic’ approach was simply to run the machine into the ground and then pay for a mechanic to repair it. The story suggests that the optimum situation is a combination of the two. From this co-existence of knowledge and being ‘in the moment’, it becomes possible to gain insights that are not normally possible and may not be rationally explained.
