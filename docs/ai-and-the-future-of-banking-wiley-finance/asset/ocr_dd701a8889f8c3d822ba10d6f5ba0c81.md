This has also led to the use of contractors, an approach that has been prevalent in the sector, and one that allows operational flexibility in a volatile marketplace, even though it carries the risks of inconsistency and occasional bad practices.

The criticality of security and especially ‘cyber security’ should not be overlooked within this section. If, as suggested later, there is a need for banks to extend their influence into the wider marketplace (the concept of the universal or full-service bank), then as a result there will also be major security issues to contend with. These are by their nature particularly specialist in character and will become increasingly so as the complexity of cybercrime increases.

**Channel Management:** This term is used to express the different ways that a bank (or other business) can bring their services or products before their existing and prospective customers. Experts suggest that increasing the effectiveness of channel management is becoming a CSF (critical success factor) for the retail banking sector, especially as banks recognise and respond to the shift in customer behaviour and new buying patterns.

> “To drive greater financial value’, says Jim Burson of Cornerstone, who are channel management consultants, ‘bankers must shake up their branch-centric perspective and embrace a more integrated view of delivery channel performance management’.²⁴ This means that banks must be able to create a clear understanding not only of the value of a particular ‘channel’ but rather they must also understand the ROC (‘Return of Capital’) of each channel to be able to gauge its success or otherwise. Effective use of financial performance management (see Chapter 4) is a key enabler to that process.

**Product Development:** Product development is viewed as being one of the key engines of banking growth requiring not only innovation but especially also the ability to implement. Expert Dan Moutrie of Saylent Technologies suggests²⁵ that new banking products require six key tactics:

+   1. Clarity of purpose: the bank needs to understand why it is introducing new products
+   2. Simplicity of products: products that can be clearly explained and understood
+   3. Speed of introduction: a methodology that allows the bank to take opportunity of changing market conditions
+   4. Security: the capability to ensure competitive advantage and differentiation from peer banks or new entrants
+   5. Standardisation: a methodical approach to internal product development processes
+   6. Surprise: intrinsically, having the ability to bring a new product to market quickly.

A useful article by Ron Shevlin (also of Cornerstone) suggests²⁶ that the banking sector has a dilemma:

> The larger banks (who may have new product development resources) are too big to turn the ship in a timely fashion, and the smaller institutions (who are probably more nimble) have no one who knows how to turn the wheel.
