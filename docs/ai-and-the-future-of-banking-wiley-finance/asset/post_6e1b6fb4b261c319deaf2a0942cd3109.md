
In his article ‘Zen and the Art of Leadership’, writer Stephen Balzac draws an analogy between the master swordsman and the business leader.28 A master swordsman, he says, understands his craft but fights to his best when he does not care whether he lives or dies. A successful athlete, says Balzac, also takes the same approach knowing that overanalysis will make them too introspective and lead to doubt or failure. We might call this ‘paralysis by analysis’. The same applies in business, where a successful leader must be prepared both to win and to lose, and to empower his or her team, rather than micromanage them for fear of failure.

There is, however, a potential contradiction. In such a highly regulated industry as banking with compliance being a critical success factor and failure having potentially heavy consequences, there is no real scope for ‘rogue activities’. One of the greatest real challenges is that of somehow bringing together the relatively entrepreneurial style of ‘new leaders’ with the constraints of a relatively rigid industry. Perhaps the ‘emotional fortitude’ mentioned earlier also needs to include the frustrations of ‘new leaders’ who will be constrained from transforming the business by the framework within which they operate.

## FUNCTIONAL CHANGE AND ROLE TRANSFORMATION

Earlier in this book the different types of banks were considered and, as a result, readers will recognise that there are many different types of organisational structures to consider. Typically, these may comprise a senior executive or suite of C-level functional executives, supported by a team of senior managers (often senior vice presidents). In a hierarchical approach, there are usually a series of functional silos such as back-office operations, lines of business, retail branch operations and the like.

Specific functions within the bank and reflected in its manner of organisation may include credit operations, direct banking operations, fraud prevention, loan operations, loan servicing, trade operations and support, transaction processing and support, business banking, commercial lending, consumer lending, credit cards, investment management, mortgage lending, online and mobile banking, treasury and cash management and platform operations.29

How a bank or business organises itself is reliant on its business model. By ‘business model’ we mean the way in which it fulfils its objectives, including managing and selling its products and/or services, its policies and its processes. Earlier the idea of ambidexterity has been considered including the concept that it is possible to operate two (or more) apparently conflicting business models within the same organisation.30

According to management expert Peter Drucker, a business model needs to answer the following questions:

- Who is your customer?
- What does the customer value?
- How do you deliver value at an appropriate cost?

> ‘Only three things happen naturally in organizations’ says Drucker, ‘friction, confusion, and underperformance. Everything else requires leadership’.

The essence, therefore, is how do banks reorganise themselves and their business models not only to meet current imperatives but also those in the future, and how will this reorganisation take into account the use of advanced analytics and AI? According to Jim Marous:31

Most financial institutions understand the major trends that are impacting the banking industry as well as what needs to be done to respond to those trends.

The challenge for legacy banking organizations is prioritizing and deploying resources in alignment with these realizations.

The question is whether organizations are ready to embrace change, take risks and disrupt themselves.

Earlier in this chapter we considered issues of leadership and how it was important not only that leaders were able to win and lose but also that empowerment of the team is critical.

Stephen Balzac identified 10 key points:32

1. Clarification of aims and objectives
2. Provision of information at the point of delivery
3. Encouragement of constructive discussion
4. Understanding of what success and failure look like
5. Ease of providing feedback
6. Minimisation of disruption to routines
7. Establishment of key checkpoints to monitor progress
8. Analysis, evaluation and adjustment – and no penalising
9. Revision of the vision as progress is made
10. Relinquish power and delegate

It’s tempting to try and create some illustrative templates for the Bank of the Future. What is recognised is that the future bank is a probable combination of:

- Greater digitalisation
- New technologies working alongside legacy core technologies
- Greater focus on web and mobile technologies
- Centres of innovation and testing
- More APIs
- Personalisation of the customer experience

One very interesting example of transformation is that of Bank of America, whose chairman and CEO Brian Moynihan described it as being ‘a technology company, wrapped around a great bank, and that’s going to be the future of what we do’, he says ‘And that’s because our customers demand it’.

Like other large banks such as Barclays, BoA have innovation hubs that support and mentor fintechs and are constantly on the lookout for technical allies.

The objectives of these new innovation hubs might be, generally, to:

- Build new services and business models that allow the bank to become a true partner of their customers.
- Ring fence the innovation hub as a separate business unit to ensure that it benefits from a dedicated organisation and resources, and does not run the risk of cannibalisation.
- Develop banking by using new technologies and approaches, in order to give their customers a better service.

For those readers perhaps in their mid- or later career who might be thinking that this is irrelevant to them, they should reflect on the fact that whilst in 2050 they may not be active professionals, they may nevertheless still be customers and recipients of the service of the bank of the future.

But before leaping forward in time, it might be helpful to look backward a similar period of 30 years.

By the end of the 1980s there were great socioeconomic changes. This included a move away from planned economies to what some describe as ‘laissez-faire capitalism’, that is, ‘an economic system in which transactions between private parties are free from government intervention such as regulation, privileges, tariffs and subsidies’.34

At that time, 30 years ago, developing countries faced multiple debt crises requiring intervention from the International Monetary Fund. Famine in Ethiopia led to the Live Aid concert in 1985. Multinationals were increasingly offshoring their manufacturing activities to Asia. Tim Berners-Lee had formalised the concept of the internet in 1989. From a tech viewpoint, in 1984 the Motorola DynaTAC 8000X becomes the first commercially available mobile phone (a full charge took 10 hours and gave 30 minutes of air time). Game consoles introduced Donkey Kong and Super Mario Brothers. There was a rise in the use of PCs including home computers such as the Macintosh 128 which was the original Apple Macintosh.

From a retail banking point of view, according to Bank of Ireland, today only 3% of customers do their banking over the counter whereas in 1980 it was 100%. Almost all retail transactions were cheque-based including salaries leading to long queues at the counter on a Friday. ATMs were few and far between. With multiple banks and branches, the role of the Branch Manager was not only prestigious but also pivotal. He (it was predominantly a ‘he’) was pivotal to the process making key local decisions on loans and mortgages with judgements taking days and sometimes weeks whereas nowadays these are usually made at headquarters in a matter of hours.35

In 2050, the realities for the banking employee might well be that:

- There will be no branches as are currently known.
- The main relationship of the customer with banks will be entirely app orientated.
- The quaint concept of keyboards will have disappeared and screens may have disappeared as we know them, replaced by 4-D projections.
- There will be many more types of bank with niche and customisable service offerings.
- Back-office functions (as we currently know them) will be almost entirely automatic.
- Front-office functions (as we currently know them) will be almost entirely mainly robotic.
- The main operational costs for banking, typically those of people and property, will have been significantly reduced if not removed completely.
- The dominant purpose of banks will be that of managing and creating capital.
