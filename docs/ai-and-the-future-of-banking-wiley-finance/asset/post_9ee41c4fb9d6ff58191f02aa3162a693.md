
‘Many of the roles and job titles of tomorrow are unknown to us today,’ says Josh Bottomley, global head of digital, retail banking and wealth management at HSBC. ‘One thing is certain, however: artificial intelligence will not replace human intelligence. Blending the best technology with the power of people will be the difference between good and great when it comes to customer experience.’[30]

It’s an interesting viewpoint but not one that everybody might subscribe to, yet he may be right at least in the short term.

HSBC specifically identified five digital jobs for the future:

1. **Mixed Reality Experience Designer**: A role that helps augment the real world with digital data to give 3-D augmented reality experiences in design, branding and user experience.
2. **Algorithmic Mechanic**: As industry moves to a ‘no-code’ environment, with interaction with systems being increasingly based on natural language, the *algorithmic mechanic* is someone who continually fine-tunes the algorithms for optimum performance.
3. **Conversational Interface Designer**: In other words, a chatbot designer but whose skills are in creative, linguistic, and anthropological elements (i.e. relating to the study of mankind).
4. **Universal Services Advisor**: Highly skilled customer service advisor who can switch between real and virtual customer interfaces.
5. **Digital Process Engineer**: As it’s being increasingly recognised that the future won’t be about digitalising existing processes but rather about creating new platform-orientated ecosystems, this type of role fine-tunes new processes for efficiency and friction-free operation.
6. **Partnership Gateway Enabler**: This role manages the interface between the participants or ‘actors’ in banks’ future virtual enterprise, especially in respect of bilateral data flow and security issues.

It’s an interesting list, and perhaps might have been created for provocation rather than reality, especially as it has a feel of random title generation to it. For ‘random title generation’, think in terms of putting a handful of tech jargon words into a hat and then pulling them out three-by-three. (This approach, incidentally, is also called a cut-up technique – or découpage – and was used by both David Bowie and Kurt Cobain to create some of their more memorable songs.)

To supplement this list, readers might also want to look at job descriptions in other advanced sectors and industries. Any typical recruitment site carries advertisements for vacancies, such as:

- **Data Analyst Speech Chatbot Artificial Intelligence**: ‘This role collates interaction data from my clients’ Artificial Intelligence Assistant/Chat Bot (like Alexa, Siri, Bixby, Cortana) and utilises this data to understand customer behaviour and to help improve the potential of my clients’ Chat Bot’.
- **Python Developer Artificial Intelligence**: ‘Working closely with the Technical Architect and other developers, you will contribute to building robust and performant server-side code for the core product that uses Artificial Intelligence to harness knowledge from across the web to provide clients with a strategic, competitive advantage’.
- **Manager and Counsel**: ‘Take a lead role in monitoring trends, industry and regulatory developments, including in respect of Open Banking, Future of Payments, Big Data, Artificial Intelligence, Wearables, IoT, digital payments, marketing and personalisation.’

These quite genuine job adverts from the banking sector have not been specifically referenced partly because these advertised vacancies are now likely to be ‘long gone’. However, it’s hoped the descriptions give an indication of what is ‘hot’ in the job world, roles that command significant (and in some cases eye-watering) remuneration packages, and some of the buzzwords that might need to be included in CVs to attract the attention of analytically infused recruitment processes.

One important aspect to consider is that recruiters and employers are not just looking for generally competent individuals whom they are willing to train on the job. Rather they seem to be looking for capable and seasoned individuals who can hit the ground running and make an immediate impact. The scale of demand is arguably worrying; for example, HSBC alone is looking for 1000 people. When this is extrapolated into the wider banking industry and multiple other sectors responding to the AI era, then there is quite possibly a resource and talent challenge and that is one of how to find enough of the right calibre of suitably competent candidates. Adequate training and experience don’t happen overnight. As Malcolm Gladwell put it, some suggest that to become a true ‘master’, you need to put in 10,000 hours of experience.[31]

By comparison, Fitch Learning say that to become a ‘game changer’ in the future of financial services might require six years of training. However, perhaps banks don’t need ‘game changers’ or ‘masters’ at this stage but rather they need highly qualified and competent *foot soldiers*.[32]

Where will these competent foot soldiers come from? There are many prospective candidates in the pipeline but will they have the right skills when they emerge? Will they have the ability to apply themselves to particular industry verticals, or the specific challenges of individual market sectors? In the long run, does that matter? Perhaps a future operating model, at least in the short term, is the ability for data scientists to co-work with banking experts. In time, it’s likely that there will be no distinction between these two professions, and that banking experts will need to have adequate ‘new age’ technological capability.

*Anecdotally, a data scientist with expertise in fisheries and oceanography was being ‘courted’ by financial institutions to turn his hand to customer acquisition through data management. Surely the biblical expression ‘Fishers of Men’ was never intended to be used in this way?*

Advanced analytics and AI are increasingly being judged to be important not only to the future of banking in general terms but also to the future profitability of individual banks. Banks (and others) are likely to be increasingly ruthless in acquiring specialist practitioners, and in doing so will start to push up salary costs. The impact of this could be to widen the gap between Tier 1 and Tier 2 industries in respect to analytical maturity. A shortage of skilled people and increased salary costs will lead to banks wanting to develop alternative strategies, especially in advanced analytics, machine learning and ultimately AI. New disaggregated business models might need to emerge. With banking as ‘first movers’ in comparison to other industries, a shortage of talent might be more likely to create some form of a burning platform or catalyst, and in doing so, to force the development of AI more quickly.

The banking industry is in a key transitional stage, which will be analytically and ultimately AI-fuelled and there will be important questions, the answers to which will underpin their strategy:

1. How quickly will AI become not only a significant part of the banking agenda but a cornerstone of a bank’s strategy going forward?
2. When will we see reference to AI in the annual reports not only of the major banks but of the intermediary and smaller ones as well?
3. When will we see banks flip over from being financial services providers using technology, to becoming technology companies with banking knowledge?

## CONCLUSION

This chapter is only meant to be an introduction or ‘prologue’ to the main topic, which is that of analytics, AI and the ‘Future of Banking’.

In an earlier book by this author, consideration was given as to how AI would impact the ‘Future of Work’, relating to all professions and industries.[33] To allow that issue to be fully considered effectively required an understanding of what is meant by ‘work’. Adopting the same approach, it’s helpful to start to understand what is meant both by ‘money’ and by ‘banking’, although both are big topics and only the surface has been scratched here. Others, such as Galbraith and Ferguson, have written about these subjects in much greater depth. Galbraith’s book Money: Whence It Came, Where It Went[34] was written in 1975 and is of its time, failing to mention the digital economy, which was at that time not even on the radar. Ferguson’s book The Ascent of Money,[35] republished in 2009, only gives passing comment in his original version to the impact of digitalisation, which is a reflection of how much has changed within a decade or so.

Ferguson also toyed with the idea of calling his book, with due homage to Charles Darwin, ‘The Descent of Finance’. He recognised finance as a topic that was – and continues to be – evolutionary. His argument, like Darwin’s, was that until we understand the fundamental truth about money, and inter alia, the fundamental truth about banking, then we can never really understand the future. In the financial evolutionary process, we need to absolutely recognise that the purpose and functions of banks is evolving through analytics and AI. Artificial intelligence might be to the banking industry what a meteor strike was to the dinosaur population. Through this process of ‘evolution’, banks will continually need to create new and sometimes radical processes and financing models, not only to maximise their future profits and maintain their existing profitability, but also to survive in a recognisable form. As Darwin discovered, it will not be the biggest and the strongest that survive, but rather the most agile.

‘We will never understand the fundamental truth about money’, says Ferguson. But he recognises that money and financial markets are, in some way, ‘a mirror of mankind, revealing every hour of every working day the way in which we value ourselves and the resources of the world around us’. If money is the mirror, then aren’t banks the frame that contains the mirror?
