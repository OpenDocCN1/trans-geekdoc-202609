
## Responding to Cyber Attack

In addition, the NCSC recommend the following advice²⁶ in the event that a business is subject to cyberattack:

- **Secure the network:** By locking down the network this will restrict the hackers from having access to data but at a cost of disrupting the business’s own operation.
- **Activate the ‘Response Team’**: NCSC recommend that there is a trained response team in place representing key parts of the business (IT, HR, Legal), or there is an external arrangement with a certified cyber security incident provider (such as from the ‘Council of Registered Ethical Security Testers’).
- **Report the incident**: In Europe, for example, the GDPR rules require a business to notify the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) within 72 hours. Cases of fraud or extortion should be reported to the police. (In 2018 the ICO fined Uber £385,000 under the Data Protection Act for a 2016 breach when the firm admitted to paying hackers $100,000 to destroy data on 50 million users and keep quiet about it.)
- **Review and learn**: Before returning to business as usual, it is recommended there is a complete audit of the business’s cyber strategy, and it is adjusted accordingly.

Cyber security experts Analecta, a US-based company focusing on small and medium-sized business, suggest that susceptibility to cybercrime can be reduced by 75% with adequate training, which covers issues such as legal compliance, motivation and engagement, and improved responses to attack, and develops appropriate competences.²⁷

The top five challenges to the adoption of cyber awareness programmes for businesses appear to be:

- 1. Adequate resources
- 2. Adoption or embracing of the awareness programme
- 3. Support from management
- 4. Support from end users
- 5. Finding time to take part

Whilst a banking organisation may have both policies and capabilities to manage cyber risk within the walls of its own enterprise, the problem is that its business partners may not. Constance Bommelaer, Senior Director, Global Internet Policy at ISOC, who adds that collaboration among all the actors within an ecosystem is required to promote complete security, puts it this way:

> It may be the ecosystem of banking services that your business depends on, your ISP, or the legal framework in your country that puts your business at a security disadvantage.

To have absolute confidence, and win the hearts and minds of their customers, and with ‘trust’ increasingly becoming a critical success factor, banks need to effectively manage (directly or indirectly) their supply chains, including their technology. This is a more proactive rather than reactive role. It isn’t enough to ask suppliers to provide ‘evidence’ of security compliance but rather that the banks must play a much more dynamic role in the proactive management of data throughout the supply chain. The use of so-called ‘white hat hackers’ who hack in an ethical way might also be employed by firms to identify areas of vulnerability.

Additionally, banks must recognise that whilst technology is a problem in cyber security, many ‘leaks’ are of a relatively simple nature. These leaks can involve employees who are either careless and reuse passwords; who are disgruntled and deliberately steal information; or who are gullible enough to open unsolicited emails. Sophisticated criminals are increasingly focusing on social engineering by finding enough about an individual on social media to be able to send a communication that looks credible enough to open.

But where will the burden of that operational delivery in the supply chain rest in practical terms? Perhaps with the supply chain management professional whose main criteria, historically and maybe arguably, has been in getting the best deal or structuring the right commercial relationship between the parties. For that traditional supplier manager, data protection and cyber security management may be no more than a ‘hygiene factor’ and part of a ‘box-ticking exercise’. But perhaps, looking forward, it could be the most important criteria of them all.

One problem, however, is that smaller businesses along most supply chains are often considered poor relations. To what degree should banks – and other larger organisations – provide a certain level of free advice when it comes to cyber security, especially given the large costs involved in compliance? If the cyber gaps need to be effectively plugged, banks may need to take a leading role in that process. Some sort of ‘utopian society’ could be created in commerce where the larger organisations, such as banks, help the smaller ones, in the interests of both. Already there’s much free information available online and many vendors provide free access to conferences, but perhaps there’s a better way of exchanging cyber safety knowledge for the benefit of all?

It’s impossible to disregard the potential general weakness of supply chains. If the idea is that at least one version of the ‘Future of Banking’ is that of a traditional business model becoming ‘disaggregated’, that is, that future banking becomes in effect part of some sort of collection of supply chain components, then there’s significant pressure on banks (and the other ‘actors’ in that operating model) to get their cyber risk management house in order. It could be a critical success factor in the future of the banking industry.

## Cyber Readiness

According to the Hiscox Cyber Readiness Report 2019, cyber readiness spend is on the increase. The report indicates that the average spend for 2018 is now $1.45 million, up 245% from 2017 and seemingly accelerating.

Despite this, assessing the firms against a cyber readiness model:

- Only 1 in 10 (10%) achieved expert status.
- More worryingly, nearly three-quarters (74%) ranked as ‘novices’, with a sharp drop in German and US firms achieving expert status.

Failures in the supply chain are also particularly noticeable, with two-thirds of firms experiencing cyber-related issues in 2018. The results are especially noticeable in the technology, media/telecoms and transportation sectors.

There’s a temptation to rely on government action and regulation to become the saviour, but Constance Bommelaer, Senior Director, Global Internet Policy at the ‘Internet Society’ (ISOC), suggests that the challenge of cyber is too strong for this to be resisted in what some might call traditional institutional ways. She blames the governmental and institutional approach that is fundamentally based around ‘policy making’ and is reactive rather than proactive. The answer, it appears, is not entirely in the world of ‘politics’ even if (according to Hiscox), the introduction of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) does appear to have had an impact, with 84% of continental (and 80% of UK) firms having made some changes.

The potential sales market for cyber security vendors is significant. According to market experts, the size of the cyber security market will be nearly US $250 billion by 2023, having shown double-digit CAGR since 2018. The US are leading the way in expenditure in terms of the global picture, with financial markets being sector leaders.28

For the purposes of market categorisation, they segment the cyber sector into three key elements:

- IAM (identity and access management), encryption, governance, risk and compliance
- UTM (unified threat management)
- SIEM (security information and event management)

Of these they view the IAM sector, especially encryption, as being one of the areas of focus, especially due to the amount of information being held on the cloud and on personal devices. They believe that encryption will become more topical as there is greater interest in biometric capabilities, such as facial and voice recognition. In such a competitive marketplace, technical vendors inevitably are keen to position themselves favourably. Some of the major players such as Symantec Corporation, Check Point Technology Ltd., Palo Alto Networks, Fortinet and FireEye, amongst others, are vying for position against the likes of broader players like SAS and IBM.

In such a large and wide market, especially in a culture of startups and Fintech, there’s always room for new entrants who perhaps can offer a different viewpoint, as well as technical capability. One company that comes to attention is UK-based ‘Darktrace’, founded in 2013 by mathematicians and intelligence experts from the University of Cambridge, MI5, GCHQ and the CIA. Based in Cambridge and San Francisco, their capability ‘Enterprise Immune System’ uses AI to identify potential cyber threats, allowing security teams time to act.
