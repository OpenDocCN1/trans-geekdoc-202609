Already we are beginning to see the emergence of the Chief Customer Officer (CCO) as a senior role, often at executive level, who is responsible for the relationship between an organisation and its customers. This new role is a further addition to the suite of CxO jobs, and is usually a board-level appointment, looking at (and influencing) all issues, such as contact centre, billing and all other functions where there is a direct customer-facing operation.

In their article ‘Why Your Company Needs A Chief Customer Officer’,14 the authors suggest that businesses increasingly need a customer champion as companies ‘reorient and reprioritise’ their activities around the customer. The article points to the need to have two specific functions:

- ‘To unify all customer initiatives throughout different functions’
- ‘To inject a new way of thinking and acting throughout organisation’

It also mentions four key targets:

- 1. Bringing the customer to life; that is, creating empathy within the organisation in terms of the importance of the customer and the nature of the relationship between them and the wider enterprise
- 2. Reaching outside the organisation, typically by allowing the customers to help create new processes and products
- 3. Involving the front lines; that is, recognising that customer-facing staff are often best placed to make suggestions regarding customer service and product development
- 4. Embracing data, especially with respect to the creation of a 360-degree view of the customer to ensure that satisfaction is measured at all contact points

It’s easy to be slightly sceptical about the need to create another C-level role, which, if anything, might further complicate established responsibilities and lines of authority. Notwithstanding this, with greater access to information especially through social media and with a heightened awareness of what comprises good service, the customer is in the ascendant in terms of being the most important person in the room.

## Becoming Truly Digital

What this means is that it is insufficient to take existing value models and value chains and simply digitalise them. A truly digital transformation requires organisations – including banks – to be prepared to deconstruct their existing models and to reconstruct them for the digital age, and more importantly, for their digital customers.

But what we mean by ‘digitalisation’ appears to have different interpretations. It’s certainly more than ‘on-line banking’, which provides speedy and efficient 24/7 access. In their 2014 report ‘What digital actually means when it comes to banking’,15 Celent, a research, advisory and consulting firm who are focused on financial services technology, proposed a definition of ‘digitalisation’ for the industry.

They said that digital banking is about three key elements in order to deliver demonstrable and sustainable economic value:

- 1. The delivery of a customised but consistent (financial institution) brand experience to customers across all channels and points of interaction
- 2. An experience underpinned by analytics and automation
- 3. An environment that requires a change in the operating model, namely products and services, organisation, culture, skills and IT

This definition implies that digitalisation is primarily a way of creating a customised brand experience, but this is an arguable point of view. Digitalisation needs to be more than a brand or personalised (or customised) distribution model but together with these, a completely different way of thinking about the banking business. (The issue of ‘brand’ is considered more in Chapter 6.)

McKinsey suggest that digitalisation can give as much as a 30% revenue boost to mainstream revenue¹⁶ (i.e. through high turnover products such as personal loans and payments), as well as taking out between 40% and 90% of back-office costs as a result of automation, workflow and self-servicing capabilities for customers. They indicate that effective digitalisation can double staff productivity (aka ‘halve the labour force’) as well as improving customer service. It is a way of reducing or removing current legacy frictional processes in favour of smoother, straight-through operations.

At its most simple level, digitisation is a mechanism leading to low overheads. It opens the door for RPA (Robotic Process Automation). More importantly it is a mind-set issue for the bank, who need to place absolute focus on becoming digital. (See Figure 2.4.)

## Completely Accepting Technological Change

Banks need to engage more effectively with technology in the same way that their customers are also embracing technological change. It’s more than just getting rid of legacy systems but also about creating scalable, enterprise-wide systems that are relatively futureproof. This may require new innovative partnership models between traditional financial services organisations and technology providers.

The technological discussion opens the door to broader and often problematic issues such as integrating disparate systems as a result of merger and acquisition, creation of effective third-party partnerships, and of course the problematic issue of implementation.

(In April 2018 the UK’s TSB Bank, part of Banco Sabadell, attempted to transfer 1.3 billion customer records, leading to ‘computer meltdown’ and resulting in more than 204,000 complaints, the loss of 80,000 customers, and £330.2m in extra costs, before any regulatory fines were taken into account.)¹⁸

As we look to the future, banking must not only fully integrate with but also utilise rapidly emerging technology. This includes the use of advanced analytics and artificial intelligence. Fundamentally, this may need a repositioning of the strategic intent of the major banks. It is insufficient for them to behave as banks and try to become digital: rather it represents a major shift in their entire business model and go to market approach, as in the case of Bank of America, now describing itself as a technology company that happens to do banking.

## Reimagining Banking

Ultimately the questions are:

- What is the future role of banking?
- What is the place of banking in society going forward?
- How might that impact on future banking strategies?
- How can we ‘reimagine’ banking?

Consulting a dictionary doesn’t throw too much light on what we mean by ‘reimagination’ (other than that it is the process of ‘reimagining’, which is no more than a tautology). Merriam-Webster’s dictionary describes ‘imagination’ as ‘the act or power of forming a mental image of something not present to the senses or never before wholly perceived in reality.’¹⁹

In his book *Re-imagine!* Tom Peters doesn’t attempt to define what is specifically meant by ‘reimagination’ but rather identifies it by its characteristics (much in the same way that ‘big data’ has been defined by its components of volume, velocity, veracity and variety, etc.).²⁰ In his opening pages Peters refers to reimagination as:

- A new brand of employee with a high appetite for change
- A new breed of enterprise that is agile, innovative and entrepreneurial
- A new social contract that provides a catalyst to break through the rules and invent vivid new futures

It sounds relatively evangelical and it’s a theme that seems to resurface in many current business strategies. ‘Reimagine Food’ is the tagline for an international food hub.²¹ IBM, Tommy Hilfiger and others are using AI to collaborate and ‘Reimagine Retail’.²²

Yet at the same time, such an evangelical approach with a ‘transferable’ tagline fails sometimes to reflect the mood of caution and anxiety that still exists. How can organisations and industry ensure that all these different versions of a ‘Reimagined World’ can happen without the impact of adequate corporate governance in place? ‘We can reimagine banking’, the major players might say, ‘but perhaps only if we are able to test it in a safe “sandbox” environment first.’ (See Figure 2.5.)
