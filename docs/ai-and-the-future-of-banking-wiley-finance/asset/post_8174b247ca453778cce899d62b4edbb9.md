
more than restyling existing High Street branches with a new coat of paint, by removing the tellers and replacing them with sofas and more intelligent ATMs or just about having a fancier brand logo.

Reimagining is about banks understanding their true value to the customer and reinventing themselves in an era of uncertainty. It recognises that the whole concept of money is not only changing, but for many demographics has already changed. Reimagining banking as a concept starts to recognise that the time is quickly approaching when automatic systems replace many job roles, and that those systems may comprise robotics that are more intelligent than those they have been built to serve.

## Reinventing Risk Management

In such an unknown and uncertain world, the management of risk will take on increased importance. Not just in terms of operational risk, which considers issues such as failure of systems, people or processes, but also with respect to new categories where business suffers vulnerability. Political instability, coupled with climate change plus technological advances, starts to push current ideas of risk management to the limit. In a heavily regulated industry such as banking and financial services, the ability to manage new levels of unpredictability increasingly becomes a critical success factor.

In a future AI-infused financial system, how might the risk process be changed? What new capabilities are needed of future risk managers, whose business function will be augmented (if not substantially replaced) by automated systems and AI?

In his recording ‘The Risk Manager of the Future’, Brian Ruane of Willis Towers Watson^23 comments that in his view the trend is clear. Risk managers, he says, will not only need to be smarter and exhibit greater leadership in order to be rewarded with a ‘seat at the table’ but ‘they will be compensated accordingly with prestige, influence and, yes, money’. It looks like an interesting place to be.

In their 2017 report ‘The Future of Risk Management in the Digital Era’ McKinsey reinforce those views^24 by suggesting that future risk managers will focus on strategic and high-value decisions whilst lower-value decisions are automated and there will be less manual handling. They suggest a model in which risk executives ‘deploy a centralised “nerve centre” where newly powerful self-learning models will harness improved connectivity to set limits dynamically and to detect emergent risks (credit, market, and operational) – evaluating those risks immediately, setting cross-risk mitigation strategies in motion, and dynamically adjusting limits.’

Is this hype or optimism? The 2017 IIF/McKinsey digital risk survey found that:

- 70% of banks had digital risk on their radar with a middling level of management attention.
- 10% have it as a high priority.
- 22% of banks have invested more than 25% of their annual risk budget to digitalise risk management.

The needs and challenges of global players to manage compliance issues internationally cannot be underestimated. Increasingly it is getting beyond human management and whilst this might be operationally delivered at a local level, global organisations may more frequently demand that this is centralised in some sort of automated, ‘intelligent’ AI-infused way. Risk managers cannot fail to see the risk involved in a lack of control through a devolved model of operation.

Attitudes to risk management are usually a function or by-product of specific events or experiences, as are budget allocation and prioritisation. When risk management becomes the zeitgeist of the moment, as will invariably happen at some time in the future, then AI will have a significant part to play. In the meantime, regtech providers may need to bide their time and hold their nerve. Major incidents have yet to happen. Their time is yet to come.

## CONCLUSION

In this chapter the issue of banking imperatives has been considered both in terms of those which currently exist and those which will be needed in the future. Imperatives have been operationally ‘devolved’ to what might be considered as business ‘objectives’, but these in themselves might be becoming increasingly irrelevant in favour of a more dynamic method of both personal and business measurement of progress.

This in itself throws up some interesting organisational challenges about how personal contribution is measured in a new AI-infused age. Many ‘Personal Development Plans’ are objective-based, yet in a more volatile business world traditional ‘objective-based’ measures are becoming less relevant and important. Skills and knowledge are being replaced by agility and flexibility and this places new pressures on banks to effectively measure progress in a different way at a corporate, divisional and ultimately individual level. We may be reaching a point where the concept of ‘performance management’ needs to be reinvented.

It’s not just a challenge for banking. As roles and responsibilities increasingly change through the uses of automation and AI, operational functions within the enterprise are also being both reinvented and redefined. Although they may not have yet realised it, the HR professional is being pushed more and more to the front line of change, which few are prepared for. There will be an inevitable spinoff in terms of performance management, competence-based pay, employment law and the like.

It’s not just a matter of applying today’s banking imperatives and thinking about tomorrow’s but of recognising the implication of these changes both on organisations and individuals. The revolution towards Banking 4.0 will affect all the stakeholders, both back office and front office. There is no hiding place. The new mantra will become ‘competences, not objectives’.

At a strategic level, the notion that banks might need to extend their traditional ecosystems into other areas also opens the door to convergence of imperatives. At the end of the day, banks, retailers, telecoms providers and healthcare providers are dealing with the same end user, the ‘consumer’, albeit in different guises. In a truly customer-centric environment, the customer’s needs should come first but it’s more complex than that. Effective use of AI could help create some new form of ‘balanced scorecard’ for the next millennium, one that not only reflects customer needs but also risk and operational effectiveness.
