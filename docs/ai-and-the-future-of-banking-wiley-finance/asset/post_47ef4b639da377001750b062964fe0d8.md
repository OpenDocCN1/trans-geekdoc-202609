
When comparing the current situation to the past, it’s suggested that the most likely reason that cryptocurrency won’t overtake that of sovereign money is the lack of political willingness. One shouldn’t be so complacent because the politicians may not be in control of the new digital economy in the same traditional way going forward. The report says, ‘central banks – especially in major currency areas – will not face the risk of a diminishing demand for their money and, hence, losing control over monetary policy’.

The report calls for international standardisation, which is no small undertaking in the absence of a unifying body. In their conclusion, the report comments:

> VCs (Virtual Currencies) respond to real market demand and, most likely, will remain with us for a while. ... VCs should be treated by regulators as any other financial instrument, proportionally to their market importance, complexity, and associated risks.

This might sound rather a condescending approach to the matter. Perhaps were it not for the Chinese example it could be a valid conclusion, but money and political control have often remained significantly hand in hand. Advanced analytics and AI have already been established as the foundations of disruptive technology, regardless of what industry or profession is being considered. The technological impacts of Blockchain and cryptocurrencies fall into that category. The importance of Blockchain on the future of banking is critical in the impact that it will have not only on process but also on governance.

There will be a technological cost to be met but also a human cost as well. As with greater automation, robotics and AI, these complementary technologies will inevitably transform the banking industry.

## Blockchain and Open Banking in Africa

In the longer run, the ability of consumers to look across different banking systems looks quite appealing. Already the insurance and utilities industries operate forms of cost comparison sites. It’s difficult to see why the financial services industry should not provide a similar setup.

Elsewhere beyond Europe, the need for change goes beyond simply consumerism. The demand to find the best banking deal for SMEs might prove to be the difference between their survival and collapse. The degree of ‘under banking’ (where people don’t have access to mainstream banks and therefore resort to alternative financing such as loan sharks) in emerging territories also encourages the use of a form of Open Banking.

Already countries are considering not how these new technologies will change the face of banking but rather how they will change the face of a continent. Considering Africa, for example, the annual Blockchain Africa Conference brings together key experts who are focusing on the use of Blockchain in that region. Since 2015 all the key big banks in South Africa have been actively researching its use, and according to Ross A. Mauri, general manager of IBM’s Z Systems, ‘All South Africa’s leading financial institutions have some proof-of-concept or early stage technology related to the Blockchain’. This isn’t only a South African initiative but has extended Africa-wide.

‘The emergence of Blockchain technology, big data, on-demand services, cloud computing and the Internet of Things (IoT) are all spurring inclusive growth on the African continent. And it is the convergence of these exciting technology innovations that is changing the face of Africa’, says Van Der Beek, of Goodwell Investments, who are a Dutch investor focussing on the African market.²⁶

It’s difficult not to take eyes off Africa at the moment. As has been seen with China and other Asian regions, the challenge of urbanisation is likely to have a major impact. Africa is urbanising more quickly than anywhere else on the planet.²⁷ (See Figure 9.22.) It’s estimated that Africa’s 1.1 billion citizens will double by 2050 and more than 80% of this will occur in cities. According to McKinsey, by 2025 there will be 100 cities in Africa with more than 1 million inhabitants, twice the number of cities in Latin America.

The concern, naturally, is whether the infrastructure is mature enough or capable of developing sufficiently quickly to meet this level of growth. There is especially likely to be hyper-growth of the slum areas, where 60% of those living in the cities already live. Without commensurate development of the infrastructure, there is a risk of yet another humanitarian crisis. It’s important to look for positivity in this difficult situation. Essential development opens the door to considerable economic growth, despite the nervousness of businesses and the private sectors to provide private funding (rather than rely on the public purse) due to issues of what they describe as ‘instability and regulatory confusion’.

Figures 9.23 and 9.24 are provided by way of context relative to the sheer numbers involved and the degree of urban growth.

Change is inevitable and whilst Africa is just beginning to see the benefits of the Era of Data and all that it entails, the opportunity for transformational growth remains not only tempting but potentially irresistible, above all for those with a longer-term view of the continent.
