To do this, reliable forms of measurement must also be used with an understanding that the same data will be used not only to manage the business, but also to meet regulatory requirements.

It’s also helpful to bear in mind that different industries and similarly different parts of the financial services sector are at varying levels of maturity, with banking, for example, having a greater level of maturity than insurance. Departments responsible for asset and liability management have extremely high levels of analytical insight. Analytical tools are used in what is a niche area and whilst some of these may not be obviously transferable across the enterprise, a culture of data-driven decision making may well be.

Movement of individuals across departments and also industries is likely in time to have a levelling effect. Whilst some industries and departments are more advanced than others, there will nevertheless be some degree of knowledge and capability transfer as well as some cultural resonance.

Retail bankers might reasonably take a lead from the Consumer and Retail Goods (CRG) sector. Invariably consumer retailers have their finger on the pulse in terms of what is selling and what is not, and what level of discount needs to be offered to encourage the customer. Customer behaviour management is as much a financial issue as it is a marketing and psychological issue and to do this effectively requires absolute and indisputable agreement about issues such as revenue, profitability and growth, and how they are linked to product, channel and reputation.

Bankers would be well advised to look outside their peer groups to find a benchmark of progress. It is clear that in terms of customer analytics, retailer and telecom companies are far ahead of bankers in terms of their analysis of buying behaviours but that gap is rapidly closing. With highly analytical companies such as Amazon entering the banking space, it’s critical that traditional banks look and learn – and do it very quickly.⁴

> *A true anecdote: A room full of bankers and insurers listened avidly to a High Street retailer describing how, through loyalty cards, they understood the customers’ buying behaviour with absolute granularity and could not only pitch an attractive offer, but anticipate where and when that offer should be made. The audience were especially interested in one specific question, ‘What is the banking equivalent to loyalty cards?’*
