
## CUSTOMER ANALYTICS

At the heart of banking is an understanding of the value of, and the opportunity that comes from, the profitability of the customer. Ultimately banks need to be interested in four key elements:

- What is the customers’ propensity to buy?
- What is their likelihood to take specific action – and if so, what is the relevance of this?
- What is their likelihood to churn, or to leave the bank (especially in an Open Banking situation)?
- What is the lifecycle opportunity or lifetime ‘value’ of the customer?

To do this requires a bank to understand and anticipate the needs of both existing and prospective customers, which then allows them to place the right offer before them. This might be done at a granular (i.e. individual) level, or a broader-based ‘campaign’ approach.

### Customer Insight

The notion of a ‘customer 360-degree’ insight is not a new idea, yet many organisations, including banks, still struggle to operationalise it. Conceived in the early ’90s, the concept has moved beyond a relatively straightforward CRM (Customer Relationship Management) tool to one increasingly affected by modern trends such as:

- Mobile technology
- Chatbots and other automated services
- The advance of ‘digital’, including the Internet of Things
- Machine learning and AI
- The availability of analytical, cloud-based solutions and capabilities providing insight ‘on demand’

There are four key stages to creating an effective customer 360-degree insight.

### Sourcing data

This may comprise:

- (a) Behavioural: Data that defines the behaviour of the customer such as banking records, loans, products already taken and uses of overdraft.
- (b) Sentimental: Attitudinal data derived from general or even individual social media content (as far as practically possible).
- (c) Interactional: Data derived from how many interactions the customer has had with the bank, either across the counter, through the website or through a contact centre.
- (d) External: How to capture the vast amount of external data that might refer to the customer’s location, workplace and use of devices (for example) and to recognise and reflect in an offer what is particularly relevant to the customer.

### Data Reservoir

From this assortment of data, it becomes possible to create a single repository or ‘reservoir’ of relevant information, and in doing so, create a 360-degree view of the customer. Without effective data management and tooling, data collection by itself is irrelevant.

The various different types of data – structured, unstructured and from different sources – require a variety of technological tools to extract the information from the sources. The intention isn’t to comment on or to recommend these, but these data management tools might include, for example, the management of:

- (a) Continual data feeds, to ensure that offers are both timely and relevant
- (b) Content management, which effectively captures and sorts data
- (c) Text analytics, which makes sense of free text and voice translations from contact centre interactions and web contact
- (d) Device data analysis, such as locational details from smartphones
- (e) Customer identification, which tracks single customer identification across multiple apps

All of these processes need to be developed around an appropriate culture of governance and security, especially as the bank is potentially vulnerable to infiltration as it metaphorically opens its doors to external sources of data.

### Creating Insight

In the previous chapter, we reminded ourselves that neither data nor analytical processes were the destination but rather it was what was done with this analysis that makes the difference. Fundamentally, the aim of this exercise is to establish with an increased degree of probability what is the customer’s propensity to buy a product or service. This insight will become increasingly important as banks offer Open Banking and, de facto, facilitate increased mobility of customers between banking organisations. (See Chapter 9.) Better insight becomes critical, therefore, to allow banks to be able to identify which customers are most likely to leave so, in a way that is similar to the insurance problem of customer churn, appropriate attention can be given to matters of retention.

Additionally, as banks consider other business models such as ‘universal banking’ and ‘full-service banking’, which are in effect brand extensions, they will also need to gauge the propensity of their existing customers to purchase a broader financial (and even possibly non-financial) product set.

Such insight may not necessarily all be used for ‘outward-facing’ activities. With greater data, banks will be able to gain more holistic insights about their customer base, which, when linked with sentiment analysis, may assist the bank in establishing more effective tactics and strategies and help them to understand why their strategy has been successful or otherwise.

The ability of a bank to understand these outcomes in the context of the performance of their peers – or competitors – increasingly becomes a critical success factor. The increased availability of external information from published reports such as Bloomberg, when coupled with internal information, starts to become essential. We might call this ‘contextual analytics’.

### Key Deliverable

The aim of all that has gone before is to create such a degree of granularity that the bank has, in effect, a ‘segment of one’ – in other words, an approach of ‘hyper-personalisation’. Greater granularity, which provides a higher level of customer segmentation, will hopefully lead to more timely and appropriate offers.

Analysis will also provide an element of prioritisation: who is most likely to buy, who is most likely to leave.

The manner in which this offer is made is also important, with special care being needed to ensure that the messaging is consistent regardless of the channel (aka ‘omnichannel’ marketing). With an increased focus on mobile devices as a key communication medium, the ability to express these offers in a readily ‘digestible’ way, on a small screen, also becomes a critical success factor.

The essence of modern ‘360-degree customer insight’ thinking is to move the relationship between the supplier and the end user away from the ‘relational’ – in other words, Party A trying to sell to Party B – to one that is ‘experiential’ – in other words, the intention is to create a whole new level of connectivity based on the experience of the customer when engaging with that particular brand.

The motivation for such an approach is that if the customer enjoys a better experience, they start to enjoy a deeper, more loyal and therefore more sustainable interaction. They become an advocate or ‘ambassador’ for the brand (as we recognise in Chapter 6, the idea of ‘brand’ is increasing in importance). Beyond this, a deeper relationship translates in financial terms to a more sustainable ‘lifetime value opportunity’.

The opposite of this is that current customers who ‘digitally’ interact with the bank and suffer a ‘clunky’ experience if the system is slow, frictional or unsafe will be more inclined not only to leave but might even tell their friends of the poor experience, which could affect business growth. The impact of this with respect to Open Banking cannot be underestimated. A ‘clunky’ or ineffective app can have a significant effect on customer experience, especially amongst Gen Z. The corollary is that a smooth, attractive app has the potential to win customers as well as any smooth marketing campaign.

Effective digital banks focusing on the customer also increasingly recognise that they need to draw both from the customer’s past and their present, in order to anticipate what might be the customer’s possible future. It’s also a way of anticipating the customer’s potential lifetime value to the bank. This chronology-based approach provides a different, perhaps more customer-centric approach to the four key stages immediately mentioned above but one that is complementary and lends itself to AI applications.
