Overall, it is important to emphasise that analytics is not the destination but rather what is done with the analytics, and that analysis should be viewed in that spirit. Analysis provides a means to an end, by changing processes and practices, and in doing so can create a combination of hard and/or soft benefits. These benefits may comprise:

+   - Higher levels of customer satisfaction leading to higher levels of retention or cross selling/upselling
+   - Greater operational efficiencies
+   - Better risk management
+   - More effective investment policies

Insight provided from analytics should not only feed in to the development of best practices but also to manual and automatic decisioning, and ultimately to strategic and operational judgments. Overall the analytical process should not sit in isolation to the wider business but rather must be an integral part of the organisation, one we might describe as an ‘analytical enterprise’.

# 'Next-Generation' Cognitive Analytics

'Next-generation' analytics is increasingly likely to be ‘cognitive’ in nature, not only by providing probabilistic insight based on some degree of machine learning but also with a more natural human interface (as opposed to requiring machine coding). Cognitive analytics is a stepping stone towards ‘full-blown’ AI. However, ‘cognitive’ is not ‘artificial intelligence’ or ‘AI’ out of the mold of HAL in Kubrick’s 2001: A Space Odyssey but rather represents a different relationship between the computer and the user.

There’s already a great deal of confusion relative to the use of the terms ‘cognitive computing’ and ‘artificial intelligence’. It’s important not to be too churlish about these definitions. After all, the man and woman in the street often describe the ability of a system that makes predictions such as choice of music or viewing as a form of AI.

Purists argue that they are different; generalists say that they are more or less the same. The concept of ‘cognition’ implies that the computing platform in some way replicates the operation of the human brain by combining different technical capabilities such as language processing, visual recognition, sophisticated algorithms and speech. The net outcome of this combination of capabilities is that the system ultimately ‘assists’ human decision making, rather than replaces it. By comparison, true ‘AI’ completely does away with the need for human intervention and for that reason sits at the very peak of the ‘hierarchy of analytics’.

Cognitive analytics will have a major part to play in the banking challenges of skill shortages and the so-called demographic explosion.

Forms of cognitive computing are already being used in healthcare and asset management, in the context of ‘wealth advisors’, and it is only a matter of time before it finds its way into mainstream retail banking activities.
