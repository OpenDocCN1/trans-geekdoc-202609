
As banks progress towards fully accessible, ubiquitous banking that isn’t dependent on a human at the end of a phone network but rather replaced by some sort of automated system, the concept of café banks is also likely to be affected. Robotics advice coupled with self-service coffee?

In his article for International Banking, Urs Bolt refers to the concept of the ‘humanoid banker’ and looks further into the issue of robotic advice. He asserts, ‘Banking is more than technology, but it cannot progress without it.’⁴ By the same measure, perhaps café banks specifically have to be more than just coffee, but they cannot exist without it.

## Millennials Not Happy with Dealing Only with Robots

A survey carried out by Global Data suggested that ‘only 5% of UK millennial investors use robo-advice services, preferring human expertise over AI technology.’

In making the case for robo-advice, Sergel Woldemichael, Wealth Management analyst at Global Data, remarked that whilst digital services offer operation efficiencies through automation and the ability to reach new audiences, their research indicated that the ‘vast majority’ of more digitally advanced younger generations still require human interaction before they will make an investment decision. He added:

> Pigeonholing the next generation into a digital chamber is the wrong way to view millennials. Mobile investment apps are unable to cater for the emotional needs of humans when seeking financial advice. A hybrid model is the best bet for the moment and for the near future. Evidently, these digital natives are still seeking a human to collaborate with and gain valuable insight from.⁵

It’s an interesting perspective but one that could suffer from geographical distortion. Checking out which countries are the most ‘tech-savvy’, there are different results dependent on which research is considered but one typical set of results is shown in Table 11.

## Virtual Reality: Banking and Gaming Converge

Virtual reality is defined as ‘a realistic simulation of an environment, including three-dimensional graphics, by a computer system using interactive software and hardware’¹⁰ It lends itself to the banking and financial services market and especially to the Gen Z community, providing:

- An ability to revisit the customer experience, which can be tested and checked out rather than taken for granted
- The potential for ‘pure segmentation’ rather than the broad-brush approach of multi- and omni-channel
- A capability to differentiate beyond the relatively generic High Street presence of traditional banks
- An approach more specifically aligned to the Gen Z customer
- A less expensive distribution model than traditional ‘bricks and mortar’
- The potential to create new revenue streams from interested and curious stakeholders
- A possible modelling of income streams and financial outcomes before individual customers commit to making a decision

Two major questions are those of whether customers are yet ready for this type of technology, and whether banks are ready to utilise it. By way of comparison, conference companies have already explored the idea of ‘virtual conferences’, with mixed success. Virtual banking seems to be some sort of a crossover between finance and ‘gaming’, but gaming appears to have a very high level of interest amongst Gen Z.

So what exactly does this crossover point look like?

Therein lies one of the paradoxes. Chapter 6 considered the different needs of the different segmentations – Gen X, Y, Z – recognising the need for banks and financial services to try and offer products and services to a complex and different set of ‘actors’ who have different needs and aspirations. The concept of omnichannel is promoted as an approach that allows a single brand to get its message to multiple channels and audiences with consistency, but that may perhaps be so difficult as to be almost impossible.

Maybe because of this, the likelihood is that banking as it is traditionally organised may need to disintermediate or dismantle itself. Core functions like capital management might remain the same, but marketing and distribution channels could become more segmented and more specialised.

## UNIVERSAL BANKING AND BEYOND

The concept of the ‘universal bank’ is one whereby banks provide a wide range of services, including credit, loans and advisory services on investment. A common approach in the US and Switzerland, a ‘universal banking system’ allows a bank to offer a range of services but does not oblige them to do so. Some of the major banks, such as Deutsche Bank, HSBC and ING Bank, are already considered to be universal banks. The general intention behind the concept is to leverage scale and customer insight, and also to gain greater access to capital.

It allows the combination of an investment bank and a commercial bank to provide both services from a single entity. One issue to consider is to what degree banks may be prepared to go beyond the current model to one where the brand is extended into new business areas such as auto and property services. (See Figure 8.17.) Although universal banks tend to confine themselves to banking services such as retail, investment and stock-broking, it may be necessary for them to understand what a customer looks for in a bank.

In their 2016 report,¹² Finextra asked what customers expected from their bank. Not surprisingly, there were issues relating to satisfactory discharging of all the hygiene factors of the banking process, such as speed, efficiency and security, but the report implied that ‘connected consumers’ are expecting much more, particularly asking for ‘banking services which integrate with their everyday life’. This might be described as banking services extending to a ‘Lifestyle Ecosystem’.

Some banks are already moving in that direction by bolting on additional capabilities such as home emergency, travel and health insurance, but these add-ons still feel clunky and far from being seamless in terms of the consumer experience. Usually they are a series of ‘hand-offs’ to independent third-party providers with little insight into the customer’s own needs, instead viewing these relationships as insular and ring-fenced. In many cases, the third parties are also working for others and are unlikely to be able to provide any service or brand differentiation.

If the banks are to follow a more strategic and broader approach to universality, there are major issues to overcome, such as:

- Effective bidirectional flow of information from third party to bank, and vice versa
- Greater understanding of customer needs
- Better supplier management controls and methodologies
- More effective brand differentiation in the supply chain
