
Coupled with this is likely to be the emergence of contextual analytics. Banking organisations will become better at knowing and optimising their own performance but unless consideration is given to what is happening outside their own organisation – for example, amongst their competitors – then these viewpoints are being made in a vacuum. The American scientist Alan Kay expressed it succinctly: ‘Context is worth 80 IQ points.’¹⁰

In the cold light of day, two key objectives need to be adopted by bankers:

- 1. First, outperform direct competitors.
- 2. Second, achieve the strategic objectives of the organisation.

To do one and not the other is a job only partly completed. Often (but not always) these two key objectives go hand in hand. As some banks focus all the more on softer measures of success – for example, engagement with the community or responding to social needs – success may increasingly also be measured by consumer sentiment and brand recognition as well as by profitability.

## Extracting Value from Data

Data in either its structured or unstructured forms does not naturally flow into analytic outcomes, which usually take the form of reports, predictions or recommended actions, but is dependent on intermediate processes. These exist between the data and the analytics, and might generally be known as the ‘plumbing’ of the systems. This essential process allows all organisations to extract value or ‘monetise’ the data. (See Figure 3.7.)

How this is done in practice is a matter for technical experts, but in simple terms the raw data needs to be captured, then brought into the system where it is filtered, cleansed and usually stored. Massive volumes of data lend themselves to complex sorting systems or ‘landing zones’, most of which have their own language and jargon. Often a ‘datamart’ or ‘staging layer’ is created to ensure that an analytical outcome can be created relatively quickly. The process by which data is moved through the system is referred to as ETL, or ‘extract, transform, load’.

There are other alternatives, such as ‘data warehouse appliances’, which provide a parallel processing approach and create a modular, scalable, easy-to-manage database system. These high-speed solutions allow very rapid computing power by providing an alternative to traditional linear processing, and often come with pre-bundled analytical and geospatial capabilities. In effect, this is a ‘plug and play’ approach to data and analytics. This serves as a reminder that, in the same way as was experienced with the internet in the early days, both organisations and individuals are increasingly pressing for computing power in the form of analytics to be provided ‘at speed’. It doesn’t seem that long ago that, in a domestic environment, for example, connecting to the internet was accompanied by some form of whistling and other strange noises down the telephone line.

Now (at the time of publication) instant 4G connectivity is expected anytime, anywhere – within reason – and we are already seeing the first examples of 5G with new levels of hyper-delivery. Perhaps in that light, if one level of differentiation

API management is a critical component in the transformation of banks to a digital format. APIs, or Application Programming Interfaces, are a set of functions and procedures for building software. They are also viewed as critical in the Open Banking implementation. In effect, they are a set of programming instructions for developers to allow them to access web-based systems¹¹ and to allow banks and other organisations to:

- Accelerate application creation, allowing the speedy and effective implementation of mobile, omnichannel and IoT strategies.
- Improve the management of a partner ecosystem, ensuring scalable integration with global security standards, which will be critical as banks seek to extend their brand influence and universality.
- Create effective environments for innovation, experimentation, hackathons and new business models.
- Allow the bringing together of startup and innovation ecosystems.
- Provide clear separation ensuring security, agility and governance.

‘Cloud’ computing also needs to be considered. One good and simple description of cloud computing, often just referred to as simply ‘the cloud’, is the delivery of on-demand computing resources. This includes everything from applications to data centres. These resources may be on a pay-for-use basis, often accessible through wireless or increasingly at no cost to the consumer as an added-value offering. There’s a growing trend for High Street branches of banks to offer complimentary Wi-Fi to visiting customers and also, incidentally, to passers-by as an attempt to encourage them to visit the premises.

For the record, the concept of ‘the cloud’ is not a process in the sky or somewhere in the ether but rather is an expression to reflect a capability. Users should not be misled by the fact that there are usually no cables or physical connectivity involved. As with the other processes described above, the technology is too complex to be considered in detail and cloud computing as a topic is in itself worthy of its own book. Cloud computing also provides another example of how a paradigm shift in the thinking of the banking industry needs to take place.

The entire concept opens the door to new thinking and those who do not have an open mind will be disadvantaged. In their 2014 document ‘Predicts 2015: Cloud Computing Goes Beyond IT Into Digital Business’,¹² Gartner indicate that business leaders will need to ‘constantly adapt their strategies to leverage increasing cloud capabilities’. The use of a ‘private cloud’ is also a critical element in terms of the adoption of a digital strategy. A ‘private cloud’ is one accessible only within the business enterprise. The game has moved on significantly in a decade and there are few (if any) computers now sat beneath the desks of employees. There are obvious benefits in the use of a private cloud:

- Greater access to computing power and scale coupled with improved management of risk within the enterprise.
- The ability to leverage the entire enterprise rather than operating in silos, as well as providing a more customer centric capability.
- The potential to allow products and services to be developed, and to come to market, much more quickly. To that extent, access to cloud is a key element of an organisation’s agility and flexibility.
- Impact on talent management and resources with the ability of businesses to ‘off-shore’ their development. Some may argue that this offshoring is mainly a cost-driven exercise, which is almost certainly the case at least in part. However, the ability to incorporate different geographies becomes a game-changer.

Cloud is the foundational ‘platform’ by which advanced analytics and AI will be launched for the banking industry, and is the cornerstone of the bank of the future. It is now more than ever critical that business users have some understanding not only of current IT capabilities but potential developments likely to happen in the future, so that they can effectively manage their business and create new and compelling strategies. This requires them to stay ahead of the times, and to be accurately and effectively briefed by internal experts and external thought leaders.

The speed of progress, even within the banking industry, begs questions of the right technology strategies to be adopted, regardless of the nature of the bank. The key options seem to be:

- 1. **The bank building its own, customised capability:** This may be cheaper at the outset, but the low cost of ownership at the beginning may be rapidly overtaken by the cost of maintenance and essential upgrades. In many cases, self-built systems often develop a ‘legacy status’ that is difficult to improve especially as the original technical architects may have left the organisation (or perhaps were only ever external ‘contractors’).
- 2. **Banks buying a piece of technology, or a system:** The use of the technique of consultative selling has allowed vendors to sell expensive technologies more effectively. Implementation issues remain complex but the economic buyer for the bank will at least satisfy internal auditors by purchasing from a vendor with a proven track record. Buying from a single vendor may also lead to a growth in dependence and a form of commercial ‘lock-in’.
- 3. **Partner with a technology provider:** At face value, partnering is an attractive approach apart from the fact that many strategic partnerships seem only to have a limited life. Purchasers are also often anxious about being ‘locked in’, and their having reduced opportunity for changing partners if the circumstances require it. In many cases, as with smaller fintech providers, the relationship starts off as a partnership and, with time, can become an acquisition.
