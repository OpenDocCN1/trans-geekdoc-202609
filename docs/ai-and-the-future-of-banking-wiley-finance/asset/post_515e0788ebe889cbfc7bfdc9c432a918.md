‘A decade from now,’ he says, ‘a smartphone will look dated and slightly ridiculous’, and ‘computer screens that we spend our time glued to will disappear’. ‘All reality’, he says, ‘will be augmented reality’, and what we recognise as traditional advertising will be replaced by images projected from a contact lens directly onto the retina. It’s a bold, perhaps slightly frightening technologically infused vision. Maybe like Nostradamus, people might remember the predictions that were correct but conveniently forget the ones that were hopelessly wrong. Even so, Domingos has a point regarding the speed of change and that it might happen more quickly than we expect. Or maybe not.

There is, however, a certain inevitability about change that will impact the banking industry. That is why at the very beginning of this book some time was spent in exploring ‘the nature’ of banking and money, because this will inevitably feed through into our thinking about the future of banking.

## The Full-Service Bank

Earlier in this chapter we explored the concept of the universal bank, that is, one that provides a wide range of services. Chapter 6 also considered the importance of brand to the banking industry and how this will impact on consumer loyalty, especially amongst Millennials and Gen Zers. Figure 8.17 extends this concept to a ‘full-service financial institution’, which takes account of all their customers’ lifestyle requirements, as well as their financial management needs.

In the same way that auto manufacturers create finance propositions by having partnership relations with banks to allow cars to be both financed and purchased, this alternative model of ‘extended universal banks’ allows them to provide things like mobility solutions, which might extend to road, rail or aircraft capabilities. In other words, the consumer of the future may buy an aircraft ticket through their bank as opposed to just using the bank’s payment system. The buying leverage of the major banks will mean that preferential terms can be obtained for customers, better than those available on aggregator sites. Similarly, providers of vehicles or indeed any significant commodity will be able to leverage the bank’s own customer base as a form of distribution channel.

In terms of the customer journey, in this model the customer will have a lifetime relationship with the bank as their ‘trusted partner’. Salaries, savings, pensions, investments and loans will all rest in one banking organisation. Banks will not only understand the life cycle of their customer but help them to plan for it, both whilst in work and afterwards. This model stretches the concept of the universal bank deeper and broader into the requirement of the ‘connected customer’, and requires the bank to have a more ambitious ecosystem than currently exists.

In terms of creating a wider ecosystem, a number of key actions would be required:

In this model and in addition to the bank itself, participants and ultimately key stakeholders are likely to comprise existing established ‘actors’ as well as start-ups. Development may occur within the bank but in some sort of ring-fenced division.

## The Digital Bank

In this scenario, the Bank of the Future will remain much as we know it today, but operating at a branch level in an enhanced way. For example, in 2019 UK-based NatWest bank opened a new digital store in the city centre of Leeds in the north of England. It aims to give customers the space to drop in, complete their banking online in a secure environment using their own or the bank’s devices, and have expert staff on hand to provide assistance. The new ‘digital store’ also allows customers to prebook a meeting online with a personal banker for their more complex needs, avoiding time wasted in queues.

Beyond banking, the new store also acts as an events space where anyone, not just NatWest customers, can visit and listen to finance-related presentations such as how to avoid being affected by cybercrime. It also attracts children to attend child-friendly events as part of NatWest’s ‘Moneysense’ programme.<sup>24</sup>

According to research by Caravan Omnibus Surveys, 88% of American adults still feel a need to visit a physical branch location, yet there’s recognition that the old branch model needs to be transformed into what some are calling a ‘bricks-and-clicks’ approach.<sup>25</sup>

Some banks, such as Umpqua Bank in Oregon, US, are adopting a ‘hub-and-spoke’ approach by having a fully serviced hub branch and a series of self-service satellites in local neighbourhoods. Barclays already provide complete self-service branches open 24/7 and with access to services entirely by ATM and telephone. Tangerine Bank (formerly ING Direct in Canada), identified as the best online bank in Canada in 2019, have even tested pop-up branches.<sup>26</sup>

Variations on this model include the concept of ‘banking pods’, which might comprise the ‘ATM of the future’ that allows customers to undertake more complex transactions, robotic advisors, virtual banking (through the use of video links), and agent branches, where a form of franchised version emerges.<sup>27</sup>

What seems clear is that there could still be a role for traditional bank branches in one form or another, even in a digital, AI-infused age. Why should this be? This question needs to be addressed not only by banks but by all brands. According to brand experts BWP group, it’s partly because consumers are distrustful of advertising. According to their surveys, 95% of Gen Z are expecting brands to court them and 80% even want brands to entertain them. Increasingly, consumers are seeing retailers use ‘showrooming techniques’ to reinforce their brand, allowing the consumer to visit, touch, feel and experience before making an online purchase.<sup>28</sup>

If a brand exists only online, is this sufficient to fully engage with it? Whilst this may be relevant for commodity goods, how does this translate to the financial services industry? Surely financial services are more important than perfume and cars (although not every consumer may see it that way).

There is something more subtle to be addressed. Banking is about safety, accessibility, prudence and security. Can these be accurately reflected physically through the branch network, whether clicks or bricks? Have banks something to learn from, say, Apple Stores, which sell not only devices but fundamentally sell something equally as intangible as money: their operating system?

## The Disaggregated Bank

The concept of ‘disaggregation’ refers to something divided into its component parts.<sup>29</sup> In this alternative model, one option might be that the banking industry disaggregates or ‘dismantles’ what is, in effect, parts of its internal supply chain, which are non-core to the banking business. It implies that many of the other functions within a bank are not ‘core’ to the real business of banking. In effect, the cash teller in this model is ‘just’ an intermediary in a functional sense, rather than a net contributor to value.

Branch presence remains reassuring in the banking industry and is a matter of brand reinforcement, but perhaps it might be done in a different sort of way. The challenge for many is ‘brand or no brand’. Millennials have ‘debranding’ on their agenda. Banks need somehow to extend their brand without being explicit and without taking on more cost and operational risks.

The High Street branch presence is no less important than the ‘rural’ presence. Away from the town and city, the bank provides an important social contribution to the community. By closing these rural banks in the name of ‘operational efficiency’, it creates disconnect between the bank and the society it seeks to serve. In doing so, banks fail to recognise the social obligation they have to the community and which underpins the concept of ‘money’ in the first place. Community banks and cooperatives may have a significant part to play in the future of banking.

But – for the purposes of this particular model – this option might be that the banks have no ‘front office’ of their own but rather that they act through third parties and intermediaries (which may be franchised). The technological equivalent might well be something like ‘Intel Inside’, the branding used by Intel as one of the largest co-marketing campaigns through members/partners licensed to use the Intel technology in their devices. Taking an opposing view, Bank of America say that they do not want to operate with other distributors as a ‘BoA inside model’ but rather want to be seen in a more customer-facing light.<sup>32</sup>

Intel remain in the technological banking equation even if their marketing is somewhat different nowadays. Their focus is very much on 5G technology, which in itself will start to revolutionise the banking industry, and may permit new, different models as a result of superfast connectivity.

5G, or 5th-generation wireless systems, is viewed as being the technology that satisfies the International Telecommunications Union (ITU) IMT-2020 requirements. The requirements cover vision and deliverables for 2020. 3GPP Release 15 deals with standardisation, providing what they describe as ‘high throughput, low latency, high mobility and high connection density.’

5G is about:

- Super-fast speed, which improves download and upload speed
- Latency, which they describe as lightning-fast real-time user experience on mobile devices, so much so that consumers will experience banking and payment transactions instantly on their device. 5G will mean zero waiting time.
- Unity, as increasingly systems and especially devices will be able to communicate with one another at ‘super speeds’, which will impact on uses ranging from remote devices to biometrics
- Improved automation, including, for example, paying fines

In the trading environment it will allow transactions to be made in micro-seconds, and as a result, it is forecast that trading companies (and bank investor functions) will be amongst the early commercial adopters. 5G also appears to reopen the argument for disaggregation once more.<sup>33</sup>
