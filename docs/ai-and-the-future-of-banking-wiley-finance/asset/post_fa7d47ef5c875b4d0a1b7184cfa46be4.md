
What might then be a typical persona specifically of an AI-infused ‘leader’ in this future scenario?

- 1. The issue of gender and ethnicity will be increasingly addressed.

Forty years ago, with the introduction of the Equal Pay Act, the issue of gender equality was recognised, and whilst there are more women in the boardroom, the senior echelons of banking remain dominated by both male and white ethnic groups. According to Bloomberg data, in the overall financial services industry, only 12% of corporate board members are currently women and (at time of writing) only Banco Santander had a woman chairperson.36

- 2. There will have been a convergence of technology and finance training, so that banking leaders of tomorrow will have hybrid capabilities.

Early role models will initially be financiers with technical awareness working alongside technologists with finance industry awareness. With time these two roles will come together into a single, unified function. By that time, how individual users interact with technological systems will also have been transformed using voice- or thought-activated instructions and contactless security access. Those now considered as the generation of ‘digital natives’ will be considered by then to be ‘digital ancestors’.

- 3. Individual learning will have been transformed.

Experience will be gained not only in a ‘live’ environment but also through augmented reality and virtual banking scenarios. Game playing will be a crucial component of the learning experience. Traditional career paths will no longer exist. The most important leadership trait will be that of being an influencer.

- 4. The key role of the leader will be that of ‘managing the mission’ of the bank, whatever that might happen to be in precise terms in 30 years’ time, given the changing nature both of banks and of money over that period.

This might typically include the management of strategic knowledge assets, retention and development of core competencies, exploitation of areas of expertise, preservation of intellectual property and identification and nurturing of talent.

## CONCLUSION

This section focussed not on technology and the application of AI-based systems but rather on our individual and corporate responses to these changes. Effective implementation of any transformation programme of any type is not solely dependent on technology but usually is a feature of leadership, organisational structure and ultimately how individuals inside the organisation react to the change. There is little doubt that, for many, change is both unsettling and threatening. Promises that automation and the application of advanced methods will free up individuals for more valuable, interesting jobs are often looked on with suspicion, as are suggestions that whole new and as yet unspecified ‘other’ industries will emerge to fill unemployment gaps.

It is against this background of uncertainty that the banking industry will transform itself.

Banking leaders will need to somehow blend the need for risk-averse, compliant direction with the need to respond to changing times, changing customers, and the increasing threat of both challenger banks and also new quasi-financial institutions, typically retailers and others intruding into the banking landscape. To do this effectively will require leaders to adopt new and relevant competences, which perhaps they may learn from mentors who are younger and less experienced than them. Old dogs will need to learn new tricks, and who best to learn them from but a younger, more dynamic generation?

As the nature of leadership changes, so too will organisational structures, which will increasingly focus on the consumer and brand experience. Because of this, there will be an inevitable transformational drag on bank employees themselves, whose route to success will not be one of working harder and longer but one of recognising and responding to the changes happening around them.
