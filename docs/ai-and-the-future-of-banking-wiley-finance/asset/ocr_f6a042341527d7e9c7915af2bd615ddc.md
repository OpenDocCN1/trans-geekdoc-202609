These are enormously complex areas, and are sufficient to have whole books written on the subject rather than a passing part of a chapter. The definitive book in this area is possibly *Investment Analytics: Revolutionizing Professional Investment with Artificial Intelligence, Big Data, and Cloud Computing*³⁸ by Bernard Lee, but there is no shortage of other reading matter. In his book *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*, Marcos Lopez de Prado postulates, ‘what if economists started to consider non-linear regression’, and how ‘to deal with 100 variables, instead of 3’.³⁹

Lopez de Prado also refers to an ‘industrial chain’ to the process, comprising:

+   - Data sources and data platforms
+   - Feature analysis: by researchers or automatically generated
+   - Strategy development: using manual model selection or through automated machine learning
+   - Backtesting: both sequential and randomised
+   - Deployment of outcome, in an automatic deployment way
+   - Portfolio oversight: both in real time (live) or post audit

Of these it is suggested that it is only the first (*data sources and platforms*) that cannot be automated – that is to say, cannot be the subject of AI-infusion.

This point is reinforced by Yves Hipschich, who rather novelly introduces the concept of AI in finance under the moniker ‘finqince’ (sic).⁴⁰ He also suggests the role of AI in trading principally comprises two key elements:

+   1. Prediction engines, which embed statistical methods and technical rules in an operating environment of machine and deep learning
+   2. Decision rules, which set the transactional framework for the system, comprising entry rules, stop loss levels and position sizing for example

# CONCLUSION

In this chapter, different types of the use of analytics has been considered but, in the nature of the breadth and depth of the subject, there are areas that command deeper investigation.

The increasing push for hyper-customer centricity at scale can only lend itself to increased automation, robotics and AI. Understanding the customer is only one part of the equation, with the most important part (for the bank) being what to do next. With retention becoming a bigger issue with open banking, knowing what the customer wants, or will do next, becomes especially important.

Elsewhere, in trading, for example, the challenge (or opportunity) for better cross-asset trading optimisation and portfolio management requires deep insight, which is fundamentally analytical in nature, and ultimately gives high levels of insight into markets and pricing, and informs trading strategies.
