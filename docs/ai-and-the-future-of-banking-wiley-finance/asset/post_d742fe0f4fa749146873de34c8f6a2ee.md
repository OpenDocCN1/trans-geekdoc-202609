## Key Elements of Banking Analytics

### SUMMARY

In this section the current use of analytics in the banking industry will be considered in more detail as an introduction to the topic, before going on to look at the topic of machine learning and AI in more detail in the next chapter.

The examples given are grouped into the four key areas: financial performance management, customer analytics, risk management and operational efficiency.

Of these, the most important is arguably that of financial performance management because it is in effect the ‘engine room’ of the banking organisation. Without effective visibility into the achieved revenue, costs and channel profitability, the organisation is operating blindly. Clear understanding of the financial situation is critical to understanding the potential return on investment for the adoption of new technologies. The other three areas of customer analytics, operational efficiency and risk management are primarily separate functions (although in some cases the risk function is ‘owned’ by the Office of Finance).

The way that the examples have been arranged has been to group each set of analytics and then consider their relevance to different types of banks. A different approach might have been to look at the use of analytics on a ‘bank-type’ by ‘bank-type’ basis, allowing future specific bank-type books to be written, such as ‘Analytics for Investment Banking’, which could have greater depth. However, this book aims for a more generalised nature, and the author begs acceptance of the approach taken.

## INTRODUCTION

Analytics is already heavily used in banking and is a key enabler to many quite diverse parts of the organisation. This section will review the current uses of analytics in the banking industry, but this is far from a comprehensive picture as each separate section merits detailed investigation by the reader.

The essence of all analytical analysis in banking is that of the management of finances and the operations of the Office of Finance. This is the ‘engine room’ of the banking organisation. Clear understanding of the profitability of divisions, channels and products rests at the heart of bank profitability. Beyond this are considered issues of customer insight, followed then by risk management, the latter driving into more detail in relation to operational risk, credit risk and market risk, by way of example. The diversity of the industry lends itself to multiple types of risk management and not all can be considered in sufficient depth within a single volume, but will perhaps provide a taster as to the issue and complexities involved.

Additionally, matters such as operational analytics (for example, branch performance management) are included. Whilst the concept of branches as we normally understand them is, if not in doubt, then certainly under discussion, it is useful to understand how the success of branches is currently measured. Later on, in Chapter 8, the way that branches might develop in the future will be explored, including the concept of ‘debranching’.

Even taking all the above into account, the use of analytics in banking is a broad church, and one that will continue not only to broaden but also to deepen. It is an integral part of the AI journey and the route towards the Bank of the Future.

## OFFICE OF FINANCE MANAGEMENT

It is in the nature of some readers to think that analytics in the office of finance need not be industry specific or indeed banking specific, to think that all finance officers and accountants are more or less the same, and that they undertake the same functions. To some degree they are right in that accounting practices across all industries by necessity have a degree of standardisation to enable organisations to be benchmarked against one another. Equally, there is an argument that financial analytics are too complex to be understood and managed by other than professionally trained accountants.

On the other hand, many industries do necessarily have distinct characteristics, and banking falls within that grouping. Banking has many functions, including the need to manage structured uncertainty in multiple lines of business and product sets, and manage that risk with a view to creating measurable benefit from that risk, and ultimately turn a profit.

It is also important that the office of finance in banking has a mechanism that enables them to manage the full breadth and depth of their organisation in a consistent way. This manifests itself in multiple and often diverse products and offerings, multiple routes to market and channels and in more complex organisations, to reflect multiple geographies. Put quite simply, the banking environment has become too complex for financial management by spreadsheet, even for smaller banks.

A future, more ‘agile’ banking industry is one with shorter product development lifecycles, new distribution models and perhaps also a tendency to sunset products or continually add value – and will require banks to be much more proactive in their response to the market. This in turn will place greater demands on the office of finance, whose increasing role recognises their strategic impact on the bank as a whole.

Beyond this, as new techniques emerge such as advanced analytics and AI, banks especially need to be able to measure and understand the ‘hard’ benefits that accrue. With many decisions regarding innovation on the horizon, there is an increasing need to measure Return on Investment (ROI) with certainty and, in doing so, support the need for further investment.

But it is not ‘just’ a matter of financial performance management. There is an increased focus on regulatory requirements, especially in the light of losses following adverse exposures to securitised asset portfolios, that is, assets that have been pooled together (such as mortgages, loans and credit card advances), whose related ‘cash flows’ are sold on to third-party investors as securities such as bonds. There is a burden on banks to prepare high-quality regulatory reports, and these are foundational to the monitoring of a bank’s safety and soundness. Because of this greater attention, banks are subject to stronger focus on risk management, as well as the need to comply with complex regulatory environments (such as Basel, IFRS and others). Cross-border operations and the many effects of globalisation also add to that risk, so the ability to have a clear view of performance is more important than ever, using flexible, comprehensive and robust processes.

The Office of Finance is being viewed more and more as a dynamic, interactive part of the organisation rather than simply a ‘bean counter’. This requires the finance professional to be effectively engaged with the wider business, taking part in strategic (and in some cases even tactical) considerations, and acting as a trusted advisor rather than a form of custodian of finances.

In many cases, especially in smaller banks, responsibility for risk management and regulatory compliance also falls to the Office of Finance, when arguably some are less well-prepared to undertake such a role.

The relationship between the CEO and the CFO is critical in understanding the relationship between finance and the wider business. If the CEO is the ‘pilot’ of the organisation, the CFO is the co-pilot, and co-operatively they must work as a fully integrated team for the benefit of the business. These statements are not, of course, banking specific, but rather represent the changing nature of many professions as a result of the digital and big data revolution.

Cedric Read’s book eCFO: Sustaining Value in the New Corporation² recognises that, going forward, many of the functions undertaken will need to be transformed, including the finance function. If the reader is to be left with one enduring thought about this book, it must be that the world of banking is being transformed by the era of big data and analytics, and nothing will quite be the same again. Such transformation also extends to the Office of Finance.
