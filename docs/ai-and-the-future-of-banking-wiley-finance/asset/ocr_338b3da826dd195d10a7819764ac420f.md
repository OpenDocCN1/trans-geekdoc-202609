Specifically considering wealth management, for example, the opportunity for the use of better, more advanced analytics and even robo-advisors to help optimise portfolios is highly probable. This involves the optimisation of portfolios, including alerting, greater insights into customer acquisition and ultimately the use of enhanced financial advisor solutions to add yet further value to the customer value proposition.

Increasingly complex issues of risk and compliance seem to demand greater automation. This area especially reflects the growing convergence between data, analytics, compliance and supervision. In looking at this from an angle of operational effectiveness, banks need to think about the degree that the process can be automated, which is a large step on the road to the use of AI.

There are some esoteric issues to consider with respect to the use of analytics in these very deep and complex areas, especially as we consider the increased use of automation in investment banking, for example. It requires some imagination but let’s consider that, with machine learning and various AI applications, the important algorithms have evolved to a point that any improvement only gives marginal (if any) benefit. As a result, investment managers might see no competitive advantage in the algorithms used, whether they are built in-house or purchased off the shelf. In fact, there might be no incentive to even try to build these capabilities internally as the cost of upgrade and maintenance will become too prohibitive. We enter into a world of ‘investment software as a service’.

Speed of transactions has already been recognised as a competitive differentiator, with transactions undertaken in micro-seconds. Perhaps with time speed will cease to be, or at worst become less of a differentiator. All investors will refer to and analyse the same sources of information: Bloomberg News, Twitter or whatever their future iterations will look like or be named. Larger institutions will have the advantage of being able to look inside the enterprise, with internal data perhaps giving some additional insight (although it’s not obviously apparent what that internal additional ‘valuable’ info might be).

So where then will the competitive element rest? Surely one of the key differentiating factors will be new forms and sources of data, probably from different and previously unconsidered sectors. Maybe it will be data that is deliberately sought, through new or different devices, or in different formats.

These new data might comprise:

+   - Internal data to the organisation itself, in which case larger organisations appear fundamentally to be at a competitive advantage
+   - New sources of data, data that already exists but hasn’t yet been ‘mined’
+   - Historically unstructured data, which has now, somehow, been sorted
+   - New types of data that haven’t yet been collected but were it to be so, it would add to the mix and provide deeper insight
