Jesse Lund, head of Blockchain at IBM, says about the use of cryptocurrency in this context:

> I think cryptocurrencies could very well serve as a viable settlement instrument. We’ve started with Lumens, which is the native asset of the Stellar network, but we already have the capacity to introduce other cryptocurrencies that could include Bitcoin or Ether. Of particular application is that of remittances or money transfers. The World Bank estimates that migrants across the world send home an estimated US $574 billion, which is about double the amount they were sending a decade ago. Over $138 billion was transferred from the US to other countries as remittances in 2016 alone, and migrants from Latin America and the Caribbean sent record amounts home. According to Pew Research, these remittances are more than just a family lifeline in many cases but a major contributor to some local economies especially in developing areas. Yet these payments struggle because of the need to involve intermediaries for this process, adding time, cost and complexity to the arrangement. The use of a Blockchain solution is said to provide for clearing and settlement in near-real time, and also to allow funds to be transferred at a lower cost than traditional correspondent banking.

The essence of the IBM World Wire solution is that it provides a ‘private and permissioned’ Blockchain protocol for the banking sector, as opposed to one that is more ‘public and permissioned’. The differentiator is that this is a banking-specific solution that meets the broader criteria of the Stellar protocol, which calls for ‘thousands of transactions per second’. According to Lund, ‘This network really has the potential to radically transform foreign exchange entirely’.

From a customer point of view, there are implications of speed, reduced cost and greater efficiency. For the banking industry, this is yet another indication of the use of technology reducing (or even removing) traditional jobs in the banking workplace.

Foreign exchange is described as a ‘market that dwarfs the stock market’.¹⁰ Principally there are two types of foreign exchange market, Interbank Market and Foreign Exchange (or ‘Forex’).

## Interbank Market

The Interbank market is one where, although there are few banks involved, the scale is enormous and sufficient to open the doors to manipulation, especially in the Forex business environment, where there have been previous accusations of rate fixing. Interbank transactions can be between 10 million and 100 million per day. Increased automation has a part to play in the governance of these deals and in helping detect anomalies.

Banks also take part in what is called the SWIFT market, formed in 1974, which allows them to transfer foreign exchange to each other. SWIFT (Society for World-Wide Interbank Financial Telecommunications) is a messaging system that allows banks to securely transmit information between one another through a series of standardised codes. It's a predominantly code-based approach:
- First four characters: the institute code (i.e. the bank code)
- Next two characters: the country code
- Next two characters: the location/city code
- Last three characters: optional, but can be used to assign codes to individual branches

As with Stellar.org, SWIFT is non-profit-making. In this case it comprises a cooperative model owned by members who pay a subscription to take part, plus support fees dependent on the category of use. SWIFT’s particular challenge is in respect to the number and complexity of transactions, and the increasing degree of automation that is occurring. Whilst SWIFT appears to have a dominant position at the moment, there’s a real call to action to invite SWIFT to respond to changing times or suffer the consequences. After all, in this banking industry, how many capabilities created over three decades ago will carry the test of time without changing in the future?

In more recent times, bearing in mind the focus on analytics and AI, it is recognised that ‘SWIFT has recently introduced dashboards and reporting utilities which enable the clients to get a dynamic, real-time view of monitoring the messages, activity, trade flow, and reporting. The reports enable filtering based on region, country, message types, and related parameters.’¹¹

When compared to the forward thinking approach of Blockchain, SWIFT might be argued as holding a ‘defensive position’ concerning issues of scalability, reference data and compliance. These by themselves are not impenetrable positions and it will be interesting to see how SWIFT responds to, or complements with, the Blockchain proposition. Perhaps one test is to consider whether a new entrant to the banking sector would prefer a SWIFT solution or a Blockchain-type solution. Probably both in the interim, but which of these options is likely to be more sustainable?

## Forex Market

The second type of foreign exchange is what is called the Forex or ‘over-the-counter’ market. This market isn't as highly regulated and can be subject to substantial opportunism. It's very much around ‘spot trading’ at the time of exchange or at some time in the future. Every day currency is ‘fixed’ against benchmark foreign currency rates in London which are determined by the buy and sell traders 60 seconds either side of 4 pm, and the benchmark rates of 21 currencies are determined within that one-minute window. The industry has been found to be subject to abuse through rate manipulation and allegations have been made against traders colluding by sharing information about orders prior to the 4 pm ‘fix’, and by what is known as ‘banging the close’ – that is, aggressive buying or selling of pending orders prior to the 4 pm deadline.¹²

There’s no need at this point to go into detail about the pros and cons of these trading approaches other than to consider the impact of more detailed analytics and automation. Perhaps more than the human workforce aspect, the use of advanced analytics is important both to help in the decision-making process and also to detect anomalies. Retrospective analysis by Bloomberg journalists over a two-year period identified that on the last trading day of the month there was a sudden surge (of at least 0.2%) occurring before 4 pm, as often as 31% of the time, followed by a quick reversal.

The management of currencies and transactions looks a little like a game but there are trillions of dollars at stake held by investors and pension funds. ‘Gaming theory’ ideas may be worth considering in the context of these issues, especially as automation of some or all of the rules might be on the horizon. Two particular gaming ideas come to mind, in the context of an interconnected economic environment and on which much has been written and analysed:
- The ‘prisoner’s dilemma’ is an example of a game where two rational participants fail to cooperate or to trust each other even though it is in both their interests to do so. It’s a situation often seen in the climate change discussion.
- In ‘grim trigger’, both sides will initially cooperate, but if one of the participants defects once, then the other will defect (or cheat) all the time.

## Investment Banks and Blockchain

In their 2017 report¹³ Accenture, together with benchmarking firm McLagan, indicated the use of Blockchain could enable investment banks to cut their infrastructure costs by between US $8.0 billion and $12.0 billion by 2025. In their survey of eight of the ten largest investment banks in the world, they concluded that they could ‘reduce infrastructure costs by an average of 30 percent, helped by better data quality and transparency’. Beyond this, it’s suggested that ‘costs associated with compliance, business operations such as trade support and centralized operations such as know-your-customer checks, could fall by up to 50 percent’. These savings do not allow for implementation costs. Others are more sceptical, suggesting that this is just another attempt to jump on the ‘Blockchain bandwagon’.

The decision rests at the door of the regulator, who, it is suggested, might be reluctant to upset the applecart and introduce new solutions and technologies in the clearing and settlement infrastructure without being absolutely certain about the safety, security and resilience of these new approaches.

Other countries appear to be more bullish about the use of this technology. In his article ‘The Bank of China Sees Future in Blockchain’,¹⁴ writer Anujit Mukhopadhyay reminds us that the Bank of China – one of the largest in China and a state-owned enterprise – are looking to transform their business not only through the use of data and AI but also through the use of Blockchain. The bank already uses Blockchain in as many as 12 projects (as of 2018), including cross border payment, digital currencies and data sharing, amongst others. By 2018 they had already filed 11 patents for Blockchain technologies, making them the largest Blockchain innovator in the Chinese financial services sector. According to Mukhopadhyay, one particular patent is that of compressing data in the blocks, which gives scalability. BoC’s goal is not only to digitise its entire operation but also to transfer all its operations to a digital platform and remove the need for hard currency.
