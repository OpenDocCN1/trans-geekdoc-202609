In 2009, Mossiman, Dussalt³ and others identified the issues attaching to the Office of Finance:

+   - A lack of information to regulate what has happened and what will happen
+   - The relevance, visibility and credibility of financial information
+   - The need for balance between short and long term, detailed focus and the big picture
+   - Finding a path between top down vision and bottom up circumstances

They recognised the challenge of multiple decision makers within the organisation, often holding budgets and more importantly carrying accountability for delivery; the balance between cash flow versus working capital; decisions regarding capex versus opex; and the management of the treasury. Not much of that world has moved on over the past ten years.

In the same way that the IT department has needed to change, and perhaps in some aspects has been slightly marginalised in favour of the line of business decision-maker, the role of the Office of Finance is also going through change, especially as more advanced analytical methods become both available and more affordable. This change will continue to happen as AI systems increasingly become embedded into the organisation, automating many of the traditionally routine tasks through a process known as RPA (Robotic Process Automation). Financial decisions are increasingly being placed in the hands of the line of business, as systems replace centralised control. Big data has brought with it ‘big empowerment’, and also what we might describe as ‘big accountability’. At an operational level, the Office of Finance inevitably needs to justify their decision to withhold funding or restrict budgets, for example, more than ever before.

The rules of engagement have also changed as well, especially around the use of analytic capabilities. Line of business executives and staff with specialist needs are increasingly able to identify tools that meet their particular operational needs. Technology vendors are making their tools free of charge (‘freemium’) in an effort to get engagement. Self-service identification of analytic tooling is dangerously becoming the norm and it’s important that the adoption of these ‘tools’ is managed carefully.

The expression ‘dangerous’ is not used lightly, in that multiple tactical purchases potentially lead to a disjointed outcome. The banking organisation is broad and complex, and requires standardisation to ensure alignment, although new ‘tools’ are emerging that help ‘glue’ disparate systems together. Independent decision making and choice of technology can lead to tooling for specific needs but this may be contrary to the greater good of the organisation. Fragmented purchasing cuts across everything that procurement hopes to achieve – standardisation, price leverage, management of upgrades, integration of tooling and strategic procurement rather than a series of tactical relationships that may be more difficult to manage and maintain.
