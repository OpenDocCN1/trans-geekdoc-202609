## Blockchain in Indian Banking

Both Delhi and Mumbai consistently feature not only in the list of megacities for today, but also for ten years hence and at the turn of the next century. It would be remiss not to consider India in more detail.

India appears to be a not-so-secret force in terms of the development of analytics and AI, and Analytics India Magazine is an excellent source of reference for reporting progress in that continent.¹⁸ In her 2019 report ‘AI is the Global Force that Will Transform the Indian Workforce and Create Jobs’, writer Richa Bhatia reminds us that the Indian AI industry has grown by 30% since 2017 and that there is a surge in AI roles being created in ‘Machine Learning, Natural Language Processing, Neural Networks, Analytics, Cloud Computing & Pattern Recognition’.¹⁹

Beyond this, Open Banking is also high on the Indian agenda as a result of the Indian Government’s Unified Payments Interface (UPI), which is an instant real-time payment system developed by National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI). The UPI system not only allows a consumer to transfer money between two bank accounts, but also allows the consumer to pay merchants directly from the bank account using a smartphone.

In 2017, HDFC Bank launched an AI-based chatbot that it claims ‘can answer millions of customer queries across multiple channels instantly’.²⁰ In 2019 Indian commercial bank Federal were reported to have started an Open Banking project.

Blockchain remains very much in the Indian focus, with India having signed a Memorandum of Agreement as part of a BRICS initiative (Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa) as part of a plan under an ‘Interbank Cooperation Mechanism’. ‘The current agreement allows the development banks of BRICS countries to study the applications of innovative technologies in infrastructure finance and bank products optimization.’21

In the same way that the Chinese financial market has undergone massive growth in recent years, so too has the Indian financial services sector. The annual report ‘Analytics and Data Science in Indian Financial Sector: A Deep Dive 2019’,22 undertaken by AIM and Jigsaw Academy, points to the use of analytics to focus on the three key imperatives covered earlier: operations, customer analytics and risk. However, they additionally recognise that the market is looking at the application of advanced analytics and machine learning across the entire enterprise.

The size of the Indian analytics marketplace is already estimated at US $1.2 billion annually. There is a vast availability of talent, with 60,000 data science and financial analytics professionals working in the sector and this figure increases by 7,000 per year, yet such is the pace of change that they still view the main challenge as being that of inadequate resources. The Indian ‘centre of gravity’ for financial analytics is Mumbai, where 33% of analytics professionals work in the finance sector. Despite this it’s a relatively immature profession, with 45% of analytics professionals having less than 5 years’ experience. Only 23% have more than 10 years’ experience.

The AIM/Jigsaw report considers in some depth issues such as location, salaries and even gender, in the context of the Indian market, and concludes:

- 1. Banks and financial institutions in India are pursuing a data-driven agenda with ‘digital technology increasingly becoming the backbone of Financial Institutions’.
- 2. Banks already recognise the key pillars of change, which are technology-strengthened processes and development of people.
- 3. Innovation is being driven forward by AI Centres of Excellence and through the creation of strategic partnerships.
- 4. Specific use cases are already being explored for AI and machine learning to drive greater profit and growth.

One of the key issues Indian banks are particularly focusing on is that of corporate training, as organisations begin to recognise that every employee must become data literate and ‘data smart’ if that organisation is to gain competitive advantage from the data. They have identified that as AI and automation increasingly bites, there will be an impact on job roles. This will involve a need to redesign job frameworks (as AI wealth advisors replace humans, for example). Because of this, banks need to ‘team up with stakeholders to develop tailored solutions for talent management and develop work-ready workforce’. Talent analytics is in the ascendant in terms of emerging technologies.

Indian banks are also on the Blockchain journey, with the first Blockchain solution being adopted in India in 2016 by ICICI Bank in partnership with Emirates NBD, a banking group in the Middle East.23

In January 2019 it was announced that a consortium of 11 Indian banks had launched a Blockchain initiative for the funding of MSMEs (micro, small and medium enterprises) aimed at making supply chain financing more efficient and secure. The solution was facilitated by a third party known as the Blockchain Infrastructure Company (BIC). In India this MSME group are recognised as being underbanked and account for just 17.3% of the credit of Indian banks, despite representing 40% of the workforce (117 million people) and contributing 37% of India’s GDP. The individual credit requirements of these companies range between US $14k and $140k.

The role of BIC is to ensure collaboration between the banks. According to Abhijeet Singh, Head of Business Technology at ICICI Bank, one of the consortium members, ‘A Blockchain network can only thrive if the entire ecosystem is working in synergy through a single network’.

Despite this, the Indian regulator has still taken a strong position by requiring consortium members to dispose of other cryptocurrencies. This stance had been predominantly influenced by money-laundering concerns but was seen by some as an anti-cryptocurrency strategy at a time when other countries such as Thailand and South Korea were positively encouraging them. In 2018 the South Korean government started an investment programme of over US $200 million of investment, with the aim of creating 100 new Blockchain companies hiring 10,000 people over the next 4 years.

Elsewhere in Europe the mood seems to be one of standardisation. In their analysis ‘Virtual Currencies and Central Banks Monetary Policy: Challenges Ahead’ by the policy department for Economic, Scientific and Quality of Life Policies of the European Parliament, the authors conclude:

> Virtual currencies are a contemporary form of private money. Thanks to their technological properties, their global transaction networks are relatively safe, transparent, and fast. This gives them good prospects for further development.
> 
> However, they remain unlikely to challenge the dominant position of sovereign currencies and central banks, especially those in major currency areas.
> 
> As with other innovations, virtual currencies pose a challenge to financial regulators, in particular because of their anonymity and trans-border character.

It’s a helpful report in that it starts to summarise the impact and evolutionary approach to ‘virtual currencies’ over the past decade, and also the regulatory approach. Perhaps more importantly it considers whether virtual currencies can replace the Central Bank’s monopoly on money issuance. Ultimately it concludes that it won’t, but this conclusion seems mainly based on the historic debate between ‘private money’ and the Central Bank as the ‘bank of last resort’. The authors justify this conclusion on the basis that the idea of ‘private money’ arose for three key reasons:

- 1. The rapid increase of demand for money and credit, which cannot be met alone by the Central Bank
- 2. The ‘free market economic school’, which advocates free banking and the need for private money issuance
- 3. The lack of political consensus to create a centralised monetary authority and banking regulation
