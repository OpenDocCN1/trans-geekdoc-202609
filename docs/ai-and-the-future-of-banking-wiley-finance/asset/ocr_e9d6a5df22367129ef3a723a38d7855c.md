# **FIGURE 3.6** Hierarchy of Analytical Capability

+   - **Predictive Analytics** seeks to make predictions on the balance of probabilities, that is, what is likely to happen next. An example of this is the projection of sales and branch performance, enabling the bank to take a view as to what corrective campaign action might be needed.
+   - **Prescriptive Analytics** not only anticipates what will happen next but what should be done about it, usually through some form of rules-based system. It is called ‘prescriptive analytics’ on the basis that it ‘prescribes’ (or suggests) a course of action. One example of this might be the activities happening within a contact or service centre. Commonly also known as ‘next best action’, which might be better expressed as ‘best next action’, it provides the contact centre agent with, for example, insight to help position the best next proposed offering to help to close a deal.

Predictive analytics and prescriptive analytics are probabilistic in nature, in that there is no absolute certainty as to the predicted outcome, but rather the algorithms anticipate the most likely outcome based on the information available. It follows that the more data there is, and the better the data, then the likelihood of accurate prediction and more effective prescription will occur.

Statisticians will be aware of the notion of the ‘false positive’. There are different meanings for this term dependent on the industry application. Generally, it’s taken to mean that of an incorrect outcome (or analysis) based on information that results in a ‘positive result’ when in fact a negative result should have been more correct. It’s a statistical anomaly and is often seen in filtering out junk mail, or in fraud analysis where an innocent transaction is identified as being ‘doubtful’.
