The concept of Open Banking arises principally from a lengthy 700-page report from the CMA into the retail banking market. In 2016 it recognised the dominance of the major banks both in terms of personal accounts (PCAs) and the structure of banking for SMEs. Part 1 of the report considers market conditions in detail, while Part 2 introduces a series of remedies to make PCA and SME banking markets work better for customers.

What was described as a ‘remedy package’ in effect comprises foundational measures of standardisation, service quality information, customer prompts and three key pillars:

+   1. Current account switching measures, including better governance of switching processes and customer access to transactions
+   2. PCA overdraft measures; alerting, maximum charges
+   3. Additional banking for small businesses; including SME switching tools and loan rate transparency

Clause 168 of the report states:

> > We are requiring the largest retail banks in both GB and NI to develop and adopt an API banking standard so as to share information to a specified timetable and we are requiring it to be an open standard so as to enable it to be widely accessible.

This will enable intermediaries to access information about bank services, prices and service quality. Customers who are satisfied about privacy and security safeguards, and are willing to give consent, will be able to share their own transaction data with trusted intermediaries, which can then offer advice tailored to the individual customer.

The overall aim was for it to be easier for customers to identify the best products for their needs, but there are some interesting challenges. The burden almost seems to be on the consumer to be the prime mover as far as any change of bank is concerned. The consumer has to assume that the provider of the APIs being used is meeting minimum standards and has been approved. Regardless of whether the vetting process is adequate, cynical consumers will be wary of previous poorly delivered, government-initiated IT schemes such as the failed NHS patient record scheme described as the ‘biggest IT failure ever seen,’ said to have cost £10 billion and ultimately abandoned.⁴

Some experts believe that the concept of Open Banking (OB) is most probably a slow burner. It is highly political in nature, and major banks inevitably are reluctant to encourage processes that could lead to loss of customers and revenue. Others suggest that whilst OB may not be a destination in itself, it may nevertheless provide some sort of a catalyst for new collaborations and partnerships. EY’s report suggests, ‘81 per cent of UK fintech firms surveyed are getting ready for Open Banking, while 29 per cent of respondents said they were already fully prepared’.⁵
