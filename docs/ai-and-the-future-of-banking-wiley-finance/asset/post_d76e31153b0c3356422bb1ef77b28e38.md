Already experts are thinking about a ‘modern’ customer 360-degree, which takes into account issues such as open data, and are beginning to ‘reimagine’ a new form of Customer Experience Management (CXM) process. Ultimately, the secret for the business user is to be able to find the right technological tools – Adobe, MS, SAP and others – and to integrate these into their business. The deeper issue is that of the specific relationship of the individual with their bank. What information are individuals prepared to ‘trade’ for better service and more appropriate products, and how might this be reflected in terms of regulatory compliance?

For example, GDPR (‘General Data Protection Regulation’) governs the way that personal data is used and amongst other elements requires the individual to expressly give permission for that usage. By comparison, CCPA (‘California Consumer Privacy Act’) gives consumers the right to know the sources from which personal information has been obtained. Overall, both are part of a general ‘march’ towards protecting the data ‘rights’ of the individual.

There’s a distinct recognition that more detailed granularity of data aligning the bank to the individual (or individual business) is the optimum solution, one which matches product and service to consumer need, but arguably achieving this in actual practice is still some time off. In the meantime, banks may need to cope with the relatively broad-brush approach of segmenting their customer and prospective customer base, and adopt what we might call a ‘campaign management’ route to growth (which is covered later).

## Credit Ratings

One subset of ‘customer 360-degree insight’ as far as banking is concerned is that of the customer’s credit rating – that is, the evaluation of the credit risk of an individual or business and their ultimate ability to repay any loans or liabilities that might attach. It is a form of forecast of the likelihood of the debtor defaulting. Lenders have historically used a mathematical model to calculate this risk, usually based on credit reports and additional information provided by the applicant and ultimately ‘signed off’ by a human. Generally, people with higher scores are seen as being of lower risk, and therefore a better ‘bet’ for providing a loan.

Different factors are normally considered:

- Number of existing credit obligations
- History of payments
- Number of previous applications for credit (‘credit searches’)
- Information in public records such as court judgements against the applicant

Regarding the use of AI, banks are increasingly using AI-based algorithms to establish risk with more accuracy and this is transforming the way that traditional scoring models have worked. AI-based systems not only identify traditional factors but also take into account other hidden patterns that might not be immediately apparent. Although criticised for being ‘black box’ applications and potentially running the risk of data privacy issues, AI-based systems allow more factors to be taken into consideration, supposedly providing:

- Greater risk accuracy
- Prevention of data entry problems
- More applications, as those without a credit history are now able to apply for a loan
- Potentially higher acceptance rates for banks with greater certainty

## Branch-Specific Campaigns

At its most granular, banks need to understand the individual needs of their customer and to present them with a service and series of products that meet those needs. In practical terms this isn’t easy. It’s a similar problem faced by other financial service providers such as insurers and also by retailers, both of whom also aim to create a 360-degree view of their end user. Companies such as Amazon and Netflix seem to be much closer to meeting that ambition, so for traditional banks this has to be seen as both a threat and as a template for change. The likelihood is that, with time, AI will increasingly create hyper-customised offerings that are more granular in nature.

However, in the meantime, the ability of banks to provide focused solutions is relatively limited. As a result, they need to adopt a more broad-based approach of running semi-traditional ‘campaigns’ that aim to capture a larger group of like-minded customers. These are operationalised in terms of branch activities but more importantly through wider group actions, usually communicated through media campaigns. There are strong similarities in terms of the analytical approach needed, as covered by the four key stages of analysis mentioned above.

Considering the aspect of branch-specific campaigns, the essence of these is to help the branch enhance existing customer relationships through local cross-selling to their local customer base, and to target the right customers with the most appropriate products and services. It is questionable whether a branch-oriented approach is still relevant, and to what degree it will change going forward. At a branch level, the aim may be to improve lead generation, to increase branch revenue and to reduce campaign costs but the reality is whether customers are greatly affected or influenced by any branch marketing activity any longer, and if so, for how much longer?

The question really to address is whether a branch makes a difference in the exercise of campaign management other than some limited degree of product or service awareness. In any event, can’t this be done more effectively on a High Street advertising banner, newspaper ad, the TV or other form of media (especially social media, covered in the next section)?

As the future role of the bank is considered, including the importance of the banking brand and the role of the branch specifically, it’s also important to think about the relevance of the branch from a social or community point of view, and if so, how can it be relevant? It doesn’t seem so long ago that the bank was at the heart of the community, a place where charitable deposits were made for worthy causes or from the donations of the local churches, and where local decisions about loans for community-based projects were made. In those days, the role of the bank manager with decision-making autonomy was revered and respected, whereas nowadays that role has considerably diminished, or has even disappeared.

The erosion of the traditional banking network in the pursuit of operational efficiency and higher profits seems to have resulted in the proverbial baby being thrown out with the bathwater. In a way, banks that were able to provide a social contribution to the community were also able to exert ‘soft influence’ on their customer base, which is much more difficult to do with a more centralised, digital, online presence. Some insurers, by comparison, who operate an agency model still appreciate this ‘soft benefit’. Of course, local banking isn’t for everyone but with an aging demographic and the possibility of more people being out of work due to the AI ‘revolution’, one wonders whether it is just a matter of time before the wheel turns full circle and that localised branches become more topical.

*In the author’s village, for example, a recent well-known international brand opened a coffee shop, which, apart from the pub, is now proving to be one of the most popular places to meet. Why shouldn’t a bank strike a national deal (or two) with such vendors? As will be discussed when considering ‘café-banks’, local proximity and convenience are becoming increasingly paramount. In some countries, for example, medical treatment is already available at some of the local (larger) supermarkets and shopping malls. One has to draw the line somewhere. Would (or could) one provide ‘sensible’ banking or financial advice in the local bar or public house – and if so, how might it be regulated?*

## Impact of Social Media Campaigns

It’s becoming increasingly clear that banks need to be much more effective in managing their use of social media to deliver both timely and appropriate offers to their customer base, either at an individual or a campaign level. In specifically considering Millennials, for example, more effective use of social media will be one of the keys to unlock revenue and profitability for this particular segment.
