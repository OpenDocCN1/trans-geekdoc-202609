Contact centres were originally introduced in the 1980s as a different way to handle high-touch, low-value transactions as an alternative to the customer taking up more expensive time in the branch. That was, of course, whilst there was still an accessible branch network. Since then, branch numbers have tumbled and are likely to continue to do so.

Booz identified three key levers for cost improvement in the contact centre, comprising:

+   - The prevention of avoidable calls, by addressing customer needs more effectively. Booz identifies that 50%–60% of all inbound calls had arisen as a result of banking errors. A second tier of calls comprised the need for the customer to call again, as their initial query had not been properly serviced. (This fails to account for the customers who have waited to be served, only to be repeatedly advised ‘we are undergoing high numbers of calls at the moment’, as if the contact centre were unable to predict call volumes – maybe over a lunchtime or early evening – and simply hadn’t staffed up adequately.)
+   - Reducing the customer need to call the centre by improving self service capabilities. This has given rise to a new category of call, that of customers not being able to access their own accounts online due to issues of passwords.
+   - The reduction of call transfers by addressing needs properly from the outset by having an effective routing system. Sometimes those automatic routing systems seem to be almost ‘circular’, and take a caller back to where they started from. In some (perhaps many) cases, the caller simply decides to wait for an advisor to speak to.

In terms of revenue improvement for banks using call or contact centres, Booz recommended:

+   - A deeper, better view of the customer so that they could understand their needs better
+   - Increased standardisation in the process
+   - Upskilling the agents, to improve the effectiveness of the process where there were opportunities for upsell or cross-sell

Even in 2008 the cost benefit of getting it right was significant. It was suggested that a 5%–10% reduction in calls could result in a saving for a bank of up to $10m annually.

The cost benefit of moving to a more effective self-service regime was also considerable. Booz said that the cost of a contact centre intervention was $4 per call, whereas an online resolution to an issue might only be $0.10 to $0.15 per call. Banks that can shift between 5% and 20% of their calls away from contact centres to online resolution could save up to $20m annually.

In terms of effective routing, their report identified that one bank had rerouted 5m calls that could – and should – have been managed by the first contact centre agent. They estimated that a 20%–40% reduction in referrals or escalations could also reduce costs by $10m annually.
