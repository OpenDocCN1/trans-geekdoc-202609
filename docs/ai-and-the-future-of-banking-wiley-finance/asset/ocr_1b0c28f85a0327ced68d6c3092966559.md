A future, more ‘agile’ banking industry is one with shorter product development lifecycles, new distribution models and perhaps also a tendency to sunset products or continually add value – and will require banks to be much more proactive in their response to the market. This in turn will place greater demands on the office of finance, whose increasing role recognises their strategic impact on the bank as a whole.

Beyond this, as new techniques emerge such as advanced analytics and AI, banks especially need to be able to measure and understand the ‘hard’ benefits that accrue. With many decisions regarding innovation on the horizon, there is an increasing need to measure Return on Investment (ROI) with certainty and, in doing so, support the need for further investment.

But it is not ‘just’ a matter of financial performance management. There is an increased focus on regulatory requirements, especially in the light of losses following adverse exposures to securitised asset portfolios, that is, assets that have been pooled together (such as mortgages, loans and credit card advances), whose related ‘cash flows’ are sold on to third-party investors as securities such as bonds. There is a burden on banks to prepare high-quality regulatory reports, and these are foundational to the monitoring of a bank’s safety and soundness. Because of this greater attention, banks are subject to stronger focus on risk management, as well as the need to comply with complex regulatory environments (such as Basel, IFRS and others). Cross-border operations and the many effects of globalisation also add to that risk, so the ability to have a clear view of performance is more important than ever, using flexible, comprehensive and robust processes.

The Office of Finance is being viewed more and more as a dynamic, interactive part of the organisation rather than simply a ‘bean counter’. This requires the finance professional to be effectively engaged with the wider business, taking part in strategic (and in some cases even tactical) considerations, and acting as a trusted advisor rather than a form of custodian of finances.

In many cases, especially in smaller banks, responsibility for risk management and regulatory compliance also falls to the Office of Finance, when arguably some are less well-prepared to undertake such a role.

The relationship between the CEO and the CFO is critical in understanding the relationship between finance and the wider business. If the CEO is the ‘pilot’ of the organisation, the CFO is the co-pilot, and co-operatively they must work as a fully integrated team for the benefit of the business. These statements are not, of course, banking specific, but rather represent the changing nature of many professions as a result of the digital and big data revolution.

Cedric Read’s book eCFO: Sustaining Value in the New Corporation² recognises that, going forward, many of the functions undertaken will need to be transformed, including the finance function. If the reader is to be left with one enduring thought about this book, it must be that the world of banking is being transformed by the era of big data and analytics, and nothing will quite be the same again. Such transformation also extends to the Office of Finance.
