In reality, doesn’t this occur every day? Aren’t decisions constantly made based on incomplete or even inaccurate information? Leaders make decisions of any nature without paying full regard to the data from within and outside their environment. If they fail to take that into account, the door is potentially opened to a claim under a Directors Liability insurance. In such a situation where a liability claim is made against an executive who goes against the advice of the analytics, insurers may consider this to be a breach of the duty of care and this may affect their liability under the liability policy.

Overall, there’s not much case law on the interaction between decision-making, leadership, data, and analytics. This is a nascent area of the law and lawyers will at some point need to get their heads around the whole issue of AI and its consequences. If they are likely to be challenged around the topic of insurance liabilities with respect to self-driving cars, for example, then lawyers will have a ‘field day’ in respect of the legal obligations in AI-infused decision-making.

The future of our leaders and managers in the near term may be that of being ‘gatekeepers’ of data and analytics. As gatekeepers, they will need to look at the information and add their own intuition and experience before making a final decision. We can describe them as ‘analytically infused’ leaders.

## Attributes of AI-Infused Leaders

In such an environment of growing uncertainty and volatility, it’s important to consider the key attributes needed for leadership. The most effective leaders of today have already evolved from the directive styles of yesterday into a new form of leadership that is empowering, nurturing and ‘value-driven’. Traditional bureaucratic business models are also changing more to ones that are flexible, agile and effective. While a top-down approach still exists, it is often to provide coaching and encourage bottom-up thinking, which allows the enterprise to flourish, albeit in a system of governance.

The role of the leader is to communicate, to ensure that the values of the organisation remain intact and to ‘manage the mission’ of the business. The mission needs to resonate not only with the collective psyche of the organisation but also with the values of the individuals within it. To do this effectively the leader needs to be continuously learning and to have self-awareness rather than arrogance. Not only do they become ‘masters’ but also they become role models for others in the organisation to aspire to.

Future leadership encourages collaboration both inside and external to the organisation, including with their customer base. Through this the leader is forever asking questions and seeking new approaches to problems. Andy Woods, CEO of Adnams brewers explained it like this; ‘The job of a leader is to sprinkle water on the talent and to let it grow’.^16

In his article ‘Leadership for the Future: Diversity, Creativity and Co-creation’,^17 Giles Hutchins, founder of BCI (‘Biomimicry for Creative Innovation’) writes:

> Leadership of the future is less about the theory of an idealized leadership model and more about the practical ability to navigate a journey of authenticity and inspiration; energizing and equipping oneself and others to make the right choices for the situation at hand.

As an organisation BCI are notable for a very strong ‘ethical stance’ in terms of doing business ‘inspired by and in harmony with nature’. At face value it appears almost to be a Zen-like approach to what they describe as ‘emergent, courageous, awakened, authentic’ leadership, which focuses on the Buddhist ideals of ‘awareness, stopping, calmness, stillness, peace, true thinking, and attainment’.

It’s difficult to see how such a ‘naturalistic’ approach to business might resonate entirely with, say, energy production companies, but they too appear increasingly keen to take a climate-friendly approach to their operations where practically possible. The question though is whether these are genuine desires to be environmentally friendly or whether they are just paying lip service to the environmental lobbyists. After all, one might argue, isn’t business – which includes banking – fundamentally to satisfy the stockholders? Hutchins argues that meeting financial targets is only one measurement of success and not one that creates ‘heart-filling business missions from which strong cultures are born’.

It’s an interesting perspective, which might resonate not only with digital natives but with socially oriented organisations. China Life, the financial services giant with over 600 million customers, mainly operates its business through agents. Whilst it is an effective distribution channel for their business, they also view the agents as providing a social good.

Forbes take a different angle on the topic of leadership in the future. They say that it’s a question of practicality because no individual leader (or group of leaders) has the ability to ‘sift through mounds of data’, and that empowerment is essential. Whilst this is a strong argument for the concept of the ‘analytically infused leader’, this may not provide sufficient clarity for leadership in an AI environment.^18

Dr. N.S. Rajan, Chief Human Resources Officer at Tata, which employs 600,000 staff, advocates the concept of the ‘servant leader’ that envisions, communicates, inspires and values the team. Beyond this, he advises that leaders should be listeners to understand their team’s needs.¹⁹

As leadership of the future is considered, we should also consider those groups who will ultimately become those leaders. In their 2015 report ‘The Millennial Leadership Survey’,²⁰ Workplace Trends – a research and advisory group for HR professionals – discovered that ‘91% of millennials aspire to be a leader and out of that, 52% were women. Almost half of millennials define leadership as “empowering others to succeed” and when asked what their biggest motivator was to be a leader, 43% said “empowering others”, while only 5% said money and 1% said power.’

That output needs a caveat in recognition of the relatively small size of the survey population (412 people) and that the survey was US-based rather than international, which might skew the results. Having said that, it’s most likely that AI-infused leadership will occur initially in the US due to their particular technology maturity curve and that much of the early ‘talent management’ learning may come from that continent.

Other markets such as Asia will also be affected, but they have other cultural issues of management to contend with, such as the issue of ‘status’ where an individual’s eminence is measured in terms of the number of people working for them. The Workplace Trends report also reflected that the Millennial Leadership Survey Group:

- Believed they have the right skills to become a leader, ranking the ability to communicate as being the most important skill. 51% said communication was one of their strongest skills, together with relationship-building.
- Recognised, however, that they have weak leadership skills that would need to be improved, with 43% saying that their weakest skill was the lack of industry experience (43%) or of technical expertise (41%).

Within the group there was a pushback against university and formal education, with 68% preferring online classes and only 4% indicating a preference for university. They also indicated a preference for mentoring (51%) rather than management, with 83% of millennials saying, ‘they would prefer to work for a company with fewer layers of management’. Additionally, 55% of those surveyed said that they weren’t satisfied with the leadership development opportunities being offered by their employers.

## Leadership Training for the Future

As we recognise that the nature of leadership in the future will change because of the influence of AI, it’s necessary to start to prepare Millennials for those leadership roles they will eventually reach, sooner or later. Companies such as Fitch Learning, for example, who are part of the international Hearst Group, already provide customised, online training for the financial services sector, either oriented to the corporation, customised to the individual or both. Cass Business School in London, like many other business schools, not only provides structured learning but supports this with a mentoring framework.
