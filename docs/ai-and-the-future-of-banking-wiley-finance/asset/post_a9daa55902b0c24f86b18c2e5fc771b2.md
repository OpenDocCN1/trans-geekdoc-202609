The placing of High Street branches is usually based on potential footfall and proximity to other retailers, but conveniently ignores those relatively geographically remote locations where high-net-worth customers are invariably based, or where perhaps banks (as they leverage their brand extension) might want to revisit.

Some might argue that the concept of branch performance management is relatively archaic in that it is a set of metrics reflecting a banking period of the past. It’s a fair criticism, especially in light of online banking and increasing centralisation. We should, however, assume that there is the potential for some sort of banking organisation footprint in the community, and that such a footprint needs to be measured.

Elsewhere we have drawn analogies to the banking and the retail sectors. With the current increase in online purchases relegating shops to becoming no more than ‘showrooms’, we have to ask the question of retail banks as to how far they can (or might) go with respect to replicating the retail model.

Perhaps banking will increasingly become ‘an experience’, which is especially relevant given the relatively transient nature of money as explained in the opening chapter. In that chapter we said that, in effect, money doesn’t exist and that the entire banking infrastructure and ecosystem is based on promises and trust. (The correlation between brand, brand values and trust is covered in Chapter 6.)

So perhaps we should momentarily cast our attention to the ‘banking experience’. We see this in three forms:

- The online experience, which might relate to the quality – or clunkiness – of the banking apps being used
- The call centre experience, covered later
- The physical branch experience, which we’ll look at in a little more detail at a later point

With regards to the ‘branch experience’, in her article ‘9 Keys to Branch Sales and Service’,<sup>25</sup> Meredith Dean of FMSI (Financial Management Solutions Inc) suggests that at least in the front office, three key things matter:

- Waiting time: *How long do we have to wait before someone comes and asks what we want?*
- Assist time: *How long does the customer interact with the service representative?*
- Service to Product ratio: *In other words, how long does it take to upsell/cross-sell?* Ideally, according to FMSI, product interactions ranged from 69% of customer contacts at top-performing branches to 30% on the low end.

FMSI also suggests a 9-point plan for effective operations in the front office, comprising:

1. More ‘self-directed technology’ such as ATMs
2. Use of a real-time monitoring system that ensures customers aren’t waiting too long to be seen
3. More effective management of lobby ‘traffic’
4. Manage privacy by having ‘private places’
5. Improve training of staff for cross-sell/upsell activities
6. Use tablets in the lobby to help queue management
7. Use remote check capabilities to book appointments
8. Improve staffing through automated staff scheduling to manage supply/demand imbalances
9. Improve, or ‘professionalise’ the ability of staff members

So, what do branch performance metrics look like at the end of the day? Later, in Chapter 8, we will challenge ourselves to look 30 years hence. There we’ll draw together some of the ideas that have resonated through this book. Certainly there are some key features. Won’t the same customer metrics and performance measures still apply? And if (or more likely when) humans are replaced by some sort of robotic capability, how will these metrics change?

Finally, the softer benefits of branch performance and physical presence need to be considered. ## Contact Centre Service

An essential part of the banking business model is the contact centre. If the local branch can’t sort it, then there’s usually a phone in the banking lobby. Or you can dial from home, and can avoid the branch anyway. But sometimes it’s difficult to get through. ‘First,’ he, she or it says, ‘I need to ask you some security questions.’ I am then being asked what my standing orders are, direct debits, my last transaction or even place of first kiss (honestly). . .

Before considering how analytics can improve contact centre operations, shouldn’t we first consider why contact centres are needed in the first place? In their 2008 report ‘Redefining the Mission for Banks: Call Centers Cut Costs, Grow Sales, or Both’, analysts Booz and Co posed the question ‘How can the mission of the contact centre be redefined’, making the point that a part of the enterprise that was meant to improve customer service, and to reduce cost, appears to have done neither.<sup>27</sup>

Contact centres were originally introduced in the 1980s as a different way to handle high-touch, low-value transactions as an alternative to the customer taking up more expensive time in the branch. That was, of course, whilst there was still an accessible branch network. Since then, branch numbers have tumbled and are likely to continue to do so.

Booz identified three key levers for cost improvement in the contact centre, comprising:

- The prevention of avoidable calls, by addressing customer needs more effectively. Booz identifies that 50%–60% of all inbound calls had arisen as a result of banking errors. A second tier of calls comprised the need for the customer to call again, as their initial query had not been properly serviced. (This fails to account for the customers who have waited to be served, only to be repeatedly advised ‘we are undergoing high numbers of calls at the moment’, as if the contact centre were unable to predict call volumes – maybe over a lunchtime or early evening – and simply hadn’t staffed up adequately.)
- Reducing the customer need to call the centre by improving self service capabilities. This has given rise to a new category of call, that of customers not being able to access their own accounts online due to issues of passwords.
- The reduction of call transfers by addressing needs properly from the outset by having an effective routing system. Sometimes those automatic routing systems seem to be almost ‘circular’, and take a caller back to where they started from. In some (perhaps many) cases, the caller simply decides to wait for an advisor to speak to.

In terms of revenue improvement for banks using call or contact centres, Booz recommended:

- A deeper, better view of the customer so that they could understand their needs better
- Increased standardisation in the process
- Upskilling the agents, to improve the effectiveness of the process where there were opportunities for upsell or cross-sell

Even in 2008 the cost benefit of getting it right was significant. It was suggested that a 5%–10% reduction in calls could result in a saving for a bank of up to $10m annually.

The cost benefit of moving to a more effective self-service regime was also considerable. Booz said that the cost of a contact centre intervention was $4 per call, whereas an online resolution to an issue might only be $0.10 to $0.15 per call. Banks that can shift between 5% and 20% of their calls away from contact centres to online resolution could save up to $20m annually.

In terms of effective routing, their report identified that one bank had rerouted 5m calls that could – and should – have been managed by the first contact centre agent. They estimated that a 20%–40% reduction in referrals or escalations could also reduce costs by $10m annually.
