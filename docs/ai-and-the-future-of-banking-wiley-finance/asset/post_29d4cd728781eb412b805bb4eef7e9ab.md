- 3. Brands will start to ‘self-generate’ based on algorithms that detect patterns of consumer need and self-fill them quickly.
- 4. There will be an increase in brand fluidity, which constantly changes due to market circumstances and with consumers themselves being co-branders.
- 5. Actualisation of brands will create a deeper and more meaningful relationship with customers. This will replace the bombardment of customers with messages of a much deeper level of engagement requiring transparency, honesty and including an emotional connection.
- 6. Ethical spending will increase in importance. Ethically minded consumers will look for more cues and directions as to how to buy, and ethical decisions will start to edge out purchasing decisions based on value and consumption alone.
- 7. Crowds will define brands using different methods such as social media to identify brand ‘gaps’ and consumers themselves will act to fill those gaps. In this scenario, consumers view the ‘brand as a friend’.
- 8. Brand relationships will reduce in importance with ‘breadth’ replacing ‘depth’ of brand. The brand mantra will be ‘wide and often’ with multiple touchpoints, and with a much simpler brand engagement.
- 9. Post-retail branding will emerge. By 2030 online shopping will have become so convenient that the brand will somehow have to encourage the consumer to make purchasing decisions outside the normal retailing cycle, and retailers will use strong brand identity to assist that process.
- 10. Brand ecosystems will emerge such as the connected home, with significant discounts available to cross-purchasers (in return for being ‘locked into’ a brand partnership).

The above list, pointing to a possible world in a decade’s time, offers some important clues for banking and especially in the context of the branding. As with data and analytics, leadership in the context of customer service and consumer engagement has come from the retail sector, so banks would be well advised to look to that sector for the next wave of innovation in branding. Possible future opportunities to consider might include:

- The stretching of the bank’s own brand across a much wider ecosystem than currently exists, and adding value to the customer experience at every opportunity
- The impact of ethical investment by the banks to meet the particular ethical aspirations of their customers
- A strengthening of the link between banking and gamification, which may occur in different ways. Perhaps more financially orientated games might be created, including the sponsoring of gaming by financial services organisations or the embedding of banking brands.
- A more fluid and flexible AI-driven approach to brand identity which is more closely aligned to the individual brand expectations of the consumer. What this implies is that the larger banner ads may reduce in importance, especially in the digital age.

## BANKS WILL USE AI TO BECOME LIFESTYLE MANAGERS

The convergence of brand and lifestyle has long been recognised. Both consumer goods suppliers and banks/wealth managers focus on the same market, albeit from different directions, so there is increasingly greater opportunity for convergence of thinking and shared technologies:

- Banks have a potential role in providing a catalyst for consumer spending.
- Consumer goods suppliers have a potential role in helping banks not only provide available lines of credit, but also wealth management advice.

Both might use advanced analytics and AI not only to identify potential customers but also to ensure that the right products, services and lines of credit are placed before them in a timely way. One particular challenge is that of ensuring customers do not over extend themselves in terms of their financial decisions.

In considering the future of banking, the most sophisticated financial institutions will not only provide modeling for the present, taking into account different risk appetites, but also help the consumer plan effectively for the future, taking into account current and anticipated assets, current and expected commitments, lifestyle and life expectancy. Key branded organisations will ultimately become ‘partners for life’, a sort of quasi-financial marriage using advanced analytics and AI as a form of catalyst. We will conceivably become ‘married’ to the financial brand. Integral to that long-term relationship will be key issues such as consumption and credit smoothing.

## Consumption and Credit Smoothing

The concept of ‘consumption smoothing’ is a process by which consumers manage their personal lifestyles to ensure that they have adequate savings to maintain their lifestyle, including during retirement. It’s a balance between sensibility and frugality. Those who overspend during their working life and fail to save will suffer the consequences later on in life when they have inadequate savings and need to adopt a more frugal existence. Those who ‘oversave’ whilst working and lead a frugal existence at that time will (hopefully) reap the benefits in later life.

It suggests that some element of ‘frugality’ will need to occur sooner or later, and that it’s a delicate balance to plan. From an economic perspective, ‘consumption smoothing’ is all about:

- Stability of consumption, for example, the management of spending patterns or purchasing behaviour at different stages of the consumer lifecycle
- Reduction of risk and uncertainty, for example, where there is an unexpected loss of income or life event
- The ability to predict what might be needed in terms of financial reserves

Beyond economic analysis, consumption smoothing also reflects the way consumers act in terms of behavior, brand awareness and even the psychology of consumerism. In considering the psychology of ‘spending’, money is earned so that it is able to be spent. In doing so, consumers make themselves happier (possibly), so implicitly the banking system potentially appears to have some role in managing customer ‘happiness’.

Consumerism also has a major role to play in fueling the economic growth, or prosperity, of a nation. The more we spend, said Adam Smith in Wealth of Nations, the better it is for our country. As the topic of consumerism is considered in the context both of banking and AI, the uncertainty of the impact of AI in the workplace also shouldn’t be overlooked, together with the possibility that, for many workers in routine roles (including in the banking industry), their working life may be prematurely reduced.

Accepted thinking and economic theory are that high-interest, unsecured borrowing such as credit cards and overdraft management is often used as a way of managing an income/expenditure imbalance. It’s another part of maintaining stability of spending patterns, or ‘smooth consumption’ mentioned immediately above.

The concept of credit smoothing is a controversial area that implies that credit management may be preferable to that of consumption management, and that it is better for consumers to manage their debt rather than their spending patterns. There’s been much research into this and theorists suggest credit smoothing and consumer smoothing are ‘countercyclical’ behaviours, which indicate changes in consumer behavior dependent on economic conditions. Research also shows that borrowing doesn’t go up when there has been a financial ‘shock’ such as unemployment resulting in lost income, but rather the contrary.23,24

At a banking level, credit risk – which is the inability to pay – constitutes one of the greatest risks that banks can undertake.

It’s important to understand these concepts in the context of banks, AI and, ultimately, in the context of consumerism. If retail banks in particular can better understand consumer behaviour through having a 360-degree understanding of the customer, then they can better understand how best to manage the customer’s money, even allowing for consumer and credit smoothing issues. The banks may then start to take on the role of financial guardians, perhaps giving advice in the same way that a financial manager might.

Such advice will be analytically infused. The mantra that ‘analytics provide actionable insight’ remains valid, with special emphasis on ‘actionable’ but where does the burden of taking the ‘action’ actually rest? Is it with the consumer to listen to the advice and to act on it, or for an ‘intelligent bank’ somehow to be proactive around the money management exercise? At the very least, banks could be able to provide some sort of scenario modelling at a personalised, granular level that says, ‘If you don’t take this action, then these will be the consequences’.
