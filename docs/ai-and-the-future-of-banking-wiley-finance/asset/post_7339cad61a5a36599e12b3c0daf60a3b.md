## Data and Analytics Primer

### SUMMARY

This chapter aims to provide a general introduction to the topic of data and analytics, and the following chapter looks more specifically at the use of analytics in the banking sector. The topic of data and analytics is not a new story and some readers will find it possible to work their way quickly through the generic parts of this section and move onto more ‘banking-specific’ analytical issues. For those coming to the topic relatively new, this section will hopefully provide a helpful foundation.

Beyond this, it recognises that there are parts of the banking industry that are already especially analytically orientated, particularly in the area of investment. Quantitative analysts (or ‘quants’) use data and analytics as their ‘bread and butter’ and for them there are already much more detailed texts. Perhaps they will also be interested in taking a broader view of their industry, especially as many of them have the right technical skills and competences to take their abilities to a broader future role. For some of these experts, the banking industry could prove to be their proverbial ‘oyster’.

The section also renews the emphasis on issues of location analytics, which tends not to be in the forefront of thinking but remains relevant in terms of customer, commercial opportunities and geopolitical risk, to name but a few elements of this category.

### INTRODUCTION

Big data may be ‘big news’ but it is not ‘new news’. In fact, there’s even a move away from the expression ‘big’ – after all, data is just data and few would argue against the fact that there’s a lot of it about.

The emerging trend is that of moving from ‘big data’ to ‘fast data’, which is the assimilation of information and its analysis in real or near-real time. In a banking context, speed of information helps banks and other organisations provide a more customised and more relevant offer.

Variety: Data comes to the user from many sources and in many forms – structured, semi-structured and unstructured. Semi-structured data presents particular problems because it is seldom consistent. Structured is in the form of established and organised data warehouses. Unstructured data (for example, plain text or voice) has no structure whatsoever but some might reasonably argue that there is a ‘golden thread’ tying both structured and unstructured information, and that is the ‘thread’ of location. After all, it’s suggested, isn’t everyone and everything somewhere?

Currently, a significant amount of data is unstructured, perhaps as much as 80%. It’s suggested that the winners of the future will be those organisations which can obtain insight and therefore extract value from the unstructured information. In a banking context this might comprise data, for example, which is based on social media and which helps establish consumer sentiment and brand equity.

Veracity: This is normally taken to mean the reliability of the data. Not all data is equally reliable because it comes from different sources. One key measure of veracity is the ‘signal to noise’ ratio, which is an expression for the usefulness of information compared to false or irrelevant data. (The expression has its origin in the quality of a radio signal, for example, compared to the background noise.)

In a banking context this may relate to the amount of ‘spam’ or off-topic posts on a social media site where a banker is looking for insight into customers’ reactions to a new media campaign. Spam may also include information automatically created in large volumes by ‘webots’, also known as ‘WebRobots’, ‘WWW robots’ or just simply ‘bots’. Typically a bot performs repetitive tasks that could be done by a human but at much higher volumes.

As organisations become obsessed with data governance and integrity there is a risk that any data that is less than perfect is not reliable. This is not necessarily true. One major UK bank, for example, gives a weighting to the veracity, or ‘truthfulness’, of the data. It allows them to use imperfect information in their decisions. The reality is that even in our daily personal life, decisions we take are often based on the best information available, even if it is not perfect, and subsequent actions are taken accordingly with different levels of confidence.

Value: the final characteristic and one not widely commentated on is that of the value of the data. This can be measured in different ways, such as the value to the user of the data in terms of giving deeper insight to a certain issue, or the cost of acquiring key data to gain better information about, for example, the creditworthiness of a customer. The challenge for many banks – and for other organisations – is how to ‘monetarise’ the data they presently have and can collect in the future.

There’s an argument to say that almost all essential information is already out there ‘in the ether’ and it is simply a matter of finding it and creating a mechanism for absorption and analysis. Very definitely it seems that certain key types of data are critical to gain particular insights, and there is often a strong financial benefit case for actively seeking it.

In a banking context, as we consider the concept of the universal bank and the ‘full financial service enterprise’ offering a wider range of products and services, these new services and ‘next-generation’ offerings may not be created and managed by the banks themselves but rather by third-parties, working with the bank on some form of ‘revenue share’. In such a case, data shared up and down the supply chain (with the customers’ agreement) would typically assist in a go-to-market strategy and ultimately that of operational execution, so to that extent the data has monetary ‘value’ in terms of more effective marketing.

Recently an additional 8 ‘Vs’ have also been suggested.⁶,⁷

- **Validity:** Embracing data quality and master data management (which we will cover below)
- **Variability:** Including data that is dynamic and evolving, such as the identification of behavioural attributes in the source data (i.e. conscious or unconscious bias)
- **Venue:** Covering not just geographic relevance but also in terms of the platform on which the data was hosted, such as in a distributed ledger/Blockchain
- **Vocabulary:** Linked to data models, the semantics describing the data structure
- **Vagueness:** Relating to the confusion over the meaning of terms used in ‘big data’ and the tools used
- **Vulnerability:** The new security concerns that big data brings
- **Volatility:** How long the data remains relevant
- **Visualisation:** Is it possible to use visualisation to provide easier understanding of detailed information?

Purists might suggest that of the 13 items listed, there’s an element of duplication between the first 5 and the second 8. Let’s just say that sometimes there is no limit to the human imagination in terms of marketing. At the moment probably all types and categories of data that currently exist and will exist in the future can be characterised by these expressions. (For the superstitious, perhaps it might be necessary to find another ‘V’ to move the number of descriptions beyond the unlucky ‘13’, maybe ‘Virus-free’?)

## DATA MANAGEMENT AND ANALYTICS

### Data Management

The idea of data management, sometimes also known as ‘MDM’ or ‘master data management’, has been around for a long time but is attaining a new level of importance in the age of AI. In business it’s considered ‘a method used to define and manage the critical data of an organization to provide, with data integration, a single point of reference. The data that is mastered may include reference data, that is, the set of permissible values, and the analytical data that supports decision making’.⁸
