
## Cybercrime and IT Resilience

### SUMMARY

When considering the impact of advanced analytics and AI for banking, it would be incomplete to omit the issue of cybercrime and IT resilience as an area of interest. In the context of the Bank of the Future, there is an essential requirement of trust and security. Therefore, it’s necessary to recognise the increased risk and impact of cybercrime on the banking system.

The harsh reality is that if the banking industry, based on trust and increasingly based on digital transactions, can’t get this aspect of its operation right, then customer confidence will at best be eroded and at worst could be lost entirely. This section aims to give greater understanding of the nature of cybercrime at both a general and banking specific level, and how banks (by way of example) need to prepare themselves for both the present and the future. This includes topics such as cyber security toolkits and the emergence and importance of new roles.

With fraud being one of the key areas of concern, the use of analytics and AI is also considered in this particular context. Finally, the chapter closes with some insights into issues of cyber law.

CitiBank have made numerous investments in AI firms, with particular attention given to eSecurity and cybersecurity. For example, one acquisition, Feedzai, monitors customer payment behaviour for anomalies.¹

### INTRODUCTION

Cybercrime is already being described as the ‘New Frontier’ in terms of the threat landscape. The International Standards Organization’s ISO 27k series on IT risk already includes a cyber security component, specifically under clause ISO/IEC 27032, which defines cybercrime as: ‘Preservation of confidentiality, integrity and availability of information in the Cyberspace.’ (The ISO 27k suite comprises almost 70 standards, about fifty of which have been published so far, covering information risk and security management.)³

‘Cyberspace’ itself is defined as: ‘the complex environment resulting from the interaction of people, software and services on the internet by means of technology devices and networks connected to it, and which does not exist in any physical form.’⁴

A cyberattack is a deliberate attempt to disrupt computer systems or networks or to infiltrate codes. Its consequences are to disrupt processes, compromise data and infiltrate information sources leading to identity theft. Cyberattack may comprise multiple forms, such as:

- Gaining or attempting to gain unauthorised access to a computer system or its data
- Disruption or denial of service attacks (known by the acronym DDoS), often requesting ransom
- Hacking a website or defacing a site by applying a false facing page
- Virus or malware installation leading to disruption of systems and processes
- Inappropriate use of computers or applications by employees of a company in a way that it harms the company. This may extend to physical theft of data by downloading information onto disk drives.⁵

It’s a major problem that banks are already familiar with. In the UK, the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), together with the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), invites banks to voluntarily publish information about the number of IT shutdowns that they have made because of cyber intrusion and other technical issues.⁶ According to information provided, most of the major UK banks suffer one IT-related outage per month, although these are often linked to IT modifications rather than directly to cybercrime.⁷

‘UK Finance’ – the collective body for 250 UK-based banks – provide a single voice for a competitive and diverse industry and cover issues such as financial risk and economic crime. They recently commented that its members are currently investing ‘billions of pounds’ in ensuring IT and human systems were ‘robust and secure’, making the point that ‘Operational resilience is crucial in a modern financial system and is a key priority for the industry’.⁸

But it’s a continuing worry. The chair of the UK Treasury Committee, which has an ongoing inquiry into IT failures in the financial sector, said, ‘IT failures at banks are a regular occurrence. . . . These failures now seem to have an air of inevitability’. Although their findings (at the time of writing) have yet to be published, the committee are also considering issues such as:

- The frequency of operational incidents and how this might change as consumers and firms rely more heavily on technology
- The most common causes of operational incidents
- The use of metadata, which gives greater structure to the data held in the warehouse
- Real-time reporting, including the use of ‘more dynamic and responsive risk management’
- Anomaly detection, which serves to find ‘unpredictable new patterns of attack and relationships’
- Integrated case management, which in other words is the idea of collaboration across the enterprise rather than as individual businesses operating in silos
- Consistency demanding common understanding and definitions across the organisation and probably, with time, across the industry

An essential component of managing cyber risk is the need for effective communication between different role functions. These might typically comprise the Chief Data Officer (CDO), Chief Information Officer (CIO), Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) and the Chief Risk Officer (CRO). As cybercrime becomes more problematic, there is likely to be a need to revisit some of these roles and consider whether there is duplication or latency. In the future, these roles and functions may either need to be redefined or consolidated in some way.

At the very least, it becomes critical for organisations to have some preplanned approach to the management of cyber risk. The impact to the business, especially on customers and to the reputation of the organisation cannot be overstated, especially when considering one of the key decision drivers of Millennial-type users, which is that of security.

### The Internationalisation of Cybercrime

In their 2019 survey, Europ Assistance’s ‘Cyber Barometer’¹⁴ explored consumer sentiment on the topic of cyber security across nine European countries. They discovered that 46% of consumers feel worried about cyber security, especially those with elderly parents or with children. Nearly a third of respondents (31%) felt exposed to cyber security issues, and those with children felt even more concerned, at 38%. Of European countries, southern Europe seems to be more concerned, with Spanish citizens (47%) and Italian citizens (39%) feeling especially vulnerable. Considering the wider European picture, just over a quarter (26%) knew someone who has had an attack on their personal data and over 82% viewed such an attack as being ‘very stressful’.

What’s more worrying about the survey is the apparent lack of knowledge amongst consumers about what to do if their personal data was compromised. Only 45% of respondents said they would know what to do, and 48% said that they felt organisations weren’t doing enough to help them. In the US, it seems that there is a greater awareness of data security issues; 65% are more aware of identity protection solutions, compared to Europeans, at 40%.

Consumers appear especially interested in:

- Alerting services (57%)
- Assistance services, provided on a 24/7 basis (54%)
- Prevention services (47%)
- Monitoring services (48%)

According to Hiscox Insurance, at a commercial level there is still a wide disparity in cyber readiness. In ‘The Hiscox Cyber Readiness Report 2019’,¹⁵ they record that on assessment, overall US, German and Belgian firms score highest on the readiness level, with more than four-fifths of French firms still in the category of ‘novices’.

### Cyber Security Toolkits

The ‘Global Cyber Alliance’ describe themselves as being an international, cross-sector effort dedicated to eradicating cyber risk. It was formed in 2015 ‘to address systemic cyber risk through a proactive risk-based, solution-oriented approach to address and eradicate malicious cyber risks’.

> ‘What sets the Global Cyber Alliance Cybersecurity Toolkit apart is that it is an action kit,’ said Philip Reitinger, president and CEO of Global Cyber Alliance.
>
> ‘Our focus is on producing a dynamic clearinghouse of operational tools that help small and medium businesses address risk and improve their cybersecurity posture, leveraging the deep expertise of our network of global partners, such as Mastercard, and the experiences of actual GCA toolkit users.’¹⁶

They describe their mission as:

- One of uniting global communities
- Implementing solutions
- Measuring the effect

Specifically, they offer a number of ‘toolkits’ or prescribed solutions especially targeted at the SME that aim to provide ‘actionable guidance’ and tools with clear directions to combat the increasing amount of cyberattacks on SMEs. The toolkit provides:

- *Operational guidance*: With respect to cyber-related assets, such as multi-factor passwords, effective use of backups and how to prevent phishing and viruses
- *Training*: Including templates and foundational documents
- *Recognised best practices*: Including from organisations such as the Centre for Internet Security Controls, the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre (‘Cyber Essentials’), and Australia’s Cyber Security Centre (‘Essential Eight’)
