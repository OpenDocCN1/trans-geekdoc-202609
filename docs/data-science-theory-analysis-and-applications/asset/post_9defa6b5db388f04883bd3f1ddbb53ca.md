The three other primary zones of big data analytics apart from the earlier ones are in healthcare data:

- **Image Processing**: Medical images are a critical source of data that are commonly used for analysis, treatment assessment, and planning. Computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), X-ray, molecular imaging, ultrasound, photoacoustic imaging, fluoroscopy, positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT), and mammography are some examples of imaging procedures that are established in clinical settings. Medical image data can range anywhere from a couple of megabytes for a single report to many megabytes per examination. Such data require substantial storage capacity when stored for long-term use. It also requires fast and accurate computation if any decision-support automation were to be performed using the data. Moreover, if multiple sources of data obtained for each patient are additionally used during diagnosis, visualization, and treatment processes, then the issue of providing robust storage and developing efficient strategies capable of handling the vast scale of data becomes a challenge.
- **Signal Processing**: Similar to medical signals, physiological signals likewise present volume and velocity obstacles, particularly in high-resolution and capacity from a large number of monitors associated with each patient. Beyond the data size issues, physiological signals additionally present a multifaceted nature of a spatiotemporal sort. Analysis of physiological signals is often more valuable when presented alongside situational awareness, which should be embedded into the development of continuous monitoring and predictive systems to ensure its effectiveness and robustness [27].
- **Genomics**: The cost of sequencing the human genome is rapidly decreasing with the improvement of high-throughput sequencing technology. With implications for current public health approaches and delivery of care, analyzing genome-scale data for generating actionable insights in a timely manner is a critical challenge to the field of computational science. Cost and time to deliver insights are vital in a clinical setting. Initiatives addressing this mind-boggling issue include 100,000 subjects over 20–30 years using the predictive, preventive, participatory, and personalized health, referred to as P4, precision medicine paradigm and additionally an integrative individual profile. The P4 activity is using a systems approach for (i) analyzing genome-scale data sets to determine disease states, (ii) moving towards blood-based diagnostic instruments for continuous monitoring of a subject, (iii) investigating new ways to deal with drug target discovery, creating tools to manage big data difficulties of capturing, validating, storing, mining, integrating, and lastly (iv) displaying data for each person. The integrative Personal Omics Profile (iPOP) combines physiological monitoring and different high-throughput techniques for genome sequencing to produce a detailed health and disease conditions of a subject. At last, achieving actionable insights at the clinical level remains a tremendous challenge in this field. Using such high-dimensional data for investigation, discovery, and clinical interpretation requires novel big data approaches and analytics. ## 6.8 HEALTHCARE DATA MANAGEMENT

Healthcare data management is the process of storing, protecting, and analyzing data drawn from diverse sources. Dealing with the abundance of available healthcare information enables health systems to make comprehensive views of patients, personalize treatments, improve communication, and improve health outcomes [28].

### 6.8.1 Big Data and Care Management

ACOs (Accountable Care Organizations) center on managed care and the need to keep individuals at home and out of the hospital. Sensors and wearables will gather health data on patients in their homes and push the majority of that data into the cloud. Electronic scales, BP (Blood Pressure) monitors, SpO2 (Blood oxygen saturation) sensors, presence sensors like iBeacon, and yet-to-be-designed sensors will send data from a large number of patients continuously. Healthcare organizations and care managers, utilizing sophisticated tools, will monitor this huge data stream and the IoT to keep their patients healthy [29].

Besides, a majority of different sensor data will come into healthcare organizations at an extraordinary volume and speed. In a healthcare future predicated on keeping individuals out of the hospital, a health system’s capacity to deal with this data will be essential. These volumes of data are best managed as streams coming into a big data cluster. As the data streams in, organizations should have the capacity to detect any potential health issues and alert a care manager to intervene. For instance, if a patient’s heart rate spikes, the system will send an alert in real time to a care manager, who would then be able to interact with the patient to get his blood pressure back into a healthy range. Big data is the main approach to dealing with the volume, velocity, and variety of this sensor data.

### 6.8.2 ADVANTAGES OF HEALTHCARE DATA MANAGEMENT

The Advantages of Healthcare Data Management are as follows

- Create 360° views of patients and deliver personalized communications by integrating patient data from every available source.
- Improve patient engagement with accurate display and analysis based on healthcare data.
- Improve population health outcomes by tracking current health trends and predicting the forthcoming ones.
- Make informed, high-impact business decisions based on data insights.
- Understand physician actions and align them with the organization’s objectives.

## 6.9 BIG DATA IN HEALTHCARE

When healthcare organizations envision the future of big data, they often consider using it for analyzing content-based notes. Current analytics technologies generally make use of discrete data and struggle to benefit from the majority of valuable clinical data captured in doctors’ and nurses’ notes. Big data indexing techniques and some of the new work mining data in textual fields could surely add value to healthcare analytics in the future.

### 6.9.1 Big Data and IoT

Big data will truly become profitable to healthcare in what’s known as the IoT.

The IoT is a growing network of everyday objects from industrial machines to consumer products that can share data and perform tasks while you are busy with other activities, like work, rest, or exercise. Before long, our vehicles, our homes, our major appliances, and even our city streets will be connected to the Internet—making this network of things known as IoT for short. Composed of a large number of sensors and devices that generate constant streams of data, the IoT can be utilized to improve our lives and businesses from numerous points of view.

Any device that generates healthcare data about a person’s health and sends it into the cloud will be a part of this IoT. Wearables may be the most recognizable example of such a device. Many individuals currently wear a fitness device that tracks how many steps they’ve taken, their heart rate, their weight, and how everything is trending. Apps are available on PDAs (Personal Digital Assistants) that track how often and how intensely a user works out. There are also medical devices that can send data into the cloud: blood pressure monitors, pulse oximeters, glucose monitors, and a whole lot more [30].
