
## 6.9.2 PATIENT PROPHECIES FOR UPGRADED STAFFING

Healthcare data analysis helps to anticipate the number of patients to enhance staffing. One of the key data sets is one decade of hospital admission records, which data researchers crunched utilizing “time series analysis” procedures. These analyses enabled the scientists to see important patterns in admission rates. At that point, they could utilize a machine to figure out how to locate the most accurate algorithms that predicted future admission trends. The outcome is an internet browser-based interface that is intended to be utilized by doctors, nurses, and hospital administration staff inexperienced in data science to forecast visit and admission rates for the following 15 days. Additional staff can be drafted in when high numbers of visitors are expected, prompting diminished waiting times for patients and better quality of care.

## 6.9.3 ELECTRONIC HEALTH RECORDS

EHR is the most widespread use of big data in medicine. Each patient has his very own computerized record that incorporates demographics, medical history, allergies, lab test results, and so forth. Records are shared by means of secure data systems and are accessible for providers from both public and private sector. Each record is stored as one modifiable document, which implies that specialists can implement changes over time with no paperwork and no danger of data replication. EHRs can likewise trigger alerts and reminders when a patient should get another lab test or track medications to check whether a patient has been following the specialists’ instructions. Although EHR is a great idea, many countries still struggle to fully implement them.

## 6.9.4 REAL-TIME WARNING

Different instances of big data analysis in healthcare share one urgent functionality, which reminds constant caution. In hospitals, CDS software analyzes medical data on the spot, furnishing health professionals with advice as they make prescriptive decisions. However, physicians want patients to avoid hospitals to keep away from expensive in-house treatments. Personal monitoring devices, now trending as business intelligence buzzwords, can possibly become a part of a new strategy. Wearable devices will collect patients’ health data constantly and send this data to the cloud. Moreover, this data will be added to the database on the condition of health of the general population, which will enable physicians to look at this data in a broader context and adjust the delivery strategies accordingly. Organizations and care managers will utilize advanced tools to monitor this huge data stream and will respond each time the results will worsen. For instance, if a patient’s blood pressure increases alarmingly, the system will send a warning in real time to the physician who will then take action to reach the patient and direct measures to bring down the pressure. Another example is that of Asthmapolis, which has begun to utilize inhalers with Global Positioning System (GPS)-enabled trackers with the aim to identify asthma patterns, both on an individual level and looking at larger populations. This data is being used in conjunction with data from the CDC, with the aim to provide better treatment for asthmatics.

## 6.9.5 AUGMENTING PATIENT ENGAGEMENT

Numerous consumers and, consequently, potential patients already have an interest in smart devices that record each step they take, their heart rates, sleeping habits, and so on on a permanent basis. This basic data can be combined with other identifiable data to recognize the hidden potential health risks. Chronic sleep deprivation and an elevated pulse can signal a risk for future heart disease, for example. Patients are directly involved in the monitoring of their own health, and incentives from health insurers can push them to lead a healthy lifestyle. Another in-progress approach is to use a wearable that tracks specific health trends and uploads them to the cloud where doctors can monitor them. Patients suffering from asthma or hypertension could benefit from it and become more autonomous with reduced unnecessary visits to the doctor.

The utilization of big data in healthcare allows for strategic planning because of better insights into individuals’ motivations. Care managers can examine enrollment results among individuals in various demographic groups and identify what factors discourage individuals from taking up treatment.

## 6.9.9 TELEMEDICINE

Telemedicine term refers to delivery of remote clinical services using technology. It is used for primary consultations and initial diagnosis, remote patient monitoring, and medical training for health professionals. Some more specific uses include telesurgery specialists to perform operations with the use of robots and fast constant data delivery without physically being in the same area with a patient. Clinicians use telemedicine to provide customized treatment plans and avoid hospitalization or readmission. Such use of healthcare data analysis can be connected to the use of predictive analysis as observed previously. It enables clinicians to foresee acute medical events ahead of time and avoid deterioration of patient’s conditions. By keeping patients away from hospitals, telemedicine reduces costs and enhances the quality of service. Patients can avoid waiting in lines, and physicians don’t waste time for unnecessary meetings and paperwork. Telemedicine also enhances the accessibility of care as patients’ state can be monitored and consulted anywhere and anytime.

## 6.9.10 ASSIMILATING BIG DATA PER MEDICAL IMAGING

Medical imaging is crucial, and every year, 670 million imaging procedures are performed. Breaking down and storing these pictures manually are time consuming and costly, as radiologists need to inspect each picture individually, while hospitals need to store them for a few years. Medical imaging clarified how big data analysis for healthcare could change and the manner in which the pictures are read: algorithms created by analyzing countless pictures could identify specific patterns in the pixels and convert it into a number to assist the doctor with the diagnosis. They even go further, saying that it could be possible that radiologists will no longer need to look at the pictures, but rather examine the results of the algorithms that will inevitably examine and recall a larger number of pictures than they could in a lifetime. This would undoubtedly affect the job of radiologists, their training, and the required range of skills.
