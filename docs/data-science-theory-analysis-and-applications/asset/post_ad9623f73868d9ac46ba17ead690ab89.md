## 6.1 INTRODUCTION

The promise of big data has attracted considerable attention in healthcare as it pertains to drug discovery, treatment development, personalized medicine, and optimal patient care that can reduce cost and improve patient outcomes. Billions of dollars have been invested to capture data generated in large initiatives that are frequently siloed. The Continuously Learning Healthcare System is also being promoted by the Institute of Medicine to close the gap between scientific discovery, patient and clinician engagement, and clinical practice. However, the big data promise has not yet been realized to its potential as the limited availability of data does not translate into knowledge or clinical practice. In addition, because of the variability and complexity of data structures, lack of computational advances, and concerns about sharing private patient data, few large clinical datasets are made available to researchers in general [1].

The idea of “big data” isn’t new; the way in which it is defined is continually evolving. Different attempts at defining big data basically describe it as a collection of data elements, whose size, speed, type, as well as complexity require one to seek, adopt, and develop new hardware and software systems, with the aim to effectively store, analyze, and visualize data [2]. Healthcare is a prime example of how the three V’s of data – velocity, variety, and volume – are an inherent part of the data it produces. This data is spread among numerous healthcare systems, health insurers, physicians, government entities, etc. Moreover, each of these data repositories is siloed and inherently incapable of providing a platform for global data transparency. In addition to the three V’s, which is shown in Figure 6.1, the veracity of healthcare data is also critical for its meaningful use towards creating impactful research.

Due to the inherent complexities of healthcare data, there is a potential advantage in creating and implementing big data solutions within this domain. Traditional approaches to medical research have commonly focused on the analysis of disease states based on changes in physiology as a limited perspective of a certain particular approach to data. Although the approach to understanding diseases is fundamental, researching it at this level silences the variety and disconnectedness that characterize the true underlying therapeutic mechanisms [3]. Following traditional mechanical procedures, the field of medicine has begun to adapt to the present digital data age. New technologies make it possible to capture tremendous amounts of data about each individual patient over an extensive timescale. However, despite the advent of medical devices, the data captured and accumulated from these patients has remained immensely underutilized and wasted as a result.
