## 6.5 CHALLENGES IN BIG DATA ANALYSIS IN HEALTHCARE

For healthcare organizations that effectively coordinate information-driven insights into their clinical and operational procedures, the rewards can be tremendous. Healthier patients bring down care costs and greater visibility into performance, and higher staff and patient satisfaction rates are among the many advantages of transforming information resources into information experiences. The common big data challenges in the three V's are listed in Figure 6.4.

The earlier illustration explains the characteristics of big data in healthcare, which reflects the definition of the three V's through Volume, Variety, and Velocity. In order to infer the three V's, the common challenges have been listed as follows.

### 6.5.1 CAPTURE

All information originates from somewhere; however, unfortunately for some healthcare providers, it doesn't always originate from somewhere with poor information management habits. Capturing information that is clean, complete, precise, and arranged effectively for its use in different frameworks is a continuous fight for organizations. Poor EHR usability, tangled work processes, and an inadequate understanding of why big data is essential to capture well can all contribute to quality issues that will plague data throughout its life cycle.

### 6.5.2 CLEANING

Healthcare service providers are intimately familiar with the significance of cleanliness in the facility. Unclean data can rapidly crash a major data analysis project, particularly when uniting dissimilar data sources that may record clinical or operational components in slightly different configurations. Data cleaning, otherwise called purging or scrubbing, ensures that data sets are accurate, correct, reliable, relevant, and not corrupted at all. While most data cleaning tasks are still performed manually, some IT vendors do offer automated cleaning tools that utilize logical rules to compare and differentiate large data sets to ensure high levels of precision and integrity in healthcare data warehouses.

### 6.5.3 STORAGE

As the volume of healthcare data increases exponentially, some providers are no longer able to handle the costs and effects of on-site server farms. Cloud storage is becoming an increasingly popular alternative as costs drop and reliability grows. Nearly 90 percent of healthcare organizations are using a type of cloud-based healthcare IT infrastructure, including storage and applications. The cloud offers agile disaster recovery, lowers upfront costs, and requires less expansion, despite the fact that organizations must be extremely cautious about choosing partners that understand the significance of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and other healthcare-specific compliance and security issues.

### 6.5.4 SECURITY

Data security is the top priority for healthcare organizations, particularly in the wake of a rapid-fire string of prominent breaches, hackings, and ransomware attacks. From phishing attacks to malware to laptops accidentally left in a taxi, healthcare data is subject to an almost vast array of vulnerabilities. The HIPAA Security Rule includes a comprehensive list of technical safeguards for organizations storing Protected Health Information (PHI), including transmission security, authentication protocols, and controls over access, integrity, and audit. In practice, these safeguards translate into common-sense security measures [8], for example, using anti-virus software, setting up firewalls, encrypting sensitive data, and using multi-factor authentication. Healthcare organizations should regularly remind their staff members of the critical importance of data security protocols and regularly review [9–10] who has access to high-value data assets by keeping malicious groups from causing harm.

### 6.5.5 STEWARDSHIP

Healthcare data, particularly on the clinical side, has a long useful life. Even though it is not required to keep data available for about 6 years, providers may wish to use de-identified data sets for research projects, which makes ongoing stewardship and curation a vital concern. Data may also be reused or repurposed for different purposes, for example, quality measurement or performance benchmarking. It is difficult to understand when the data was created, by whom, and for what reason, and also who has previously used the data, and why, or how. Creating complete, accurate, and up-to-date metadata is a key part of an effective data governance plan. Metadata enables researchers to accurately replicate past queries, which is crucial for scientific investigations and accurate benchmarking, and prevents the creation of “data dumpsters,” or isolated data sets that are limited in their usefulness.

### 6.5.6 QUERYING

Additionally, robust metadata and solid stewardship practices make it easier for organizations to query their data and to find the desired answers. The ability to query data is fundamental for study and analysis, yet healthcare organizations must commonly overcome various difficulties before they can engage in meaningful analysis of their big data resources. First, they must overcome data silos and interoperability issues that prevent query tools from accessing the organization’s entire repository of data. If different parts of a data set are held in separate siloed systems or in different formats, it may not be possible to create a complete picture of an organization’s status or an individual patient’s health. Many organizations use Structured Query Language (SQL) to query large data sets and relational databases, but it is only effective when a user can first trust the accuracy, completeness, and standardization of the underlying data [11].

### 6.5.7 REPORTING

After providers have nailed down the query process, they should produce a report that is clear, concise, and accessible to the intended audience. However, the accuracy and integrity of the data has a direct downstream effect on the accuracy and reliability of the report. Poor data at the start will produce suspect reports at the end of the process, which can hinder clinicians attempting to use data to treat patients. Providers should also understand the distinction between “analysis” and “reporting.” Reporting is often part of the analysis of data that must be extracted before it can be fully examined, yet reporting can also stand on its own as an end result. While some reports may be geared towards highlighting a particular trend, arriving at a novel conclusion to make a specific move, others must be presented in a way that enables the reader to draw his or her own conclusions about what the full range of data implies. Providers have various options for meeting these different needs, including certified registries, reporting tools integrated with their electronic health records, and web-based interfaces facilitated by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) and other groups.

### 6.5.8 VISUALIZATION

For comprehension purposes, clear visualization of data can make it much easier for a clinician to retain and use data appropriately. Color coding is a popular data visualization technique that typically produces a quick response—for instance, red, yellow, and green are universally understood to mean stop, caution, and go.
