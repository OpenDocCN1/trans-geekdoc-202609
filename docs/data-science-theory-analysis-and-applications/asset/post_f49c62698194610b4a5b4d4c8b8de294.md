
Associations should likewise consider good data presentation practices, for example, graphs utilize an appropriate scale to represent different figures and the correct labeling of data to diminish the potential confusion [12]. Tangled flow charts, cramped or overlapping content, and low-quality designs can baffle and bother recipients, driving them to disregard or confuse data.

### 6.5.9 UPDATING

Healthcare data isn’t static, and most elements will require relatively frequent updates in order to stay current and relevant. For some data sets, such as patient vital signs, these updates may happen often. Other data, such as personal residence or marital status, may only change a few times during a person’s entire lifetime. Understanding the volatility of big data, or how regularly and to what extent it changes, can be a challenge for associations that don’t reliably monitor their data resources. Providers must have a clear idea of which data sets require manual updating, which can be automated, how to complete this process without downtime for end users, and how to ensure that updates can be conducted without harming the quality or integrity of the data set. Associations should likewise ensure that they are not creating unnecessary duplicate records when updating a single component, which may make it difficult for clinicians to access important data for the patient’s decision making [13]. ### 6.5.10 SHARING

Very few providers work in a vacuum, and fewer patients receive all of their care at a single location. This implies that sharing data with external partners is essential, especially as the industry moves towards population health management and value-based care. Data interoperability is a constant concern for associations of many types, sizes, and positions along the data development range. Major differences in the way electronic health records are designed and implemented can greatly reduce the ability to move data between different associations, often leaving clinicians without data when they need to make key decisions, follow up with patients, and develop strategies to improve overall outcomes. The industry is currently attempting to enhance the sharing of data across technical and organizational boundaries [14].

### 6.6.2 COMPLICATIONS AND CLARIFICATIONS OF HEALTHCARE DATA COLLECTION

Data collection in healthcare still requires an extensive effort. Here, we have three major barriers that keep us from using data effectively [17].

- **Deprived Data Quality**: Data validation is an underlying issue. We should take Medical Record Number (MRN), for instance. Most healthcare organizations utilize Spreadmart to generate required data; however, this process requires confirmation that the obtained data matches the MRNs. Moreover, there is always a chance for a mistype in any of the patient MRNs when writing them down manually in the spreadsheet. Additionally, some MRNs may change occasionally in the source system. Such potential outcomes should also be considered.

- **Complex Relationship**: Envision a healthcare organization that utilizes Enterprise Data Warehouse (EDW), with the aim of reducing the number of data silos. However, Excel spreadsheets are still the best way to gather and maintain self-generated data; however, this creates another data silo despite this technique. Accordingly, the process for integration of the given data silo becomes a significant challenge. For instance, we have two individuals in our organization who handle self-generated data and record it in a spreadsheet. They have some available time toward the end of the working day to enter that data. We send them spreadsheets asking that employees enter updated records, and the two will make notes in their own individual way. In other words, the data will be provided in two distinct versions.

- **Data Security**: Effective data security has always been a tremendous concern with regard to data collection in nursing and other healthcare fields. Generally speaking, data collection tools in healthcare are mostly stored on a “Personal Computer,” which frequently leads to data security issues. Utilizing cloud-based platforms for storing data might be a good solution for this issue; however, it will require some customized training for staff members. ### 6.6.3 CURRENT DATA COLLECTION METHODS

Some of the current data collection methods are shown in Figure 6.6 with highlights.

![Figure 6.6 Current data collection methods](img/5025594f89449de2a74e5b325b1e84ef_162_0.png)

1. **Sample versus evaluation**—some data are gathered for the whole population to which they apply; such data are sometimes referred to as enumeration data. One example is the decennial census, which aims to obtain counts by geographic area and basic demographic characteristics for the entire resident population of the United States. However, the term census may be used to refer to any data collection for each unit in the population. On the other hand, many data cannot be gathered for the entire population without extreme expense and/or burden on respondents. Rather, the data are gathered from a subset of the population, or a sample that is selected in a way that makes it representative of the whole population; thus, estimates can be determined from the sample that approximate those for the whole population.

2. Population-versus group-based—some data collection efforts focus on an entire population characterized only by broad demographic attributes, for example, all children under age 6 or all young women.

3. Based on administrative records versus respondents—some data are derived from records that already exist because they are necessary for the administration of a program or intervention. Examples are government records, business records, and medical records. Other data are collected directly from respondents, for instance, by interviewing people about their experiences. In the case of children, most respondent-based data are gathered from proxy respondents. A third category to consider is that relating to clinical data, for example, observational studies.
