Sometimes, we may wish to transform a schema into another that is not quite equivalent. For example, suppose that in our car rally application, we limit each car to at most two drivers, but do not classify the drivers in any meaningful way (e.g., as main or backup drivers). Let us also remove the constraint that drivers may drive only one car. This situation is schematized in Fig. 14.17.

Although no DriverStatus object type is present, the frequency constraint in Fig. 14.17 says that each car has at most two drivers. This enables us to introduce an artificial distinction to specialize the predicate into two cases, as in Fig. 14.18.

Because this distinction is not present in the original schema, the alternatives shown in Fig. 14.18 are not equivalent to the original. They are, in fact, stronger—each implies the original schema, but is not implied by it.

Notice the use of hyphens in the predicates of Fig. 14.18 to bind adjectival phrases to their object terms. This improves verbalization of the fact types and constraints. The four fact types are Car has Driver-A, Car has Driver-B, Car has first-Driver, and Car has second-Driver. When the NORMA tool generates constraint verbalization, it binds the adjective to the object noun. For example, the upper uniqueness constraint in Fig. 14.18(b) verbalizes as “Each Car has at most one first Driver”. Without the hyphen, the verbalization defaults to “Each Car has first at most one Driver”.

The schema in Fig. 14.18(b) is actually stronger than the schema in Fig. 14.18(a). If the Car role in Fig. 14.17 is mandatory, then the Car roles in Fig. 14.18(a) are disjunctively mandatory, and the top Car role in Fig. 14.18(b) is mandatory (which then implies the subset constraint).
