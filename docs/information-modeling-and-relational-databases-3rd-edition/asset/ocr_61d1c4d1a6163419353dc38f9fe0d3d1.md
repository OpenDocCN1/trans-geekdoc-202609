For a ternary association, there is only one way to have a three-role UC. There are three ways of having precisely one two-role constraint, and three ways of having exactly two two-role constraints. Finally, there is one way of having three two-role constraints. Thus, there are eight different UC patterns that may arise with a ternary fact type. Four of these involve just one constraint (Fig. 4.24) and four involve combinations (see Fig. 4.26).

This general approach for specifying UCs applies to fact types of any arity. Fig. 4.28 shows a template for a quaternary predicate with a UC spanning roles 1, 3, and 4. This indicates that each acd combination must be unique to the fact table, where a, c, and d occur in the first, third, and fourth columns, respectively, on the same row of this table. A small but significant population of the fact table is shown. Verify for yourself that this is consistent with the indicated UC.

As an example of a quaternary, consider Fig. 4.29. The UC spans the first, second, and fourth roles, and verbalizes as: For each Person1, Person2 and Language, that Person1 judged that Person2 to have at most one SkillLevel in that Language. Note the subscripts to distinguish the different roles played by Person.

It is shown later that any UCs on a quaternary must span at least three roles. As an exercise, you might like to explore all the possible cases. Or maybe not! Because elementary fact types are rarely longer than quaternaries, no examples of quinaries or longer fact types are discussed here.
