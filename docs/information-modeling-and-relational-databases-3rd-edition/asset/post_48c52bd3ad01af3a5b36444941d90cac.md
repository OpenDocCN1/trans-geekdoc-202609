The term n-role constraint denotes a constraint that spans n roles. A UC that spans just one role is a **simple** UC. The constraint in Fig. 4.23 is a two-role constraint because it spans two of the three roles involved in the fact type. A binary fact type has three possible basic constraints (two one-role and one two-role), which give rise to four possible cases. A later section demonstrates that *no (elementary) ternary fact type can have a simple UC*. So a ternary fact type has four basic UCs to be considered: three two-role constraints and one three-role constraint, as shown in Fig. 4.24.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_150_1.png)

FIG. 4.24 Allowed basic uniqueness constraints for a ternary.

| Joe Biden | JP | 2023 |

FIG. 4.25 A ternary with a spanning uniqueness constraint.

In practice, ternaries usually have just a single UC either over one role pair or over all three roles. As an example of the latter, a politician travel example is modeled in Fig. 4.25. Here, many politicians may visit many countries in many years. Such a fact type is said to be “many to many to many”. Because no whole row can be repeated, we cannot use this fact type to record multiple visits to the same country by the same politician in the same year. If you want to record this, use a different model (e.g., replace Year by Date, or add Quantity).

With a ternary fact type, we should systematically test each of the three two-role UCs to see which ones hold. Only if no two-role constraints hold should the three-role constraint be specified. As an exercise, check for yourself that the sample data is significant for this constraint.

For a ternary association, there is only one way to have a three-role UC. There are three ways of having precisely one two-role constraint, and three ways of having exactly two two-role constraints. Finally, there is one way of having three two-role constraints. Thus, there are eight different UC patterns that may arise with a ternary fact type. Four of these involve just one constraint (Fig. 4.24) and four involve combinations (see Fig. 4.26).

This general approach for specifying UCs applies to fact types of any arity. Fig. 4.28 shows a template for a quaternary predicate with a UC spanning roles 1, 3, and 4. This indicates that each acd combination must be unique to the fact table, where a, c, and d occur in the first, third, and fourth columns, respectively, on the same row of this table. A small but significant population of the fact table is shown. Verify for yourself that this is consistent with the indicated UC.

As an example of a quaternary, consider Fig. 4.29. The UC spans the first, second, and fourth roles, and verbalizes as: For each Person1, Person2 and Language, that Person1 judged that Person2 to have at most one SkillLevel in that Language. Note the subscripts to distinguish the different roles played by Person.

It is shown later that any UCs on a quaternary must span at least three roles. As an exercise, you might like to explore all the possible cases. Or maybe not! Because elementary fact types are rarely longer than quaternaries, no examples of quinaries or longer fact types are discussed here.

To visualize the connection between roles and columns of the fact table, think of the role boxes as the holes in which the objects get placed to complete the fact. No matter what the arity of the fact type, a UC across a combination of role boxes means that any instance of that column combination that does appear in the table must occur on one row only, and hence can be used to identify that row.

Because a role combination governed by a UC provides a “key to unlock” any row of the fact table, this combination is referred to as a *key* for that table. This notion will be elaborated later in the context of the (often non-elementary) tables used in relational databases.

Now, let's consider *nesting*. In Fig. 4.23, the subject ratings example was set out in flattened form. Suppose instead we use a nested approach, as shown in Fig. 4.30. Here each (Person, Subject) enrollment is treated as an object that may result in a rating. For example, the enrollment of Adams in CS112 results in a 7.

Fig. 4.23 is not actually equivalent to Fig. 4.30 unless a further constraint is added that a rating must be known for each enrollment. Such mandatory role constraints are discussed in the next chapter.

The Enrollment entity type in Fig. 4.30 is called an objectified association, an objectified relationship type, an objectified predicate, a nested entity type, or a nested object type. Most objectified associations are spanned by a UC (as in Fig. 4.30). This spanning UC indicates that each person may enroll in many subjects, and the same subject may be enrolled in by many people. The simple UC on the binary predicate indicates that each (Person, Subject) enrollment resulted in at most one rating.

Section 10.5 includes a discussion of objectification of associations with nonspanning UCs (e.g., 1:1 associations and n:1 associations). While currently allowed in ORM, such cases can violate elementarity, and are better modeled in other ways.

## 4.4 External Uniqueness Constraints

The UCs discussed so far are internal (intrapredicate) UCs, as each applies to one or more roles in a single predicate. External (interpredicate) uniqueness constraints apply to roles from different predicates. To understand these constraints, the conceptual join operation is first discussed.

Consider the schema and sample population in Fig. 4.31. The left-most fact table indicates that employee 15 drives cars with registration numbers PKJ123 and ABC000. The right-most fact table indicates that car PKJ123 is imported from Italy. The ternary fact table indicates that employee 15 drives the car PKJ123 imported from Italy. The asterisk after the mixfix predicate reading indicates that the ternary fact type is derived. The derivation rule is preceded by an asterisk.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_154_0.png)

FIG. 4.31 A compound ternary derived from a conceptual inner join of the binaries.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_154_1.png)

FIG. 4.32 An instance diagram for the model shown in Fig. 4.31.

The instance diagram in Fig. 4.32 may help clarify things. The binary facts appear as lines connecting two dots. The ternary fact corresponds to the continuous path from Employee to Country. Starting at the Car type, this path may also be verbalized as the conjunction Car is driven by Employee and was imported from Country.

The derivation rule shows how the ternary is derived by combining the binaries, with Car as the connecting or join object type. The defining expression “Employee drives some Car that was imported from Country” is a formal verbalization of the conceptual path from Employee, through the drives predicate, Car type, and import predicate, to Country. The keyword “that” declares that the driven car must be *the same* as the imported car. In other words, if an employee drives a car, we must use the very same car to continue the path to Country.

A fact type resulting from a join is a *compound fact type*, not an elementary fact type because it is essentially the conjunction of at least two simpler fact types. For this reason, *join fact types are normally excluded from ORM schema diagrams. If included for discussion purposes, they may be shaded, as in Fig. 4.31, to indicate that they are just views. Unlike elementary derived fact types, their compound nature can lead to redundancy unless each join role is covered by a simple UC.
