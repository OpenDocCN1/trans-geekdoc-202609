The ORM schema in Fig. 17.18 includes several other kinds of constraints. The inclusive-or constraint in example (a) ensures that each job applicant is either qualified or has a reference (or both). This may be expressed in Rel thus:

ic (p) requires JobApplicant(p) implies (isQualified(p) or hasReference(p))

The exclusive-or constraint in example (b) ensures that each planet in our solar system is an inner planet or an outer planet but not both. This may be expressed in Rel thus:

```
ic (p) requires Planet(p) implies (isAnInnerPlanet(p) xor isAnOuterPlanet(p))
```

The value constraint in example (c) restricts the value of GenderCode in the underlying reference type Gender has GenderCode. The value constraint in example (d) restricts IQ values to the range 0..300. These constraints may be formulated thus:

ic (g, gc) requires hasGenderCode(g,gc) implies (gc = "M" or gc = "F")
ic (x, y) requires hasIQNr(x,y) implies (x >= 0 and x <= 300)
```

The exclusion constraints in examples (e) and (f) say that nobody can author and review the same book, and that nobody can review a paper authored by someone who lectures at the same university where he/she lectures. The internal frequency constraint in example (b) ensures that each reviewer is assigned at most three papers to review. It may be formulated thus:

The composite frequency constraint in example (c) ensures that no student may enroll more than twice in the same course. This may be formulated as follows. ```
def inCourseHasNrEnrollments(s, c, n):

ic (s, c, n) requires inCourseHasNrEnrollments(s, c, n) implies n <= 2
```

The external frequency constraint in example (d) ensures that for each country, at most two politicians are both in its government and are a coregent. This constraint may be formulated using the count function or instead simply thus:

ic (p1, p2, p3) requires

Ring constraints are easy to specify in Rel. The reflexive ring constraint that people know themselves may be expressed as: ic(p) requires Person(p) implies knows(p,p). The irreflexive ring constraint that nobody is a parent of himself/herself may be expressed as: ic (p) requires not isaParentOf(p, p). The symmetric constraint on siblinghood may be specified thus: ic (p1,p2) requires isaSiblingOf(p1, p2) implies isaSiblingOf(p2, p1). The asymmetric ring constraint that nobody is a parent of himself/herself may be expressed thus: ic (p1,p2) requires isaParentOf(p1, p2) implies not isaParentOf(p2, p1).

The transitive nature of the isOlderThan relation may be formulated as follows: ic (p1, p2, p3) requires (isOlderThan(p1, p2) and isOlderThan(p2, p3)) implies isOlderThan(p1, p3). The intransitivity constraint on the direct precedence relation may be formulated as follows: ic (e1,e2,e3) requires (directlyPrecedes(e1, e2) and directlyPrecedes(e2, e3)) implies not directlyPrecedes(e1, e3).

The ancestorship relation may be defined as follows: def isanAncestorOf(p1, p2): isaParentOf(p1, p2) or isaParentOf(p1, p3) and isanAncestorOf(p3, p2). The acyclic constraint on ancestorship may be specified thus: ic (p) requires not isanAncestorOf(p, p).

The pair-subset constraint in Figure 17.20(a) ensures that each student who passed a course also enrolled in that course. This may be expressed in Rel thus: ic (s, c) requires passedCourse(s, c) implies enrolledInCourse(s, c).

The subset constraint over a join path in Fig. 17.20 ensures that each advisor who serves in a country must speak at least one language that is used in that country. This constraint may be expressed as follows:

```
ic (a, c) requires servesInCountry(a, c) implies
(speaksLanguage(a, l) and isaLanguageUsedBy(l, c))
```

## Other Database Varieties

Specialized database varieties are sometimes invoked to deal with certain types of applications. _Spatial databases_ require efficient management of spatial data, such as maps (roads, land, etc.), two-dimensional designs (circuits, town planning, etc.), and three-dimensional designs (visualization of medical operations, molecular structures, flight paths, etc.). They provide built-in support for spatial data types (points, lines, polygons, etc.), spatial operators (overlap, contains, intersect, etc.), and spatial indexes (R-trees, quad trees, etc.). This allows efficient formulation and processing of spatial queries, such as, How many houses are there within 5km of the proposed shopping center? Which flights fly over the greater LA area? Which diagrams are similar to diagram 37?

Previously, special systems were used for spatial data while a relational DBMS was used for alphanumeric data. The current trend is to manage both standard and spatial data in the one system. A typical application of a geographic information system (GIS) might proceed as follows: standard data on traffic accidents is entered; the road maps are displayed to highlight the accident sites; and this is now used to determine regions where extra precautions need to be taken (e.g., radar traps).

Historically, GIS vendors adopted three main approaches: hybrid (georelational-objects in a spatial file store are given identifiers that can be referenced in relational tables); integrated (spatial and non-spatial data are stored in relational tables); and object-oriented (all data is stored in an ODB). More recently, many spatial database applications as well as other applications using nonstandard data have been implemented using object-relational technology.
