+   - There is now a much greater understanding of the value of data. Even something as simple as a validated relationship between a person and their email address has a cash value. This has motivated organizations to collect as much data as possible, even if there appears to be no immediate use for the information.
+   - Relational databases evolved at a time when “client-server” was the dominant information system architecture. This naturally led to databases that served a limited internal pool of interests. Most organizations now recognize the advantages of being able to transfer data between disparate systems, both internally within the organization and externally with customers, suppliers, partners, and so on. This has been facilitated by interchange formats such as eXtensible Markup Language (XML) and JavaScript Object Notation (JSON), as discussed in Chapter 16.
+   - Since its public appearance in 1990, the Internet has grown by several orders of magnitude. Fast and reliable network connections are now widespread, and connectivity is taken for granted in most of the world. Many websites (and the databases that support them) have millions of users rather than the hundreds that would be considered a large user population in the 1980s.
+   - The day-to-day operations of most organizations are now reliant on some kind of information technology (IT) platform that facilitates data exchange within and without the organization. Growth in general data usage has been explosive. To a large extent, this has been fueled by today’s extensive use of smartphones. In other words, most people carry a computer and communication device with them all the time and do not need to be tethered to a static business system. For example, the publication of street maps has virtually collapsed because most people will now get the relevant map (accessing a remote database) via their phone.

As these trends have evolved, they have exposed database systems to new challenges and opportunities. Rising to these has raised questions about the best way to organize data resources, perhaps by deviating from the relational model that has been the standard for so long.

Before diving into more detail about NoSQL, it will be useful to review some selected database characteristics to provide a context for discussion to follow. For this chapter, we’ll assume that some important factors that might be relevant to choosing a database solution are equally balanced among all candidates. These include features such as security, ease of installation, documentation, vendor reputation, support, and so on. Our focus here will be on aspects that are important in discussing the distinction between relational and nonrelational databases.
