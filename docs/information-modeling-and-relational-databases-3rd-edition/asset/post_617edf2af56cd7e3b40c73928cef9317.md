
- **Schema design.** Produce an initial schema design from a natural language description of a given domain. In the relational case, strict normalization could be enforced.

- **Query design.** For a given database type and schema, write a query in the appropriate technical language to return results that have been expressed in natural language.

- **Application design.** Produce a design based on a given database schema for an application described in natural language. This could include both desktop and mobile variants.

- **Database Management.** Automation of routine operations that are normally carried out by a database administrator (DBA). These can include tasks such as index creation and updating, query tuning for evolving usage, performance assessment and optimization, and more.

- **Data cleansing.** Historical data is often plagued with problems related to the original data capture that can obstruct subsequent analysis. Training on typical problems can be used to avoid or repair these issues.

- **Code creation.** Technical code (in some given language) can be created from a description of the intent in nontechnical language. This is already operational as a “copilot” functionality in some development environments.

- **Schema migration.** As the usage of a database evolves, it may become apparent that the schema originally envisioned is no longer appropriate. Migration to an alternative schema is a complex process that can be aided by automation.

- **Advanced analytics.** The business-relevant information in the data can be processed to uncover patterns and trends that are not obvious through traditional queries.

The kinds of AI applications mentioned above often rely on a response to a prompt defining the necessary focus for the AI. Creating the appropriate prompt is at present a skill, but a skill that itself will surely become amenable to AI in time. The one area that cannot be determined by AI is what is, or is not, relevant to the user (often referred to as the UoD in an ORM context), and the user’s view of how the objects in the UoD are related. In some sense, the UoD could be seen as the ultimate prompt that cannot be reduced to anything else and cannot be defined without user knowledge. Clearly, an AI agent must understand the UoD to be effective, but how can that information be supplied? An obvious solution is provided by an ORM schema. It is certainly possible to describe a UoD through an informal discourse, but such descriptions are plagued by well-known ambiguities. Creating an ORM schema can sometimes be a painful process for a business (internal politics, unclear direction, etc.), but the eventual prize is an unambiguous description of the business intent. Once this is adopted by the business, it can be used as the basis for many facets of subsequent AI processing.

## 17.7 Summary

Relational databases first appeared in the 1970s and became the dominant form of data management during the 1980s and 1990s. Apart from any intrinsic technological merits, their wide adoption was greatly aided by the similarities among the offerings of different vendors. This made investment in the technology a low-risk option, both in terms of deployment by companies and careers for individuals. Since their introduction, there have been many proposals for alternative approaches, but the technology and its associated language, SQL, have continued to evolve. The SQL standard has expanded to include features from many competing approaches to meet the perceived demand for alternatives.

However, there is one intrinsic feature of a relational database that cannot easily change, and that is its use of a client–server model with a single server providing resources to many clients. Increasing the size of the database requires increasing the size of the server, often at a disproportionately higher cost. Spreading a database over multiple servers is not practical unless the data can be partitioned into noninteracting subsets. When relational databases first appeared, this was not a serious problem because data volumes were generally low. In recent years, large datasets have become common, and this is one of the main driving factors behind the growth of the NoSQL movement.

A secondary factor has been the emergence of many niche applications where there is some advantage to be gained by a specialized architecture. In a sense, this is a reaction against the application-neutral approach of relational systems. Optimization of one factor is not always an advantage because it always involves deoptimization of other factors, and so seeking increased performance usually involves narrowing the applicability of the solution.

Today, many organizations use a mixture of database technologies, usually including one or more relational databases but supplemented by other approaches for selected application areas. This has been greatly facilitated by the growth of cloud-based facilities that reduce the barriers to deploying and managing a range of diverse data resources. We can expect this trend to continue for the foreseeable future.

### 18.2 Data Warehousing and OLAP

Most commercial information systems are built to support heavy volumes of transactions by many users on a daily basis. Examples include banking, insurance, and order processing systems. These Online Transaction Processing (OLTP) systems typically require quick throughput for their largely predefined range of transactions, especially update transactions. To improve performance, historical data is often archived once it reaches a certain age, reducing the size of the datasets used for daily operations.

A single organization may have several OLTP systems (e.g., purchasing, sales, inventory, customer records), possibly implemented using different kinds of database management systems (DBMS) or other software applications, and the coupling between such systems may be weak or even nonexistent.

Over time, businesses became aware that the collective information contained in their various systems had great potential for analyzing market trends and improving their business processes. However, their OLTP systems were unsuitable for executives to perform this task, given the poor performance and complex interface for ad hoc analysis queries (e.g., aggregated multitable joins and nested correlated subqueries).

Moreover, insufficient integration or history made some queries simply impossible. Partly to address the problem of integrating data from prerelational systems for analysis purposes, IBM proposed the notion of an “information warehouse”. Although performance problems delayed acceptance of this idea for some years, a later proposal for a “data warehouse” by Bill Inmon (1993) was enthusiastically embraced by the business community, and nowadays most large companies have a data warehouse.

A data warehouse is an enterprise-wide, integrated, historical database of information extracted from individual data sources for the purpose of supporting analysis of the business by management. During analysis, it is read-only. Because the patterns and trends sought from the analysis tend to evolve slowly, and some imprecision is acceptable, updates are performed in bulk according to an agreed schedule (e.g., weekly).

The construction of a data warehouse for a large enterprise can be a lengthy task. To exploit the benefits as soon as possible, the data warehouse is often built iteratively, one subject area at a time. As subject areas are added to the data warehouse, they may be used to load data marts (see Fig. 18.1).
