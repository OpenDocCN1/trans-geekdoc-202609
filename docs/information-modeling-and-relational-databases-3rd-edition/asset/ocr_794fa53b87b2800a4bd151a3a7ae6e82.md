FIG. 4.11 A politician may visit many countries, and vice versa.

Now consider the populated binary fact type shown in Fig. 4.11. Note that no whole row of the fact table is repeated. This must be the case because each row corresponds to an elementary fact, and we do not duplicate facts in our database.

Although each row is unique, we cannot say the same for each politician. For example, “Anthony Albanian” appears twice in the first column. Nor is the country unique. For example, “JP” (Japan) occurs twice in the second column. For this fact type then, the only UC that applies to every one of its possible populations is that the combination of politician and country is unique. This UC is depicted by a uniqueness bar that spans both columns of the fact table. The constraint verbalizes as shown.

The sample data show that a politician may visit many (i.e., at least two) countries, and a country may be visited by many politicians. The fact population is a many-to-many (or m:n) relation. Fig. 4.12 shows the instance diagram.
