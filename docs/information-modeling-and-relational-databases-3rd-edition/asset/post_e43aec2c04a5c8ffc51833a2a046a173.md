With long predicates, nesting may be applied more than once, as shown in Fig. 14.28, where a quaternary is binarized in two stages. For simplicity, other object types and role links are omitted. We saw earlier how unaries may be converted into binaries. Nesting or coreferencing may be used to convert n-ary predicates into binaries. So, in principle, any domain may be modeled with binaries only. However, nonbinary predicates often enable an application to be modeled in a more natural and convenient way.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_727_1.png)

In our examples so far, the objectified association played just a single, mandatory role. In this case, flattening is generally preferable. But what if this role is optional? For example, suppose we modify our earlier Table 14.2 by allowing nulls in the rating column (see Table 14.4). In this population, no ratings appear for the subject CS100. Perhaps the final exam for this subject is still to be held, or marking for it has not finished. In this UoD, it is possible to record the fact that a person enrolls in a subject before knowing what rating the student gets for that subject.

Table 14.4 Enrollments may be recorded before ratings are finalized.

| studentNr | subject | rating |
|---|---|---|
| 1001 | CS100 | ? |
| 1002 | CS100 | ? |
| 1002 | CS114 | 5 |

Because nulls are not allowed at the conceptual level, we cannot model this as a single ternary. We may, however, use two fact types—one for enrollments and one for ratings. Fig. 14.29(a) shows this flattened approach, together with the sample population.

When two or more flat fact types are involved, the equivalence between nested and flattened versions is called N/Fm (nest/flatten into many fact types). The subset pattern illustrated by our example is only one of many cases for this theorem, but because it is the most common case, we set it out in Fig. 14.31.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_729_0.png)

FIG. 14.31 Another nest/flatten equivalence.

We generally favor the nested version in this case because it is more compact and it maps directly to just one table. In contrast, if the flattened version is passed to our standard Rmap procedure, two tables result. Nevertheless, the flattened approach is quite natural, and it verbalizes easily. If you prefer the flattened approach for these reasons, it would be nice if your CASE tool displayed it this way, but gave you the option of having it mapped to a single table in the same way as the nested version. However, such flexibility is typically not provided by current CASE tools. So, the nested approach is generally preferable for this case.

In the flattened version in Fig. 14.31, the S predicate is compatible with the subpredicate comprising the first two roles of R (they are played by the same object types A and B). This compatibility enables the nesting to occur. For this subset case, any (a, b) pair in the population of either must belong to the population of S. So, we objectify S and add an optional connection to C to handle the R facts.

Recall that in our CSDP, whenever different predicates have compatible role sequences (of two or more roles), we should ask ourselves to what extent the populations of these [sub-] predicates must overlap. If a set-comparison (subset, equality or exclusion) constraint exists between them, it must be declared.

The kind of nesting transformation that may now occur depends partly on what kind of set-comparison constraint exists (if any) and on whether other roles occur in the full predicates. Let's look at some more examples before summarizing the overall procedure.

Consider the output report shown in Table 14.5. This may be modeled with two flat fact types, as shown in Fig. 14.32(a). Notice the equality constraint between the two role-pairs. An assignment mark is recorded for a student in a subject if and only if an exam mark is recorded for that student in that subject.

Table 14.5 Student assignment and exam marks.

| Subject: | CS102 | | CS115 | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| StudentNr | Assignmt | Exam | Assignmt | Exam |
| 1001 | 17 | 65 | 15 | 58 |
| 1002 | 20 | 79 | 20 | 67 |
| 1003 | 15 | 60 | 12 | 55 |

The compatible subpredicates might be read as “did assignment in” and “did exam in”. Since their populations must be equal, we may objectify their conjunction did assignment and exam in. The nested version in Fig. 14.32(b) uses the wording “was assessed in”. The specific marks are catered for by attaching two specific predicates as shown. Because of the equality constraint, these are both mandatory.

When passed to the standard Rmap procedure, the schema in Fig. 14.32(a) maps to two tables, whereas the nested version in Fig. 14.32(b) maps to a single table, so the nested version is generally preferred whenever two or more functional roles are attached to the objectification.

A more general N/Fm equivalence result is shown at the top of Fig. 14.33, with four corollaries listed and illustrated below it. This may be generalized further to n predicates (n≥2) and compatible subpredicates of any arity above 1. The object types A...D need not be distinct. As long as [sub-]predicates are compatible, nesting may always be performed by objectifying their disjunctions.

The predicate T in Fig. 14.33 is a logical disjunction, so may be verbalized using the “or” to connect verbalizations of the compatible [sub-]predicates. In terms of populations, the objectified relation T in Fig. 14.33 is the union of the compatible [sub-]relations, that is, T = R[a,b] ∪ S[a,b].

Suppose we remove the equality constraint in Fig. 14.32(a). If no other set-comparison constraint exists, we replace the two mandatory role constraints in Fig. 14.32(b) by an inclusive-or constraint: Each assessment included an assignment or an exam mark or both. The uniqueness constraints in the flat version correspond to the simple UCs in the nested version (see corollary 1 in Fig. 14.33).

Adding a pair-subset constraint from the first two roles of S to the first two roles of R is equivalent to adding a subset constraint from the first role of V to the first role of U. In the context of the disjunctive mandatory constraint, this means that U becomes mandatory (see corollary 2). In terms of populations, it also means that the T relation becomes R[a,b] because if one set is a subset of a second, then their union is just the second set. Compare this with the result in Fig. 14.31.

### Corollaries:

- (1) A binary UC over R[a,b] is equivalent to a UC on the first role of U, and a binary UC over S[a,b] is equivalent to a UC on the first role of V.
- (2) A pair-subset constraint from S[a,b] to R[a,b] makes U mandatory.
- (3) A pair-equality constraint between R[a,b] and S[a,b] makes U and V mandatory.
- (4) A pair-exclusion constraint between R[a,b] and S[a,b] adds an exclusion constraint between the attached roles in the nested version.
