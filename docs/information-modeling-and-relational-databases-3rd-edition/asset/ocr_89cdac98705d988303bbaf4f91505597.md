For object-oriented applications, UML models can incorporate implementation details as well as behavior and deployment aspects not covered by the ORM and ER approaches.

Programming tasks are typically coded in third-generation languages such as C# and Java. Fourth-generation database languages such as SQL are declarative in nature, enabling users to declare what is to be done without the fine detail of how to do it, and are set oriented rather than record oriented.

Fifth-generation languages such as ConQuer enable users to query conceptual models directly. Hierarchic and network database systems store some facts in record types and some facts in links between record types. Relational database systems store all facts in tables.

Various postrelational systems such as object-oriented databases, object-relational databases, deductive databases, ontological databases, and NoSQL databases are gaining acceptance in industry.

No matter how “intelligent” software systems become, people are still needed to describe the UoD and to ask the relevant questions about it.

# Chapter Notes

Full bibliographic entries for references are included in the bibliography at the back of this book. Codd (1969, 1970) introduced the relational model of data. For a historical discussion of these two papers, see Date (1998). Codd (1990) suggests future directions for the relational model. The classic paper that introduced ER modeling is Chen (1976). Kent (2000) is a reprint of a classic book that provides many insights into the nature of information and data.

Many papers on ORM are accessible at www.orm.net and at www.ormfoundation.org. Simsion and Witt (2005) provide a readable coverage of various data modeling topics. An overview of UML can be found in Booch et al. (1999). The latest UML specification is accessible at www.omg.org/spec/UML. Halpin (2015a) provides a comparative review of information modeling in UML and ORM.

Date (2000) provides a clear introduction to most aspects of database systems. Further background on network and hierarchic DBMSs is included in Appendices C–E of Connolly et al. (1999). Gorman (1998) provides a structural review of historical data models and argues that the modern SQL standard is a data model in its own right that is closer to some prerelational models than it is to the relational model. The latest version of the SQL standard is accessible at www.iso.org/standard/76584.html.
