Since their introduction, there have been many proposals for alternative approaches, but the technology and its associated language, SQL, have continued to evolve. The SQL standard has expanded to include features from many competing approaches to meet the perceived demand for alternatives.

However, there is one intrinsic feature of a relational database that cannot easily change, and that is its use of a client–server model with a single server providing resources to many clients. Increasing the size of the database requires increasing the size of the server, often at a disproportionately higher cost. Spreading a database over multiple servers is not practical unless the data can be partitioned into noninteracting subsets. When relational databases first appeared, this was not a serious problem because data volumes were generally low. In recent years, large datasets have become common, and this is one of the main driving factors behind the growth of the NoSQL movement.

A secondary factor has been the emergence of many niche applications where there is some advantage to be gained by a specialized architecture. In a sense, this is a reaction against the application-neutral approach of relational systems. Optimization of one factor is not always an advantage because it always involves deoptimization of other factors, and so seeking increased performance usually involves narrowing the applicability of the solution.

Today, many organizations use a mixture of database technologies, usually including one or more relational databases but supplemented by other approaches for selected application areas. This has been greatly facilitated by the growth of cloud-based facilities that reduce the barriers to deploying and managing a range of diverse data resources. We can expect this trend to continue for the foreseeable future.
