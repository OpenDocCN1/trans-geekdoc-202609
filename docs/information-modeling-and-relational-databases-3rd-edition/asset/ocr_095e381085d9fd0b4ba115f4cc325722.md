We are not limited to operating only at this level because, as we have seen, we can compose collections of low-level elements into higher-level elements until we reach a more convenient level of expression. However, this still leaves the realization underconstrained, as there could be many ways of linking data to process for the same conceptual design. The choices between the alternatives are somewhat orthogonal to the conceptual elements. For example, are we building a self-contained or distributed system operating over a network? How many users do we intend to support? Are we concerned about issues such as availability and scalability? The additional information required is what might be termed the *technical architecture* of the system, which is more concerned with the engineering aspects. In principle, given a definition of the technical architecture of a system, we should be able to generate the necessary system elements required from a business model. We have already seen how this could work in examples such as Executable UML. In the future, we can expect similar ideas to be widely adopted as a natural out-growth of conceptual modeling.

# # 15.9 Process Models, Databases, and ORM

BPMN provides a standard notation and semantics for process descriptions. It has support for defining the execution of business processes but can be used to define processes at a purely conceptual (implementation-independent) level. Equivalently, ORM supports information modeling at a conceptual level, so we have the capability of defining both business information and business activities in a consistent way. But although both BPMN and ORM produce valid business models, neither approach makes any reference to the other in their basic forms. In this section, we will see how these two viewpoints can be reconciled.
