In other words: Each Politician was born in **at most one** Country. This is how the constraint is formally *verbalized* in *positive form*. The same constraint may be verbalized in *negative form* thus: **It is impossible that the same** Politician was born in **more than one** Country. The absence of a UC on the other role may be verbalized thus: **It is possible that the same** Country is birthplace of **more than one** Politician.

Fig. 4.7 shows an instance diagram for the example. Entities are depicted as dots inside the entity types, and fact instances are shown as connecting lines. Many politicians may be born in one country, but the same country may be the birthplace of many politicians. So, the predicate “... was born in ...” is said to be *many to one (n:1)*. The inverse predicate “... is birthplace of ...” is *one to many (1:n)*.

Instance diagrams such as Fig. 4.7 are often used in logic or mathematics to discuss the theory of relations. Although instance diagrams and fact tables are useful for understanding and validating constraints, they are not part of the schema itself, which is concerned only with the structure, not the population. On a conceptual schema diagram, a UC over a single role is shown by placing it next to the role (on either side), as shown in Fig. 4.8.
