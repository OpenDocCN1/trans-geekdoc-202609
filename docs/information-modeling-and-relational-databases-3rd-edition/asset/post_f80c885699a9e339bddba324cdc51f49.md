In the room example, the two roles participating in the conceptual join are both mandatory for the join object type (Room). This ensures that the conceptual join is an inner join. However, if one or more roles involved in the conceptual join is optional, the use of an external uniqueness constraint needs special care. For example, consider the three ORM schemas shown in Fig. 10.2.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_426_1.png)

FIG. 10.2 Optional roles may involve an inner join (a) or an outer join (b), (c).

The ORM schema in Fig. 10.2(a) is a fragment of an ORM metaschema. ORM requires each predicate to have at least one reading, but it is optional whether roles are given names. In the figure, the role played by RoleName is named “name”, but the other roles are not named. The external uniqueness constraint indicates that role names are unique within a given predicate. A predicate may have many unnamed roles, but if a role is named it can be identified by the combination of its name and its predicate.

This situation requires an inner join interpretation for the join path underlying the external uniqueness constraint. Let us identify the predicate of the association Role is in Predicate as P1 and its left and right roles as r1 and r2, respectively. Let us also identify the predicate of the association Role has RoleName as P2 and its left and right roles as r3 and r4, respectively. The inner join of the two fact types results in a compound ternary fact type equivalent to the following relational table scheme.

| roleId | predicateId | [roleName] |
|--------|-------------|------------|
| r4     | P2          | name       |

This relational notation underlines uniqueness constraints; if more than one exists, the primary key is doubly underlined. Optional columns are marked with square brackets. The external uniqueness constraint appears as a compound internal uniqueness constraint. Because we only require roles to have unique names in a predicate if they are named, the conceptual join between the roles of Role in Fig. 10.2(a) is an inner join, resulting in the single row population shown.

Using "?" to depict a null value, the outer join result would include four tuples: (r1, P1, ?), (r2, P1, ?), (r3, P2, ?), and (r4, P2, name). The first two tuples would then violate the compound uniqueness constraint, so outer join semantics should not be used with this example.

The external uniqueness constraints in Fig. 10.2(b) and (c) are depicted with an inner "o" through the uniqueness bar. This indicates that the external uniqueness constraint has outer join semantics, with the added proviso that nulls produced in the outer join are treated as actual values.

Hence, each course may be referenced by exactly one of the following patterns: the combination of the course title and its department (where the course is offered by a department); or its course title (where the course is offered by no department, e.g., a course offered by a visiting lecturer).

External uniqueness constraints of this nature are verbalized in two sentences, one for the inner join semantics and one for the additional semantics of the outer join. The external uniqueness constraint in Fig. 10.2(b) verbalizes thus:

- **For each CourseTitle and Department**
  at most one Course has that CourseTitle and is offered by that Department.

- **For each CourseTitle**
  at most one Course has that CourseTitle and is offered by no Department.

Consider a sample population with three courses, C1, C2, and C3, with the same title, one offered by the physics department, one by the mathematics department, and one offered by no department. The join of the title and department fact types results in a compound ternary fact type equivalent to the following relational table scheme. The external uniqueness constraint appears as the internal uniqueness constraint spanning courseTitle and departmentCode.

| courseCode | courseTitle | [departmentCode] |
|------------|-------------|------------------|
| C1         | Mechanics   | PY               |
| C2         | Mechanics   | MA               |
| C3         | Mechanics   | ?                |
| C4         | Mechanics   | ?                |

The fourth row (C4, Mechanics, ?) is rejected because in the presence of row 3, it violates the constraint that courses with no department must have different titles. To determine constraint satisfaction, the nulls on rows 3 and 4 are treated like ordinary values, and thus are equated. This effectively applies *outer join semantics*.

Outer join semantics may apply even if the disjunctive reference scheme is the only means of identifying courses, as in Fig. 10.2(c).

External constraints with inner join semantics may be used for the preferred reference scheme of an entity type only if the coroles hosted by that entity type in the constrained relationships are mandatory, as in Fig. 10.2(a).

In the previous examples, the join path underlying the external uniqueness constraint involves only one join. But in practice, the *join path* may involve many inner or outer joins. Fig. 10.3 summarizes the problem of *join path ambiguity*. To find the join path underlying the external uniqueness constraint, we need to find a path connecting roles r2 and r9, moving right from r2 and left from r9.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_428_0.png)

FIG. 10.3 Join paths may require explicit declaration of the join roles.

Our choice is shaded, but there are three possible paths between B and C. We could traverse the top predicate, performing the joins r2 = r3 and r4 = r9. We could traverse the middle predicate that implicitly underlies the subtype connection, performing the joins r2 = r5 and r6 = r9, or we could traverse the bottom predicate, performing the joins r2 = r7 and r8 = r9.

ORM does not graphically highlight the relevant joins (as done here by shading). In general, shading is not adequate because a path may traverse the same predicate more than once. A general graphical solution may be provided by numbering the roles used to perform the joins, but this can lead to messy diagrams. A tool could prompt the user to select the list of join roles and then toggle the display of the joins on/off under user control. An alternative is to use a textual language to formulate constraints with multiple, candidate join paths. Formal support for join paths is provided in the professional version of NORMA.

For non-ORM approaches, basic support for external uniqueness is required, and then the join paths may be disambiguated graphically as for ORM or textually if the method includes a sufficiently powerful formal query language. UML could use the object constraint language (OCL) for such specifications, although OCL expressions often appear cryptic to nontechnical domain experts.

As for external uniqueness, external frequency constraints have an underlying join path. If the joins involve optional roles, inner joins are assumed. For example, the external frequency constraint in Fig. 10.4 applies only to politicians who are coregents, so verbalizes as **For each Country, there are at most 2 instances of Politician such that that Politician is a coregent and serves on the government of that Country.**

Recall that ORM *set-comparison constraints* apply a subset, equality, or exclusion constraint between compatible role sequences. Sometimes one or more of these role sequences are projected from a join path. As a simple example, the join subset constraint in Fig. 10.5(a) runs from the role pair comprising the Advisor serves in Country fact type to the role pair comprising the first roles of the Advisor speaks Language and Country uses Language fact types.

The constraint means that the set of advisor–country pairs populating the serves-in predicate must be a subset of the set of advisor–country pairs obtained by projecting on the advisor and country roles of the join path Advisor speaks Language **that** is used by Country.

The join path in Fig. 10.5(a) involves a conceptual inner join on Language, equating instances playing its two roles. In other words, if an advisor serves in a country, then that advisor must speak at least one language used by that country. This constraint may be verbalized formally in ORM as **Each Advisor who serves in some Country also speaks some Language that is used by that Country.**

Like ER, UML has no graphic notation for expressing a join subset constraint, but does allow the constraint to be expressed textually in a note attached to the relevant model elements (in this case, the three associations), as shown in Fig. 10.5(b). ER could also be adapted to allow such usage of notes.

Fig. 10.5(c) shows another example discussed earlier in the book. The constraint may be verbalized in ORM thus: **If some Employee has some PersonTitle that is restricted to some Gender then that Employee is of that Gender**. A sample population illustrates the title-gender restriction.

The title-gender restriction can't be expressed in UML if we model personTitle as an attribute. However, it can be modeled in UML if we model PersonTitle as a class and attach a note for the restriction, as shown in Fig. 10.5(d). The note attached to the Employee class captures the title-gender restriction formally in OCL.
