Similar comments apply to objectification of an n-ary (ternary or longer) fact type with multiple UC spanning n-1 roles. For example, the ternary association in Fig. 10.53(a) has overlapping uniqueness constraints, each spanning two roles. The constraint on the first two roles indicates that within each sport, each athlete has at most one current rank. The constraint on the last two roles indicates that for each sport, each rank is assigned to at most one athlete (no ties are allowed).

For each current ranking of an athlete in a given sport, we record the date on which this ranking was awarded, and optionally the achievements on which this award was based. Fig. 10.53(b) depicts the same business domain in UML, where the ternary association is objectified as an association class, the award date is modeled as an attribute, and achievements are modeled via an association.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_476_1.png)

Once you objectify the ternary, the NORMA tool requires one of the two UCs to be preferred, and displays the preferred UC with a double uniqueness bar. Here, SportRanking is primarily identified by the athlete, sport combination, but NORMA requires the athlete, sport, and rank to identify each sport ranking so the fact entry is not atomic. To avoid confusion and nonatomic fact entries, we prefer to model such cases objectification of a ternary with a nonspanning UC, using one of the approaches shown in Fig. 10.54.

Now consider objectification of an n:1 fact type with multiple roles played by the objectification. Suppose we need to record for each moon the planet it orbits and the period of that orbit. Optionally, we also record for one or both of the axes (minor and major) of that elliptical orbit.

![](img/1344db3eba3d550e6edb009556fcd95c_477_0.png)

Fig. 10.55(a) models this case in ORM by treating MoonOrbit as an objectification of the Moon orbits Planet association. Fig. 10.55(b) shows an equivalent model in UML.

If you enter the ORM schema in Fig. 10.55(a) in NORMA, it internally creates implicit fact types linking MoonOrbit to Moon and Planet, using the 1:1 link fact type MoonOrbit involves Moon as the preferred identification scheme for MoonOrbit. There is no need to drag the link fact types onto the drawing window. Although a moon orbit is identified by just its moon, populating the period and axis fact types requires both the moon and planet to identify each moon orbit.

Fig. 10.55(c) shows the relational schema view NORMA generates from the ORM model in Fig. 10.55(a). The moonName primary key clearly shows that NORMA identifies a moon's orbit just by the moon. Personally, we do not like the practice of hiding the preferred identification scheme at the conceptual level, and we prefer to model such cases in ORM without objectification, using an alternative schema such as that shown in Fig. 10.55(c) or (d).

However, some users might still prefer the objectification approach over such alternatives. Objectification of a binary or longer fact with just one UC spanning all but one role where the objectification plays other multiple roles is the only kind of case where one might possibly have a legitimate reason to ignore our advice to never objectify an association without a spanning UC.

So far, we have discussed objectification in the sense of situational nominalization, where the object being discussed is a state of affairs (event, activity, etc.). The OMG's SBVR specification includes support for propositional nominalization. One of the SBVR rule examples involving propositional nominalization may be verbalized as follows: If a waiter earns an amount of money as a tip from serving a meal, the waiter must report that fact.

While one may interpret this as a case of propositional nominalization (reporting the fact rather than the act), there is no need to do so for information modeling purposes, as the rule may easily be declared using situational nominalization (reporting the act rather than the fact), as shown in compact form in Fig. 10.56. The equality constraint is between the three roles numbered 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 and the three roles numbered 2.1, 2.2, 2.3.

If the rule is modified to require reporting after the service is performed, a time limit for reporting must be declared to make the rule operational. In this case, the relevant temporal object type may be added to the model to cater for the extended rule in an obvious way. For simplicity, we recommend modeling all propositional nominalizations instead by their corresponding situational nominalizations.

As regards modeling of communication acts, when it is of interest to model these acts, they are best modeled directly like any other business domain objects. For example, in a genealogy model we might be interested in not just descriptions of states of affairs, but assertion acts made by researchers about states of affairs. Such a model might include fact types such as AssertionAct reported Proposition, AssertionAct was made by Researcher with ConfidenceLevel, etc. These comments relate to the information model only.

For modeling communication processes, the information model should be supplemented by other kinds of models (e.g., workflow models) that provide a more intuitive and direct way of understanding essential business processes/services.

## 10.6 Open/Closed World Semantics

When modeling a business domain, one may take different positions with respect to the completeness of the knowledge captured in the model. The *closed world assumption* (CWA) is the assumption that all relevant facts are known (i.e., appear in the model, either as asserted facts or derived facts) and that all the relevant business rules are known. This is the usual assumption for databases and implies *negation as failure* (the failure to find or prove a proposition implies that it is false).

Consider, for example, the populated ORM model in Fig. 10.57(a). There are four students (1001–1004), but only two of these (1001 and 1003) are modeled as intelligent. Using CWA, we may deduce that students 1002 and 1004 are not intelligent. Similarly, we may deduce that students 1003 and 1004 do not work hard. Using the asserted facts and the derivation rule shown, we infer that three students (1001, 1002, and 1003) are expected to pass. If there are no other derivation rules for the fact type Student is expected to pass, the **if** operator is interpreted under CWA to mean **iff**. Hence, student 1004 is not expected to pass.
