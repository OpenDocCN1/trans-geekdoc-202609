In this analysis, a single cyclic of voltammogram was analyzed as gray-scale image (Figure 1b), and a DNN was trained based on simulated voltammograms with about 90% accuracy for the classification of three mechanisms in molecular electrochemistry: a single-electron quasi-reversible charge transfer (E mechanism), a EC mechanism in which the E step is followed by a reversible C step, and a EE mechanism with two consecutive E steps [8]. A follow-up work from the same group expands the technique to three consecutive cycles of voltammograms, taking advantage of the three RGB (red, blue, green) input channels for the DNN originally designed for the analysis of colored images [17]. The output of such a pioneering ML-based algorithm is a deterministic assignment of reaction mechanism.

Instead of deploying pixelated images as the data input for ML analysis, Liu and coworkers defined a three-dimensional tensor {v, i(E)}n as ML model's input that numerically includes i, E, and scan rate (v) (Figure 1c) [18]. It is hypothesized that such information-rich data format is more suitable for electrochemical analysis in comparison with the pixelated images whose white space contains little information. The tensor data format can accommodate infinite number of voltammograms at various scan rates (v), and it is compatible with the current research practice that mechanistic information is extracted by comparing the evolution of voltammograms at different v values in the context of the kinetic zone diagram [8]. A residual neural network (ResNet) based on such a tenor input with up to six voltammograms under different v values is trained and established by simulated voltammograms, yielding an accuracy of 98.5% for five different molecular mechanisms (Figure 1c). In each analysis, the ResNet model not only studies the mechanism but also yields an “attention” plot illustrating which part of the voltammogram traces weights more in order to draw a conclusion. One feature here is that the yielded output is not merely a dichotomic classification, yet a quantitative distribution of propensity as a surrogate of statistical probability toward each of the considered mechanisms. While such propensity distribution has yet to be correlated to the distribution statistical probabilities with rigorous testing/validation, the demonstrated capability of analyzing voltammograms and delivering the comparative likelihood of reaction mechanisms is commensurate with the nature of mechanistic ambiguity encountered in molecular electrochemistry.

# Potential areas of development of ML-assisted mechanistic analysis

The above examples demonstrated some encouraging capabilities that ML can potentially contribute toward mechanistic analysis. Thus, the questions

come to which area of electroanalysis might need such ML-enabled capabilities. Below, we list and comment on some of the empirical challenges that the manual analysis has encountered for a variety of electrochemical methods [8,19], along with our thoughts on what a probability-driven ML-based analysis may offer.

Cyclic voltammetry has been the bread-and-butter technique for mechanistic analysis for decades [8,19]; yet, there remains at least one potential direction that the analysis and quantitative mechanistic studies can be improved (Figure 2a). Conventionally, a single hypothesized mechanism has to be deterministically decided before further numerical simulation and data fitting, despite the possible coexistence of competing mechanisms or the possible presence of multiple mechanistic explanations toward the same data within measurement and statistical uncertainties. Hence, the manual, deterministic approach of voltammogram analysis is not fully compatible with the aforementioned mechanistic ambiguity [1], especially when multiple competing mechanisms are present. After initial visual voltammogram inspection, a typical iterative routine of deciphering mechanisms in such scenarios without a priori knowledge includes heuristically hypothesizing a specific mechanism of E and/or C steps, numerically fitting the experimental voltammograms with the proposed mechanism, visual comparison to check if the fitting is satisfactory, and decision-making about whether another attempt with a revised proposed mechanism is necessary [20]. The nature of such protocol relies strongly on researcher's training in order to comprehensively consider all possible mechanistic variations and implicitly biases against the co-existence of multiple competing pathways. One classic example that epitomizes the challenges from such a methodology is the coexistence and competition between ECE (two E steps connected by an irreversible rate-limiting C step with the second E step being more thermodynamically facile than the first one) and DISP (the E step is followed by an irreversible C step with the product from the C step undergoing an disproportionation reaction) mechanisms for a net two-electron process in molecular electrochemistry [8]. There have been plentiful literature examples [21,22] to illustrate the difficulty of disentangling those two pathways in a deterministic manner. As the recently reported ResNet model is capable of analyzing voltammograms and yielding the propensity distribution of mechanisms including the ECE and DISP ones (Figure 1c) [18], it seems feasible that ML can improve mechanistic analysis in molecular electrochemistry in two different approaches: (1) a ML model can provide an estimated likelihood for each considered mechanism and guide the choice of proposed mechanisms in the existing iterative routine described above; (2) ML may establish an independent alternative route of mechanistic analysis that includes a report of mechanistic probability distribution enabled by ResNet
