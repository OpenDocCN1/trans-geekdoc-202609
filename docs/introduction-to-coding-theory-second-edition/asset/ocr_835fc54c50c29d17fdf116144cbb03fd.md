The problem we want to study is the largest size of a cap in projective space PG(k, q) and in affine space AG(k, q). For an introduction to PG(k, q), see the beginning of Section 17.1. The points of affine k-space AG(k, q) are the points outside a fixed hyperplane of PG(k, q). Represent the points of PG(k, q) by homogeneous coordinates (x1 : x2 : ⋯ : xk+1) and choose the hyperplane H defined by xk+1 = 0. The affine points, the points outside H, are the points (x1 : x2 : ⋯ : xk : 1). Each such point can be uniquely represented by a k-tuple: P = (x1, x2, …, xk). When considering linear dependencies, the hidden coordinate should not be forgotten. For example, three points P1 = (x1, x2, …, xk), P2 = (y1, y2, …, yk), P3 = (z1, z2, …, zk) of AG(k, q) are collinear if and only if there are coefficients λ1, λ2, λ3, not all = 0, such that

λ1 P1 + λ2 P2 + λ3 P3 = 0 and λ1 + λ2 + λ3 = 0.

The hyperplanes of AG(k, q) are the intersections with AG(k, q) of the hyperplanes ≠ H of PG(k, q).

In terms of codes, the largest number n of points of a cap in PG(k − 1, q) is the largest n such that an [n, n − k, 4]q code exists (see Proposition 17.28). Recall that case q = 2 is not interesting, as AG(k, 2) is a cap. In projective dimension 2 we know the answer to our problem. The ovals and hyperovals of PG(2, q) each avoid lines and are therefore contained in an affine plane AG(2, q). It follows that the largest size of a cap in PG(2, q) and in AG(2, q) is q + 1 for odd q and q + 2 for even q (see Theorem 17.4).

In PG(3, q) we have the elliptic quadrics, which form (q² + 1) caps (see Proposition 17.52).

# 17.54 Theorem. The maximum size of a cap in PG(3, q), q > 2 is q² + 1.

**PROOF** Let O ⊂ PG(3, q) be an n-cap, where n is maximal. Choose a secant g (a line meeting O in two points). Assume each of the q + 1 planes E through g meets O in at most q + 1 points. Then |O| ≤ 2 + (q + 1)(q − 1) ≤ q² + 1. This is the case in particular when q is odd. So assume q > 2 is a power of 2. We show first that every P ∈ O is on a tangent. Assume this is not the case. As every point is on q² + q + 1 lines, we obtain n = q² + q + 2. Also, every plane must intersect O in either 0 or q + 2 points. It is clear that there must be a line h which does not contain a point of O. The usual counting argument shows that q + 2 divides q² + q + 2. This leads to the contradiction q + 2 | 4. We have seen that tangents exist and that n < q² + q + 2. Let t be a tangent to O and t ∩ O = {P}. Consider the q + 1 planes through t. The existence of t shows that no such plane can meet O in q + 2 points. If they all contain at most q points of O we obtain n ≤ 1 + (q + 1)(q − 1) = q².
