# 3.5 MICROBIAL CELL FUEL (MFC)

Microbial cell fuel is a device that harnesses bacteria to directly transform organic substrates into electrical energy. This device is one of the emerging technologies that simultaneously removes organic matter in wastewater and generates power. MFCs have been used to treat low-strength wastewater with low footprint (Rittmann, 2008; Watanabe, 2008). MFC is made up of cathode, electrode medium and anode, microorganisms and proton exchange membrane (PEM). The microorganisms are found in the anode compartment of the MFC, and they utilize substrates Nicotinamide dinucleotide (NADH) transports these electrons (Choudhry et al., 2017). Furthermore, MFCs offer several advantages for treatment of wastewater such that no aeration is required, power is generated from pollutants, there is a possible reduction in waste sludge production and the growth of active microorganisms is promoted while treating wastewater. However, they have appreciable functional stability under ambient temperature for electron transfer, whereas anaerobic digesters do not have this problem since they effectively use the microbial community without cell immobilization (Pharm et al., 2006). Additionally, large contact area is required for biofilm to accumulate on the anode when MFCs are employed for wastewater treatment. Furthermore, low catalytic rate of the microorganisms is another disadvantage of using MFC (Du et al., 2007). Meanwhile, the anaerobic environment in the MFC anodic cathode does not allow for efficient nutrient removal. As a result, an MFC that can quickly aid in nutrient uptake is promising for future development (Choudhry et al., 2017). Previous research has shown that MFC is effective for removing organic materials from within a suitable retention time. Ishii et al. (2008) showed that a mixed culture from sludge could produce 22% more energy from Geobacter sulfurducens in culture in a single-chamber MFC with an air cathode. Pure culture is more effective than mixed culture in understanding the mechanics of electricity production and developing strategies to improve process parameters, while mixed culture is better than pure culture in wastewater treatment because they utilize different type of substrates (Chaudhuri & Lovley, 2003). Furthermore, the bacteria utilized in MFC should be electrochemically active and capable of withstanding the reaction temperatures (Rabaey & Verstraete, 2005). Other bacteria including Desulfobulbus propionicus, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Rhodopseudomonas demonstrate prospect for power generation in MFCs (Sharma & Kundu, 2010). Future study in this area should focus on screening, microbial identification and their capacity for wastewater treatment and bioenergy generation.
