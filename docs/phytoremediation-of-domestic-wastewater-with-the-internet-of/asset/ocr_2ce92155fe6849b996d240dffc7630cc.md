In addition, 6 hours retention time, the overall average phosphate concentration of 3.97 mg/L (*P. striatiotes*), 4.29 mg/L (*S. molesta*) and 3.65 mg/L (*E. crassipes*) was observed against the average influent phosphate concentration of 5.08 mg/L, while at 12 hours retention time, the overall average phosphate concentration of 4.38 mg/L (*P. striatiotes*), 4.11 mg/L (*S. molesta*) and 2.55 mg/L (*E. crassipes*) was recorded against the average influent phosphate level of 5.50 mg/L. Similarly, the overall average phosphate values of 4.46 mg/L (*P. striatiotes*), 5.45 mg/L (*S. molesta*) and 2.92 mg/L (*E. crassipes*) were observed at 24 hours retention time the average influent phosphate level of 11.01 mg/L. These outcomes demonstrated that the selected plants reduced the phosphate content in the influent samples at varying degrees. The natural differences in macronutrient needs for physiological mechanisms may have led to the alterations demonstrated by the three plants (Tisdale et al., 1993). Furthermore, *E. crassipes* was more effective than *P. striatiotes* and *S. molesta* plants in absorbing the excess phosphate present in the same samples. The results of the current research showed that the 6 and 12 hours treatment system did not provide favourable conditions for the plant roots to effectively absorb and assimilate the phosphate compounds. Hence, it is from these findings that retention time is a crucial factor that influences and regulates the rate of nutrient uptake and assimilation by plants in phytoremediation systems.

Furthermore, plants require phosphorus as a macronutrient. It is a major component of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP), both of which are required for energy storage.
