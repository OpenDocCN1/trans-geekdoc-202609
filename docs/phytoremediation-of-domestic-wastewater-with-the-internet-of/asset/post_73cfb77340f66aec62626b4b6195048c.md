## 4.5.3 Eichhornia CRASSIPES PLANTS

Eichhornia crassipes (Mart.) Solms also called water hyacinth is a notorious aquatic plant to the Amazon Basin of South America. The plant belongs to the pickerweed family (Pondertiaceae) and is considered one of the worst weed plants on the planet (Woldichael et al., 2011). Water hyacinth has been used in the bioaccumulation of pollutants by BOD, COD, phenols, pesticides, minerals, heavy metals and radioactive elements in phytoremediation (Khare & Lal, 2017).

### 4.5.3.1 Distribution of E. crassipes

E. crassipes are among the most distributed macrophytes in the world. They are usually found between 38°N and 38°S and were first identified as ornamental plant in the USA, South East Asia and South Africa in the 19th century. The weed plant believed to spread easily in ponds and lakes of different parts of the world because of its attractive lavender flowers. Additionally, in 1988 E. crassipes was first discovered in Lake Kyoga (Uganda) in the zones circumscribing Lake Victoria. The plants were able to survive and grow in Lake Victoria because of the favourable habitat and absence of physical disturbance such as pests, fish and other biota (Gichuki et al., 2012).

### 4.5.3.2 Taxonomy of E. crassipes

E. crassipes belongs to the Pondertiaceae family, a taxonomically complicated family that was reclassified as a member of the Comminales. E. crassipes is the only pantropical weed plant that has been identified. Except for Eichhornia natans (P. Beauv.), this family of mostly tropical freshwater aquatics has eight additional genera and species that originates from South America (Coetzee et al., 2009). ### 4.5.3.3 Description of E. crassipes

E. crassipes plants are composed of stalked rosette leaves, feathery roots, slender root stocks and flowers arranged in clusters of leaf axils. The leaves are broadly circular, gently curved, often undulating sides and have a diameter and height of 10–20cm and 0.5–1 m, respectively. Additionally, the leaves are dark green in colour and are borne on inflated petioles resembling bladders (Ecoport, 2011). The leaf veins are numerous, thick, longitudinal and densely packed. As the axial bud develops, leaves arise by rupturing a tabular leaf-like structure called a prophyll. Furthermore, the flowers have six petals with purplish-blue colour and yellow uppermost (Ecoport, 2011). The diagram of the plants displaying the flowers is presented in Figure 4.4.

### 4.5.3.4 Potentials of E. crassipes Plants in Phytoremediation of Wastewater 

E. crassipes has a high potential for phytoremediation of wastewater through biological, chemical and physical processes (Mayo & Hanai, 2017). The plants have been used in phytoremediation of wastewater due to their ability to trap and degrade pollutants in wastewater. Nurmini and Felani (2015) studied the phytoremediation of liquid waste from tapioca industry using E. crassipes plants for 28 days. The study only considered seven WQ parameters. Faisal and Zhang (2015) investigated the potentials of E. crassipes in the treatment of industrial effluent. The findings demonstrated up to 92.5% 83.7% COD, 91.8% TSS, 62.3% TDS, 80% TS, 71.6% NH₄-N, 67.5% NO₃, 90.2% PO₃, 9.7% Cs, 95.1% Ni, 99.9% Hg and 83.4% Pb reduction. Additionally, Qin et al. (2016) investigated the phytoremediation of domestic wastewater using water hyacinth and water lettuce. The result of the study indicated that E. crassipes obtained a total of 58.64% accumulating capacity for nitrogen. This result can be attributed to the leaf area and higher root activity (71,79–98.34 µ/g/h), root biomass (kg/m²), net photosynthetic rate (20.2 µmol/m²/S), active absorption area (0.31–0.36/m² fresh weight) and large root surface (0.97–1.10 mg/m²) fresh weight. Khare and Lal (2017) studied the potentials of E. crassipes in sewage treatment seven-week constructed wetland system of 5×4 ft×2.5 ft dimension to investigate the potentials. The physicochemical tests were restricted to turbidity TDS, BOD, DO, EC, COD, pH, K, and nitrogen. Goswami and Das (2018) E. crassipes plants in synthetic wastewater containing different concentrations of Cu in Hoagland solution. The findings of the research indicated up to 55%–57% Cu reduction efficiency was obtained from the initial concentration of 5–10 mg/L Cu after 21 days. In addition, Nash et al. (2019) estimated the survival level and nutrient uptake of E. crassipes in Sago mill effluent (SME). The experiment was carried out in glass aquariums of 30cm (length)×30cm (width)×30cm (depth) under controlled conditions for 30 days.

Furthermore, Safauldeen et al. (2019) used varying quantities of E. crassipes (8, 10 and 12 clumps) and retention times in phytoremediation of batik wastewater. Additionally, Sayago (2019) designed a sustainable method for phytoremediation of wastewater and generation of bioethanol using E. crassipes. In the study, the harvested E. crassipes biomass was loaded into bioreactors for bioethanol generation. The study proved the dual applications of E. crassipes for phytoremediation of wastewater and generation of biofuel using hydrolysis process. Parwin and Paul (2019) employed E. crassipes in the treatment of K for a 4-week period. Water quality parameters including turbidity, total hardness, NO₂-N, TDS, NH₄-N, K+, PO³⁻-P, EC, sulfate and bicarbonate were analyzed. Although the study evaluated the reduction efficiency and concentration factor, retention time and the estimated quantity of the plants used in the research were not mentioned. Besides, it is important to understand the nutrient assimilation, nutrient storage.
