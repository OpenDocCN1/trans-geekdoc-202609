# Preface

In 2007, the mysterious and anonymous Satoshi Nakamoto developed bitcoin, authored the bitcoin white paper, and created and deployed bitcoin’s original reference implementation. Who is this person or institution that also devised the first blockchain database? How do we know Nakomoto isn’t a country? The release of Bitcoin and blockchain by Nakamoto remains as sinister as his disappearance in December 2010. Estimates put the value of the bitcoin he left behind, and still unclaimed, at $56 billion as of November 2021.

According to Tapscott Group CEO Don Tapscott, blockchains, the cryptocurrency’s technology, could revolutionize the world economy. Blockchain technology is a distributed decentralized ledger system that offers transparency, immutability, and security. Everybody has complete trust in the blockchain database, because it’s not governed by any single organization, meaning it’s open. You can transact directly to its receiver with blockchain technology, which includes any third party. Blockchain will change the world by getting rid of centralized control. No central authority can control your assets anymore. The blockchain size of the blockchain market is projected to grow from$4.9 billion in 2021 to $67.4 billion by 2026 at a compound annual growth rate of 68.4 percent.

Bitcoin has been suggested as an attractive medium for international remittances because of the borderless nature of cryptocurrency. Since anyone can use the blockchain, there’s no need to send payments through a bank or centralized money transfer institution like Western Union or MoneyGram. Many people around the world regularly send money to their friends and family abroad. Blockchain advocates say that Bitcoin can facilitate remittances by removing intermediaries and reducing the costs of overseas payments. In 2019, the World Bank projected that the remittances industry was worth $540 billion.

Blockchain technology has ushered in a powerful new version of the Internet referred to as Web 3.0. Web 3.0 arises out of frustration with how a tiny number of powerful trillion-dollar corporations practically control the data and services on the Internet today.

One evening while watching the TV show *Money Heist,* I suddenly realized how much we, the masses, accept the government’s role in creating and governing money without question. In the episode I watched, a selfdescribed “bunch of losers” breaks into the Royal Mint of Spain, seizing hostages with one ultimate goal: to stay in the mint for as long as possible and print as many unmarked euros as they could, in effect minting their own money. *Money Heist* deftly showcases how, although we take the objective reality of money for granted, it is basically a psychological illusion.

I wrote this book because I was inspired by the potential for blockchain to wrest power from a tiny centralized minority, be it big corporations or governments, and more equitably distribute the governance of monetary currency across the masses. As I researched the material for this book, I became aware of communities worldwide that have decided to start printing or minting their currencies as an alternative for trading to government-controlled fiat currencies. These communities challenged the notion that a currency was only valuable if created and governed by the government. One example of an alternative currency is the Brixtonpound (B£), now exchanging against the pound sterling and Bitcoin. Another alternative currency I recently learned about is Akoin, a cryptocurrency developed by hip-hop artist Akon, who has raised $6 billion to build a futuristic city in Senegal.

Cryptocurrencies are not just for communities. Third-world countries realize the advantages of establishing an alternative to fiat. For example, El Salvador recently announced that they accept Bitcoin as legal tender. Bitcoin adoption could decrease the risk of current and future governments imposing policies that might affect the freedom of movement of capital and value of assets, stocks, and other financial mechanisms used within and outside of local economies.

Other countries are also getting in on the act. China is releasing its digital yuan, which some analysts see as a move to displace the U.S. dollar as the world’s reserve currency. In addition, in June 2021, China and Russia announced a joint pledge against dollar hegemony. If these superpowers succeed in wrestling free from the authority of the dollar, it could have profound implications for the global economy and even world peace. Bear in mind that in June 2021, Russia stated it would ditch all U.S. dollar assets, worth $40 billion, in its National Wealth Fund and increase holdings in euros, Chinese yuan, and gold.

Throughout history, currency morphed from being objectively valuable (when tied to the gold standard) to essentially becoming a social construct. I delved deep into trade history—from bartering to the standardization of metal coins to establishing a currency by government- controlled fiat and finally to modern-day cryptocurrencies.

The year 2021 saw a large section of society become involved in trading cryptocurrency. Blockchain-based currencies are not regulated today. As of January 2021, twenty-five individual Bitcoin addresses are holding more than a billion dollars, according to BitInfoCharts, and there are a handful of anonymous Bitcoin billionaires out there. Additionally, as of January 2021, there may now be as many as 100,000 Bitcoin millionaires. Others became millionaires by purchasing and holding $1,000of Dogecoin or $300 worth of the Shiba Inu token. However, the reality is that 97 percent of those trading cryptocurrencies lose money. I wrote this book as a guide to help those wanting to leverage this exciting new technology to trade it or to embarking on a blockchain-based career or business opportunity. I have dedicated chapters to each topic because leveraging blockchain technology requires technical expertise and access to capital that one can quickly lose.

Blockchain is superexciting and presents a host of potential opportunities. Even apart from cryptocurrencies, there is the exciting new world of decentralized finance (DeFi), which I will discuss in [chapter 13](#chapter13.xhtml). Over the last two years, the total money deposited at DeFi services has spiked from just $500 million to $247 billion. As a result, DeFi is emerging as a potent alternative and a disruptor of traditional centralized financial services for lending, borrowing, and investing.

Blockchain is also the technological platform for nonfungible tokens (NFTs). In 2021, NFTs exploded in popularity. An NFT is essentially a glorified title deed, but it has taken the digital art world by storm. The most expensive one so far is a digital collage of images by the artist who calls himself Beeple, that sold for $69.3 million at an auction at Christie’s in March 2021. In this book, I want not just to describe what an NFT is, but also to show the user how to create one.

If NFTs and DeFi were not enough, consider the impact of integrating blockchain with the metaverse. It is time to confront the notion that money in its current state is an appropriate tool for people’s social and fiscal interests.

# 01 Cryptocurrency and the Creation of Wealth

The goal of this chapter is to explore the potential for creating wealth using cryptocurrency as a vehicle. Toward this end, I will describe different approaches, including trading on a cryptocurrency exchange and the differences between short-term (day), swing, and long-term trading. We will look at different cryptocurrency exchanges, how to make your first trade, the different types of crypto trading, and what to expect while trading. Finally, I will categorize cryptocurrencies in terms of their utility versus their speculative value and propose varying approaches to each approach.

Cryptocurrency trading is a new and exciting method of financial investment that people worldwide are beginning to adopt. Often cryptocurrency is mentioned in the context of people making money seemingly out of thin air. Take the notorious Dogecoin, for example, a crypto coin named after an Internet meme. Dogecoin was started as a joke in 2013 as a way to poke fun at some of the wild speculation in cryptocurrencies. A thousand dollars’ worth of Dogecoin bought in April 2020 would have been worth $237,097 in April 2021.^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-1)) And consider the story of the New York brothers who turned $8,000 into $1 million by investing in the Shiba Inu coin,^([2](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-2)) a spinoff of Dogecoin.

There is also a flip side to making money in crypto. In 2017, the most established cryptocurrency, Bitcoin, dropped 45 percent in value during December, from $19,000 to below $11,000. By November of the following year, Bitcoin’s price was below $5,000. As a result of this crash, the entire cryptocurrency market dropped by 80 percent, a crash more severe than the dot-com bubble of the early 2000s.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-3))

On the surface, it looks as if investors in crypto are making and losing money virtually overnight. Is cryptocurrency trading anything more than just playing a lottery? Fortunately, the answer is yes. Cryptocurrency trading is complex, but with good preparation, technical analysis, and a great deal of luck, it can create immense wealth.

## Speculative Trading versus Utility Trading: What to Expect

The stories about Dogecoin and other similar coins often leave people scratching their heads, wondering what inherent value (if any) these coins may have. Why does $100 worth of Dogecoin have even that much value? Is it just a question of supply and demand? After all, it is argued, these cryptocurrencies have no utility and do not serve a purpose.

Dogecoin is an example of what I would call a lottery or speculative coin, (see [Figure 01-01](#chapter1.xhtml#fig1)). Here is a coin with no utility: its value rises and falls exclusively based on speculation. An investment of $500 in Dogecoin in 2016 would be worth over $1 million today. That said, a single Dogecoin as of December 29, 2021, was as low as 17 cents, but at its peak in April 2021, it was trading at 74 cents.

This is not to say one should refrain from investing in a currency that lacks utility. These coins derive value from the fact their price might go up. Thus they are potentially fantastic stores of wealth. Although it is easier to make money playing lottery coins likeDogeCoin or Shiba Inu than playing the actual lottery, these are lottery coins nonetheless. It is said that crypto trading has worse odds than gambling in Las Vegas. Long-term returns on blackjack are –0.28 percent (plus or minus 1.15 percent). For comparison, the long-term returns on Bitcoin are 60 percent, plus or minus 160 percent. This means that in trading Bitcoin, there is quite the chance that one will either have a big return on investment or will lose everything.^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-4)) One trading strategy might be to hold a basket of lottery coins for the long term, hoping that one might strike gold. It is important to note that about 100 investors own 65 percent of Dogecoin’s $46 billion market, and five individuals own 40 percent of it.^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-5))

Moreover, cryptocurrency is not yet regulated, so there are several “whales”; people with formidable technology tools and wealth who manipulate the prices of cryptocurrencies to their advantage. Their activities would be illegal in the stock market, which is regulated. This phenomenon is explored later in this chapter.

The more engaging and, some would say, more rational approach to crypto trading involves utility coins. As their name suggests, these cryptocurrency coins provide people with some form of utility.

To understand utility tokens, let us look at the properties of a blockchain. A blockchain is a network that has three key characteristics.

The first is that it is a *peer- to-peer network.* This means that any information sent by one entity to another goes straight to the recipient. This may not seem that impressive, but most information sent over the Internet goes through an intermediary, be it a bank, network provider, or services provider.

The second feature is that a blockchain is *decentralized.* This means that no one company or institution controls the network or its data.

The third characteristic of a blockchain is that is *immutable.* Once data has been transferred via the blockchain, it cannot be altered. As we have seen, some people utilize the blockchain by creating smart contracts on it. Whenever a smart contract requires currency, a utility token is used.

Therefore crypto trading with utility tokens is much more rational, because the technological usefulness of the smart contracts plays a major role. The most popular and established utility coins tend to utilize smart contracts and decentralized applications built on the blockchain. For example, certain utility tokens, such as Solara and Cardano, offer smart contract functionality.

The most notable smart contract blockchain is Ethereum. Founded by Vitalik Buterin in 2013, as of July 2021 this blockchain was processing over 1.2 million transactions a day, making it the largest smart contract blockchain in the world. Nonfungible tokens (NFTs), digital representations of unique artifacts or tokens, first came to life on Ethereum. Some have been sold for tens of millions of dollars. These are possibly the hottest commodities currently in the blockchain space.

The only nonutility coin that has the potential to be persistently valuable is Bitcoin. This is because Bitcoin was the first cryptocurrency. Although it is not tied to a smart contract infrastructure, it has a future in the sense that it will always be a collector’s item. Furthermore, the Bitcoin supply has been limited to 21 million coins, a number programmed into Bitcoin’s algorithm.

## Day Trading, Swing Trading, and Hodling

No matter what type of cryptocurrency you decide to trade, there are three forms of trading that you must understand to become a competent crypto trader (see [Figure 01-02](#chapter1.xhtml#fig1), and [Figure 01-03](#chapter1.xhtml#fig3)).

The first, and riskiest, is *day trading.* Day trading is a full- time job, and it can be extremely profitable. The issue with crypto day trading is that—just as with day trading in stocks—one must understand technical analysis and risk management and be quick-thinking. However, the crypto markets are ten times as volatile as the stock market.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-6)) This makes crypto day trading much riskier, but can create higher returns. In day trading, traders hold coins for a maximum of a few hours, so they must rely mainly on chart analysis instead of current events in the coin market. With every trade made using cryptocurrency, there is a commission paid to the exchange (this will be explored later in the chapter). For a day trader making multiple trades a day, there is a marginal loss in profit due to these commissions.

Despite the cons, day trading is a potentially profitable career that can be pursued anywhere, but, again, it is highly risky. It can take years to master, but as with any lucrative career, the effort may yield dividends.

The second form of trading is *swing trading.* This involves waiting for a swing in the market before buying or selling coins. Swings come in intervals of days to weeks, which means only a few hours out of the day are required to focus on swing-trading crypto. For those with full-time jobs, swing trading is a more realistic alternative to day trading. Making trades off the price swings caused by volatility is what defines swing trading. This means there is still some risk involved, although far less than with day trading. With swing trading also comes a human inclination to sell based on short-term gains. As a result, swing traders occasionally miss out on potential long-term gains.

Regardless, swing trading is a better path into cryptocurrency trading for those with existing full-time careers. While cryptocurrency is undoubtedly an exciting new space, those who are successful in trading are highly technical, have very liquid assets, and are also extremely lucky. Typically, those who were early adopters of coins (with a lot of luck) tended to be the big winners. A famous example is the Winklevoss twins, who invested $11,000 in Bitcoin in 2011 and by 2017 saw their investment grow to over $1 billion. That investment was extremely risky but paid off unbelievably.

Finally, there is *hodling,* an acronym for *holding on for dear life* that was first conceived in 2013 from a misspelling of the word *holding.* An Internet crypto trader was explaining in a forum post how people are likely to lose money in the crypto market if they lacked the expertise of a day trader or the time to do dedicated research. The trader had chosen to hold their money in the market long enough to see steady growth over time. The misspelling soon earned its own meaning: “holding on for dear life.”

Hodling is the most hands-off approach to crypto trading. After proper research into which crypto to invest in, one simply buys a substantial portion and allows time to increase its value. While day and swing trading are usually done with altcoins, which tend to have more volatility and therefore offer more opportunities to capitalize on, hodling is best done with utility coins such as Bitcoin or Ethereum. These cryptocurrencies have the best long-term investment potential because they are the best-known, and anyone getting into crypto or already in, ought to have a some of these coins in their portfolio. Hodling is also much less time consuming, much less arduous, and much lighter in terms of paying commissions.

Despite these positives, there are obviously potential gains that are missed, and it requires a lot of patience to achieve satisfactory returns.

## Steps to Making the First Investment

To get started with cryptocurrency trading, one must register with an exchange in order to purchase coins. ## Looking at the Charts

No matter the avenue chosen for trading, ample research must be done on the cryptocurrencies that one is thinking of adding to one’s portfolio. To make a well-informed trade, examining predictor charts on a specific currency is important ([Figure 01-04](#chapter1.xhtml#fig4)). Before exploring the topic of analysis, it must be stated that financial markets are extremely unpredictable. A professor at Princeton University conducted a study in which monkeys were blindfolded and taught to throw darts. The monkeys would throw darts at a random array of stock visualizations. The monkeys’ “portfolio” outperformed index funds created by professional investors 98 percent of the time.^([12](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-12)) The professors repeated these processes every year, from 1964 to 2010, and tracked the results. The process replicated 100 monkeys throwing darts at the stock pages each year. Amazingly, on average, 98 of the 100 monkey portfolios beat the 1,000 stock capitalization weightedstock universe each year. This is simply to say that while technical analysis should not be ignored, the data are purely an aid to prediction. No amount of research can accurately predict which choice would be successful.

The most common measurements are the *simple moving average* (SMA) and the *exponential moving average* (EMA), see [Figure 01-05](#chapter1.xhtml#fig2), and [Figure 01-06](#chapter1.xhtml#fig3). These measurements are the average of a currency’s price over a given period. The shorter the time period, the more reactive the SMA is. This measurement is used to predict whether the price of the currency is trending up or down.

To put it plainly, the simple moving average smooths out the volatility and allows the trader to make better decisions. The second, more complex indicator is the exponential moving average, which places a greater emphasis on recent price points. Some economists believe that the market is self- correcting. Therefore, recent prices can help predict an asset’s future pricing. Using this logic, it is thought that calculating the SMA with older price points is simply unnecessary. For hodl traders, the SMA is a useful tool because they are making decisions for the long term.

The EMA is calculated by simply adding a multiplier to the more recent prices. This makes the EMA much more reactive to recent shifts. The way traders use these averages is relatively straightforward: When the price of a given currency is below the moving average, it is trending down. When it is above the moving average, it’s trending up. One issue regarding the EMA is that sometimes a small price change will be exacerbated by the EMA formula, and the trader will get spooked and miss out on future gains. The SMA is less reactive to shifts and would not create an issue like that.^([13](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-13)) No matter the type of crypto trading pursued, understanding SMA and EMA is essential to becoming skilled and profitable.

## How Whales Cause Volatility and Why It Matters

Volatility has been mentioned a few times in this chapter, and it is not to be underestimated. Volatility in the crypto world refers to the market’s unpredictability and its propensity to change rapidly. The main culprits responsible for the volatility in crypto markets are whales: individuals or institutions that own a large quantity of a certain currency. Their actions can have major impacts on the price of the currency and the wallets of individual traders. There is speculation that roughly 1,000 people own 40 percent of the Bitcoin supply.^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-14)) A few hundred individuals could tank the market by selling only half their share. Unlike traders in the stock market, whales in the crypto space are not monitored by the SEC.

Whales manipulate the market in three ways. The most common is *pump and dump,* see [Figure 01-07](#chapter1.xhtml#fig4). A whale buys a large quantity of a coin, which spikes the price. Then individual traders who are eager to capitalize on this price rise start buying. This causes a second spike, and the whale sells at that point, making a profit off the demand he has caused.

![Figure 01-07. How Whales Pump and Dump.](images/img-34.jpg)

Figure 01-07. How Whales Pump and Dump.

The second tactic is called a *sell wall,* see [Figure 01-08](#chapter1.xhtml#fig5). A whale will place an exceptionally large sell order at below market price. Because of the size of the order, individual investors cannot sell above the whale’s order price, which causes the price of the currency to drop. This price drop alarms individual investors, and they sell their shares. The whale then swoops in and collects additional coins at a much lower price.

![Figure 01-08](images/img-35.jpg)

Figure 01-08

The third manipulation tactic whales use is *dark pools.* Whales will trade crypto among themselves, thus not affecting the public market. This allows whales to accrue substantial amounts of crypto and then execute something like a sell wall.^([15](#notes.xhtml#chapter1-15))

## Cryptocurrency Mining: Why It Matters and Where It’s Going

Typically, cryptocurrency and blockchain operate on a decentralized ledger. No single computer system controls the production or data storage of the currency. In theory this prevents the blockchain from being hacked or any entity from controlling or manipulating cryptocurrencies. The problem with this model is in the production of the computers that mine cryptocurrency. These computers also house the blockchain (see Figure [02-07](#chapter2.xhtml#fig12), next page). There are two types of computers, general-purpose ones, like Windows computers or Apple Macbooks, and computers designed to run just one specific application. The latter are known as application-specific integrated circuits or ASICs. They are extremely efficient at performing one task.

As is common in capitalism, one company has established itself as the best manufacturer of these ASICs in terms of cryptocurrency: the Chinese Bitcoin mining giant Bitmain. This circumstance has centralized the distribution of blockchain technology, potentially threatening the blockchain’s integrity. It is feared that Bitmain may have a monopoly on the production of Bitcoin. As previously discussed, theimmutability of blockchain technology is only threatened if one entity controls 51 percent of the ledger. In an ideal decentralized system, mining computers would be operated independently, and it would be almost impossible to hack more than half of the computers operating the blockchain.

![Figure 02-07. Cryptocurrency Mining.](images/img-52.jpg)

Figure 02-07. Cryptocurrency Mining.

In Rockdale, Texas, a town of 5,600 people, two of the biggest names in Bitcoin mining are battling it out for market share and cheap electricity. Bitdeer—a firm spun off from Bitmain—is four-tenths of a mile down the road from Riot Blockchain, one of the biggest publicly traded Bitcoin mining companies in America.

Another major issue with Bitcoin is its energy consumption. In February 2021 Elon Musk’s company Tesla began accepting Bitcoin as payment. A few months later, Musk announced that Tesla would no longer accept Bitcoin as payment, because of the massive amounts of energy used in its production (see [Figure 02-08](#chapter2.xhtml#fig13)). This ties back to the ASICs, as the operation of these computers is very energy intensive, and theyoperate largely on fossil fuels. These computers consume more energy in a year than the country of Sweden.^([21](#notes.xhtml#chapter2-21)) Moving the energy consumption of Bitcoin to climate-friendly fuel is difficult because of the decentralized nature of Bitcoin; it’s impossible to mandate green energy when there is no governing power for Bitcoin operation.^([22](#notes.xhtml#chapter2-22))

![Figure 02-08. Bitcoin Consumption Comparison by Country.](images/img-53.jpg)

Figure 02-08. Bitcoin Consumption Comparison by Country.

Both of these problems can be solved by a system known as proof of stake. The original model of Bitcoin is known as proof of work (POW), which requires huge amounts of energy consumption to fuel computational power (see [Figure 02-09](#chapter2.xhtml#fig14), next page).

Proof of stake avoids the energy-intensive computation required by POW, and cryptocurrency miners can mine or validate block transactions based on the number of coins a miner holds. When an individual wants to make a transaction, the supplier and receiver enter a certain amount of currency into what can be visualized as an escrow. Separate users unrelated to the transaction also enter their stakes, and their computers verify each block in the blockchain. If an error appears, it means that someone was attempting something fraudulent, and the network then distributes that user’s stake.

## The Brixton Pound

In September 2009, economists in the United Kingdom made a liquidity injection into Brixton, South London. They created a currency designed to support the local community. The Bank of England does not control the Brixton pound, which is transmitted by text from a cellphone. The Brixton pound (B£) is an additional form of payment for people who do not have a credit or debit card or do not want to get involved with banks. There are around 500,000 Brixton pounds in circulation, and over 200 businesses across Brixton accept it.^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-14))

Brixton is a significant town center with an international reputation based on its markets, cultural and creative energy, and diversity. According to the Brixton pound website, the physical, paper-based currency was followed in September 2011 by the launch of an electronic pay- by- text platform, which has since been retired.

The mission of the Brixton pound was to support diversity and build resilience in the local Brixton economy, taking into consideration the challenging economic times and dominant chain of power. The Brixton pound further sought to raise community awareness of the local social economy and facilitate a self-help model to protect the financial future of Brixton’s residents. Another aim was to promote regional sourcing of goods to decrease carbon dioxide emissions and raise Brixton’s role locally and nationally as a trailblazer in financial technology (FinTech).

The currency, which has a fixed one-for-one exchange rate with the pound sterling, is accepted at over 200 local shops and businesses. It can even be used to pay local taxes.^([15](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-15))

*“*If you spend £5 on a cup of coffee in a chain store, the majority of that money just flies straight out to whoever owns the shares in the coffee chain,” said the Brixton pound director Charlie Waterhouse.^([16](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-16)) “If you spend 5 Brixton pounds with a local, independent coffee shop, that money stays circulating in the area. It’s about empowering people to be able to make positive, economic decisions in their everyday lives.”

The innate nature of Brixton pounds to circulate within the local community exemplifies how a community currency can be used to increase the community’s economic status, especially as regards small businesses. By challenging the control of money by the central authority, users of Brixton pounds determine the currency’s value, which in turn gains it more trust. Furthermore, the Brixton pound removes coercion as participants voluntarily decide whether or not to use it.

## The Bretton Woods Agreement

In 1944, during World War II, the Allied nations met in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire.^([26](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-26)) At this meeting, the Bretton Woods Agreement was written, whereby the Allied countries vowed that their central banks would fix exchange rates between their currencies and the dollar.^([27](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-27)) Shifting to the dollar was significant, because previously most nations were using the gold standard.^([28](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-28))

The gold standard guaranteed that a nation’s currency could be exchanged for its value in gold. Under the Bretton Woods Agreement, currencies could now be exchanged for dollars instead of gold. The dollar was chosen to be the world’s reserve currency largely because the United States held 75 percent of the world’s gold.^([29](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-29))

The main objective of the Bretton Woods Agreement was to avoid trade wars. If a nation’s currency grew weak compared to the dollar, the central bank would purchase all its currency in foreign exchange markets.^([30](#notes.xhtml#chapter4-30)) The flexibility provided by this agreement promoted stability in currency markets, since globally most currencies were now linked by a single standard, the dollar.

The Bretton Woods Agreement was successful because it prevented the International Monetary Fund (IMF) from becoming a global central bank. The agreement limited the IMF, which could only lend money to member countries in proportion to the member’s contribution. The IMF was not able to print money or establish monetary policies.

The Bretton Woods Agreement ended in 1971 when the United States was experiencing high inflation. President Richard Nixon attempted to deflate the dollar’s value in relation to gold. His plan backfired, however, as people responded by redeeming their dollars for gold. As a result, Nixon took the United States off the gold standard, and that was the end of Bretton Woods. The dollar did not collapse, because it remained the dominant reserve currency for the rest of the world.

The dollar continues to persevere because it has maintained its status as the world’s reserve currency. In the fourth quarter of 2019, it made up 60 percent of all foreign exchange reserves held abroad. The second most widely held currency is the euro, but it only makes up 20 percent of foreign exchange reserves. About 90 percent of foreign exchange trading depends on the dollar.

The dollar is also the currency used most for issuing debt. About 40 percent of all issued debt is in dollars. Some countries fear that debt owed to them in dollars will become worthless if the dollar experiences severe inflation. Inflation is possible if the United States either decides to print more money or further increase its deficit spending.

The dollar’s power makes it the most viable fiat to which to link cryptocurrencies. As such, the new cryptocurrency platform presented later in this book will be a security token offering directly fixed to the American dollar.

## The Origin of Money

Historical evidence demonstrates that the disadvantages of bartering served as an economic catalyst for the development of money, but by no means was this the only or most important factor. Glyn Davies, the author of *A History of Money from Ancient Times to the Present Day,* wrote that money originated primarily because of noneconomic causes.^([2](#notes.xhtml#chapter5-2)) For example, money was used by authorities to tax or collect tribute from their subjects. Together, these needs illustrate that money was derived to facilitate deeply rooted social, religious, and political customs.

## Morality as a Social Construct

To lay the foundation for the idea of the social construct, I first want to refer to Anglo-American common law, where the formation of a simple contract between parties generally requires an agreement, consideration, and intent by both parties to fulfill the agreement. Bear in mind that a simple contract can be oral.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter5-3))

*Consideration* is a concept of English common law and is a necessity for simple contracts; it is defined as a “right, interest, profit, benefit, forbearance, detriment, loss, or responsibility.”^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter5-4)) Consideration is essentially a promise that something of value is given by one party in exchange for something of value from another party. Typically, the “something of value” is a good, money, or service. This concept has been adopted by other common law jurisdictions, including the United States.

The social aspects of morality can be understood through the philosophical concept of intentionality, which is the way individuals interpret the world and pursue their goals. Morality governs the relationships between parties within a given society. It sets the terms of engagement needed to fulfill a mutually agreed- upon objective, which is to obtain net value.

A moral transaction, therefore, is a simple contract. The contract details terms of engagement: the rights and obligations of each party as they pertain to the exchange of value. However, just because a relationship is transactional does not mean that it is equitable.

## Individual Intentionality

Members of a group might seek the means for all to benefit through enacting norms to promote fairness, equity, and justice. These norms evolved over hundreds of thousands of years as humans began to cooperate out of a basic need for survival. But in times of scarcity, humans would compete for resources. Thomas Hobbes defined this competition as the “state of nature.”^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter5-5))

## Joint Intentionality

Over time, a new logic of interdependence or rational self-interest arose. This joint intentionality contrasts starkly with the selfishness of individual intentionality. With joint intentionality, all parties benefit. Through joint intentionality, bartering, a simple system of exchange, emerged.

Successful human cooperation relies on trust and on a reliance that parties to an agreement can depend on one another to follow through on their obligations and respect one another’s rights. Peace within a society also depends on the ability to trust others to respect property rights.

## Collective Intentionality

Eventually human societies developed a set of shared values, obligations, and precepts that together represented the formal beginnings of human cultures. Members of a group sought means for all to benefit through enacting norms to promote fairness, equity, and justice. This collective intentionality became institutionalized and internalized by each member of the society as an “objective morality” by which the group identified right and wrong.

## Social Exchange Theory

Social exchange theory, a concept based on the notion that relationships between two or more people are created through a form of cost-benefit analysis, explains why we build trust and reciprocity.

All relationships are transactional, and they may be unilaterally so. The primary commodity is the value being exchanged. The problem with so many of today’s relationships is that the terms of engagement are vague, nonexistent, or assumed. Vagueness in terms of engagement is akin to distrust eating away at the fabric of the relationship.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter5-6))

The central element in the economic problem of money is its objective exchange value. This is the necessary starting point of all discussion: it is only in connection with its objective exchange value that those peculiar properties of money that have differentiated it from commodities are conspicuous.

## Social Constructs

A social construct is an entity created by human consensus.^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter6-1)) Society or parties to a simple contract agree on its definition, meaning, and value. A social construct is objectively real within the context of a society where there is a consensus, which is what is meant by the phrase *social reality.* The construct is accepted as “fact” solely in the collective minds of the perceivers. Social constructs such as rights, morality, emotions, and currency are crucial for the smooth operation of human civilization.

It is not a fact that rights, emotions, and morality, as defined, exist independently of the social constructs that we have created and agreed on. It is not a fact that love, currency, and art have value outside of what we have bestowed on them. Yet we remain convinced that these constructs are objectively real and objectively valuable. Mostly unwittingly, we live our lives as if these constructs are real.

Neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett explains the process by which a social construct is created. There are two main ingredients: collective intentionality and words (concept identification). “Make something up, give it a name, and you’ve created a concept,” she writes. “Teach your concept to others, and as long as they agree, you’ve created something real.”^([2](#notes.xhtml#chapter6-2))

Words are necessary for creating a social construct because humans have language. Language is the concept identifier, that is, the word image used to label the concept. Words are the most efficient way to communicate a shared concept within a group. Words represent concepts, and concepts are tools of nature.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter6-3))

In regard to a contract, first, parties to a simple contract intentionally agree on its definition (concept), its meaning (mental representation), and its value. Together these components constitute its collective intentionality*.*

Currency is an example of a social construct. The existence of the paper or coin that represents money is not in dispute, but its definition, meaning, and value exist solely as a function of social reality. Unlesshumans agree on what the paper or the coins represent and can be used for, the paper money is just paper, and the coins are just metal disks.^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter6-4))

“Given a rectangle of paper with a dead leader’s face printed on it, or a metal disk or a shell or some barley, a group of people categorized that object as money, and it became money,” writes Barrett.^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter6-5))

# 07 The Exchange of Value:A Brief History of Trade

As we have seen, money is a social construct. Its value directly correlates to the ability of the community that uses it to trust it as a unit of value. Trust in today’s fiat is inherently trust in the government that backs and guarantees it. ## History of Trade and Bartering

Trade is the basis for most forms of economics. Historians trace the concept of bartering as a mechanism for the exchange of value to as far back as 6000 BC.^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-1)) Villagers and merchants would exchange a variety of goods such as salt, sugar, grain, cattle, weaponry, and textiles. Eventually bartering evolved to include the exchange of services.

A community often establishes bartering during a monetary collapse, crisis, or scarcity in order to continue trading goods and services. Bartering regained popularity in the United States during the colonial era of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the Great Depression, and the 1980s recession.^([2](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-2)) People established barter groups like the Unemployed Citizens League of Denver (34,000 members), and the National Development Association.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-3)) The International Reciprocal Trade Association reports that in 2011, over 400,000 companies worldwide used bartering to earn an estimated $12 billion on unwanted or underused assets.^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-4))

A key advantage of bartering is flexibility: not having to depend on money as a medium of exchange, the parties can obtain what they need by merely exchanging the goods and services that they already have.^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-5))

There are several disadvantages to the bartering system. First, there is a need for mutual trust between the parties to the transaction. Thus there may need to be a regulatory authority or trusted intermediary to enforce or arbitrate an agreement in the event of a conflict. Other disadvantages of goods and services include the lack of a standard unit of measure, indivisibility, storage limitations, and the difficulty in making deferred payments.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-6))

Bartering is growing worldwide, partly because of shortages of hard currency. Currently, eighty-eight countries barter regularly, while in 1973, only fifteen did so. Some analysts estimate that up to 30 percent of global trade now takes place in the form of bartering.^([7](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-7)) However, the lack of development of common bartering standards and mechanisms means that countries with currency shortages cannot quickly increase their trading.

## Blockchain Bartering

Blockchain creates a decentralized platform to support trade and address the typical disadvantages of conventional bartering. Cryptocurrency serves as a common measure of value for any barters that take place. The digital currency can be used from anywhere in the world, and the location independence promotes international trade.^([8](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-8))

In 2018, the Canadian online bartering platform Bunz reportedly became the first to launch, with an already-established community— its members. The platform uses its cryptocurrency, BTZ (pronounced “bits”), which can be exchanged for goods and services or traded between members. The platform has approximately 200,000 users. BTZ is also accepted instead of cash at hundreds of local retailers in Toronto, Ottawa, Montreal, and Vancouver.^([9](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-9))

## The Purpose of Currency

The idea of currency as an abstraction emerged from bartering as a right (power)—a right to acquire value and have a medium to exchange.

Currency emerged as a standard for deferred payments, measuring the value of goods and services. Money morphed from standardized coins, such as precious metals (simultaneously a store of value and an item to purchase value), to a token (token coins, fiat money) and a right (claim to value) controlled and guaranteed by a ruling authority. Eventually, trusted intermediaries, such as banks, emerged. At the same time, the ruling body managed the creation of money and monetary policies while providing a level of immunity to protect the assets of citizens using banking services.

## Development of Money

Literary and linguistic evidence from the ancient world and primitive money from many countries demonstrate that while the disadvantages of barter provided a purely economic impetus for the development of money, it was not the main factor, and it certainly was not the most important.

According to Glyn Davies, author of *A History of Money,* “Money originated very largely from non-economic causes: from tribute as well as from trade, from blood-money and bride money as well as from barter, from ceremonial and religious rites as well as from commerce, from ostentatious ornamentation as well as from acting as the common drudge between economic men.”^([10](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-10))

## Coins as the Most Common Form of Bartering

People became accustomed to using standardized coins, and eventually coins became authenticated by the ruling authorities. In the Middle Ages, coins became the general means for payment, and they carried a substantial premium over the value of their metallic content due to their convenience. Kings could turn this into a profit. Unfortunately, the kings who owned the mints sought to increase their margins by manipulating the quantity of precious metal in the coins, as well as the amount of the coins in circulation (recoinage).^([11](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-11))

Existing coins were always recycled to maximize profit. To prevent competition from earlier issues, the authorities had to be able to identify the new coins while making them readily acceptable to the public. The coins were recycled far more frequently than could be justified by wear and tear. Still, this minting practice, which became known as *seigniorage,* was a lucrative extra source of profits that supplemented the revenue raised by the English monarchs through taxation.^([12](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-12)) This practice debased the currency, reducing the value of the coins and thereby causing inflation. The currency transitioned from being a standard coin, whose face value was worth as much as the metal in it, to a token coin, whose face value was worth more than the metal it contained.^([13](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-13))

However, revenue from minting depended on public confidence in the coinage, and consequently an elaborate system of testing was introduced. “Anyone who had to handle coins of silver or gold in any volume, whether merchants, traders, tax collectors, the King himself, the royal treasury, or the sheriffs, required reliable devices for testing the purity of what passed for currency,”^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-14)) according to one source.

Thus, despite the challenge of counterfeiters, government was able to control coin production and hence the money supply. Not until the rise of commercial banking and the widespread adoption of paper money was this monopoly broken.

## Paper Money

Standardized coins were used during the Roman era, and official coins were minted and produced in varying denominations. Coins became a standard tool for making purchases, and the idea spread far and wide throughout the following centuries for making purchases. The first coin ever minted features a roaring lion.^([15](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-15))

In a massive leap toward the transition from an objective value to a value constructed as a function of social agreement, China introduced paper money around AD 600.^([16](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-16))

In Western Europe during the time of the Crusades in the twelfth century, written instructions in the form of bills of exchange came to be used to transfer large sums of money, with the Knights Templar functioning as bankers.^([17](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-17))

During the English Civil War of 1642–51, goldsmiths’ safes were secure places for the deposit of valuable assets such as jewelry, precious metals, and coins. Owners could then use the receipts from the goldsmiths to either withdraw their assets from the goldsmith or present them as evidence of the ability to pay.^([18](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-18)) The receipts effectively served as storage of real value, and eventually they become trusted as a currency. Around 1660, these receipts developed into banknotes. Instructions to goldsmiths to pay money to another customer became the basis for the bank check.

The goldsmiths began to lend out their depositors’ gold, keeping only a tiny fraction of the stock in reserves, ushering in the beginning offractional reserve banking (see [Figure 07-01](#chapter7.xhtml#fig18)). If all the depositors came to the goldsmith simultaneously to withdraw their gold, he would have been unable to make payment. In such a case the receipts would no longer be worth the value of the gold they represented, but only as much as the goldsmith was able to pay.

Unlike standardized coins, paper money has no intrinsic value, but initially it represented a “claim to value,” usually denominated in a precious metal like silver or gold. In Britain, paper money was initially backed by silver, but in 1816, the gold standard emerged.^([19](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-19))

Eventually, paper money became so popular that it became regulated by the governing authorities. In the United States, this fiat (unbacked) currency was printed freely by the federal government, certain states, and even individuals, such as Benjamin Franklin.^([20](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-20))

The power of fiat money depended directly on the ability of the ruling authority (the government) to guarantee its value. Trust in fiat effectively meant faith in the government that controlled its supply and guaranteed its value—in short, the nation’s stability and economic strength.

## Liquidity Injections

The Federal Reserve, the central bank of the United States, is probably the most influential economic institution in the world.

Imagine a scenario in which the U.S. Treasury prints $10 billion in new bills; the Federal Reserve conjures up a new dollar balance of $90 billion and credits it to the major banks. While the economy receives a monetary influx of $100 billion, that is only a tiny percentage of the actual money creation.

The real money creation takes place through the fractional reserve banking system, whereby the banks lend out those new balances to the broader economy. In this system, banks are required to maintain a legally mandated reserve ratio of between 3 and 10 percent, but they can lend out much more. The initial $100 billion in bank reserves could potentially result in a nominal monetary increase of $1 trillion. The credit markets essentially become a funnel for money distribution.

In March 2020, the Federal Reserve announced they were reducing the reserve requirement ratio to zero percent across all deposit tiers. This comes as the COVID-19 pandemic continues to affect much of the way financial institutions both operate and serve their customers.^([25](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-25))

Approximately 97 percent of the money is created when commercial banks extend loans. The phrase “banks create money” is a contentious and hotly debated topic in modern-day politics and economics, and has generated public anger.^([26](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-26)) Some analysts, however, insist that this is an erroneous representation of the bank’s role in the money creation process. Banks can also serve as intermediaries between buyers and sellers—for example, in the purchase of a house.

Today money exists mainly in the electronic bits and bytes of our online ledgers and transactions through credit cards, ATMs, etc. In 1946 the first credit card was introduced, and there has been a steady and growing interest in various forms of electronic money since the 1990s.^([27](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-27)) In some ways e-money is a logical evolution from the wire transfers that came about as a result of the widespread adoption of the telegraph in the nineteenth century.

## What Is Cryptocurrency?

A cryptocurrency is a digital currency that uses blockchain and cryptography technologies to process and verify transactions securely. A cryptocurrency can be used as a medium of exchange internationally and differs from a fiat currency in that no central bank or authority controls it.Instead, it is held in unique blockchain databases that no one can manipulate unless specific conditions are met.

## Cryptocurrency Advantages

Anyone can obtain a cryptocurrency, even though the current process can seem technically complicated. Nevertheless, it enables people across the globe to interact with one another through online transfers.

Fast settlement times are another critical feature associated with cryptocurrencies. Unlike in any existing electronic currency settlement system, currency settlements can occur instantly. Many cryptocurrencies have minimal transaction fees, which is appealing considering the high fees generally associated with wiring money internationally.

## Disadvantages of Cryptocurrency

The greatest obstacle for cryptocurrencies to overcome is the lack of knowledge surrounding their usage. As a result, financial regulators in an array of countries do not take the time to understand them. Instead, some, like China, have outlawed them entirely.^([28](#notes.xhtml#chapter7-28))

Cryptocurrency transactions cannot be reversed once executed, which some view as problematic. If a wrong deal is made, the only solution is to ask the receiver to refund it. The receiver can object to the refund request, which leaves the sender without recourse.

The value of cryptocurrencies fluctuates frequently, so that crypto coins have high volatility. This is a complicating factor in terms of both understanding and use.

The existing cryptocurrencies are not yet mature enough to replace regular payments with fiat money. The crypto market is still in its early days of development, and significant evolution in crypto is needed to create a financial system that can replace the current one. Most likely it will not come from any of the existing cryptocurrencies, but with the integra tion of policy and blockchain technology. But given the strong opposition to cryptocurrencies around the globe, it will likely take time before they enter the mainstream financial sector.

# 08 Blockchain Primers

Many people believe that 2008 was the year when Satoshi Nakamoto first conceived blockchain. His influential paper on Bitcoin is commonly thought of as the beginning of blockchain technology. However, the reality is that blockchain was first developed in 1991 by Stuart Haber and W. Scott Stornetta, when they attempted to create a way to timestamp digital documents.^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-1))

Haber and Stornetta invented what became known as the Byzantine Fault Tolerance Solution. It was first introduced in 1982 by Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak, and Marshall Pease.^([2](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-2))

The problem contains the following conditions:

A commanding general must send an order to his n-1 lieutenant generals such that

1.  All loyal lieutenants obey the same order.
2.  If the commanding general is loyal, then every loyal lieutenant obeys the order he sends.

The “n” in the problem refers to the number of lieutenant generals to whom the commanding general must send his order. The “1” indicatesthat the commanding general must send orders to 1 fewer than the number of lieutenant generals he currently has.

One issue presented in this problem is that the commanding general’s only way of communicating is with paper notes, which must be hand- delivered by a messenger. The messenger must cross through enemy territory on foot to deliver the paper order, with the possibility of being captured. If captured, the messenger can be turned into a traitor and forced to send incorrect orders to the lieutenants.

An additional complication is that even if the messenger is not compromised, one of the lieutenants could be. As a result, the commanding general must account for the possibility that one or more of his messengers or lieutenants is a traitor.

To counter this problem, the commanding general must implement a strategy to ensure that all his loyal lieutenants follow the same action plan, which would eliminate any threats from potential traitors. This solution is known as the Byzantine Fault Tolerance solution, or algorithm OM (0). Specifically, the algorithm states:

1.  The commander sends his value to every lieutenant.
2.  Each lieutenant uses the value he receives from the commander or uses the value RETREAT if he receives no values.

If the commander is the traitor, shown above, then a different outcome occurs. In this case, lieutenants 1, 2, and 3 all receive different messages of x, y, and z, respectively. Sharing such messages means that each lieutenant would receive a final message of “xyz.” This message does not have a majority, and thus all lieutenants act on the default option of retreat.

Both these cases outline how the Byzantine Fault Tolerance can prevent infiltrators from corrupting a messaging system by using a consensus protocol.

The Byzantine Fault Tolerance also opened the door to consensus algorithms, which were fundamental to blockchain’s creation to timestamp digital documents in 1991 by Haber and Stornetta. The system they created required that the actual bits of the document got time stamped, not only the document medium. Another requirement was that the data and timestamp could not be forged.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-3))

After establishing these conditions, they then proposed using oneway hash functions and digital signatures to process documents instead of the actual documents themselves. A hash is a mathematical function that converts an input of arbitrary length into an encrypted output of a fixed length. Thus regardless of the original amount of data or file size involved, its unique hash will always be the same size.^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-4)) This procedure meant that they would submit documents to be linked, and then certificates of the linkage would be distributed to other clients. The clients would then have to timestamp the hash too. Their mechanism proved useful, and consequently the world’s first blockchain came into being.

## A Brief Introduction to Bitcoin

Nakamoto further advanced the blockchain technology in 2008 when he released his white paper “Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System.” This paper utilized the principles established by Haber and Stornetta, demonstrating the influential role they played in the development of the new blockchain. Nakamoto’s blockchain was different in that it was a purely peer-to-peer network that would “allow online payments via electronic cash to be sent directly from one party to another without going through a financial institution.”^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-5))

“Peer-to-peer network” refers to various computers on a network, each considered to be a node. Each node then corresponds to a specific user that has two unique keys.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-6)) The first key is called a *public key.* This key is available for anyone to see on the public ledger. The second key is a *private key:* only the user knows their own private key, which functions asa password for them to authorize transactions. Users also have tokens on peer-to-peer networks, which can be any type of data (but are commonly presented as a type of cryptocurrency).

Given these components, payment can be sent by the following mechanism: First, a user enters the public key of a recipient whom they would like to receive a token and then enters their private key, which executes the transaction. The transaction is not complete at this time; instead, it is only broadcast to the network. Once on the network, miners compete to verify the transaction occurred so it can officially be added to the ledger in a block. Only after the network has verified the transaction does the transaction truly occur^([7](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-7))

The competition for verifying transactions is perhaps the most innovative part of Nakamoto’s blockchain, as it relies on the incorporation of the proof of work concept as a consensus algorithm.

Proof of work was first created in 1993 by Cynthia Dwork and Moni Naor (though it was not named so until 1999).^([8](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-8)) It was developed to be used as a way to block network spam and denial of service attacks. In its simple definition, proof of work is merely an algorithm that functions by users executing the computation of some cryptographic problem to find a solution. Once the solution is obtained, it can be verified by all other users and added to its corresponding blockchain.^([9](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-9))

Bitcoin uses a specific type of proof of work algorithm developed in 2001 by the National Security Administration (NSA), called the SHA- 256 Proof-of-Work Scheme. The SHA-256 Proof-of-Work Scheme is considered a hash proof-of-work algorithm because its output is always a 256-bit hash regardless of its input. This means there is never a way to identify the length of the initial data, which is a security advantage.^([10](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-10))

The SHA-256 proof-of-work algorithm is responsible for building blocks within the Bitcoin blockchain network and acting as a source of trust between users in the network.^([11](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-11))

The execution of a new block using the SHA-256 proof of work algorithm begins by giving each block a header. The header is composed of a timestamp, software version number, hash of Merkle Root, nonce, target hash, and the hash of the previous block. This data is then used to solve a complex mathematical problem.^([12](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-12))

First, a 0 is added to the block header. This additional new number added to the block header is known as a *nonce.* The new block header is then hashed to see if its resulting hash value is less than the target hash. If it is not, then the nonce is increased by adding 1 to the block header and hashing it again. This process continues by trial and error and takes a great deal of computational power to find the appropriate nonce.

Since so much power is needed to solve blocks, people share their computing power and work together. These people are called *miners.* Each miner’s computer actively works on solving the block’s nonce, and results are shared, including incorrect nonces, which means the odds of finding the solution increase over time.^([13](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-13))

With Bitcoin, a new block is added somewhere at the rate of about one block every ten minutes.^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-14)) Once a block is added, it is almost impossible to change, as that would require changing all preceding blocks. This immutability adds protection from tampering and ensures that the longest chain serves as the chain’s framework.

## Blockchain Provenance

Blockchains are safe from tampering because of proof-of-work consensusbased algorithms. This exemplifies another benefit of the system: it can demonstrate provenance. Provenance is a record of ownership from the block’s earliest known history.

Provenance has been at the core of economies since the beginning of time. For example, parties may need to authenticate ownership of an animal to be traded or a house to be sold. Verifying ownership of assets protects both parties by demonstrating the authenticity of each asset.

Generally, third parties are used to verify ownership or demonstrate provenance. However, blockchains can do the same in a much fastermanner by providing their infrastructures to register both parties in a transaction and authenticate each one’s assets. Therefore the blockchain infrastructure limits the number of intermediaries needed for complex operations, which can accelerate overall transaction times.^([15](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-15))

The tracking of ownership capability via tokens is one of blockchain’s most functional applications. These tokens represent digitalized versions of data that generally occur in an array of transactions. Each token represents a full audit of the data, which indicates time and place of origin.^([16](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-16)) This is critical in business, trade, and finance, as countless documents are constantly being processed between various parties. Often these parties are not familiar with one another and so need a way to establish trust. Tracking tokens makes it possible to establish trust, as each token reveals its ownership history.

## Blockchain Immutability

Immutability is the ability of the blockchain to remain unchanged and unaltered.^([17](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-17)) Blockchain achieves its immutability from its decentralized and distributed framework. Since various nodes are used, multiple replicas of the data exist on the blockchain. A benefit of this framework is that it makes false blocks easier to identify. It also adds integrity to the network’s data because it preserves original copies: each block within a blockchain network contains not only its own unique hash but also the hash of all the previous blocks before it.

Immutability is further enhanced using digital signatures, which are commonly seen on private blockchains. Digital signatures are also known as *hashes.* The difference between a digital signature hash and blockchain hash is that a digital signature uses public-key encryption and private key decryption to validate individuals’ sending data.^([18](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-18)) Blockchain hashing, on the other hand, depends on the network’s algorithm, which is generally a consensus-driven proof-of-work algorithm. As blocks are generated, they must agree with the algorithm, which depends on most of the network concurring with the newly added block. Once a block is added, it cannot be removed or altered.

The immutability of blockchain means that data can be shared more efficiently with higher levels of trust more cheaply than existing technologies do.

## Smart Contracts

Aside from their immutability and their ability to prove provenance, blockchains can also enhance contract security by using smart contracts. According to Investopedia,

> A smart contract is a type of digital contract that contains an agreement between multiple parties directly in its code. The smart contract self-executes the terms and agreements determined and is maintained on a blockchain network. Furthermore, the code of the contract is irreversible and fully traceable.^([19](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-19))

The irreversibility of the smart contracts adds trust to transactions and agreements without the use of a central authority figure or legal system. As a result, parties can interact with one another more directly with the same level of confidence. Smart contracts are significant in that they function on a predetermined if-then base, and only proceed if the conditions outlined are met.

The most well-known blockchain for executing smart contracts is Ethereum. Ethereum is popular because it enables developers to write smart contract programs. These programs are written in a custom language known as Solidity and are developed on the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM).^([20](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-20)) The EVM can run any algorithm, and the program codes developed on it are known as decentralized applications (dApps), which consist of smart contracts. The dApps act as the front end for userinteraction but require a browser extension so users can engage with the blockchain and verify their identities. The apps also require Ethereum’s currency, Ether, to function.^([21](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-21))

As of February 2021, there were over 3,000 dApps on Ethereum’s blockchain network, and this number increases every month.^([22](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-22)) Most dApps are related to supply chain management. The benefit of using blockchain and smart contracts for supply chain management is that it eliminates third parties for verification, creates trust, and expedites processes. Trust and speed are essential, especially as the pace of globalization continues to speed up.

For example, most businesses interact internationally, which means freight brokers are needed to facilitate transactions between suppliers and purchasers. These brokers can be quite costly and are incorporated throughout various stages of the purchasing process, such as negotiating item costs, shipping costs, international tariffs, and more. Furthermore, traditional supply chains rely on signed paper documents, which can get lost in delivery or compromised by theft. Converting currencies is another issue.

Supply chain management with blockchain and smart contracts alleviates all these hurdles and significantly increases efficiency. The blockchain framework itself provides proof of identity for parties and maintains unchangeable digital records of transactions, which are available to all parties involved, guaranteeing trust.^([23](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-23))

Smart contracts manage workflows by automatically executing the required steps in a supply chain system based on predetermined parameters. This guarantees that each party receives its expected payment, product, or service. Furthermore, the use of stable tokens in smart contracting can act as a generic payment method to eliminate the need for currency exchanges. In general, this means blockchain smart contracting can not only accelerate supply chain protocols but also establish trust and substantially reduce costs to all parties.

## Bitcoin Changes Social Contracts

Nakamoto’s cryptocurrency became the first medium for the exchange of value created by the blockchain community. Bitcoin is a currency that parties voluntarily adopt to participate in trade. Unlike government fiat, which is forced upon people, participation in this electronic cash system is optional. As such, it disrupts the traditional fiat-based social constructs dominating economics.

Bitcoin gives individuals the option of choosing which institution they want to support or have more trust in. People can select traditional fiat, backed by their government, or the cryptocurrency, backed by a network of unknown miners.

Those who choose fiat do so because they believe in the security of government-generated currencies. In contrast, cryptocurrencies are not controlled by individuals, but by a large group of anonymous people— miners. Miners exist all over the world and make up the decentralized network of the blockchain. Regulatory entities do not control them; instead, the framework of the blockchain, which does not change, performs this function. Therefore it seems more reasonable to trust cryptocurrency on blockchains, like Bitcoin, because they are immutable, decentralized, and function democratically.

Although Bitcoin cannot be compromised by one individual or institution, it is at risk of being compromised (though that is a challenge). The only way for the system to be compromised is through *a 51 percent attack.*

In a 51 percent attack, an attacker tries to control most of a network by reverse engineering existing transactions.^([24](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-24)) First, the corrupt miner must find the solution to a block being generated and then not broadcast it to the rest of the blockchain network. Instead, the corrupt miner withholds the solution so that two versions of the blockchain exist, the original and his withheld copy.

The original blockchain continues to be used by the uncorrupt miners, while the corrupt miner uses the withheld blockchain. Because hehas not broadcast his block to the rest of the network, it goes unnoticed, which enables him to use his acquired tokens (Bitcoins) on the original blockchain.

But what about the withheld blockchain? Well, the tokens do not appear as spent on the withheld blockchain, meaning that the corrupt miner still has access to them. The withheld blockchain is still able to continue acquiring blocks that can be verified by the corrupt miner.

The ability to continue verifying blocks is where trouble arrives, since the blockchain is coded to follow a consensus algorithm or majority. In other words, the blockchain proceeds by following the heaviest chain, which is considered its truth. The heaviest chain is used to set balances.

Since most miners add blocks to their blockchain version more quickly than the rest of the network, a race develops over hashing power. The greater a miner’s hashing power, the faster they can add blocks to their blockchains, which the corrupt miner aims to exploit.

When the corrupt miner succeeds in adding blocks to his withheld chain faster than the other miners on the network, he now holds the longest chain. When he finally broadcasts such a chain, the remaining blockchain network detects it and considers it the truth. As a result, all the remaining miners’ protocols shift, and they start to hash on the corrupted miner’s blockchain.

With the corrupted miner’s blockchain now validated, the miner can spend his token again; this is known as a 51 percent or double-spend attack.^([25](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-25))

The likelihood that this will ever happen is slim to none, simply because such an attack would be very tough to execute. To begin with, the corrupt miner would need more hashing power than the rest of the entire network, or at least 51 percent.

Considering the number of nodes in Bitcoin’s blockchain, (over 12,000 as of August 2021), this is highly improbable.^([26](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-26)) It would require spending millions of dollars on hardware equipment and computational power that is simply impossible for a single individual to obtain. Even if these conditions were somehow met, the chance of the corrupt miner getting caught is nearly 100 percent. The mining hardware alone would occupy a warehouse, and the huge amount of electricity needed for running it would be detected. Furthermore, the miner would have to cover his Internet tracks and then somehow launder the money generated. All in all, this operation requires too much effort to be successfully executed, at least on the Bitcoin blockchain.

On other blockchains, the threat of a 51 percent attack is more likely to materialize, due to centralized crypto mining. In centralized crypto mining, specific groups of miners are pooled together, and many of them use ASICs (application-specific integrated circuits). ASIC chips can mine specific hashing algorithms, which means select cryptocurrencies, at rates faster than traditional GPUs (graphics processing units).^([27](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-27)) When ASICs are released for mining specific coins, they often dominate all other miners. The problem with this domination is that computation power can centralize, as the manufacturers of the newly created ASICs have the highest processing power. As a result, ASIC manufacturers, such as Bitmain, have the potential to control over 50 percent of the overall hashing power on the blockchain, meaning a 51 percent attack could be executed. The best solution to reducing the risk of ASIC centralization and 51 percent attacks is to generate new hashing algorithms that are ASIC-resistant.^([28](#notes.xhtml#chapter8-28))

## Blockchain’s Necessity Summarized

As technology expands and assets become more and more digitalized, it becomes imperative to maintain trust. The best solution for this is blockchain technology.

Blockchain allows for digital assets to be exchanged anonymously and removes the need for third parties to facilitate the transferring of assets, which makes transactions cheaper. Furthermore, all transactions on public blockchains are viewable and automatically created in realtime, enabling participating parties to transact faster despite their identities being unknown.

In addition to accelerating transactions, blockchain also instills trust. The trust stems from natural built-in security measures that protect from hacks, falsified data, or alterations by humans. One such measure is blockchain’s decentralized network. This makes compromising blockchains difficult by removing any single point of entry. Another innate security feature is the fundamental algorithms used, such as Bitcoin’s proof-of-work algorithm (which seems to follow the Byzantine Fault Solution). These algorithms can function with digital signatures to confirm transactions, which further increases integrity.

## Blockchain and Venture Capital

Blockchain technology facilitates innovative new ways for businesses to raise venture capital, including security token offerings (STOs), which can accommodate hundreds of investors as opposed to tens for a conventional venture capital (VC) fund.^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-1))

A fledgling business armed with a white paper, website, and a custom cryptocurrency can canvas their vision to potential investors. The investors then fund the company with fiat in exchange for a proportional amount of the business’s newly minted cryptocurrency. The investor is gambling that the value of the cryptocurrency received will increase dramatically, as the business idea matures along with widespread support. This initial investment was generally through the form of initial coin offerings (ICOs), but after they fell into disrepute, STOs became the legitimate way forward.^([2](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-2))

ICOs acted as fundraising mechanisms for new projects by allowing the proposed digital coin to be exchanged for Bitcoin, Ether, or fiat money. ICOs were related to initial public offerings (IPOs), whereby investors can purchase shares of a company.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-3)) Unfortunately, ICOs raised millions of dollars from the public with zero regulation, which allowed unscrupulous scammers to ruin the ICO concept.^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-4)) As a result, in 2017 the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) classified tokens from ICOs as securities, and outlawed them on the basis that they are misleading to investors.^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-5)) In 2017, ICOs raised $5.6 billion, but today investors are hungry for a legal alternative to fuel entrepreneurial innovation and wealth.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-6))

The legality of STOs is an obvious advantage over ICOs. The market potential for STOs lies in the fact that they allow investors to invest legally and maintain digitally secure stock certificates.^([7](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-7))

To evaluate an STO, the Howey test is used. The Howey test assesses whether a token offers the opportunity to contribute or invest money in return for a share in company profits. If a token does provide this opportunity, then it is considered a security token offering.^([8](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-8))

Another method for determining if a token qualifies as an STO or not is based on whether it is built on a blockchain system acting as a distributed ledger. In this case, the token is considered a “security instrument” under Section 2(a)(1) of the Securities Act of 1933. This act focuses on investment contracts, and in the case of cryptocurrencies, considers blockchain a secure investment contract.^([9](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-9))

The regulations associated with STOs make them costly in terms of complying with SEC regulations, so STO enforcement may stifle investment opportunities. Another approach that may be practical for businesses to at least start with is the *utility token.*

Utility tokens are user tokens or app coins which act as a digital coupon that can be redeemed for discounts or access to future products and services offered by the company. Utility tokens are not considered investments and are therefore exempt from the federal laws governing securities.^([10](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-10))

Holders of utility tokens gain exclusive, direct access to the business that issues them, as the token has no use or value elsewhere.^([11](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-11)) The utility token can serve as an asset such as a vehicle deed for a car manufacturer. The car manufacturer thereby eliminates the need for intermediaries (i.e., car dealerships).

## The Token Taxonomy Initiative (TTI)

With all these tokens, things can seem a bit complicated. To alleviate this confusion, a new standard for digital tokens has been established. This standard is called the Token Taxonomy Framework (TTF).

The TTF was established in 2019 by members of the Token Taxonomy Consortium. The consortium’s mission is to facilitate the connection between business and blockchain by creating a set of universal definitions that both developers and business professionals can understand.^([12](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-12)) Specifically, it focuses on the different uses of tokens so that people can know how to use them in a variety of industries. As Marley Gray, chair of the Token Taxonomy Consortium and principal architect of Microsoft, states:

> Whether it’s a ticket, supply chain documents, stocks, property titles, loyalty points, or other as-yet unthought-of blockchainbased products and services, token use should be able to implement ow across platforms. If we want to take full advantage of what collaborative platforms have to offer, cross-platform transactions must be able to communicate with one another. Standards are where that starts, and this Framework makes unlocking the untapped potential of tokens on the blockchain possible.^([13](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-13))

The standards outlined by the TTF are shared on a GitHub repository, meaning the information is publicly available for use. Information contained here provides intelligence on various token concepts through use cases, terminology, taxonomy, and technology discussions. There is also a set of reusable tools, which enables users to design their tokens quickly.

Tokens are classified based on certain characteristics, which make up a token’s *base.*^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-14)) The first characteristic is the *token type.* There are two types of tokens: *fungible* and *nonfungible.* Fungible tokens have interchangeable value with one another, so that any quantity of them has the same value as an equal amount of another token in its same series or class. An example of a fungible token would be physical cash. A nonfungible token (NFT) is not exchangeable with other tokens of its same type, because they have different values. An example of a nonfungible token would be a property title.

A token’s *unit* is the next characteristic that can be used to define it. A token unit can be *fractional, whole,* or *singleton.* Fractional tokens canbe divided into fractions and represented as decimals. Whole tokens are represented only by whole numbers: they do not allow for subdivision. A singleton is simply represented by a quantity of one with no subdivisions.

*Value type* is the third characteristic of a token identity. Tokens can either be *intrinsic* or *reference* types. Intrinsic type means the token itself holds value, while reference type directs to some other source of value.

*Representation* is another characteristic that defines tokens. A representation can be *common* or *unique.* Common tokens share properties and are stored in one central location. Unique tokens, on the other hand, have unique features and can be individually traced.

The final token characteristic is the token’s *template.* Templates can be *single* or *hybrid.* Single tokens are made from one parent, while hybrid tokens combine a parent token with one or more child tokens for their creation.

Aside from token characteristics, the TTF provides a composition framework for creating tokens based on reusable parts. These parts are base token types, token properties, and token behaviors. The parts are then further broken down, and their residual components are known as *component artifacts.* The component artifacts are ultimately combined to complete the token’s creation.

The TTF’s token template determines the type of component artifacts present in a token. This template is composed of a template formula and template definition, which is derived from the template formula. The token template describes how a token will be used and is made up of reusable base types, behaviors, behavior groups, and properties.

Properties of a token refer to how data is contained by a token and how it records its data. Token behaviors can be:

1.  Transferable, meaning ownership can be transferred.
2.  Nontransferable, meaning ownership cannot be transferred.
3.  Subdividable, meaning the token can exist as a decimal.
4.  Singleton, meaning the token is made from the number 1.
5.  Mintable, meaning tokens can be created.
6.  Role supported, meaning tokens have roles defined to limit or allow specific actions.
7.  Burnable, meaning tokens can be removed from the supply.

Token behavior groups reflect behaviors that are joined in groups based on similar usage.^([15](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-15)) For example, Supply Control is a behavior group made up of mintable, burnable, and role support behaviors.

Template definitions contain the artifacts that will be used to create a token using the template formula’s instructions. Therefore, the template definition is a token’s specification and is used by developers to code. The image below is an example of a token definition derived from the token formula.

Despite their intricate appearance, the token template formulas and definitions are easy to use. This feasibility results from the interface established by the consortium that allows for simple click and drag creation of tokens. ## Microfinance: Solving the Illiquidity Problem

Microfinance is a financial service extended to low-income clients to help them increase their income. These services include credit lines, insurance funds, loans, and savings accounts. Most people who are eligible to receive microfinance are small businesses and low-income entrepreneurs who lack sufficient credit.^([17](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-17))

Microfinance programs were created with the promise of alleviating poverty by means of group-lending contacts with joint liability. However, many of these programs only exacerbated the economic tensions between low-income groups and lenders, who would implement high repayment rates. As a result, greater income inequality developed, which directly counters the purpose of microfinance.^([18](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-18))

Another problem is that microfinance institutions (MFIs) are lightly regulated. Furthermore, only a limited number of MFIs meet the requirements for remittances or savings. Furthermore, clients seeking microfinance often lack collateral to secure the funding from the financer.^([19](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-19))

This flaw in microfinancing causes most low-income people to result in merely saving up. Saving up means accumulating the value needed for a commodity or service in its entirety and then obtaining it.^([20](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-20)) The need for low-income individuals to have full value for costly endeavors only deepens the poverty lines, as the low-income sector is forced to seek high-interest loans or, worse, completely abandon their efforts to obtain funding.

## The Decentralized Identify Foundation of Blockchain Origins

Identity in blockchain technology is based on a *decentralized public key infrastructure* (DPKI). DPKI is derived from *public key infrastructure* (PKI), which is composed of select policies, hardware, software, protocols, and set roles for managing confidential and personal information.^([21](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-21)) Together, these components outline how to securely manage public-key encryption and digital certificates. Most online transactions, such as e-commerce and banking, utilize PKI because it provides more security than simple passwords to confirm the identities of the parties involved in a transaction.

PKI provides security by following the X.509 standard.^([22](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-22)) This standard defines the general security structures included in PKI. The first security structures accounted for in PKI are *certificates of authority.*^([23](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-23)) These make up the cryptographic framework of PKI. The certificate of authority is issued either by an automated process or under human supervision. It is used to match public keys with identities.

Next, PKI includes a *registration authority,* which is responsible for processing and authenticating requests for digital certificates. A final measure for security is its use of *trusted third parties* (TTPs). A TTP acts as the ultimate validation authority by identifying entities on the certificate of authority based on supplemental data it has about the entity.^([24](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-24)) Trusted third parties provide safer transactions for both buyers and sellers.

Despite these three layers of identity verification, PKI can still be compromised. The most vulnerable component of a PKI is its trusted third parties. These parties can be corruptible points of failure due to *man-in- the-middle attacks.* A man-in-the-middle attack occurs when an attacker intercepts a communication between two parties and either steals the information for malicious purposes or changes that information.^([25](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-25))

## Identify Formulation in DPKI

The best way to protect identities is by using DPKI. Here identity registration is established and maintained by the individuals who own the identity on a blockchain network. Therefore the blockchain network acts as an identifier registry, assuming the owner of the identity retains control of their private key.^([26](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-26))

In DPKI, private keys are generated directly to the identity owner over its decentralized ledger and maintained using software.^([27](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-27)) The registered identifier is then given a keypair for registering and updating their data linked to the identifier and public keys, which act as the identifier’s subkeys. Subkeys are used by identity owners to sign communications.^([28](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-28))

## Personal Identity Hubs

Many people agree that DPKI’s most significant application is in regard to *personal identity hubs.* A personal identity hub holds all data for a specific individual and allows that individual to control all their information directly.^([29](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-29)) For example, say a patient needs to send hospital records to their primary physician for review. The patient must contact the hospital and then wait on it to send the physician its files. The medical history is transmitted directly from the hospital to the physician without the patient obtaining a copy.

If personal identity hubs were developed for medical data, a digital record of the patient’s data would exist on the patient’s hub so that they could send the information directly to their physician or anyone else. Furthermore, the patient would be able to pull up such records at any moment for review. Instant access can be a lifesaving technology. For instance, if someone were to get into an accident and be knocked unconscious, they would be unable to share their medical history. However, having a personal identity hub could give emergency care providers access to information—such as specific medical conditions or allergies to certain medications—that may be critical to saving the person’s life.

Personal identity hubs not only give individuals quicker access to their data but can be used for identification, which is a major advantage, considering that approximately 1.7 billion people around the world haveno proof of identity. Providing these people with identification can help them to engage in transactions within an economy.

## Digital Signatures

DPKI also uses *digital signatures.* A digital signature is based on a cryptographic mathematical problem algorithm.^([31](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-31)) It is used to authenticate identities and adds integrity to data while demonstrating *nonrepudiation.*^([32](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-32)) Nonrepudiation is an assurance that the sender of information is provided with proof of delivery and the recipient is provided with proof of the sender’s identity, so neither can later deny having processed the information.

Digital signatures work by hashing, signing, and verifying data.^([33](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-33)) Hashing data, regardless of size or type, means to convert it into a hash value that is the same length as all other hash values generated. The hashed data is then signed by its sender using public-key cryptography.

Public-key cryptography enables the parties to verify that the data has not been tampered with. First, the sender’s data is encrypted with their private key. A message is sent along with the sender’s public key. The recipient then uses the sender’s public key to read the message.

Integrity is assured because the sender can verify that the message was unchanged, as an altered message would generate a new signature. Authenticity is preserved because, assuming the sender is the only one with access to the private key used to create the signature, the message should only be from the sender. Finally, digital signatures guarantee against repudiation, as the sender cannot dispute whether a message was signed or not.

## Private Permissioned Ledgers

A *private permissioned ledger* is a blockchain network where only authorized parties can add nodes.^([34](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-34)) They are the only ones with access to the data on the blockchain. It functions as a closed environment in which anyone can participate. The benefit of a private permissioned ledger is that that governance can be used to dictate the algorithm. Another advantage is that a select person or group of people can be held accountable for the data; however, this also means privacy becomes the responsibility of the ledger.

Today many financial institutions abide by Know Your Customer (KYC) compliances. These compliances focus on making ure identitiesare known in transactions.^([35](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-35)) However, a problem with these current systems is that it can take institutions a long time to verify all the documents submitted by a client. A private ledger with built-in KYC could shorten these long validation periods. If KYC were to be applied to private ledgers, data could be verified almost instantly. Furthermore, having KYC on the ledger helps enforce antibribery laws.

Anti–money laundering (AML) compliance programs should also be standard on private ledgers, as they help to track illegal transactions.^([36](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-36)) Money laundering accounts for 2 to 5 percent of global GDP, and cryptocurrency has a dark history of being at the center of many laundering schemes.^([37](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-37)) Having AML compliance built into blockchains can help to reduce money laundering.

In general, information on private ledgers should be on a need-to- know basis and regulated by all corresponding frameworks and compliances relevant to the ledger. Keeping data as private as possible is essential to preventing the greatest threats to such ledgers, such as quantum computing.

*Quantum computing* refers to a device that can perform calculations using quantum mechanics. This means that a quantum computer can use qubits instead of bits to do calculations.^([38](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-38)) A qubit allows for superpositioning, meaning that a 0 and 1 can be present at the same time, thereby making quantum computers faster than any other device.^([39](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-39)) The speed of quantum computers allows them to reverse-calculate the algorithms used on a blockchain, which means they can falsify digital signatures, personal data, or even take control of digital assets.

The best protection against quantum attacks are quantum-resistant algorithms or the quantum Internet, which is still decades away.^([40](#notes.xhtml#chapter9-40)) Regardless, private ledgers should plan for this future, and protect against coming technological advances.

Tokens used in private ledgers are valued based on what the market decides the value should be. There is only a fixed number of tokens available, which means they are not subject to inflation. Regardless of this immunity, cryptocurrency tokens still fluctuate a great deal in price, since they abide by the law of supply and demand. They are further influenced by other factors too, such as their utility applications.

## How the U.S. Government Feels about Cryptocurrency

Cryptocurrency first appeared on the U.S. government’s radar in 2017, after the Bitcoin bubble. With Americans circulating millions of dol lars through this mysterious platform, the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodities Futures Trading Commission stepped in (see [Figure 10-01](#chapter10.xhtml#fig23)). In the United States, any transfer of money above $15,000 is required to be taxed.^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter10-1)) In a meeting with the New York Federal Reserve, the chairman of the SEC explained that Bitcoin was a topic of great urgency, as the unregistered transfer of funds of such magnitude had the ability to destabilize the market. Soon after, the SEC restricted any exchange-traded funds (ETFs) from including cryptocurrencies. It also decided not to register initial coin offerings (ICOs). ## China’s Dance with Digital Currency: The Digital Yuan and Bitcoin

Bitcoin has fought an arduous battle trying to prove it can and should replace traditional fiat currency. In China, that is what cryptocurrency is actually doing. Unlike Bitcoin, this currency is issued by the Chinese government and is known as a central bank digital currency (CDBC). CDBCs were touched on in the last chapter, but the important distinction between Bitcoin and CBDCs is that the latter are not decentralized. There are other key differences, but it is vital to understand that CBDCs are not stored on a chain of networks: they are stored, distributed, and administered by a nation’s central bank. Another important point regarding CBDCs is they are not stablecoins. These are not digital currencies tied to the sovereign currency of that nation but rather they are actually *the* currency of the country.

The benefit of CBDCs is that they are backed by the government of that nation. Currently, when a central bank wants to put money into circulation, it prints new money and uses it to enlarge bank reserves at commercial banks. The banks then spend that money through fractional reserve banking, and the new money finally makes it into the economy. With CBDCs, new money would be transferred directly from the central bank to the digital “pockets” of the citizenry.

The Chinese CBDC is known as the digital yuan. It was first created in April 2020, and since then 20.8 million Chinese citizens have created their own digital yuan wallets. It has now reached eleven cities and provinces in China, facilitating nearly 71 million transactions in these areas. The Chinese government has multiple goals for the digital yuan: the firsttralized control over the digital yuan, meaning it can better influence monetary policy, freeze accounts, and fix money transfer errors. There is some speculation that an undisclosed goal of the digital yuan is to undermine the global dominance of the U.S. dollar. Today the digital yuan is used mainly for domestic transactions, specifically as a way to simplify retail purchases and facilitate more economic activity during the COVID-19 pandemic.

In 2019, global trade amounted over $19 trillion. The dollar is currently the world’s largest reserve currency, as it has been for the past seventy-five years, since the end of World War II. This is mainly due to the growth and overall strength of the U.S. economy. Globally there is a lot of trust that the U.S. will pay its debts, and thus the U.S. dollar is perceived to be the safest currency in which to store a country’s reserves. Being a global reserve currency means that central banks around the world hold large amounts of dollars. They do this to avoid having to convert different currencies when involved in international trade deals. The U.S. dollar makes up about 60 percent of global currency reserves, and is used in 75 percent of global transactions^([9](#notes.xhtml#chapter10-9)) (see [Figure 10-03](#chapter10.xhtml#fig25)).

# 11 Regulatory Concerns forBlockchain Technologies

There are many technical and regulatory concerns regarding blockchain usage, as the applications for blockchain technologies are truly endless. In this chapter, I will focus on its primary present-day uses. These include contract implementation, record keeping, and financial transactions, all of which are typically monitored by some regulatory entity.

This chapter will also explore how several American governing authorities are approaching the regulation of cryptocurrencies. Some of the reviewed authorities include the Security and Exchange Commission (SEC), the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), and the Financial Action Taskforce (FATF).

The chapter will conclude with a more in- depth conversation on smart contracts, and a summary of the Token Taxonomy Framework developed by the Enterprise Ethereum Alliance.

## The Problem of Jurisdiction

Blockchains can be classified as either *open* or *closed.* Open blockchains are either *public and permissionless* or *public and permissioned.*^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-1)) In public permissionless blockchains, anyone can join the network and volunteer to contribute to its upkeep. On the other hand, public permissioned blockchains require permission to enter the system as a node, and the owner dictates which members can contribute to its governance. In both open blockchains, the network is viewable to all members.

Closed blockchains are either consortiums or private permissioned blockchains. Only an authorized set of members can view the consortium, and only approved members can contribute to its network. Permissioned blockchains are either fully unviewable or have viewing restricted to a limited set of permissioned nodes. Only the network operator can alter the network.

Both open and private blockchains occur over decentralized networks, meaning that nodes can exist around the globe. As a result, there is a high chance that transactions could be happening under multiple jurisdictions, which would mean that they would encounter various laws and regulations.^([2](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-2))

While stumbling across multiple jurisdictions is nearly inevitable in both blockchain systems, private blockchain systems can at least lessen the effects by creating internal governance and legal frameworks to dictate transactions.

## Data Privacy and Security

Accountability is an essential part of the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). These regulations require the party controlling personal data to be defined and to be responsible for protecting such data. When compared to GDPR, Blockchain has an inherent property of distributing data to all the participants in the network to achieve decentralization. Second, data on a blockchain is immutable to maintain data integrity in the network.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-3)) The party must have a set controller and processor. The controller refers to the member or members in charge of dictating how data will be processed. A processor, on the other hand, is the party that processes the controller’s data. In standard cloud computing protocols, individuals uploading data are to be considered controllers, while the cloud system’s operator is its processor.

Most blockchains function with peer-to-peer networking, meaning that controllers and processors cannot be easily identified. For example, since the blockchain is made up of various nodes that run the necessary algorithms for the blockchain to function, it would seem that these nodes should be considered processors. However, because the nodes do not process personal data, but rather perform calculations to generate additional blocks, they are not considered processors under the GDPR.^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-4))

A private permissioned blockchain represents the best way to determine controllers and processors so that the blockchain technology meets the criteria set by GDPR. In these blockchains, a network member or set of network members can be assigned as the blockchain’s operator, which means they can determine controllers and processors. For instance, they can require their nodes to be located within the same jurisdiction. These specific nodes would be the processors, while the individuals uploading data would be the controllers. Additional restrictions can also be implemented, such as prohibiting users from uploading harmful files.

More security lies in blockchain technology’s immutability and provenance. Together, these characteristics make it nearly impossible for someone to tamper with data held on a blockchain network. Furthermore, the numerous decentralized nodes cumulatively hold the highest computing power for the operation of the blockchain. Harnessing this power would require the system to be altered by an external factor, which is exceptionally difficult.

## Cryptocurrency Regulation and Taxation

Federal agencies have argued about how to regulate cryptocurrency, which had a market value of more than $2.8 trillion as of November 2021.^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-5)) The first question is, does cryptocurrency, a currency with no absolute value, fall under SEC jurisdiction? According to court records, the answer is yes. In September 2021, a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request found that the SEC may be investigating Tether and Tether Operations Limited. Tether (USDT) is a blockchain-based cryptocurrency. It is a stablecoin, meaning its tokens in circulation are backed by an equivalent number of U.S. dollars. Stablecoins track traditional fiat currencies, like the dollar, the euro, or the Japanese yen, which are held in designated bank accounts.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-6))

In May 2021, SEC chair Gary Gensler said he would like to see more regulation around cryptocurrency exchanges. Crypto tokens that are issued in the same way as classic investment tokens do fall under the SEC’s jurisdiction.^([7](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-7))

According to the CNBC website, the IRS treats virtual currencies, like Bitcoin, as property, meaning that they are taxed in a manner similar to stocks or real property. The IRS refers to cryptocurrency as virtual currency, and these transactions are taxable by law, just like transactions in any other property. If you buy one Bitcoin for $10,000 and sell it for $50,000, you face $40,000 of taxable capital gains. Taxpayers transacting in virtual currency may have to report those transactions on their tax returns. Classifying cryptocurrency as virtual currencies makes tax accounting more complicated, especially as they can be exchanged for real currency or used as a substitute.^([8](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-8)) Accountants must stay aware of these classifications to understand how cryptocurrencies are intended to be taxed. While this concept is relatively simple, it isn’t always clear what constitutes a “taxable event.”

In a May 2021 article titled “Cryptocurrency Poses a Significant Risk of Tax Evasion,”^([9](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-9)) the CNBC website noted that the Treasury seemsparticularly concerned about wealthy Americans who shift taxable assets into the crypto economy to avoid tax. “Cryptocurrency already poses a significant detection problem by facilitating illegal activity broadly including tax evasion,”^([10](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-10)) according to a Treasury report, which outlined the Biden administration’s tax-compliance agenda. Failure to classify these options demonstrates that there is still much development needed for proper tax regulation.^([11](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-11))

Lawmakers in Washington have been grappling with the thorny topic of crypto regulation for some time, and senior figures, from treasury secretary Janet Yellen to SEC chair Gary Gensler, all believe more oversight is necessary.

As of this writing, the popular cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase now issues 1099-K forms to customers with at least 200 orders during the year, with the total value of those orders equal to or greater than $20,000.

Crypto exchanges in the United States fall under the regulatory scope of the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and must register with the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN). They are also required to comply with obligations against money laundering and the financing of terrorism.^([12](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-12))

## The Federal Trade Commission

The FTC is a bipartisan federal government agency that focuses on protecting American consumers while promoting competition in the U.S. economy.^([13](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-13)) The FTC has jurisdiction over civil antitrust issues such as deceptive or fraudulent practices. The enforcement of antitrust laws by the FTC helps to keep markets open and free.^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-14))

In June 2015 the FTC announced its first lawsuit in a cryptocurrencyrelated case.^([15](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-15)) The case involved a smartphone rewards application called Prized. Prized advertised that users could play games on the app to earn coins that could be used to buy real-life items such as clothes or gift cards.^([16](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-16)) Users, however, could not purchase anything with their coins.Rather, the application installed malicious software on their devices. The malicious software forced the user’s device to mine virtual currencies such as Dogecoin, LiteCoin, and QuarkCoin for the developer. The case was brought in the federal court of New Jersey and ultimately ended with the application developers paying a settlement.

Since that first case, dozens more have been filed by the FTC. As of May 2021, FTC data shows a huge spike in cryptocurrency investment scams.^([17](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-17)) This increase in case filings has caused the FTC to create a new industry classification—fintech, or financial technology.^([18](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-18))

The scope of the FTC’s jurisdiction over FinTech includes mobile payments, crowdfunding, virtual currencies, applications, ecommerce sites, and more. It aims to regulate these sectors by undertaking enforcement action against companies that harm consumers through deceptive practices. However, a problem exists in that FinTech tools are often either made outside the United States, where the FTC lacks jurisdiction, or by unknown entities.

## Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN)

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) is part of the United States Treasury Department and monitors financial transactions. It was established in 1990 to combat and halt terrorist financing, money laundering, and other financial crimes.^([19](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-19))

Its primary regulatory functions fall under the Currency and Financial Transactions Reporting Act of 1970. Responsibilities of FinCEN center around early detection and deterrence of financial crimes. Examples of some FinCEN responsibilities include issuing and interpreting regulations set forth by government statues, supporting such provisions, and maintaining government- wide access to FinCEN data.

After the September 11 terrorist attack in 2001, FinCEN gained another set of responsibilities when the PATRIOT Act was implemented. The PATRIOT Act included federal anti–money laundering (AML) andcounterterrorism financing (CFT) programs, which FinCEN would oversee. FinCEN also gained another role as the Foreign Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) for the United States government.^([20](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-20))

It was not until May 2019 that FinCEN issued a set of guidelines for applying its regulations to businesses that participate in money transmissions with virtual currencies. In January 2021, FinCEN released new proposals that would subject financial institutions and exchanges to onerous recordkeeping and reporting requirements for certain digital currency transactions.^([21](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-21)) In summary, they asserted that any entity involved in the exchange or administering of a token must register as a money services business (MSB) if they were not already registered with the SEC or Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). Registering as an MSB with FinCEN is a no-cost process that is done by completing Form 10715. However, FinCEN also requires that the MSB must comply with recordkeeping, reporting, and AML program requirements under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and designate a compliance officer to report suspicious activity.^([22](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-22))

## Financial Action Task Force (FATF)

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) was established in 1989 during a Group of Seven (G7) meeting in Paris. It was designed to combat international money laundering.^([23](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-23)) As America’s FinCEN would later do, the FATF expanded its reach by adopting policies to combat terrorist financing. More than 200 countries are committed to FATF and the implementation of the standards it sets forth, mostly related to international AML/ CFT regulations.

The president of FATF changes every year, and in 2019 it was Marshall Billingslea of the United States. Billingslea advocated focusing on the regulation of virtual currencies. Specifically, instructions were developed for virtual asset service providers (VASPs) to abide by the travel rule.^([24](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-24)) All VASPs were required to obtain the identity of all senders and recipients in a virtual asset transaction. The so-called travel rule requires obligations to obtain, hold, and transmit required originator and beneficiary information in order to identify and report suspicious transactions, monitor the availability of information, take freezing actions, and prohibit transactions with designated persons and entities.

The FATF also decided that VASPs should be registered, at least in the jurisdiction in which they were created. VASPs would be further monitored by a competent authority that could impose sanctions as a disciplinary measure or suspend the VASP’s license.

In October 2021, the FATF updated its *2019 Guidance for a RiskBased Approach to Virtual Assets and Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs).* The FATF standards require countries to assess and mitigate their risks associated with virtual asset financial activities and providers; license or register providers and subject them to supervision or monitoring by competent national authorities.^([25](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-25))

## Smart Contracts

One of the most common uses for blockchain is to execute smart contracts. Smart contracts are self-executing agreements that function on a conditional basis, giving them the ability to execute contracts more efficiently than in standard practices. Smart contracts deal exclusively with the execution of contracts and not their enforcement. As a result, they cannot technically be considered legally binding, even though they otherwise have all the components of a legal agreement.^([26](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-26)) For smart contracts to be legally binding, they would have to be able to threaten litigation as a means of enforcement.

Given blockchain’s immutable nature, this is almost impossible— for example, if a smart contract was determined to be unfair to a party member and needed to be modified. Anything implemented on the blockchain is permanent.

Another issue concerning blockchain’s immutability in regard to smart contracts is that disagreements or conflicts are challenging to remedy. Most grievances occur after a contract is initiated, which means a smart contract would have to include instructions from the start on what would be done in the event of a grievance. Thus immutability means that all participating parties must have a complete understanding of the smart contract involved.

Understanding smart contracts can be complicated for the typical contract attorney, because the contracts are written in a computer coding language. Therefore, contract agreements in English must be translated into another language that the blockchain can understand.

Blockchains can utilize numerous languages for smart contract execution, which fuels apprehensiveness towards its adoption. To begin with, having multiple languages makes it harder for parties to find representatives knowledgeable in both the conditions of the contract and the coding language. As a result, third parties must get involved in the development of the contract, which can cause some clients to lose trust. The client is unsure if their needs are being met. Their intended representative had to outsource the contract development to a software engineer, because the representative cannot read the developed contract code himself.

## Ethereum Solidity

To help alleviate the problem of numerous languages utilized by blockchain for smart contract execution, Ethereum established a universal smart contract coding language called Solidity. Solidity is an object- oriented programming language that is used to create smart contracts for use on the Ethereum blockchain network. Any program designed with Solidity can run on the Ethereum blockchain by using the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) to upload it.^([28](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-28)) An uploaded program or application is referred to as a dApp or *decentralized application.* Once on the EVM, dApps process via compile-time instead of run-time and use Ether to execute transactions.^([29](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-29)) Ether is a token that is mined and required to pay for program transactions. It is like fuel needed to run a vehicle.

The Enterprise Ethereum Alliance (EEA) was founded in 2017.^([30](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-30)) Ethereum’s blockchain network has gained a lot of attention over the past couple of years, and they are revolutionizing the smart contract industry. In fact, as of September 2021, over 3,000 dApps run on the blockchain.

The EEA connects significant enterprises, such as Microsoft, with the Ethereum blockchain. Its mission is to be an open-source standard and to address enterprise deployment requirements. As it is an opensource platform, any code created by a company is available for all others to see. A benefit of this is that the EEA can reduce technology deployment costs for both other large enterprises as well as for small businesses.

To date, the EEA spans over forty-five countries and is composed of nineteen different technical, industry, and legal advisory teams. As such, the EEA is the leading actor in the blockchain smart contract industry and is setting the foundation for how smart contracts will be written, implemented, and addressed legally. For example, it has specific rules for using the Ethereum blockchain, an intellectual property rights policy, confidentiality and nondisclosure policies, and antitrust policies. Furthermore, it advocates for Know Your Customer (KYC) and AML regulations.

## Token Taxonomy Framework (TTF)

The most significant contribution of the EEA has been its Token Taxonomy Framework (TTF), which outlines specific token terminology and offers a method for creating tokens that can be used to drive smart contracts.^([31](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-31)) The TTF is based on its token definitions, which outline specific components needed by a business for various purposes.

The TTF is available on GitHub, which is a public repository where developers can obtain the framework. The framework contains a token template that facilitates automatic code generation that can be verified and certified.^([32](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-32)) A reference document is then created for each token, which explains its structure and usage in simple language that all business developers can understand.

The TTF sets standards to tokens, and many enterprises have started to adopt the framework for their own tokens. For example, Digital Asset has begun to apply the TTF for DAML smart contracts. DAML is a development framework for creating systems of record that securely connect across business processes and data boundaries while retaining privacy, guaranteeing data integrity, and enforcing strict authorization. Allowing such systems to cross boundaries—legal, regulatory, organizational, or other—enables them to form seamless economic networks.^([33](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-33)) To help enterprises mint their own Ethereum tokens, Microsoft recently announced TTF support for Azure Blockchain Tokens.^([34](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-34))

## Real Property Records

Another application of blockchain technology is in real estate transactions. Presently disputes over property sales and property transference overwhelm court systems. There are too many statutes that vary from state to state and too many defeasible estates.^([35](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-35)) A defeasible estate is created when a grantor transfers land conditionally. Upon the happening of the event or condition stated by the grantor, the transfer may be void or subject to annulment. (An estate not subject to such conditions is called an indefeasible estate.) Historically, the common law has frowned on the use of defeasible estates, as they interfere with the owners’ enjoyment of their property and make it difficult to create a valid future interest. Blockchain technology could help to reduce these disputes through a public real estate record system. In such a system, buyers could document deeds directly on the blockchain. The deeds would be time-stamped, and because of blockchain’s ability to prevent tampering, deeds could not be added twice.

A blockchain real estate record system could provide the necessary provenance for validating and verifying the identities of property owners and the corresponding title deeds. Integrating such a system with financial institutions would enable lenders to check that purchasers have the funds needed to complete the real estate sale. Overall, this would add protection for both buyers and sellers.

In Illinois, blockchain has already been implemented to track property sales and transfers.^([36](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-36)) So far, it has been a success and has even helped to clear up legal disputes involving incorrect information. These disputes are easiest to close with blockchain, as all input to the record is traceable and cannot be reversed.

Another benefit this system has been shown to have is its potential to remove the need for title insurance.^([37](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-37)) Title insurance is a type of indemnity insurance that protects its policyholder from losses that can be incurred from defects in a property title.^([38](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-38)) The cost of title insurance varies but is generally at least $1,000.

Smart contracts can also be used in conjunction with blockchain record keeping for automating sales, tax payments, and deed recording. These contracts can use a form of escrow to demonstrate to sellers and buyers that their funds or assets are verified and authentic and can be released.

Smart contracts could be used to speed up the mortgage approval and settlement process by codifying instructions and automating current manual workflows.^([39](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-39))

## Financial Transactions

The financial industry currently operates with centralized authority control, which means hackers need to compromise only a single point of entry. As a result, a financial hack, such as the theft of money, would cause suffering for all the bank’s clients. The best way for financial institutionsto reduce the risk of attacks is to switch to a distributed ledger technology or blockchain.

Blockchain technology removes the weakness of a single point of entry by relying on numerous nodes within its framework. It also adds trust through its use of unchangeable algorithms to function. These algorithms thus create “trustless trust” by depending on network members to validate each transaction.^([40](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-40)) Trust in blockchain can help financial institutions eliminate intermediary costs, which raise the price of transactions, especially international ones. Since blockchain transactions are updated continuously and on each node, there is little risk for data loss or corruption.

## The Threat from Anonymity

Users on a blockchain network are often identified by their public keys, but this does not equate to knowing the user’s identity or location. Furthermore, a user can always make additional public keys and even new digital wallets. Anonymity on blockchain networks thus makes it difficult for law enforcement teams to investigate financial crimes.^([41](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-41))

For blockchain networks to gain widespread adoption, it would be necessary to solve the regulatory problem of anonymity, but philosophically, the idea of regulation can be viewed as going against the very foundation of blockchain. As we have seen, the odds of a data breach with blockchain technology are very low, thanks to the consensus algorithm used by the network. The consensus algorithm makes it so that someone would need at least 51 percent of the entire network’s computation power for a breach to occur.

The best way to protect personal data on blockchain technology is to use personal identity hubs. Personal identity hubs can be user-operated so that only the owner of the data can choose when and where to release data. This can enhance privacy by eliminating third parties for identity verification.

## Ripple (RXP)

Ripple is an open-source, permissionless, decentralized private ledger capable of settling transactions in three to five seconds. It is also the name of the token used by the Ripple ledger. The Ripple token is represented as XRP^([42](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-42)) Ripple. It does not have a blockchain; instead, it has its own patented technology known as the Ripple protocol consensus algorithm (RPCA).^([43](#notes.xhtml#chapter11-43))

The RPCA solves the Byzantine Generals Problem by reducing the high latency that is usually inherent in conventional blockchains. Latency is a measure of delay. In a network, latency measures the time it takes for data to get to its destination across the network. High latency occurs in standard blockchains because all nodes must communicate synchronously.

In the RPCA, the consensus algorithm avoids this necessity by using subnetworks within the leading network. Subnetworks are composed of selected member nodes that Ripple calls *validators.* There are over 150 validators on the network, with over thirty-five on the default Unique Node List; Ripple runs six of these nodes.XRP can be sent directly, without a central intermediary, which makes it ideal for currency exchange. ## DAML Smart Contracts

DAML is a next-generation application platform that makes it easy for anyone to write simple, concise, multiparty smart contracts and deploy them to their choice infrastructure. DAML is a language created for the exchange of value.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter12-6)) Smart contracts are machine-encoded promises to perform specific actions.

Digital Asset Holdings, the company behind DAML, lays out five key properties of smart contract systems that a contract language must enable. Paraphrased, these properties are:

1.  Proof of rights and obligations. For the consequences of a contract to be compulsory, the entrance must be voluntary. To automate the processing of an agreement to be automated, one must be able to reliably reason about all possible future outcomes of any given state of the contract.
2.  Confidential execution. For a contract to be confidential, only the parties affected by the contract can have the right to view the contract details.
3.  Evidentiary trail. For a contract to be enforceable, incontrovertible evidence of its authorization by all stakeholders must be preserved.
4.  Formal verifiability. For a contract to be guaranteed to perform as intended, it must be subject to formal verification methods.

# 13 Introduction to Decentralized Finance

Imagine having a global open platform for every financial service that you use. Think about stock markets, savings, loans, and insurance. While cryptocurrencies have increased accessibility and costeffectiveness for cross-border monetary transactions, DeFi takes this a step further. DeFi is an experimental form of finance that does not use central financial intermediaries like banks, brokerages, and exchanges to offer traditional financial instruments, but instead utilizes a modern and transparent approach provided by smart contracts on the blockchain. DeFi represents a set of applications developed on public distributed ledgers to facilitate a new era of financial services.^([1](#notes.xhtml#chapter13-1))

DeFi platforms enable quick lending and borrowing of funds, predict price movements on assets, the earning of interest similar to savings accounts, and the cryptocurrency trading. The entire DeFi ecosystem is based on decentralized applications, also called dApps or protocols, providing a peer-to-peer financial network on the blockchain. 4.  Lending platforms. Lending platforms are based on smart contracts that aim to replace conventional intermediaries like banks.

The phrase *money legos* is often used to refer to a key DeFi, also known as composability. The concept is simple: Composability is the interoperability of DeFi protocols, resulting in efficient, creative financial services and products for DeFi end users. Some examples of these protocols are Aave, Yearn, Synthetix, Curve, and RenVM (amongst others) that mesh together to enable, process, and deliver new financial products only possible in DeFi. The open- ended and permissionless nature of DeFi protocols allows you to stack dApps together to unlock incredible yield farming returns, deploy flash loans, or take out loans that repay themselves. Simply by using an app on your mobile phone, you can leverage cryptocurrency that you own, using it as collateral to raise a loan or as the basis for making a loan to a peer on the decentralized network. While this scenario describes a simple application of DeFi, the possibilities for the future are almost limitless. A smart contract is a computer program based on the blockchain network that executes by itself on certain conditions. They are highly helpful in establishing business relations and collaborations. Smart contracts enforce the terms and agreements of a business deal, carry out financial transactions, and serve as binding contracts, encapsulated in software code. They can be exchanged and used among the concerned parties without the need for any intermediary service.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter13-3))

For instance, if you want to ensure the payment of funds to a seller, you can use smart contracts as a means to validate each transaction with a unique security token. Smart contracts and DeFi allow for direct transactions between peers without having to rely on third-party transaction channels. A decentralized smart contract can replace the security approval processes and validation provided by these trusted third-party services.

Unlike derivatives, one unique selling point of crypto synthetics is the potential to earn rewards or yield by staking or holding on to an asset for an extended period of time.

One example is the yield farming launched on the Jumpstart platform of OKEx, where users can mine digital assets by staking OKB tokens.^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter13-14))

1.  6\. Staking. Staking is usually the first exposure that digital asset holders have to decentralized finances, and is one of the simplest use cases of DeFi. Staking allows you to delegate digital assets to a validator node in order to hold digital assets inside a compatible wallet. Staking helps you earn rewards in exchange for securing the blockchains. Some of the most common stackable assets are Cardano (ADA), Tezos (XTZ), and Tron (TRX).^([16](#notes.xhtml#chapter13-16))
2.  7\. Arbitrage. Crypto arbitrage is the practice of buying and selling different assets or currencies between different marketplaces to generating a profit. One classic example of arbitrage is vintage clothing. A given set of old clothes might cost $50 at a thrift store or an auction. At a vintage boutique or online, fashion-conscious customers might pay $500 for the same clothes. Consider the following arbitrage example: TD Bank (TD) trades on both the Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX) and the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). On a given day, let’s assume the stock trades for C$63.50 on the TSX and for US$47.00 on the NYSE. Let’s further assume that the exchange rate is US$1 = C$1.37, so that U$47 = C$64.39. Under this set of circumstances, a trader can purchase TD shares on the TSX for C$63.50 and can simultaneously sell the same security on the NYSE for US$47.00, which is the equivalent of C$64.39, ultimately yielding a profit of $0.89 per share ($64.39-$63.50) for this transaction.^([17](#notes.xhtml#chapter13-17))

## The Rise of NFTs

In June 2017, Matt Hall and John Watkinson of Larva Labs launched CryptoPunks, the first nonfungible tokens to be deployed on Ethereum. Through CryptoPunks’ unique 10,000-character features stored on the Ethereum blockchain, the first NFTs were created. With the supply of these “punks” capped at 10,000 unique characters, these virtual characters had scarcity and rarity programmed into them from the get-go.

In other words, each punk had its own unique features implanted in the Ethereum blockchain, like strands of unique DNA in a living cell.

In an interview with Art Market Guru, Hall explains:

> In early 2017, John Watkinson … had been fiddling around with a pixel character generator, and it was getting to the point where it was making lots of really cool, interesting characters. We just weren’t quite sure what to do with them. No idea really seemed to be strong enough until we discovered Ethereum and had the idea to make them unique and ownable on the blockchain. From there, we started the work of trying to actually figure out if it was possible and in what form it should take when actually implemented on Ethereum.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-3))

Following the success of CryptoPunks, in December 2017 Dieter Shirley of the CryptoKitties team and William Entriken proposed the ERC-721 standard,^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-4)) which made the implementation of NFTs on the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) seamless. The ERC-721 standard was largely based on the blueprint of the CryptoPunk code. Since the launch of CryptoKitties, several proposed standards for the optimization of NFTs and several alternatives to Ethereum have been proposed.

The success of CryptoPunks and CryptoKitties sparked the flame for this market. According to JPMorgan, the NFT market has seen explosive growth in 2021 as sales of the digital art items hover at a monthly rate of about $2 billion.

## What Are NFTs?

Assets that are identical across all conceivable value metrics are termed “fungible,” (see Figure [14-01](#chapter14.xhtml#fig30)). Examples include Bitcoins and dollar bills, which are all worth exactly the same amount.

By contrast, NFTs are a special kind of cryptoasset in which each token is unique. Because every NFT is unique, they can be used to authenticate ownership of digital assets like artworks, recordings, and virtual real estate, or even pets.

NFTs are digital representations of nonfungible valuables registered on a blockchain, each with a unique cryptographic identifier. The purpose of this identifier is to define the unique identity and ownership of the token on the blockchain, with real-time stamps of every transfer associated with that NFT. Right from the creation of an NFT transfers of ownership, every activity involving an NFT is recorded immutably on the blockchain, with timestamps for each transfer.

While there are endless possibilities for entities that can be represented as NFTs, they are typically used for digital works of art, like collectibles, images and videos. ## NFTs and the Subjective Theory of Value

The premise of the subjective theory of value is that the same item is valued differently by different people as a function of their respective individual emotions, preferences, thought processes, and life experiences. While a Jack Dorsey fan is willing to pay $2.5 million for the minted version of Dorsey’s first tweet, another Twitter user couldn’t care less about it. Also, while you may consider it ridiculous to break your pocket for a piece of digital artwork, a friend may consider it equally ridiculous to spend any money on a mere splash of random colors called an “art piece.” This is because for one person these items have a subjective meaning, while for another it is perceived purely from an objective and rational viewpoint. The subjective value of an asset explains why NFTs defy rational thinking and command outrageous prices.

## NFTs and Smart Contracts

Metaphorically speaking, smart contracts are the technological Lego pieces from which all sorts of NFTs are composed. All NFTs exist on smart contract blockchains, notably Ethereum. In that regard, smart contracts allow NFTs to exist as rare, unique, spendable representations of nonfungible entities on the blockchain.

As explained on the Verge: “Non-fungible” more or less means that it’s unique and can’t be replaced with something else. For example, a bitcoin is fungible—trade one for another bitcoin, and you’ll have exactly the same thing. A one-of-a-kind trading card, however, is non-fungible. If you traded it for a different card, you’d have something completely different.”^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-5))

According to the official Ethereum website, NFTs are marked by the following special properties:

1.  Each token minted has a unique identifier.
2.  They’re not directly interchangeable with other tokens.
3.  Each token has an owner, and this information is easily verifiable.
4.  The tokens live on Ethereum (which is one blockchain that NFTs are built on) and can be bought and sold on any Ethereum-based NFT market.

In other words, if you own an NFT:

1.  You can easily prove you own it.
2.  No one can manipulate it in any way.
3.  You can sell it, and in some cases, this will earn the original creator royalties on the resale.
4.  Or you can hold it forever, resting comfortably knowing your asset is secured by your wallet on Ethereum.

If you create an NFT:

1.  You can easily prove you’re the creator.
2.  You determine the scarcity.
3.  You can earn royalties every time it’s sold.
4.  You can sell it on any NFT market or peer-to-peer. You’re not locked into any platform, and you don’t need anyone to intermediate.^([6](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-6))

This is similar to how gold-backed dollar bills, which were redeemable for actual gold from any bank in the United states, operated. While not always redeemable, NFTs are cryptographic representations of non- fungible assets existing through smart contracts on a blockchain.

## Understanding Smart Contracts

Before the age of electronic computing, contracts and formal agreements required custodial oversight to be binding and enforceable. For instance, banks are needed to endorse a check for withdrawal, and courts are needed to endorse certain types of transactions.

Similarly, a smart contract is a computer program or a transaction protocol intended to automatically execute, control, and document legally relevant events and actions according to terms programmed in the smart contract program.^([7](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-7))

One prime objective in smart contract design is to eliminate the need for trusted intermediaries, which are involved at every level in a contractual relationship. Smart contracts replace these intermediaries with open-source codes. In place of the usual legal and supervisory infrastructures designed to oversee contract executions, smart contracts can be run on open-source codes. This helps in the general reduction of cost, saves time, and to a large degree prevents the possibility of corrupt practices.

Smart contract technology has been instrumental in the digital representation of nonfungible assets, which comes in handy for the creation and transfer of nonfungible identifiers. The Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM), for instance, has several ERC standards for the creation of various contract types. ERC is an acronym that stands for Ethereum Request for Comments. ERCs are application-level standards for Ethereum and can include token standards, name registries, library/package formats, and more.

ERC-721 is a free open standard that describes how to build non- fungible or unique tokens on the Ethereum blockchain. While most tokens are fungible (every token is the same as every other token), ERC-721 tokens are all unique. Think of them like rare, one-of-a-kind collectables.

The first recorded use of smart contracts was back in 1998 by Nick Szabo, with his Bitgold digital currency invention, which was built on a centralized network. The Ethereum blockchain, however, made it possible to execute immutable and completely autonomous smart contract protocols over a decentralized blockchain network.

## Domains

Domain names are readable translations of a website’s Internet Protocol (IP) address. Since IP addresses aren’t very readable, domain name registers were developed to provide a readable format for them. For example, Google’s IP address is 172.217.10.14, which isn’t very readable. In place of that, the domain name system (DNS) registry translates that to the readable version we all remember: [Google.com](https://google.com/).

DNS servers operate in a global network, which helps browsers translate domain names to IP addresses. Furthermore, Internet service providers connect users to the appropriate IP addresses based on their domain specification.

The global DNS network is controlled by the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN). This organization handles the top-level development and architecture of the overall domain name system. With this system, the user really does not own their domain: rather it is usually leased out to them by the ICANN, who by extension lease the domain-to-domain name providers. The implication is that domain name providers have full control over how much the domain name is going to be leased out for and can decide to claim a domain from a user.

With the blockchain domain name system, domain owners are in full control of their domains via their private keys, with ownership evident in the public address. This gives full custody to the owner, and domain purchase is a one-time affair.

Since domain names are nonfungible assets, like a fingerprint, NFT standards are how they are created on the blockchain. There have been typically 400 to 1200 [NFT.com](https://nft.com/) domain names registered per day in recent months, and more than 30,000 NFT-related domain names reg istered in 2021.^([10](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-10)) In March 2021, Unstoppable Domains, a company known for building blockchain domain names, sold the NFT domain “win.crypto” for about $100,000.^([11](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-11)) “While anyone can buy a traditional domain name through a registrar, users cannot buy permanent ownership,” said the company’s cofounder Brad Kam. “Traditional website domains sell for an average of ten dollars, and require a yearly registration fee to renew the domain. The historic sale of ‘win.crypto’ shows that users see value in permanent, verifiable domain ownership.”

With NFTs as a domain, owners can control activities on their domains with the use of their private key. The idea of domains as NFTs gives a website owner full sovereignty over their domain names, regardless of who endorses it or not. This will pave the way for absolute free speech for website owners.

Also, the Ethereum Name Service^([12](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-12)) uses NFTs to provide a memorable Ethereum address, which you can send to someone to receive ETHs. With NFTs as a domain, instead of having a numeric address, you can have a readable wallet address bearing your name or whatever name you decide on.

## Government Registries as Oracles for NFTs

At the time of writing, there isn’t any regulation in place for nonfungible tokens. In other words, NFTs are not yet recognized as intellectual property or as title deeds for physical properties anywhere in the world. This makes their regulation all the more complex. However, as the space matures, government regulations and frameworks indicating how NFTS should be perceived and identified legally will likely emerge. We may see the emergence of blockchain-powered government registries that will act as oracles for the verification of existing NFTs as well as new NFT mints.

Since there are no such systems in use yet, how they will work is still undefined. However, similar applications and existing innovations give us a good idea of what we are likely to see.

## Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs)

With the tokenization of title deeds and IP rights on the blockchain, the use of DAO networks as self- verifying oracles will be ideal for the verification of most digital NFT types. An “oracle” sends data from the outside world, such as the daily temperature or the number of votes a political candidate received, to a blockchain such as Ethereum. A smart contract on the blockchain can then use the data, typically to decide whether to dispense money and to whom. Although these types of oracles do not yet exist for NFTs, decentralized price oracles are used by several decentralized finance platforms for external data such as price.

Similarly, centralized blockchain oracles will be useful for government registries in verifying NFTs. This will be ideal for NFTs representing IPs and title deeds, since they need to be government approved.

## Copyright Infringements and Verifying Ownership with NFTs

As noted, because the NFT space is largely unregulated, art theft using NFTs has become widespread. “Artists like Derek Laufman have had their work minted as NFTs and sold without their permission.”^([14](#notes.xhtml#chapter14-14)) Someone with a fraudulent OpenSea account claimed to be Laufman, and the impersonator was selling artworks from Laufman’s social media accounts.

Laufman is just one of many artists who have been victims of NFT theft. The process is usually simple: steal an artist’s work, mint it on an NFT marketplace or auction site, and sell it as yours, or impersonate the owner and sell it under the guise of being the artist.

There still is no standardized way of verifying NFTs. However, in the coming years we may see the integration of digital rights management (DRM) systems with NFT minting platforms and marketplaces, making it possible to restrict the ownership of an NFT to a given individual or institutional entity.

For example, we may see the integration of NFTs into established authentication tools like Google Authenticator, with the tokens ID verifiable via its QR code format on such authentication DRM systems. We may see specialized mobile devices, desktop devices, or peripheral devices (similar to the Kindle device and hardware wallets) used for the storage, authentication, and management of an NFT portfolio.

## The Future of NFTs

Like Bitcoin, NFTs will eventually settle into every aspect of life. With government registries around the world serving as oracles to verify who owns what, all forms of property rights will eventually be tokenized and attached to a unique private key.

Also, with the fractionalization of nonfungible tokens into fungible tokens, we are likely to see NFTs merging with decentralized financial platforms for the purpose of liquidity. There will come a time when NFTs will serve as collateral for taking out fungible loans.

With the way the space is evolving, there are really no limits to what we will be seeing.

Supporters of the metaverse envision its users working, playing, and staying connected with friends through everything from concerts and conferences to virtual trips around the world.^([3](#notes.xhtml#chapter15-3))

“With the Internet, you can be online without necessarily interacting with other people, but the foundation of the metaverse is about digital human interaction. It’s about people sharing a virtual universe together, be it for work, school, exercise, or simply for fun.”

Central to the metaverse concept is the idea that virtual, 3D environments, accessible and interactive in real time, will become the transformative medium for social and business engagement. The metaverse has the potential to become a robust $800 billion digital economy by 2024,^([4](#notes.xhtml#chapter15-4)) where users can create, buy, and sell goods and services. Ideally, the metaverse will facilitate interoperability, allowing you to take virtual items like clothes or cars from one platform to another. With digital worlds continuing to embed themselves in our daily lives, the metaverse could represent over a $1 trillion market opportunity, according to crypto investment firm Grayscale.^([5](#notes.xhtml#chapter15-5)) The metaverse might allow you to take the avatar you create everywhere as quickly as you can copy your profile picture from one social network to another. These metaverse visions are compatible with Web 3.0, a term covering decentralized Internet services where users retain more personal control over the data they put online.

In his 1992 novel *Snow Crash,* Neal Stephenson famously coined the word “metaverse,” referring to a 3D virtual world inhabited by avatars of real people. The metaverse has partly become a dream for the future of the Internet. To some extent, it has become a neat way to encapsulate new technologies, such as virtual reality and augmented reality and some current trends in online infrastructure, including the growth of real-time 3D worlds.

## DECENTRALIZATION

Web 3.0 is all about making the Internet more egalitarian by creating a new version with decentralized, open, autonomous social networks, search engines, and e-commerce marketplaces with more excellent utility. The decentralization of Web 3.0 is built on the infrastructure of the blockchain decentralized ledger technology (DLT).

Aside from decentralization, a defining feature of Web 3.0 is that it’s also permissionless and trustless. Web 3.0 is permissionless because it’s free of control by centralized authorities. In a Web 3.0 world, people control their data and bounce around from social media to email to shopping using a single personalized account, creating a public record on the blockchain of all of that activity. Web 3.0 is trustless because intermediaries aren’t necessary for transactions between multiple parties. In my chapter “Introduction to Decentralized Finance,” I have described DeFi, a function of Web 3.0, facilitating the execution of real-world financial transactions on the blockchain without the need for centralized authorities such as banks or the government.^([23](#notes.xhtml#chapter15-23))

Staking is the means by which many cryptocurrencies verify their transactions, and it allows participants to earn rewards on their holdings.

Blockchain technology is central to the metaverse and Web 3.0, Goldman Sachs said in a research report. The report claimed that blockchain is the only technology that can “uniquely identify any virtual object independent of a central authority.” This ability to identify and track ownership will be crucial to the functioning of the metaverse. ## Data Regulatory Compliance

Many companies today operate in more than one country, and thus face multiple regulatory regimes.^([5](#notes.xhtml#bm1-5)) For government or industry regulatory compliance, or e-discovery, organizations may have to prove to auditors or the courts that there has been no malicious or negligent corruption of digital records, workflows, and logs.

## Business Efficiencies

Reconciling transactions across individual and private ledgers takes a lot of time and is prone to errors. Distributed records management systems involving multiple parties are experiencing slow processing, high costs, and high risk of errors, and the result is often unreliable and inconsistent data.

Digital signature technologies come at a high cost, and the certificates are difficult to acquire.^([6](#notes.xhtml#bm1-6)) Additionally, there’s also uncertainty around the impartiality of the third-party provider.

Moreover, this is the era of smart contracts, transactional elements of a legal agreement executing as code on the blockchain. How do we reconcile the smart contract with the corresponding digital contract records? As the industry moves toward the implementation of smart contracts, contract professionals (such as lawyers and auditors) may need to be able to read and decipher them, or even learn how to write them.

## The Solution

Blockchain provides a trusted, independent, and cost-efficient mechanism for multiparty transactional records management. There are inherent problems with the public distributed ledger technology (DLT), particularly around the pseudonymity of the parties’ identities, as well as privacy and confidentiality. We can leverage additional enterprise tech nologies to mitigate this. I’ll cover this in the solution architecture (see Figure Appendix-03).

Blockchain stores a cryptographic hash of the data, workflow processes, and signatures for each record, rendering them effectively immutable, more valid, more authentic, and more reliable. Blockchain is appealing to auditors and litigators, because it effectively certifies corrupt-free data as proof or record.

Blockchain technology could also be used to verify the workflow steps that a record went through during its creation and management. The technology can create a cryptographic hash of each step, effectively creating an immutable proof of process for the record.

Blockchain is an essential technology for records management professionals to understand, because it has broad implications for securing and authenticating intellectual property at a lower cost and higher efficiency. A records repository can store any digital object, including audio, video, or even software. A cryptographic hash of the record can be storedon the blockchain, together with a timestamp, serving effectively as proof of copyright.

Blockchain also provides for an advantage over legacy centralized digital signature technologies. The signatures, fingerprints, and timestamps created for authentication purposes are stored on the distributed ledger, providing proof of data integrity and authenticity without the need of a third party.

## Scenario

This is a simple workflow that describes how the proposed solution architecture can be leveraged to synchronize the smart contract with the contract records for multiple parties.

1.  Ann uploads a contract record into the records management repository (essentially a document library) and generates a URL hyperlink for the document.
2.  Ann digitally signs the contract, using a blockchain application programming interface (API) and generates a unique cryptographic hash for the document.
3.  Ann configures a workflow as the contract proposer and configures Bob as the reviewer.
4.  When Ann clicks “Save,” a smart contract proposal is created on the permissioned distributed ledger. She includes as properties the cryptographic hash and document URL as unique references.
5.  Bob receives an email notification with a link to review the contract record. Bob is prompted to accept or reject the contract proposal.
6.  Bob accepts the contract proposal and is redirected to sign the contract record.
7.  After signing the contract record, the original smart contract is archived, and a new fully executed contract is created on the dis tributed ledger, with the two parties having entered voluntarily into the contract.
8.  The smart contract is now synchronized with the contract record, joined by a reference to the hash and the document URL.

## Solution Architecture

The architectural components:

1.  Smart contract workflow functionality blockchain permissioned distributed ledger.
2.  Smart contract application functionality (software as a service, or SAS).
3.  Enterprise records management portal (SharePoint online or on-premise).

## Smart Contract Workflow Functionality

Ideally, workflow would be governed using a smart contract language for modeling rights and obligations for multiparty processes in any business, providing high integrity and privacy guarantees. The smart contracts would encode the rights of the parties as choices that they can exercise, and obligations as agreements that they agree to.

A permissionless distributed ledger technology conducts transactions pseudonymously. The identities of parties can be hard to establish. Regulatory compliance dictates that parties to a transaction be identifiable. The smart contract workflow functionality provides for a permissioned ledger, reinforcing the essential properties of a smart contract:

1.  Proof of rights and obligations
2.  Confidential execution
3.  Evidentiary trail
4.  Formal verifiability

The smart contract programming language should be intuitive and support formal methods for catching design-time errors. The language should also be accessible enough for lawyers and contract professionals to at least understand, if not write.

The solution architecture will ultimately be a next-generation, secure, transparent, and regulatory-compliant infrastructure, synchronizing the records, workflow processes, and signatures for the smart contract and contract records for multiple parties to a transaction.
