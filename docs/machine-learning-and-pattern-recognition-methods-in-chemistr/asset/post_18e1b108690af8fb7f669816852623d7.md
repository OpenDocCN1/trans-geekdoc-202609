The effect of equality constraints on the reduction of the rotational ambiguity can be very different. The relative position of the known component in the area of feasible solution (AFS) explains the behavior. A measure is suggested that the impact of the equality constraints (Sawall et al., 2020). The representation of this measure in the area of feasible solutions shows strong variations in the restrictiveness of equality constraints. This characteristic number can also be considered as restrictiveness measure. A relatively large reduced AFS shows that the equality constraint cannot reduce the rotational ambiguity. The profile that is locked by the equality constraint is compatible to many other solutions. In contrast to this, a small reduced AFS means that the choice of the equality constraint is very restrictive for the factorization problem. An equality constraint strongly reduces the AFS if it has a high restrictiveness.

Similarly, a weak AFS reduction corresponds to a low restrictiveness. A restrictiveness estimator is based on the volume of the reduced AFS under the equality constraint. The constraint is a measure of restrictiveness named $\kappa_1$. The ambiguity estimators not only calculate for single points, but compute the estimators for every point in the AFS. ## 6. Hard models constraint

In evolving systems where concentration changes over time according to chemical kinetics or chemical equilibria (the so-called hard modeling) can be used in a powerful way to calculate concentration profiles by imposing the mathematical functions of the chemical model as constraints. These kinds of constraints generally reduce the range of feasible solutions and often result in unique solutions for components that can be described with a proper physical-chemical model, provided the initial conditions are precisely known (Beyramysultan et al., 2014). In this section, the effects of a hard modeling constraint on the reduction of the extent of rotational ambiguity have been investigated (Golshan, Abdollahi & Maeder, 2012; Khodadadi Karimvand & Abdollahi, 2018).

A simulated data set was analyzed. The kinetic model used for data was the irreversible parallel reactions in the form of $A \xrightarrow{k_1} B; F \xrightarrow{k_2} D$, assuming that only species $A$ and $F$ are present in the reaction mixture. The kinetic equations, Eqs. (7.23)-(7.26), can be used to describe the concentration profiles of the reactants $(A, F)$ and the products $(B, D)$ of the considered two first-order reactions.

```
$[A_i] = [A_0] \exp(-k_1 t_i)$
$[B_j] = [A_0] (1 - \exp(-k_1 t_i))$
$[F_i] = [F_0] \exp(-k_2 t_i)$
$[D_i] = [F_0] (1 - \exp(-k_2 t_i))$
```

Also to recognize in the realm of three-component systems, we assume that F has no absorption in the spectral region investigated. Concentration profiles for the reactants and the product simulated based on chemical models (Eqs. 7.237.26), with the rate constants equal to $k_1=0.05s^{-1}$ and $k_2=0.15s^{-1}$, with $[A_0]=[F_0]=1M$. Measurement times were between 1 and 47 or 4 and 47 s. The simulated absorbance spectra of data X were then calculated by post-multiplication of concentration with pure spectra. To more rigorously test the methods, random errors by normal distributions with mean zero and standard deviation up to 0.3% of averaged absorbance were added to the simulated data. The concentration and spectra profiles and the resulting “measured” spectra for data X are plotted in Fig. 7.12. It contains 24 spectra corresponding to the calculated profiles. The measurement time for this data has been chosen between 1 and 47 with an increment of 2. If starting at time zero the rotational ambiguity is very small.

The grid search method performs the calculation of all feasible solutions fitting the data and fulfilling the constraints of non-negativity and the hard model, also delivering the rate constants for the modeled reaction. The introduction of the hard constraint results in perfectly defined concentration profiles for $A$ and $B$ and reduced AFS for the concentration.
