# 1.3 Hyperspectral imaging as a method for microplastics detection

One major challenge in microplastics research is the lack of standardized analytical and quantification techniques, making it difficult to compare results across studies (Horton et al., 2017). Many of the common methodologies include density separation of plastics from solid matrices by filtration, which is time-consuming and can lead to the loss of material, especially when polymers differ and separation solutions are present (Frias et al., 2018). There are several chemical analytical methods that rely on thermal degradation and visual analysis. Two common thermoanalytical methods are pyrolysis chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry and thermogravimetric, but these will not be discussed here as they are destructive and provide a measurement of polymer weight rather than particle dimensions or amount of particles (Käppler et al., 2016). Visual analysis is a valid technique to identify particle particles as small as 0.25–2 mm based on their physical features, but this method is highly subject to error and variation greatly between observers (Ruggera, Gori, & Lubello, 2020). Stereomicroscopy is capable of analyzing samples in the micron order of magnitude and providing physical characteristics and abundance; however, it does not provide information on composition (Yang, Zhang, Kang, Kang, & Wu, 2021).

Given the limitations of methodologies based on thermodegradation and visual analysis, hyperspectral imaging has emerged as a reliable, non-destructive, non-invasive technique to characterize microplastic waste, thereby expanding the possibilities for plastic monitoring (Serranti, Palmieri, Bonifazi, & Cázar, 2018). For the purposes of characterizing and identifying microplastics, the spectra can be collected by Raman, FTIR, and NIR—although a wide variety of other techniques are possible. Hyperspectral imaging reveals in such a way that a spectrum in a full wavelength range is collected for each pixel. The spectral resolution, then, refers to the interval between each wavelength in the selected wavelength range, with a smaller interval corresponding to a higher spectral resolution (Amigo, 2020).
