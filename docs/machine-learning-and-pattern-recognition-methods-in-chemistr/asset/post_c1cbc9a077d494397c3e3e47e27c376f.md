
With correct information on the zero concentration regions of components, Manne’s theorems will be successfully applied to produce the unique solution(s) provided that the necessary conditions were fulfilled. However, these conditions regard the properties of the most unknown true profiles of the components. This is why, we can call the perceived phenomenon as the profile-based uniqueness instead of using the term “data-based uniqueness” (Rajkó et al., 2015).

In the literature of chemometrics, resolution theorems of Manne are discussed and explained by local-rank information. But, recently, it was noted that local rank is a mathematical concept that is not necessarily in concordance with chemical information (Akkari Lakh et al., 2017).

In what follows, the concordance of the local-rank information is pursued with the presence of windows in a simulated bilinear data set. The SMCR calculations for obtaining the Borgen plots and feasible solutions were made by the FAC-PACK software (version 1.20—the generalized Borgen plots module). This software can be freely downloaded at http://www.math.uni-roostock.de/facpack/

Simulated example is a typical noise-free data, which is used for basically evaluating the concordance of the local-rank with the presence window of chemical species. The simulated data set is obtained from the chromatograms and spectra shown in Fig. 7.3, corresponding to a mixture of three co-eluting components in a chromatographic system monitoring with a diode array detector. As it can be deduced the dimension of the simulated data set is 34 rows (elution times) by 51 columns (wavelengths). As it can be deduced from the elution and spectral profiles, there are three different local-rank windows on the rows of the data matrix comprising windows with rank one, two, and three.

Now, the concordance of the local-rank information with the presence of windows in the simulated data is investigated. The agreement of the local-rank information with actual presence windows of the components in noise-free matrices is of great importance because the local-rank constraint is applied based on the correctness of this concordance. For instance, when selectivity is applied as a constraint, it is forced that just one component participate to reconstruct the rows or columns with local-rank-one. The main question arises here: Is there any possibility that more than one component participates in the local-rank window? Alternatively, in general, may rank deficiency be present in the subwindows of a data matrix?

## Uniqueness in resolving multivariate chemical data

![](img/1aed977a858463e62fc6af1793e4677c_160_0.png)

**Fig. 7.6 (A)** One of possible elution patterns among all the simulated data; (B) local-rank windows in rows of data.

As stated before, the coordinates of spectra of the local-rank window would be in row space because all have the same shape. Thus, the six spectra of local-rank window are indicated as a single circle in Fig. 7.5A–C; other data points are not shown for clarity. Regarding Borgen plot in Fig. 7.5, the local-rank window is located inside the feasible region of the third component. Thus, spectral profiles with rank one can be considered as pure spectrum for the third component. Fig. 7.5A represents a set of feasible solutions that indicate local-rank window in data. However, one can find several sets of solutions with this property by fixing the position of the third spectrum and changing the other positions. As can be seen in elution profiles in Fig. 7.5A, there is a selective window for the third component (elution times 29–34 in arbitrary unit), and it shows that the contribution of other components for building the local-rank window is zero. However, it is illustrated in Fig. 7.5B that a linear combination of spectral profiles of the second and third component can construct rows with rank one, i.e., the linear combination of points 1 and 2 in Fig. 7.5B. The corresponding elution profiles confirm the presence of two components in the rank-one sub-matrix.

The local-rank window, in the way, may be constructed from linear combination of three different components, e.g., the points 3, 4, and 5 in Fig. 7.5C. Consequently, due to rotational ambiguity, one, two, or three components may be present in this local-rank sub-matrix. Possibly previous chemical knowledge confirms the presence of more than one component in this window. In such cases, the number of recognizable contributions in local window is lower than the number of chemical components, and for this reason we call “local rank deficiency.” It is important to note that all sets of feasible solutions of a system satisfy the local-rank information, but the presence patterns may differ from one set to the other because of local-rank deficiency. Thus, a number of various presence windows may exist in the feasible region of a component.

Therefore, if we have not any additional information just nonnegativity, we have to use data-based uniqueness concept (Rajkó et al., 2015), and the forced improper application of Manne’s theorems can cause artifact/biased results, sometimes to a critically high degree. That is why the Manne’s theorems are necessary but not sufficient for getting a unique resolution (Rajkó et al., 2015) unless with further information about the presence window of components. If the presence window of each component is available, then information based on the zero concentration region can be used, and Manne’s theorem can be successfully applied. For example, prior information before elution windows in case of chromatographic data or presence/absence of components in case of multiple data is valuable information to use SMCR analysis in which applying Manne’s theorem will produce a reliable result.

An efficient tool to study the conditions for achieving unique profile(s) using zero concentration region information is principal component analysis (PCA) (Pearson, 1901; Tauler & Walczak, 2009). The possibility to express data as basis sets of both row and column directions helps to understand the relationship between the abstract data. On the other hand, there is a natural duality between the row and column spaces of the data matrix using minimal constraints. First time, Henry (2005) introduced the duality principle in chemometrics and described it for multivariate receptor modeling of compositional data of airborne pollution. Later Rajkő (2006) showed that there is a natural duality between the row and column spaces of data matrix using the nonnegativity property of data set. He generalized the duality principle for the calculation of feasible solutions and also visualized it for a two-component system. It is remarkable that this mathematical relation between row and column spaces provides an efficient tool to transfer the information of the considered space to its dual space.
