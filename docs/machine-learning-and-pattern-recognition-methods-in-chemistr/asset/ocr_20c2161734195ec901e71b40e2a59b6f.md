In cases where the SMCR research hypothesis is true, the final model contains no active constraints. However, in many cases, due to noise and nonlinear behaviors of chemical and/or spectroscopic response, active constraints are present in the resultant SMCR model. The detection of active constraints is important from both a theoretical and from a practical point of view. Tests for active constraints can be simply done by turning the constraints off at the solution point and then comparing the constrained fit with the unconstrained one. In the case of strong active constraints, when the difference between the two fits is greater than what might be expected from noise, a significant deviation from the original hypothesis may occur. The research hypothesis is rejected if many strong active constraints are present at the final solution. It worth noting that in most cases, instead of producing a unique solution, these methods produce a range of feasible solutions, which fulfill the constraints. Therefore, if one gets a lack-of-fit with no active constraints, one cannot prove that the hypothesized model is correct, as in testing all scientific hypotheses.

Most often, the first step in fitting SMCR models is to select some initial starting point followed by iterative refinement while considering physicochemical limitations in the form of constraints to converge to a meaningful solution.
