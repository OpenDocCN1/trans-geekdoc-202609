In another study, simulated and experimental data sets were used to evaluate the advantages of applying soft trilinearity constraints. In an augmented simulated data, the nonideal trilinear behavior of the analyte was modeled by shifting its peak maxima and also changing its peak width in different matrices. In this work, the impact of using different levels of trilinearity constraints was investigated on the range of feasible solutions of one of the data components (the analyte of interest). It was illustrated by choosing smaller α, the size of the analyte AFS and the width of the analyte concentration band decreased. As was shown in Tavakkoli et al. (2020), the estimation of the control parameter α is a crucial step in the implementation of soft trilinearity constraints as it determines the accuracy of the results. This parameter can be tuned based on prior knowledge about the chemical system under study. The results of concentration profile showed the risk of obtaining a distorted profile and biased quantification. In addition, the PARAFAC2 model, which is known to give unique solutions in the analysis of non-linear data, was used for analyzing the simulated data. However, the solution of PARAFAC2 with non-negativity constraints was not within the range of feasible solutions, and as a result, the obtained concentration values were highly biased. This was because PARAFAC2 can only model certain types of non-trilinearity conditions, not all the possible ones, especially when there are changes in the peak widths. Similar conclusions were obtained in the analysis of the experimental data. For more detail, please refer to Tavakkoli et al. (2020).

# 4. Conclusions

By applying soft constraints in SMCR methods, small deviations from the constraint settings are allowed. Apart from the algorithm used, this approach may yield more accurate results in many circumstances than obtained by applying hard constraints. The estimated profiles of the SMCR methods with soft constraints are more likely to have fewer active constraints and distortions. These advantages can be attributed to the increased control and flexibility in the implementation of the constraints. Several software implementations are available to users for incorporating a variety of soft constraints, most of which are described in the text. Among them, those who calculate the whole range of feasible solutions under soft constraints are especially helpful. Soft constrained AFSs often have fewer active constraints and are more likely to fit the original research hypothesis in comparison with hard constrained AFS segments. The boundaries can be tuned to allow acceptable levels of noise in a data set to propagate as small deviations into estimated profiles and estimate spectra that would otherwise not be allowed with hard constraints. In this chapter, the advantages of implementation of soft hard constraints are reviewed using several simulated and experimental data, which were published before. It has been demonstrated that in many cases, applying soft constraints, for one of the components, can improve the accuracy of the AFSs. Additionally, the approximation of the AFSs with soft constraints is often smooth and time efficient.
