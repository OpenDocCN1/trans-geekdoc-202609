To the end of monitoring and characterizing microplastics in sediments, some protocols recommend a lower size limit of 100 µm based on sampling capabilities (Frias et al., 2018). Microplastics are further categorized according to their origin. Primary microplastics are those manufactured to microscopic sizes, whereas secondary microplastics are derived from the breakdown of larger particles in the environment (Frias & Nash, 2019). With the persistence of hundreds of thousands of years, all of the plastic introduced into the environment remains unmineralized; instead, plastics fragment is due to ultraviolet (UV) degradation, oxidation, and abrasion (Barnes, Galgani, Thompson, & Barlaz, 2009). This degradation means methods of characterization that depend on spectral libraries are expected to fall short, given the spectra of weathered plastics differ greatly from those of standard plastics (Dong et al., 2020). Taking all into account, the very characteristics that make plastics useful—persistent, inexpensive, water-resistant, versatile—make them worrisome in marine and terrestrial environments.

# 1.2 Persistent, ubiquitous—And miscounted?

The persistence of plastics contributes to their ubiquity in the natural environment. Even the most seemingly pristine and far-flung sites are not safe from plastic pollution. Thanks to long-range transport, plastics can be found in places as remote as polar ecosystems (Haland & Hercke, 2019). Their size, meanwhile, allows for uptake by living organisms. The recent (and undiscovered) of microplastics in human placentas is one concerning example of this phenomenon (Ragusa et al., 2021).
