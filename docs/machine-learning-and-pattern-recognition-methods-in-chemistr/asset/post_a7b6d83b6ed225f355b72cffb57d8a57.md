## 6.1 Spectral curve-fitting constraint

Hard modeling constraints can also be applied for spectral profiles if mathematical functional forms are imposed (peak-shaped models). Spectral hard modeling is also known as curve fitting or peak fitting. Curve-fitting methods have an old history in the literature and are used for quantitative resolution of systems. In these methods, spectra are fitted to sums of peak-shaped curves, e.g., Gaussian or Lorentzian shapes or combinations of Gaussian and Lorentzian functions, which are known as Voigt functions (Thorne, 1974).

The effect of spectral hard modeling constraints on the feasible solutions of soft methods for simulated and real data sets was investigated. For each data set, the area of feasible solutions (AFS) were calculated. The AFS was transformed into real profiles and fitted to the summation of Voigt functions. All points in the feasible region fit the model well; thus, one can conclude that the peak-shaped models did not reduce the range of feasible bands. The data sets were adequately fitted to different Voigt models with the minimum number of peaks that gave a statistically sufficient model.

Achieving an accurate, precise model of concentration profiles with hard modeling methods requires a significant amount of chemical information about the studied system. When sufficient chemical information is known, applying models for concentration profiles in SMCR methods can result in real, unique solutions. Lacking this type of information in the peak-shaped models of spectra results in non-unique solutions for spectral fitting. There are laws that govern the shapes of concentration profile models, i.e., the law of mass action, but there are no strong laws about spectra. We can only approximate them with a selection of Gaussian, Lorentzian, or Voigt functions. Applying a Voigt peak-shaped model without additional information as constraints on spectroscopic signals does not have any effect on rotational ambiguity in resolving bilinear data.

## 7. General rule for uniqueness (GRU) (Karimvand et al., 2020)

In research, the duality concept is applied to propose a general rule for unifying all the different information and constraints that can produce a unique solution in SMCR methods. This general rule for uniqueness (GRU) describes how different information about a chemical component can help to resolve its corresponding profile uniquely. Moreover, GRU provides a better understanding about the uniqueness concept and can be a guide for developing new constraints in SMCR to get more accurate solutions.

The duality concept is a powerful tool in explaining and interpreting numerous features of MCR methods. Based on this concept, a general rule was employed to explain the unique solutions of MCR methods as a result of applying different constraints. The GRU states that any information able to fix the subspace of all complementary components in space results in a unique solution for the analyte in the other space. This generalization is presented for constraints such as trilinear, equality, zero concentration region, local rank, non-negativity under DBU conditions, and even to the NAS. GRU is a proper index to find the conditions and information in different chemical systems probably leading to a unique solution in applying MCR methods. Hence, by fulfilling the proposed rule of uniqueness, it is possible to successfully introduce new constraints and apply different constraints to achieve a unique solution.

### 7.1 Generalized rule for uniqueness (GRU)

Based on the Bouguer-Lambert-Beer law, a spectroscopic data set can be written as a product of two matrices, C and A, containing the concentration and spectral profiles of individual species, respectively. Hence, a bilinear decomposition of a given data set is

```
D = CAᵀ (7.27)
```

where D is the measured matrix with I (observations) and J (variables). Additionally, a bilinear data can be decomposed by singular value decomposition (SVD):

```
D = USVᵀ (7.28)
```

In Eq.(7.28), U and V represent the left and right eigenvectors, respectively, and S is a diagonal matrix of singular values. Also, X and Y are the coordinates of row (V-space) and column (U-space) Uniqueness in resolving multivariate chemical data 169

row (V-space) and column (U-space); in turn, 0 is a matrix containing zeroes with the suitable dimension ($I \times J$). Moreover, Eq. (7.28) shows a natural duality between non-negativity property of the data set. Each point defined by t in the considered space corresponds to a unique hyperplane. 

```
n = tS⁻¹
```

where t represents the coordinates of a fixed point, S is the matrix of singular values, and n is the hyperplane coefficients. Point t in one space has a certain hyperplane defined by Eq. (7.30) in another space (dual space).

```
nᵀ z = 0
```

In the equation of hyperplane, z is a general variable representing the coordinates of an arbitrary point in V- or U-spaces and 0 is a scalar.

Hence, a certain point in the abstract space corresponds to a unique hyperplane in its dual space. A unique profile in the result of an MCR method is equivalent to a certain point in the corresponding abstract space of the profile. In fact, the duality concept states that a proper hyperplane should be defined in the dual space to achieve a unique solution for any analyte (the correct and sufficient meaningful physical/chemical information can define the correct and proper hyperplane corresponding to true solution). Therefore, accurate information must be used to determine the hyperplane by the subspace related to all complementary compounds (this specific hyperplane is a subspace of all interfering compounds without the contribution of the analyte). Thus, the information of the profile constraints resulting in a unique solution in the vector space of the data would lead to fix a hyperplane in its dual space.

> The necessary condition to get a unique solution for the analyte is the possibility of the definition of the hyperplane related to all interfering components in the dual space by implementing adequate physical/chemical constraints (or information).

Indeed, for a (N) component system, a unique solution always fixes a point in one space and an (N–1)-dimensional hyperplane of component components in the dual space. This is a general rule of uniqueness in SMCR methods.
