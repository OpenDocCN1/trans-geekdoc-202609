# 6.1 Spectral curve-fitting constraint

Hard modeling constraints can also be applied for spectral profiles if mathematical functional forms are imposed (peak-shaped models). Spectral hard modeling is also known as curve fitting or peak fitting. Curve-fitting methods have an old history in the literature and are used for quantitative resolution of systems. In these methods, spectra are fitted to sums of peak-shaped curves, e.g., Gaussian or Lorentzian shapes or combinations of Gaussian and Lorentzian functions, which are known as Voigt functions (Thorne, 1974).

The effect of spectral hard modeling constraints on the feasible solutions of soft methods for simulated and real data sets was investigated. For each data set, the area of feasible solutions (AFS) were calculated. The AFS was transformed into real profiles and fitted to the summation of Voigt functions. All points in the feasible region fit the model well; thus, one can conclude that the peak-shaped models did not reduce the range of feasible bands. The data sets were adequately fitted to different Voigt models with the minimum number of peaks that gave a statistically sufficient model.

Achieving an accurate, precise model of concentration profiles with hard modeling methods requires a significant amount of chemical information about the studied system. When sufficient chemical information is known, applying models for concentration profiles in SMCR methods can result in real, unique solutions. Lacking this type of information in the peak-shaped models of spectra results in non-unique solutions for spectral fitting. There are laws that govern the shapes of concentration profile models, i.e., the law of mass action, but there are no strong laws about spectra. We can only approximate them with a selection of Gaussian, Lorentzian, or Voigt functions. Applying a Voigt peak-shaped model without additional information as constraints on spectroscopic signals does not have any effect on rotational ambiguity in resolving bilinear data.
