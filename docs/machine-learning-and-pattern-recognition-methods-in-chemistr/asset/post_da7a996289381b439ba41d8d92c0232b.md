# 1. Introduction

Self-modeling curve resolution (SMCR) techniques are widely applied for resolving chemical data to the pure-component spectra and composition profiles. In most cases, the resolution of bilinear chemical data sets using self-modeling curve resolution (SMCR) methods produces a range of features in multicomponent systems. Hence, the most significant challenge of the chemometricians who use the SMCR methods is to reduce the range of feasible solutions by applying proper information in the form of constraints. The mathematical solutions generated by SMCR obey the applied constraints coming from a priori physicochemical information about the system under investigation.

The appropriate constraints can narrow down the band of feasible solutions, even in most favorable cases, can produce unique solutions, and improve the accuracy of the results of SMCR methods. This highlights the importance of studying the constraints used in SMCR methods, and the need for new constraints to achieve more accurate results. Several have demonstrated that applying some constraints in SMCR methods, including trilinearity, zero concentration region, correspondence, local rank, and non-negativity, result in unique profiles.

Undoubtedly, the non-negativity constraint is a natural and most frequent used for resolving many kinds of chemical data, especially spectroscopic data that significantly restrict the feasible solutions (Lawton & Sylvester, 1971). Interestingly, under some conditions, this simple natural constraint is sufficient for achieving a unique profile (partial uniqueness) (Rajkó, Abdollahi, Beyramysoltan, & Omidikia, 2015). In 2015, the necessary conditions for a partially unique resolution based on the non-negativity constraint were proposed with the so-called data-based uniqueness (DBU) theorem. DBU can predict the unique profiles when imposing only the non-negativity constraint by investigating the selectivity and zero values of the resolved profiles.

Manne (1995) proposed "resolution theorems" that describe the conditions for getting unique concentration and spectral profiles under local-rank information (Manne, 1995). However, successful prediction of the unique profiles based on the resolution subjects is to the implementation of the zero-region constraint in iterative curve resolution methods (Tavakkoli, Rajkó, & Abdollahi, 2018). Trilinearity is another powerful constraint for exploiting the trilinear profile over a multiset data analysis (Ghaffari & Abdollahi, 2018). Trilinearity constraint can be imposed when experiments are repeated under different initial conditions. It follows a trilinear model which can be expressed as the product of a triad of linear vectors. In addition to the mentioned constraints, entering the information about the shape of a specific profile to the SMCR methods as the equality constraint leads to a unique solution (Beyramsoltan, Rajkó, & Abdollahi, 2013).

The other group of constraints that is used for narrowing the feasible band is information about the absence of a chemical species in a part of the contribution (or spectral) profile. This information can be applied as zero-region constraint when analyzing a single data matrix in iterative curve resolution methods. In case of multiset data analysis, the information about the presence/absence of each component in the matrices analyzing together is considered as the correspondence of species constraint as “nonexistence information.” The circumstances that the nonexistence information can result in a unique solution are derived and can be find in Akbari Lake, Rajkó, and Abdollahi (2017).

Defining and introducing hard modeling constraint generally reduces the range of feasible solutions and often results in unique solutions. The hard constraint is introduced to force some or all of the concentration profiles to follow chemical model, which is refined at each iterative cycle of the optimization process of soft modeling. Therefore, the concentration and spectral profiles, and the physicochemical parameters (e.g., kinetic rate constants) can be obtained although there are compounds or interferences that do not take part in the explicit model.

The mathematical concept of duality was initially introduced by Henry in the chemometrics literature (Henry, 2005). Later, Rajkó showed that it is possible to determine the feasible regions of two-component systems using the duality principles. Duality as a mathematical concept provides a relation between the row and column spaces of a data matrix. Simply, the duality principle states that each data point in a space (e.g., the column space) corresponds to one special directed hyperplane in the dual space (the row space).

In this chapter, the circumstances and the rules that govern to achieve the unique real solutions through any of the constraint will be explained. A collected knowledge in this subject is completely rare in literature.

# 2. Non-negativity constraint

Non-negativity is the first and most general natural constraint in different fields of chemistry. For instance, when the data are measurements of counts, color intensities, amount of absorption, or spectral amplitudes, numbers do not have a physical interpretation. Thus, non-negativity is frequently applied as the minimal constraint of the multivariate curve resolution (MCR) studies. Under non-negativity constraints, feasible regions are bounded (Lawton & Sylvester, 1971). The non-negativity constraint reduces the range of feasible solutions. In some favorable cases, a unique solution can be obtained. Data-based uniqueness (DBU) refers to decompositions that non-negativity constraint is sufficient (Rajkó et al., 2015). Different levels of uniqueness were defined in Rajkó et al. (2015) as non-uniqueness, fractional uniqueness, partial uniqueness, and full uniqueness.

## 2.1 Data-based uniqueness

Data-based uniqueness condition can be defined as the following theorem in two equivalent forms in general, i.e., the theorem is formulated in such a way that is independent of the number of components.

### 2.1.1 Data-based uniqueness theorem (Rajkó et al., 2015)

Form 1: If the Borgen plot (or its generalization for arbitrary dimensions) contains a point belonging to the coincidence of vertices of the inner and outer polygon (inner and outer polyhedrons), and, as result, this point will be the matching vertices of the two Borgen triangles (all Borgen simplices), then the point signs the unique solution for the component.

Form 2: If a component has a selective window in one direction/way/mode and considering the other direction/way/mode, this component has a sub-window in which the contribution of this component is zero while all interfering components appear inside this window, then the component can be uniquely decomposed in this second direction/way/mode.
