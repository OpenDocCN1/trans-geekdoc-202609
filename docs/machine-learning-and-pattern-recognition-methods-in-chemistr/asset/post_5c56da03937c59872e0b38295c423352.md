In cases where the SMCR research hypothesis is true, the final model contains no active constraints. However, in many cases, due to noise and nonlinear behaviors of chemical and/or spectroscopic response, active constraints are present in the resultant SMCR model. The detection of active constraints is important from both a theoretical and from a practical point of view. Tests for active constraints can be simply done by turning the constraints off at the solution point and then comparing the constrained fit with the unconstrained one. In the case of strong active constraints, when the difference between the two fits is greater than what might be expected from noise, a significant deviation from the original hypothesis may occur. The research hypothesis is rejected if many strong active constraints are present at the final solution. It worth noting that in most cases, instead of producing a unique solution, these methods produce a range of feasible solutions, which fulfill the constraints. Therefore, if one gets a lack-of-fit with no active constraints, one cannot prove that the hypothesized model is correct, as in testing all scientific hypotheses.

Most often, the first step in fitting SMCR models is to select some initial starting point followed by iterative refinement while considering physicochemical limitations in the form of constraints to converge to a meaningful solution. The starting point or “guess” can be selected using different methods, including the “needle search” method (Gemperline, 1984, 1986) or evolving factor analysis (EFA). The method of constrained alternating least squares is used to iteratively refine the “unconstrained” estimates to yield better “constrained” solutions. In solving SMCR problems, the constraints are applied during the fitting process to ensure a smooth and monotonic convergence of the initial solution to the final result. The iterative process by the application of alternating least-squares steps is repeated until no further improvement is seen in the estimated concentration or spectral profiles or when the maximum number of iterations is reached.

## Soft constraints in curve resolution problems

Normally, the spectroscopic techniques that are employed do not give a selective response for each chemical species present in a mixture, and the chemical separation techniques (for example, chromatography) cannot isolate one or more species from the mixture in a pure state; thus, the applied constraints do not add sufficient information to completely resolve the curve resolution problem. In such cases, in most MCR fits, nonunique solutions are obtained. In such cases, it is recommended to calculate the whole range of feasible solutions for both identification and quantification purposes. There are different SMCR techniques for calculating the range of feasible solutions either by determining a useful approximation or by defining exact ranges. The interested reader is referred to a good review on this subject (Golshan, Maeder, and Abdollahi, 2013). However, in some circumstances, unique solutions can be obtained in SMCR under the implied constraints. The conditions that are required for getting unique solutions have been the subject of several papers (Karimvand et al., 2020; Lakeh, Abdollahi, and Rajkö, 2022; Rajko, Abdollahi, Beyramsoltan, and Omidikia, 2015).

**Hard soft constraints.** One of the important factors that can affect the reliability of the MCR solutions is how the constraints are applied. Generally, there are two ways for introducing physicochemical information to the MCR model: hard and soft constraints. “Hard” constraints are strictly imposed on the model, while “soft” constraints accept small deviations from the previously known information. For example, small negative deviations are permitted when applying soft non-negative constraints. In practice, soft constraints are useful to allow for the effects of noise and ideal behaviors of chemical mixtures and responses. Several studies illustrate that applying soft constraints in SMCR models usually favors fewer active constraints and lower lock-of-fit in comparison with hard constraints. Thus, soft constraints are more likely to fit the original research hypothesis discussed before. Several algorithms have been proposed for implementing soft constraints in SMCR methods and also for studying the effect of these constraints on the range of feasible solutions. The main algorithms for imposing soft constraints are penalty alternating least squares (P-ALS) (Gemperline & Cash, 2003; Richards, Miller, & Gemperline, 2008), polygon inflation (Rahimdoust Mojdehi, Sawall, Neymeyr, & Abdollahi, 2016; Sawall et al., 2015), and grid search algorithm (Lakeh, Abdollahi, & Gemperline, 2020; Tavakkoli, Abdollahi, & Gemperline, 2020). The constraints that are implemented softly so far are non-negativity, equality, closure, unimodality, known-value, windowing, and trilinearity.

In the remaining sections of this chapter, we describe each different algorithm of applying soft constraints in detail, including how these algorithms are used to construct the above-mentioned constraints. After presenting the details of algorithms, we present the effect of soft constraints on the range of feasible solutions using several simulated and experimental data sets.
