Since each of the columns of the matrix corresponds to the regression coefficients for the prediction of the corresponding column in the matrix **Y**, Eq. In the context of multivariate linear regression, the use of MLR has several advantages. First of all, estimation of the optimal set of model coefficients is based on a clear loss function, which represents the straightforward generalization of the least-squares criterion to the case of more than one predictor. Moreover, its mathematical structure is such that no additional parameters or metaparameters be tuned in a model selection stage are involved, which translates to the fact that once the X-Y training pairs are identified, the solution is univocal. On the other hand, model calculation requires the inversion of the matrix $\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}$, which can represent a severe limitation in many practical situations. Indeed, conditions commonly met when dealing with chemical data, such as having a lower number of training samples than measured variables and/or a high correlation among the predictors, lead to the impossibility of inverting the matrix $\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}$, which becomes ill-conditioned. This translates to the fact that unless some variable selection strategy is adopted (sometimes also coupled with orthogonalization approaches), MLR is seldom applied to chemical data.

A solution to the limitations discussed above is represented by the use of abstract (latent) variables, instead of the experimentally measured ones, as since the chemical data are highly correlated, it is possible to exploit such correlation to achieve a substantial reduction in the dimensionality of the problem through data projection onto a low-dimensional subspace of highly informative orthogonal directions in the multivariate space (latent variables or components). The coordinates of the sample along this new set of directions (which are often referred to as *scores*) can be used as predictors to build regression models. In particular, due to their orthogonality, scores on the different components are completely uncorrelated among one another. Moreover, the number of relevant components is usually lower than the number of training samples, allowing to overcome the limitations related to ill-conditioning of the predictor matrix.

### 1.2 Principal component regression (PCR)

In principal component regression (PCR) (Jolliffe, 1982), as the name suggests, all information originally present in the X matrix is used to model the Y. The corresponding orthogonal directions (also called *loadings*) are collected in the matrix **P**. The coordinates of the sample along this new set of directions (which are often referred to as *scores*) can be used as predictors to build regression models. In particular, due to their orthogonality, scores on the different components are completely uncorrelated among one another. Moreover, the number of relevant components is usually lower than the number of training samples, allowing to overcome the limitations related to ill-conditioning of the predictor matrix.

However, its two-step procedure (where each step has a different objective) makes it not necessarily the best option to rely on. The scores extracted from the X matrix by PCA provide the best approximation of the X data, but it is not guaranteed they are also the best predictors for the Y response. Indeed, since the scores are calculated from the X matrix, only the variability in X contributes to the definition of $B_{PCR}$, so that the method is said to be biased.

In this framework, different methods for multivariate linear regression have been proposed in the literature, the most commonly used are

```
$Y = \mathbf{TP} + \mathbf{E} \quad (18)$
```
where $\mathbf{T}$ are the scores, $\mathbf{P}$ are the loadings, and $\mathbf{E}$ is the residuals. The regression coefficients are then given by:

```
$\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{P}(\mathbf{T}^T\mathbf{T})^{-1}\mathbf{T}^T\mathbf{Y} \quad (19)$
```
where, analogously to what was discussed in Eq. (2.16), the values of $B_{PCR}$ are numerically:

```
$\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{B} \quad (20)$
```

As a consequence, differently than what happens in MLR, building a PCR model requires the optimization of an additional parameter, i.e., the number of principal components. This is normally achieved by testing models with an increasing number of principal components and selecting the one resulting in the lowest prediction error on the training data (usually computed by cross-validation).

### 1.3 Partial least-squares regression (PLS)

In PLS (Geladi & Kowalski, 1986; Martens, & Wold, 1983; Wold, Johansson & Cocchi, 1993; Wold, Sjöström, & Eriksson, 2001), the X and Y blocks matrices are jointly decomposed to produce scores with maximum covariance; those scores are then used as predictors for the response(s) through least-squares fitting. Accordingly, the resulting scores will represent a compromise between fitting the variance in X and approximating the dependent variable(s).
