# Uniqueness in resolving multivariate chemical data 147

In the next step, the coordination of $\mathbf{c}_1$, $\mathbf{t}_i$, in the column space of the other sub-matrices must be calculated:

```
$\mathbf{t}_i = \mathbf{c}_1\mathbf{U}_i, \quad i=2,3,\dots,k \quad (7.11)$
```

Afterward, the complementary subspace of $\mathbf{c}_1$ in all sub-matrices can be calculated based on the dual concept:

```
$\mathbf{t}_1 \mathbf{S}_1^{-1}\mathbf{z}_1 = 0 \quad (7.12)$
```

The $\mathbf{z}_i$ represents the coordinates of an arbitrary point on the defined hyperplane in the V-space of $\mathbf{D}_i$. There is $K$ hyperplane for $K$ sub-matrices of the augmented data matrix, and their intersection defines a total hyperplane in the row space of the $\mathbf{D}_{aug}$. The dual point of the total hyperplane in the column space of the $\mathbf{D}_{aug}$ can generate the trilinear plane, while the selected initial $\mathbf{t}_1$ is correct. Thus, all hyperplanes in the row space of all sub-matrices must be transformed to the row space of $\mathbf{D}_{aug}$ by linear transformation of matrix spaces. $\mathbf{T}_k$ is a transformation matrix:

```
$\mathbf{T}_k = \mathbf{V}_k^{-1}\mathbf{Vaug}, \quad k=1,2,\dots,K \quad (7.13)$
```

Based on the linear algebra, coordinate-wise representations of vectors of a basis set can be transformed to their equivalent representations of another basis set. This transformation is called change of the base. Therefore, it is necessary to transform $r_K$ arbitrary points of each hyperplane in $\mathbf{V}_K$ to the row space of $\mathbf{D}_{aug}(\mathbf{V}_{aug})$ by the transformation matrices ($r_K$ is chemical-rank of $K$ sub-matrix):

```
$\mathbf{Z}'_K = \mathbf{Z}_K\mathbf{T}_K \quad (7.14)$
```

$\mathbf{Z}_K$ and $\mathbf{Z}'_K$ are the coordination of arbitrary points in the $\mathbf{V}_K$ and $\mathbf{V}_{aug}$ before and after transformation, respectively. Thereafter, the total hyperplane (coefficients of the polynomial) should be calculated by $\sum_{k=1}^K(r_k-1)$ points $(\mathbf{Z}'_K)$.

As a simple example, $x+2y+3z=0$ is a hyperplane with three coefficients. $[1 23]$ are the coefficients of a plane (intercept is zero) as well as coordinates of a vector which is orthogonal to the plane (intercept is zero). Accordingly, in order to find the coefficients of the plane, it is necessary to look for a vector which is orthogonal to the plane. $[x\ y\ z]$ are the coordinates of any arbitrary point on the plane. Euclidean inner product of two vectors, $(\vec{\mathbf{m}},\vec{\mathbf{w}})$, can be defined as: $(\vec{\mathbf{m}},\vec{\mathbf{w}}) = \vec{\mathbf{m}}^T\cdot\vec{\mathbf{w}}$. Here, $\{\vec{\mathbf{m}}^T=[1 2 3]\}$ with $\vec{\mathbf{w}}=[x; y; z]$ and $(\vec{\mathbf{m}},\vec{\mathbf{w}})=1x+2y+3z=0$.
