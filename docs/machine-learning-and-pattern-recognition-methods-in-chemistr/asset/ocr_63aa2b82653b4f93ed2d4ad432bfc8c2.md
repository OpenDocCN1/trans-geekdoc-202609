The plastics themselves are not the only cause for concern, as their large surface area invites the adsorption of persistent organic pollutants (POP) and persistent, bioaccumulative, and toxic (PBT) chemicals (Kershaw, 2015). Lacking quantitative methods means little is known about the co-accumulation of microplastics with toxins, which are known to include polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and heavy metals (Xu Ma, Ji, Pan, and Miao, 2020).

In the oceans, the problem extends to all five of the main ocean compartments—the surface, the water column, the seafloor, biota, and on the shoreline or buried in intertidal sediments (Kershaw & Rochman, 2015). Following 680 nets on 24 separate expeditions across the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans, as well as the Mediterranean Sea, Eriksen et al. (2014) estimate that there are over 5 trillion plastic particles floating in the ocean. Concentrations as high as 890,000 pieces of plastic per square kilometer were measured in the Mediterranean Sea (Eriksen et al., 2014).

However, there is a gap in the understanding of the exchange between compartments and a lack of knowledge of the quantity of plastic in each of compartments, except surface waters. Though the concentrations of plastics observed at surface are concerning, 4–23 more plastic is deposited on land than into oceans each year (Horton, Walton, Spurgeon, Lahive, & Svendsen, 2017). Beaching, or the deposition of plastics on beaches by tides and waves, is one process by which this occurs. In most cases, plastics with lower density than seawater float in surface waters and wash up on shores, especially at times of high wind speed, waves and daily river flow in estuaries (Lefebvre et al., 2021). Even so, there is an enormous mismatch between the amount of plastic believed to the natural environment and the amounts observed by scientists, in what is called the “missing plastic problem” (Cressey, 2016). The previously mentioned sampling expeditions found an order of magnitude more microplastic than predicted in their pre-estimation (Eriksen et al., 2014). As such, understanding terrestrial impacts and refining the techniques to identify and quantify plastic litter on land is of the utmost importance. Furthermore, this undercount of microplastic undermines the efforts to calculate the risk arising from plastics in the natural environment, because it is difficult to provide a confident measure of exposure levels (Kershaw, 2015).
