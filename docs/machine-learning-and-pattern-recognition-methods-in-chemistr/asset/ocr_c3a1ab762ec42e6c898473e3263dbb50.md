$\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{V}^T + \mathbf{E}' = \mathbf{X} \mathbf{V}^T + \mathbf{E}' = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{Y}^T + \mathbf{E}' \quad (7.2)$

where X and Y are the coordinates of rows and columns of the D in its row and column space. E' is the residuals or non-modeled parts of the D. In general, due to the experimental noise and different ways of matrix decomposition (see Eqs. 7.1 and 7.2), E are almost different.

Based on the duality concept, each data point in one space (which can be a column, a row, or any arbitrary point in the space) determines the coefficients of a hyperplane in its dual space. Let's suppose x is the coordinates of each row of the D in the V-space, while its corresponding dual hyperplane in the U-space can be defined as:

$\mathbf{x} S^{-1} \mathbf{z} = 0 \quad (7.3)$

This equation demonstrates an equality relation to determine a hyperplane as the dual of the x in the row space. In addition, z is a general variable vector representing the coordinates of the points on the hyperplane in the U-space.

Furthermore, y is the coordinates of the D columns in the U-space, and its dual hyperplane in the V-space:

$\mathbf{y} S^{-1} \mathbf{q} = 0 \quad (7.4)$

where q is a general variable representing the coordinates of the points on the hyperplanes in the V-space. It is to highlight that the duality relation is not limited to a data rows and columns. All points in the U-space of a bilinear data (regardless of the position of the point) have dual hyperplanes in its V-space, and vice versa. Accordingly, a hypothetical point, h, in the U-space has a dual hyperplane in

$\mathbf{h} S^{-1} \mathbf{q} = 0 \quad (7.5)$

where h is a vector containing the coordinates of any hypothetical point in the U-space. Thus, it is possible to define its dual hyperplane based on the duality concept in the V-space.
