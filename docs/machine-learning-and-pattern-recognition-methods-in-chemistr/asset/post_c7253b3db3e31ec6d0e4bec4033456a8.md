# 1.6 Classification

In contrast with exploratory techniques such as PCA, classification refers to supervised multivariate analytical methods that are designed to sort samples into classes based on a series of measurements or theoretical values (Marini, 2010). These measurements or theoretical values define the classes, groups, of objects, and can be referred to as training sets (Marini, 2010). A validation step (internal cross-validation or external validation) is necessary to ensure the reliability of a model (Amigo, 2020).

Two of the primary categories of classification techniques are pure classification (discriminant) techniques, wherein the focus is on differences between samples of different classes, and class-modeling techniques, wherein the focus is on the similarities among samples of the same class (Marini, 2010). This introduction will focus on linear classification methods, but it is worth commenting that non-linear classification methods (e.g., artificial neural networks and k-means clustering) are also used to a certain extent.

### 1.6.1 SIMCA

Soft independent modeling by class analogy (SIMCA) is a classification method based on the use of principal component models to describe each of the classes (Marini, 2010). Each class in a training set is a quantitatively defined group of objects. The model for each class (e.g., Category 1) is then described by a set number, $A$, of principal components, according to:

```
$D_1 = T_A P_A^T + E$
```

where $D_1$ is the submatrix of the full data set containing only the samples of Category 1. $T_A$ and $P_A$ are the matrices composed of the first $A$ scores and loading vectors, respectively, and $E$ is the residuals (Marini, 2010).

### 1.6.2 PLS-DA

As its name implies, partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) is a discriminant classification technique based on partial-squares (PLS) algorithm. In the case of hyperspectral imaging, PLS-DA treats each pixel as if it were a sample and assigns it to a class by the regression model developed from the training set. Each pixel is projected onto a regression model and the probability of the pixel corresponding to each class is calculated, and a threshold determines which class pixel is assigned (Amigo et al., 2015). The sample is then assigned to the class it has the highest-probability of belonging to (Marini, 2010).

### 1.6.3 Validation

The validation parameters used in a classification model are sensitivity, specificity, and error rate, all of which are generally expressed in percentages. These parameters are based on the number of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN) during the internal cross-validation or external validation (Amigo et al., 2015).

```
$Sensitivity = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}\nSpecificity = \frac{TN}{FP + TN} \quad (5.3)\nError rate = 1 - \frac{(Sensitivity + Specificity)}{2}$$
```

Sensitivity refers to the ability of the model to confirm that a sample belongs to the class to it has been assigned, and is the ability of the model to confirm that a sample does not belong to the classes to which it has not been assigned (Amigo et al., 2015).

# 1.7 Objectives of this book chapter

Despite the aforementioned promising benefits of NIR-HSI, there are important analytical concerns that require a deep understanding of the interactions between NIR radiation, microplastics, and the matrices in which microplastics are located. These concerns beg a series of questions:

- Signal interference: Will the spectrum of the microplastic be the same as the spectrum of pure plastic? If so, to what extent can the signal of a microplastic be differentiated from the signals of the surrounding elements?
- Scattering: Will the spectrum of a pixel containing microplastic be affected by the scattering promoted by the surrounding elements? If so, to what extent will this change the microplastic spectrum relative to the spectrum of pure plastic?
- Spatial resolution: What is the smallest spatial level at which we can be confident that the microplastic signal will still be visible?
- Spectral resolution: Is there a combination of wavelengths in the spectra that remain selective for the microplastic?

This chapter gives some answers to the aforementioned questions and sets the basis for understanding the extent to which NIR-HSI and classification methods can be used to detect microplastics. The hypothesis is that NIR-HSI, together with the proper classification method, is a viable technique to identify microplastics and measure their size directly in the sand, avoiding time-consuming separation techniques. For this aim, we collect a NIR-HSI image of a well-standardized microparticle of known identity (butadiene styrene) standardized sand (acting as marine sediment) to assess the realm of classification chemometric techniques. This will help us understand the extent to which the spectra of microplastics are influenced by their surroundings.

# 2. Materials and methods butadiene styrene (ABS) pellet

The polymer used was the acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS). It was in the form of pellets of approximately 5mm in diameter. ABS was chosen from the list of polymers produced in the greatest quantities in Europe (Plastics Europe, 2020). Microparticles ranging in size from 100um to 2mm were produced from the pellet using mechanical abrasion.

The identity and size of ABS were confirmed using Raman microscopy. An Invia confocal Raman spectrometer coupled to a DMLM Leica microscope (Renishaw, Gloucestershire, UK) was used to measure the size and confirm the identity of the polymer. A still image of each microparticle on a glass plate was collected with $20×$ magnification. The still image collected of each individual microplastic particle was used to calculate the dimensions. A 785-nm excitation laser was used with 350mW output power and 150mW as 100% laser power.

# 2.2 Sample preparation

Samples of sand were collected from the top surface layer (approximately 2cm deep) of Plentzia Beach 1h after high tide in Plentzia, Spain (43° 24'40″N 2°56'46″W). The sand was rinsed with tap water, and any debris was removed and dried in an oven at 100°C overnight. The sand was then placed in an oven at 450°C for 1 h to remove any remaining traces of organic material. The sand was stored in a glass container.

# 2.3 Near infrared hyperspectral imaging

Near infrared (NIR) hyperspectral images were collected using a Headwell Photonics NIR hyperspectral camera (Model 1002A-0037, Fitchburg, MA, USA). X-Control software was used to calibrate the camera settings, and Hyperspec Rev. A.11.13.1 software was used to set the acquisition parameters. Images were collected in the spectral resolution of 4.85 nm and 142 bands with an acquisition time of 100,000µs, a temperature of 260K°, and a focal distance of 30 cm. The spatial resolution was set to 10µm. The light source was aimed at the sample at a 45° angle. Calibration was performed by taking a 0% reflectance measurement with the objective closed and 100% reflectance measurement with a Spectralon plate. The camera measures in line-scan modality. Scan length was adjusted as necessary to record all of the samples (Fig. 5.2).
