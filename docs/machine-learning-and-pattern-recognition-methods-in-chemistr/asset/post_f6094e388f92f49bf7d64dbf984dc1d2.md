To the end of monitoring and characterizing microplastics in sediments, some protocols recommend a lower size limit of 100 µm based on sampling capabilities (Frias et al., 2018). Microplastics are further categorized according to their origin. Primary microplastics are those manufactured to microscopic sizes, whereas secondary microplastics are derived from the breakdown of larger particles in the environment (Frias & Nash, 2019). With the persistence of hundreds of thousands of years, all of the plastic introduced into the environment remains unmineralized; instead, plastics fragment is due to ultraviolet (UV) degradation, oxidation, and abrasion (Barnes, Galgani, Thompson, & Barlaz, 2009). This degradation means methods of characterization that depend on spectral libraries are expected to fall short, given the spectra of weathered plastics differ greatly from those of standard plastics (Dong et al., 2020). Taking all into account, the very characteristics that make plastics useful—persistent, inexpensive, water-resistant, versatile—make them worrisome in marine and terrestrial environments.

## 1.2 Persistent, ubiquitous—And miscounted?

The persistence of plastics contributes to their ubiquity in the natural environment. Even the most seemingly pristine and far-flung sites are not safe from plastic pollution. Thanks to long-range transport, plastics can be found in places as remote as polar ecosystems (Haland & Hercke, 2019). Their size, meanwhile, allows for uptake by living organisms. The recent (and undiscovered) of microplastics in human placentas is one concerning example of this phenomenon (Ragusa et al., 2021).

There are ecological consequences to this proliferation of microplastics. In the marine environment, microplastics are ingested at trophic levels ranging from zooplankton to marine mammals, and including filter feeders such as Mytilus edulis. The plastics themselves are not the only cause for concern, as their large surface area invites the adsorption of persistent organic pollutants (POP) and persistent, bioaccumulative, and toxic (PBT) chemicals (Kershaw, 2015). Lacking quantitative methods means little is known about the co-accumulation of microplastics with toxins, which are known to include polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and heavy metals (Xu Ma, Ji, Pan, and Miao, 2020).

In the oceans, the problem extends to all five of the main ocean compartments—the surface, the water column, the seafloor, biota, and on the shoreline or buried in intertidal sediments (Kershaw & Rochman, 2015). Following 680 nets on 24 separate expeditions across the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans, as well as the Mediterranean Sea, Eriksen et al. (2014) estimate that there are over 5 trillion plastic particles floating in the ocean. Concentrations as high as 890,000 pieces of plastic per square kilometer were measured in the Mediterranean Sea (Eriksen et al., 2014).

However, there is a gap in the understanding of the exchange between compartments and a lack of knowledge of the quantity of plastic in each of compartments, except surface waters. Though the concentrations of plastics observed at surface are concerning, 4–23 more plastic is deposited on land than into oceans each year (Horton, Walton, Spurgeon, Lahive, & Svendsen, 2017). Beaching, or the deposition of plastics on beaches by tides and waves, is one process by which this occurs. In most cases, plastics with lower density than seawater float in surface waters and wash up on shores, especially at times of high wind speed, waves and daily river flow in estuaries (Lefebvre et al., 2021). Even so, there is an enormous mismatch between the amount of plastic believed to the natural environment and the amounts observed by scientists, in what is called the “missing plastic problem” (Cressey, 2016). The previously mentioned sampling expeditions found an order of magnitude more microplastic than predicted in their pre-estimation (Eriksen et al., 2014). As such, understanding terrestrial impacts and refining the techniques to identify and quantify plastic litter on land is of the utmost importance. Furthermore, this undercount of microplastic undermines the efforts to calculate the risk arising from plastics in the natural environment, because it is difficult to provide a confident measure of exposure levels (Kershaw, 2015).

## 1.3 Hyperspectral imaging as a method for microplastics detection

One major challenge in microplastics research is the lack of standardized analytical and quantification techniques, making it difficult to compare results across studies (Horton et al., 2017). Many of the common methodologies include density separation of plastics from solid matrices by filtration, which is time-consuming and can lead to the loss of material, especially when polymers differ and separation solutions are present (Frias et al., 2018). There are several chemical analytical methods that rely on thermal degradation and visual analysis. Two common thermoanalytical methods are pyrolysis chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry and thermogravimetric, but these will not be discussed here as they are destructive and provide a measurement of polymer weight rather than particle dimensions or amount of particles (Käppler et al., 2016).

Given the limitations of methodologies based on thermegradation, hyperspectral imaging has emerged as a reliable, non-invasive technique (Serranti, Palmieri, & Cázar, 2018). In the context of microplastic studies, classification strategies are the most used chemometrics (Da Silva, Murphy, Amigo, Stedmon, & Strand, 2020). The spectral resolution, then, refers to the wavelength range; the term pixel must be defined. A pixel is the smallest individual spatial subdivision of an image. Following this definition, spatial resolution can be expressed as X × Y, where X is the number of pixels per row and Y is the number of pixels per column. However, when discussing the spatial resolution, it is important to remember that each pixel represents a given surface within the image (Amigo, 2020). The visual representation of a hyperspectral image is a hypercube defined in three dimensions: the X and Y spatial directions and the spectral (λ) dimensions. As such, the mathematical notation to represent a hypercube D is X × Y × λ (Amigo, Babamoradi, & Elcleroastizabal, 2015) (Fig. 5.1).

## 1.4 Chemometrics

Given the overwhelming amount of spectral and spatial information produced, advanced machine learning methods [a.k.a. chemometrics (Massart, 1997)] are required to glean meaningful from the quantities of data produced by the instruments used. In the context of microplastic studies, classification strategies are the most used chemometric methodologies (Da Silva, Murphy, Amigo, Stedmon, & Strand, 2020).

## 1.5 Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) is an unsupervised exploratory chemometric tool. That is to say, it explores variation within the multivariate datasets, but it is not a method of classification. Applied correctly, PCA can provide an overview of multivariate data, shed light on the interrelated of variables and samples, and quantify patterns (Bro & Smilde, 2014). More specifically, PCA attempts to decrease the dimensionality of complex data sets by finding orthogonal directions in the multivariate space of describing the maximum variance as D = TPᵀ E. Using the hyperspectral image in Fig. 5.1 as an example, D is the unfolded matrix with dimensions of XY × λ, containing the data for XY pixels and λ variables. T, the score vector or scores, has dimensions of (XY × R), and P, the loading vector or loads, has dimensions of (λ × R), where R refers to the number of principal components (Da Silva et al., 2020). E is the residual vector (Bro & Smilde, 2014).
