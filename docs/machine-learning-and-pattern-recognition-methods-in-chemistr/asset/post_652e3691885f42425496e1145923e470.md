# Preface

Human has always been looking for a way to do two things: to count and to measure. Humanity owes the knowledge of the definitions of coordinates, and in principle, the very existence of measurements in the sciences to the invaluable efforts of the French mathematician and philosopher René Descartes. In most cases, measuring requires a tool that can be used to characterize an object. At first, an object is stimulated by a variable and then its response is recorded. In other words, all beings have a minimum of characteristics that are either common or unique to them. That is to say, measurement aims to recognize differences and similarities between individuals. Observations and measurements create a large amount of data designated to each member. By projecting these features in a coordinate system, we create a space where each member occupies a specific position. Let us not forget that objects in such a space can be close or apart. Their distinction is essentially defined in terms of distances. Depending on the nature of the discipline, for instance whether it is natural or social sciences, a study demands a particular interpretation of space and similarity in regard to the members. In experimental sciences such as chemistry, measurements and observations, and any investigation of objects in space uncover buried relationships, patterns, and distribution to find a quantitative mathematical relation that relates the attributes and properties of objects to responses. The former leads to subjects such as pattern recognition, classification, and clustering, and the latter results in concepts such as regression and calibration.

Machine learning methods and artificial intelligence can perhaps be traced back to the work of the English scientist Alan Turing. Then, tremendous advances in the development of new and powerful techniques in all scientific and technical disciplines have led to the provision of tools that have contributed significantly to the correct understanding of many phenomena. The use of machine learning and artificial intelligence in solving chemical problems to measure multiple species, despite the considerable overlap in the response and the identification of new compounds, is inevitable. Considering the explosive growth of low-cost devices and the large amount of big data they produce, multivariate machine learning methods play a crucial role in rapidly processing and extracting useful information from various numerical, textual, and image data types. Deep learning and neural networks are powerful tools in the hands of chemists to solve many of the problems they face.

In this book, we have tried to show concisely but precisely the role of these methods in solving chemical problems.

Jahan B. Ghasemi
Tehran, November 2022

### 1. Introduction

Self-modeling curve resolution (MCR) (Lawton & Sylvestre, 1971) describes a set of powerful tools for estimating the pure component spectra and composition of profiles from the spectroscopic data matrices. The only requirement of this mathematical resolution is the linearity of the chemical data, meaning that the total response at one particular wavelength should be the sum over all the contributions of each spectroscopically active constituent according to Beer-Lambert Law. At a constant optical path length, the Beer-Lambert law can be described by Eq. (1.1):

$A_{\lambda} = s_{\lambda1}c_1 + s_{\lambda2}c_2 + \dots + s_{\lambda m}c_m = \sum_{j=1}^{m} s_{\lambda j}c_j$ (1.1)

where $A_{\lambda}$ is the absorbance at wavelength $\lambda$, $s_j$ is the molar absorbivity of the active components in mol L$^{-1}$ cm$^{-1}$, and $c$ is the concentration in mol L$^{-1}$. Eq. (1.1) can be written as the absorbance for every sample if complete spectra are taken over several samples. This system of equations allows the application of a matrix notation shown in Eq. (1.2):

$A = CS^T + E$ (1.2)

where $A$ $(ns \times n\lambda)$ is the matrix of absorbance that consists of absorption spectra measured at $n\lambda$ wavelengths from $ns$ samples. Columns of matrix $C$ $(ns \times nc)$ contain the pure concentration profiles, and rows of matrix $S$ contain the pure spectra of the absorbing species. $E$ $(ns \times n\lambda)$ is the matrix of residuals and contains the measurement errors.
