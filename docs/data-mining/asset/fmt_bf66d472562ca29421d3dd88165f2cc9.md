
Breiman, in the technical report Breiman (1996c), proposed a different bias–variance decomposition for classification. This has caused some confusion in the literature because three different versions of this report can be located on the internet. The official version, entitled “Arcing classifiers,” describes a more complex decomposition that cannot, by construction, produce negative variance. The original version, entitled “Bias, variance, and arcing classifiers,” follows Dietterich and Kong’s formulation (except that Breiman splits the bias term into bias plus noise).

There is also an intermediate version with the original title but the new decomposition that includes an appendix in which Breiman explains that he abandoned the old definition because it can produce negative variance. However, in the new version (and in decompositions proposed by other authors) the bias of the aggregated classifier can exceed the bias of a classifier built from a single training set, which also seems counterintuitive.

We discussed that bagging can yield excellent results when used for cost-sensitive classification. The `MetaCost` algorithm was introduced by [Domingos (1999)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur138). The random subspace method was suggested as an approach for learning ensemble classifiers by [Ho (1998)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur267) and applied as a method for learning ensembles of nearest-neighbor classifiers by [Bay (1999)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur25). Randomization was evaluated by [Dietterich (2000)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur133) and compared with bagging and boosting.

`Random forests` were introduced by [Breiman (2001)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur66). `Rotation forests` are a more recent ensemble learning method introduced by [Rodriguez, Kuncheva, and Alonso (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur460). Subsequent studies by [Kuncheva and Rodriguez (2007)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur337) show that the main factors responsible for its performance are the use of principal component transformations (as opposed to other feature extraction methods such as random projections) and the application of principal component analysis to random subspaces of the original input attributes.

[Freund and Schapire (1996)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur191) developed the `AdaBoost.M1` boosting algorithm and derived theoretical bounds for its performance. Later, they provided bounds for generalization error using the concept of margins ([Schapire, Freund, Bartlett, and Lee, 1997](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur474)). [Drucker (1997)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur145) adapted `AdaBoost.M1` for numeric prediction. The `LogitBoost` algorithm was developed by [Friedman, Hastie, and Tibshirani (2000)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur197). [Friedman (2001)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur195) described how to make boosting more resilient in the presence of noisy data.

[Domingos (1997)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur137) described how to derive a single interpretable model from an ensemble using artificial training examples. `Bayesian option trees` were introduced by [Buntine (1992)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur76), and majority voting was incorporated into option trees by [Kohavi and Kunz (1997)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur322). [Freund and Mason (1999)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur190) introduced `alternating decision trees`; experiments with multiclass `alternating decision trees` were reported by [Holmes, Pfahringer, Kirkby, Frank, and Hall (2002)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur274). [Landwehr, Hall, and Frank (2005)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur343) developed `logistic model trees` using the `LogitBoost` algorithm.

`Stacked generalization` originated with [Wolpert (1992)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur573), who presented the idea in the neural network literature, and was applied to numeric prediction by Breiman (1996a). Ting and Witten (1997a) compared different level-1 models empirically and found that a simple linear model works well; they also demonstrated the advantage of using probabilities as level-1 data. [Dzeroski and Zenko (2004)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur155) obtained improved performance using model trees instead of linear regression. A combination of stacking and bagging has also been investigated (Ting and Witten 1997b).
