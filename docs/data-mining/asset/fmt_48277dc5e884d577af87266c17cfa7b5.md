
According to the perspective advanced by computational linguist Emily Bender and colleagues, since the “knowledge” encoded in LLMs is gleaned from statistical patterns in text without grounding that knowledge in experience of the real world the systems cannot truly “understand” the meaning of this knowledge. With text data as their only input LLMs have access to “`form`,” such as observable realizations of language, but they do not have access to “`meaning`,” that is, the relation between form and the external world beyond language. Nor do they have access to the communicative intent of the authors that would be required to ground their “understanding” in relation to the real world. In this perspective LLMs are only “`stochastic parrots`”—they randomly stitch together linguistic forms based on the patterns found in their training data without reference to meaning. Therefore, the argument goes, when LLM systems are asked to reason about things beyond their input data that require true understanding of the external world they would be expected to fail.

Not everyone is convinced by this argument, or at least, that its practical implications are severe. The opposing school of thought, championed by computational linguist Christopher Manning, embodies the `distributional hypothesis`, the principle that motivated word embedding models. The distributional hypothesis, as stated by linguist J.R. Firth in the 1950s, is that “a word is characterized by the company it keeps.” More concretely, meaning can be estimated based on the contexts in which a word appears (without reference to external phenomena). Manning coined the term `distributional semantics` for the research field that seeks to quantify and implement the distributional hypothesis, including word embeddings and related models.

The strengths of LLMs bring their own ethical concerns. Most of the previously mentioned concerns around deepfakes apply to the misuse and abuse of LLMs, including identity theft, fraud, romantic and financial scams, disinformation and election interference, cyber-warfare, spam, and so on. Cheating in academic assessments using ChatGPT is already endemic, as it can answer many assessed tasks automatically with no thought or effort on the part of the student. LLMs may make some jobs obsolete, putting people out of work. (Of course, AI and LLMs will create some new jobs as well.) Some scholars believe that the rapid progress in LLM technologies may soon produce `artificial superintelligence` (`ASI`), which could represent an existential threat to life on Earth, although this view is not presently the majority opinion among experts. Citing these types of concerns, a 2023 petition by the Future of Life Institute called for all AI labs to pause the training of all AI systems more powerful than GPT-4 for at least 6 months. The petition has so far received more than 33,000 signatures, including high-profile technologists such as deep learning expert Yoshua Bengio, AI expert Stuart Russell, Apple Co-founder Steve Wozniak, and tech CEO/entrepreneur Elon Musk. Not everyone thinks this is a good idea. For example, in an opinion published by Bloomberg Tyler Cowen, economics professor at George Mason University, argued that a pause would simply shift the development of the technologies to countries that did not agree to the pause (and which might be even less responsible), harming US national security and hampering potentially beneficial innovation (such as AI for curing cancer, or better-aligned AI systems created by small businesses) while accomplishing very little to improve the situation.

The environmental and climate impacts of LLMs are also an issue. In 2022 training GPT-3 consumed the equivalent annual energy of approximately 200 Germans while producing nearly 500 metric tons of CO[2]. Since this does not include postdeployment energy costs, the actual amount of energy consumed is likely much higher. There are also ethical concerns around OpenAI’s practice of the use of cheap labor, primarily from African countries, to manually flag problematic content for training their system to avoid generating it.

## Self-Driving Cars

`Autonomous vehicles` (`AVs`), better known as `self-driving cars`, are vehicles that can drive on their own without human input ([Fig. 15.4](#Ch016_571-615_B9780443158889000169.xhtml#f0025)). In 2023 the California Public Utilities Commission’s (CPUC) approved two commercial AV companies, Cruise and Waymo, to operate commercial “`robotaxi`” vehicles autonomously across San Francisco, with paid fares, at any time of day. Both companies had previously operated robotaxi services subject to restrictions on factors including the hours of operation and number of vehicles, and they were not allowed to charge a fare unless there was a “backup” human operator present. Prior to that vehicles that had some automation capabilities—though not fully autonomous—had been on the road for some time, including Tesla’s “Autopilot” feature and AI-based features from many automobile manufacturers for collision prevention, automatic parking, and so on.

Although self-driving cars have the potential to be safer than human drivers, the safety concerns around them are a thorny ethical issue. The aforementioned CPUC approval met with a lot of public comment both for and against the resolution. On behalf of city transit and planning officials, San Francisco City Attorney David Chiu formally requested that that state regulators temporarily halt the approved expansion of robotaxi services and redo the hearing approving it. “AV technology has a place in San Francisco, but we’re concerned it is not yet capable of safely operating in our complex transportation environment,” Chiu said in a statement. “These vehicles may be able to drive themselves, but they can’t regulate themselves. The CPUC must do that to protect passenger and public safety. Since the CPUC’s decision to allow unfettered expansion of AVs for commercial passenger service in San Francisco, AVs have continued to present safety risks and interfere with first responders.”
