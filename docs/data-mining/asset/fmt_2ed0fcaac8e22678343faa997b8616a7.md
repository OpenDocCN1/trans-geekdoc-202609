
It should be noted that Target is far from the only retailer to employ data mining, and it is likely that other firms have employed similar tactics. The retailer Walmart has built what is, as of 2023, one of the largest private cloud platforms in the world. It uses this for real-time analysis at its analytics hub, which it calls its “Data Café.” The Data Café helps to, for example, diagnose the reasons for sales declines for particular items in a particular store such as stocking mistakes and pricing errors.

## Data Breaches

Even when not used for dubious purposes, the storage of detailed large-scale customer data incentivizes hackers to attempt to steal it, putting customers at risk. Data breaches at large tech companies have exposed personal data from hundreds of millions—or even billions—of accounts. A data breach at the US company Yahoo in 2013, revealed in 2016, was later revealed to have impacted three billion accounts, while breaches at the Indian company Aadhar (2018) and the Chinese company Alibaba (2019) each impacted around one billion users. Data leaked in these breaches included answers to security questions (Yahoo), biometric data and bank account numbers (Aadhar), criminal records (Alibaba), and phone numbers and email or physical addresses (all three of the earlier mentioned breaches). A 2016 data breach at The FriendFinder Network, home of several adult-oriented social networks and hookup sites such as Adult Friend Finder, included names, email addresses, and phone numbers, with potentially embarrassing consequences, and doubtless marital implications. Similarly, Ashley Madison, a “dating site” for people to cheat on their spouses and partners, had a data breach in 2015 that revealed 32 million accounts’ personally identifiable information and stated sexual desires, thus exposing infidelity and affairs. Several of the affected members committed suicide after the breach.

## Government Surveillance

Of course, companies are not the only entities that collect potentially invasive information. Edward Snowden’s 2013 leaks revealed secret US National Security Agency (*NSA*) programs that were being used to spy on people both in the United States and abroad. The leaks revealed the Planning Tool for Resource Integration, Synchronization, and Management (*PRISM*) program, which collects data from US internet companies, was monitoring as much data as the entire Library of Congress every 14.4 seconds, even as early as 2006. The XKeyscore program allows the NSA to search and analyze such data. According to Snowden, XKeyscore could be used to read anyone’s email, watch traffic to and from any website, watch any computer, and track any laptop as it moves around throughout the world.

## Defining Privacy

The examples we’ve discussed show that privacy is a serious issue. But what is privacy, exactly? We should first note that *computer security*, which protects computer systems against unauthorized access and disruptive attacks, is not privacy, or at least, it is not sufficient by itself to ensure privacy. The most sophisticated security system in the world won’t help protect your data if an organization decides to share your data with the world. Alternatively, a simplistic definition of privacy might be: “privacy means keeping our secrets and revealing as little information on us as possible.” Since we are all being constantly tracked online, by this definition, our privacy is reducing rapidly. So then, is privacy already over for us, and should we simply give up on it?

According to some who subscribe to this definition, we may have already met the *death of privacy*. Back in 1999, Scott McNealy, the CEO of the company that created the Java programming language, Sun Microsystems, controversially said, “You have zero privacy anyway … Get over it.” According to Vint Cerf in 2013, one of the leading figures in the creation of the internet, “Privacy may be an anomaly” and “It will be increasingly difficult for us to achieve it.” Cerf said that this is largely our fault: “Our social behavior is quite damaging to privacy. Technology has outraced our social intellect.” Facebook founder Mark Zuckerberg put it even more bluntly: “The age of privacy is over.”

Fortunately, if we are willing to scale back our view of privacy to a more realistic conception for the big data era, we shall realize that all is not lost. A more useful definition of privacy, due to legal scholars and privacy experts Neil M. Richards and Jonathan H. King, from their 2014 paper “Big Data Ethics,” is as follows:

“Privacy should not be thought of merely as how much is secret, but rather about *what rules are in place (legal, social, or otherwise) to govern the use of information as well as its disclosure*.” (Emphasis mine.)

If we adopt this definition, in which we seek to appropriately *control the flow of information*, privacy is still alive and thriving, aided by laws that ensure privacy protections on information flows. For example, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) is a US law, established in 1996, that protects patients’ healthcare information by regulating the flow of such information. Under HIPAA, covered entities, which include physicians, healthcare providers, hospitals, etc., may not disclose protected health information (PHI) without patients’ consent.

Although it was originally considered overcomplicated, HIPAA has largely succeeded in making patients feel safe in sharing their health information with physicians and clinicians while still allowing the flow of information that facilitates treatment, research, and public health. Under certain restrictions, it can even enable the release of medical data that can be analyzed via machine learning. HIPAA allows researchers to obtain PHI without patient consent if an institutional review board (IRB) or a privacy board determines that there is minimal risk from the research and obtaining consent would be impractical. Under HIPAA, datasets for which a designated set of 18 types of personally identifiable information (PII) have been removed, to anonymize the data, can be shared for research or commercial purposes.

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the European Union’s (EU’s) 2018 landmark privacy regulation law, is another important law that protects privacy in terms of information flows. The GDPR applies to any organization that collects data on people living in the European Economic Area, which includes the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway. It also impacts non-EU companies that do business with the EU. As such it has impacted privacy policies well beyond the EU. It may be difficult to enforce outside of the EU, however. The GDPR aims to give users control over their personal information, and it states that personal data may not be processed without a legal basis for doing so. The key principles of the GDPR are:

*   • Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency
*   • Accountability
*   • Purpose limitation
*   • Data minimization
*   • Accuracy
*   • Storage limitation
*   • Security.

Some legal scholars have argued that the GDPR mandates a right to explanation, that is, an explanation of a decision made by an AI or machine learning algorithm. This has motivated much research on explainable AI techniques. However, this view has been disputed by other scholars, and its legal status is currently unclear.
