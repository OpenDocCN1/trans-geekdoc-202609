
While most AI bias issues occur unintentionally, it is conceivable that a malicious actor could deliberately create an AI system that exhibits discriminatory bias, perhaps to implement their prejudiced views (which they may hold as factual), to intentionally advantage one demographic group or disadvantage another, or in pursuit of profit at the deliberate expense of fairness. This practice has been called *digital redlining*, with reference to the term “*redlining*” that describes the intentional withholding of products and services such as loans, housing, insurance, and grocery stores to neighborhoods where minoritized groups primary live and that were deemed risky or unworthy of investment. Since it is easy to unintentionally make a machine learning system that exhibits discriminatory behavior, it would be straightforward to intentionally build a discriminatory machine learning system “accidentally on purpose” while feigning innocence; this is called *masking*.

A real-world case of digital redlining happened in the late 1970s and early 1980s with the admissions process at St. George’s Hospital Medical School in London, England. A computer program was written by a staff member to assist in screening applicants for admissions interviews. The program was manually modified to match the way that the staff were making admissions decisions, and by 1979, it had 90%–95% correlation with the decisions made by the selection panel. Following the existing practices at the school, the algorithm explicitly reduced the chance of an interview for women and members of racial minority groups. Since the application form did not include a question on race, candidates’ race attributes were estimated based on their surname and place of birth. The institution was found guilty by the Commission for Racial Equality of racial and sexual discrimination in its admissions process. The editors of the *British Medical Journal* called this incident a “blot on the profession.”

### Proxy Variables

A common strategy to combat discriminatory bias in a machine learning system is to remove sensitive demographic attributes (sometimes called *protected attributes*, or *sensitive attributes*) such as gender, race, age, ethnicity, LGBT status, national origin, and disability from the system. Ideally, this would prevent a machine learning model from learning problematic associations between protected attributes and labels; it can no longer associate a positive or a negative weight with membership in a particular demographic. This approach is sometimes called *fairness through unawareness* ([Fig. 7.5](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#f0030)).

However, this is generally insufficient for ensuring fair behavior. The problem is that even if the protected attributes were removed, other attributes may be correlated with them and thus act as proxies for those attributes. The machine learning algorithm will thereby still have indirect access to the protected attributes via these *proxy variables*.

For instance, the neighborhood (or zip code) that an individual lives in is frequently highly correlated with their race due to housing segregation. In the United States this situation arose due to policies and practices such as the aforementioned redlining, which was codified into laws in the 1930s and 1940s (e.g., the National Housing Act of 1934 and the 1944 GI bill), with laws dismantling and prohibiting redlining introduced in the 1960s and 1970s (e.g., the Fair Housing Act passed in 1968), against the backdrop of so-called Jim Crow segregation policies from the 1870s up until 1965. Although redlining is now illegal, US housing remains highly racially segregated. The situation is perpetuated by phenomena such as “*White flight*,” where White people tend to leave neighborhoods as more people of color begin to live there, and *gentrification*, where an influx of wealthier residents increases the cost of living in a neighborhood, driving out the less-wealthy residents.

Geography can even be associated with gender. The city of San Jose, California, for example, is sometimes colloquially called “Man Jose” because of a higher-than-normal proportion of men vs women, at least among those in the single population in their 20 s and 30 s, due to the male-dominated tech industry boom in the San Francisco Bay Area. In China in 2015, the final year of the country’s one-child policy, 116 boys were born for every 100 girls. Age also has a geographic association, due in part to popularity of retirement destinations. As of 2020, Florida and Maine have more than 21% of residents aged 65 and older, while Utah has less than 12%. Geographic information that is given to a classifier could hence act as a proxy for race, gender, age, or other protected categories, enabling it to learn to encode patterns of discriminatory bias.

While it may be possible to remove some obvious proxy attributes such as neighborhood or zip code, protected attributes can have complex interactions with many other measurable characteristics. If we were to remove any attribute that had any association with a protected attribute, we may have few, if any, attributes remaining to use for prediction.

Proxy variables are a convenient weapon for the purposes of masking since a would-be masker could leave these variables to do their discriminatory damage in a subtle way while claiming to have addressed AI bias through deleting the protected demographic attributes. If the protected attributes were fully deleted from all company databases, or never recorded at all—perhaps with the stated goal of preventing bias—it may become difficult to audit the system for discriminatory bias. It is conceivable that this situation could be brought about intentionally to mask the bias in a machine learning system.

## Nontechnical Interventions

Now that we’ve learned about how bias and unfairness can arise in machine learning systems, the natural question is what to do about it. Much of the research on this topic has centered around technical interventions to machine learning algorithms that aim to prevent them from learning harmful patterns. However, AI bias is not a purely technical problem but a complex socio-technical-legal problem at the interface between AI algorithms, humans that are impacted by AI algorithms, social processes that impact our society and hence our data, ethical values and norms, the law, and politics. To think that this issue can simply be solved by technical means alone would be naïve. To truly address AI bias and its implications, we need to consider its nontechnical, human-centered aspects and implement nontechnical interventions as well as technical ones.
