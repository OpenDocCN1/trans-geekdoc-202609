
# Differential Privacy in Machine Learning

Suppose that we want to achieve differential privacy to ensure that the parameters of a machine learning algorithm can be released without risk of reverse-engineering the data of any individual in the dataset. It is not generally feasible to compute the sensitivity of an entire machine learning algorithm, so we cannot usually apply the `Laplace mechanism` or the `Gaussian mechanism` directly to the output of the learning algorithm. Furthermore, the algorithms typically work on individual data points, not aggregate data, so we cannot just privatize the data ahead of time. Instead, the typical strategy for privatizing a machine learning algorithm is to add noise to each iteration of the machine learning algorithm, to a minibatch gradient descent (or `Adam`, etc.) update. Typically, we compute the sensitivity of the individual updates in each iteration, then privatize them by adding appropriate noise. Leveraging composition theorems, we allocate a per-iteration privacy budget `ϵ[i]`<mml:math><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>&#1013;</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>i</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:math>![Equation](images/F00008Xsi85.png) for each iteration `i`<mml:math><mml:mi>i</mml:mi></mml:math>![Equation](images/F00008Xsi86.png) so that the entire learning algorithm is `ϵ[overall]`<mml:math><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>&#1013;</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">overall</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:math>![Equation](images/F00008Xsi87.png)\-differentially private.

A classic example of this approach is the differentially private stochastic gradient descent (`SGD`) algorithm proposed by Martin Abadi and colleagues in 2016 for differentially private deep learning ([Fig. 7.2](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#f0015)). The algorithm processes subsets of the data (minibatches) at a time, updating the parameters via estimated gradient steps for each minibatch. The method achieves differential privacy by first clipping these gradients to control the `l2`<mml:math><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>l</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:msub></mml:math>![Equation](images/F00008Xsi88.png)\-sensitivity, then adding noise to the clipped gradients via the `Gaussian mechanism`. The authors proposed a novel and sophisticated method for keeping track of the privacy budget under composition, called the `Moments Accountant`, which allowed them to make better use of a given privacy budget and hence reduce the amount of noise that must be added to achieve the target degree of differential privacy.

## Federated Learning

As we have discussed, one of the key reasons why companies want to acquire our data, which raises privacy concerns, is that they desire to train machine learning models on user data gathered from many people. But what if training could be done without ever sending our data to the server? A methodology called `federated learning` aims to accomplish this. In federated learning, the users’ data stays on their local devices, such as their smartphones or laptops, and is never directly sent to the organization’s databases or machine learning servers. Instead, we only transmit locally computed machine learning models or such models’ parameters, gradients, and so on. Typically, a central server coordinates learning based on the transmitted local models, although decentralized federated learning is also possible.

In each iteration of the original federated learning approach, called `federated average` (`FedAvg`), the `Central Server` first sends its global model to each device node. The devices then update their local copy of this model based on their local data. Next they send gradient information to the `Central Server`, which averages over the results to update the global model. The process is then repeated until the algorithm terminates. See [Fig. 7.3](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#f0020) for an illustration of this scheme.

![Fig. 7.3 A typical federated learning scheme. Decentralized federated learning, without a central server, is also possible. Credit: James Foulds](images/F00008Xf07-03-9780443158889.jpg)

[Fig. 7.3](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#Bf0020) A typical federated learning scheme. Decentralized federated learning, without a central server, is also possible. Credit: James Foulds

The main advantage of federated learning is that users’ data never leaves local devices, which limits any obvious information disclosure. However, private information about the users could still potentially be reverse engineered from the models unless additional steps are taken to ensure that this does not happen by ensuring differential privacy. Federated learning is also vulnerable to data poisoning attacks from the local devices unless additional steps are taken to prevent this. It is difficult to monitor or prevent discriminatory AI biases, since the central server never gets to see the actual data in order to check for such problems. The substantial communication costs that this approach requires are also a downside. Nevertheless, federated learning has promise as a privacy-preserving machine learning technique if these issues are carefully managed (e.g., by combining it with differential privacy and robust machine learning methods).

# 7.2 Fairness and Bias in Machine Learning

The field of data mining is seen as a roaring success. Machine learning models have been deployed to make decisions that affect myriad facets of daily life, from friend recommendations on social media to determining who should be prioritized to receive medical services. We know that humans are often unreliable decision makers, subject to all manner of prejudices and implicit or cognitive biases. In public and academic circles it has long been believed that artificial intelligence can avoid these biases through impartial, objective, logical, and data-driven decision making. Unfortunately, the reality is that this is far from guaranteed. The field is undergoing a reckoning around `algorithmic bias` and fairness issues. We begin by examining several application domains where these issues arise.

## AI Fairness in Hiring Decisions

Suppose that you have finished reading this book and studied hard to complete your degree or certification program, and you are now ready to get your first job as a data scientist. You polish your resume, search online for suitable job openings, and apply to more than a few interesting opportunities in your area, carefully tailoring your application to appeal to each company that you applied to. Feeling nervous and excited, you wait and hope for good news—an invitation to a job interview. How would you feel if you learned that you never even had a chance because a machine learning system decided to throw out your applications before any employee even looked at your materials? How about if that system did so for completely unfair reasons that had nothing to do with your qualifications or aptitude, and it provided no opportunity for you to learn of its mistakes or redress them?
