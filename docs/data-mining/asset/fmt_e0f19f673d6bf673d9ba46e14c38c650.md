
## Adversarial Examples

With deep neural networks being deployed to make consequential decisions about our lives, both big and small, there are inevitably people who would benefit from interfering with `DNNs`’ outputs. If a financial transaction is legitimately detected as fraudulent, for example, the fraudster behind it would stand to gain by fooling a `DNN` fraud detection classification model into predicting that the transaction was legitimate. Spam email classification is a similar scenario. In an extreme case, if a person’s enemies are motivated to commit murder they might accomplish it by tricking the person’s autonomous vehicle into behaving dangerously by causing its sensor systems to predict that a stop sign is only a yield sign, or that a red light is green.

Adversaries can engineer these outcomes by constructing *adversarial examples*, data points that have been perturbed very slightly, ideally imperceptibly to humans, in such a way that their predicted class labels are changed.

While many classifiers are vulnerable to such attacks, deep learning models are particularly at risk because they learn flexible decision boundaries and can overfit to small imperfections in the training data. It is frequently possible to deliberately construct adversarial examples automatically. The adversary does not need to alter the training data (“*data poisoning*”) or the learning process, since adversarial examples are constructed at test time and exploit existing flaws in the model’s generalization behavior. It is possible to construct adversarial examples even when the attacker does not have access to the internals of the model, though, of course, that would make the attacker’s job easier. The perturbation needed to change the class label may not be detectable by a human observer. Adversarial attacks can even be achieved in the physical world by attaching a sticker with the perturbation pixels onto a surface to be classified, such as a stop sign.

## 11.6 Knowledge Distillation

The success of deep learning is due in large part to the ability to leverage massive datasets to train large, high-capacity models—big data and big models. But big models have their downsides, too. They are expensive to deploy, with substantial computational cost for each prediction made. This computational burden at deployment time can further impede their use on machines with lower computational power, including mobile phones, internet-of-things (`IoT`) devices such as those used for smart homes, and edge devices. These models are also difficult to interpret due to their complexity. A solution to all these problems is to construct a smaller model that replicates the behavior of the original, complicated model, hopefully with high fidelity. This is called *knowledge distillation (KD)*. The `KD` approach has been applied in numerous domains including large language models, machine translation, and speech recognition. It also has been used to transfer knowledge between data modalities from video to text.
