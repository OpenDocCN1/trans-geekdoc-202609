Some of the concerns around AI art have been framed as copyright infringement issues. AI art models are typically trained on billions of images scraped from the Internet, often without consent of the original artists or compensation for the use of their work. As of 2023 there were at least two copyright infringement lawsuits against AI art companies including Stability AI (who created Stable Diffusion), DeviantArt (who created DreamUp based on Stable Diffusion and art submitted to their platform), and Midjourney. In a blog post announcing one of the lawsuits lawyer Matthew Butterick said that “because all the visual information in the system is derived from the copyrighted training images, the images produced—regardless of outward appearance—are necessarily works derived from those training images.” He also voiced concerns that AI art will make it difficult for artists to compete. According to Butterick, “At minimum, Stable Diffusion’s ability to flood the market with an essentially unlimited number of infringing images will inflict permanent damage on the market for art and artists.”

Not all artists are against AI art, however, and some digital artists who use generative AI in their work are fighting their own battle regarding copyright. In most countries and legal jurisdictions copyright protection is only granted to works that are authored by a human, which excludes work created by AI. The US Copyright Office clarified its position in a 2023 policy document, stating that “In the Office’s view, it is well-established that copyright can protect only material that is the product of human creativity. Most fundamentally, the term ‘author,’ which is used in both the Constitution and the Copyright Act, excludes non-humans.”

Jason M. Allen requested copyright protection for Théâtre D’opéra Spatial but was declined, as the US Copyright Office refused to grant copyright to the AI-generated portions of the work, and Allen refused to restrict his claim to only the postprocessing work he performed on top of the original AI-generated image. Allen has subsequently worked to advocate for copyright protection for digital art created with the assistance of generative AI technologies, founding an organization called Art Incarnate to champion AI art. In a mission statement on Art Incarnate’s website Allen said “With your support, together we can create the momentum needed to bring to bear the necessary arguments to rightly recognize creative users of any tool, regardless of how advanced, as the rightful authors of the works they actively choose to create.”

### Large Language Models

As we covered in [Chapter 11](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121), “Advanced deep learning methods,” *language models* are natural language processing (NLP) models that compute probabilities of sequences of words. When described this way they sound boring and not particularly useful. This belies the fact that *large language models* (*LLMs*)—language models with many parameters that have been trained on massive datasets—have tipped the NLP field on its head and are poised to do the same thing quite possibly to our entire society. Some LLMs, such as *Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers* (*BERT*), are designed to be useful for learning latent representations of text that can solve many (or even most!) NLP tasks with state-of-the-art performance. Other LLMs, such as *Generative Pre-trained Transformer* (*GPT*) models, are designed to generate realistic human-like text and respond to queries or prompts. These models have burst into the public consciousness with the release of US company OpenAI’s ChatGPT system, a chatbot based on GPT language models. ChatGPT has proved startlingly successful at not only producing coherent and stylistically cogent language in response to user-given prompts but at solving novel and challenging tasks across a variety of domains, from mathematics to medicine, to law, and beyond. It can write code, write poetry, and create graphics. Some researchers have claimed that the generality of the capabilities of the most recent OpenAI GPT model, GPT-4, suggests that it already reaches the beginning stages of an *artificial general intelligence* (*AGI*) system. ChatGPT and similar LLM technologies are expected to bring benefits to areas such as customer service, marketing, banking and financial services, tourism, and hospitality. In the academic sector they will help students learn with personalized tutoring.

Both the strengths and weaknesses of LLMs bring potential ethical challenges. In terms of weaknesses, while LLMs can often produce correct answers to difficult questions they can also produce fluent, plausible-sounding responses that are completely incorrect. They “*hallucinate*” false statements as readily as they produce facts, and it is not always easy for a human to identify these hallucinations. The fluency and apparent coherency of the generated text may increase the chance of *automation bias*, that is, inappropriate trust in an AI system.

LLMs can replicate or even amplify *discriminatory biases* by echoing stereotypes that may be harmful or offensive, potentially leading to representational or allocative harm. They may produce *hate speech* if not adequately filtered to prevent this (and such filters can sometimes be circumvented with clever prompts in a process known as “*jailbreaking*”). At best, they typically reflect the dominant hegemonic worldview that pervades their training data. They may reveal personally identifiable information (PII). Their training data may not be sufficiently diverse or representative of marginalized populations. If not frequently retrained based on recent data they may reflect outdated social norms. LLMs are generally not transparent or explainable so their behavior cannot easily be scrutinized. Errors in machine translation via LLMs can lead to harmful misunderstandings, such as the Palestinian man who was arrested by Israeli police when his Facebook post that said “good morning” in Arabic was incorrectly translated to “hurt them” in English and “attack them” in Hebrew.^(<a aria-label="noteref" href="#fn5" id="Cfn5">5</a>5)
