## Some considerations in defining fairness include:

* Are we addressing *harms of representation*, in which a model perpetuates harmful stereotypes that represent a protected group in a hurtful way? Or are we addressing *harms of outcome*, in which the predictions of the model are used for decision making, whose outcomes cause tangible or economic harm? As an example, the sentiment classifier that assigns negative sentiment to stereotypically Black names, which we discussed, creates a harm of representation because it perpetuates hurtful, racist opinions in the way that it represents people. On the other hand, if we used that same sentiment classifier to analyze resumes and it led to fewer Black people being offered a job interview, this would be a harm of outcome.
* In the present context, *should fairness correspond to equality* between protected groups, or are there legitimate reasons why equality is not the ideal solution, such as confounding factors or differing levels of qualification? One example of this situation arose in a study of admissions to the University of California Berkeley in the 1970s. The study found that while women were admitted to the University at a rate less than for men, this was explained by the fact that women tended to apply to departments that were more selective compared to men. When analyzing the results on a per-department basis, women were generally admitted at a higher rate than men. This counter-intuitive and impossible-sounding scenario is in fact possible due to a phenomenon known as *Simpson’s paradox*. The study concluded that there was no gender bias in the admissions process. Psychological research has concluded that when “fairness” (in a meritocratic sense) is in conflict with equality, humans tend to prioritize fairness over equality. If (meritocratic) fairness conflicts with equality, we must reckon with how to balance these competing goals.
* Are there underlying *differences between protected groups*, and should we accept the consequences of those differences in the data or correct for those differences? In research on discriminatory decision making in criminal justice contexts such as risk scores for recidivism, for example, some researchers have argued that since different demographic groups have different distributions over features, they would be expected to have different distributions over idealized risk scores (e.g., the probability of committing a crime). This phenomenon is termed *infra-marginality*. Proponents of this theory argue that because of the different distributions of risk between demographic groups, which are assumed to be legitimate, meritocratic, and well estimated, differences in outcome probabilities or error rates between demographic groups may not correspond to unfair discrimination. On the other hand, if the differences in risk are due to unfair reasons, we should not necessarily take the implications of those differences at face value.
* Should we aim to correct biases in society, beyond biases in data? In other words, should our intervention aim to perform *fair affirmative action*?

### Does Your Problem Definition Contain Bias?

When solving a real-world problem through data mining, we must convert the problem into a question that data mining can solve, which is typically the prediction of a target variable via classification or regression models. The process of defining the data mining problem is a stage in which we might inadvertently introduce bias, that is, by making a decision that systematically disadvantages or advantages a protected demographic group. In many cases the class variable is selected because it is a conveniently measurable proxy for the thing we actually care about. For instance, in credit scoring the measurable class variable “this person repaid a loan” is used as a proxy for something more elusive, “this person should be given a loan.” It is important to remember that this is a proxy that was chosen for convenience and should not be viewed as the absolute, objective, and definitive final word on creditworthiness. More generally, we must be careful that the choice of class variable is not harmful. In hiring, the probability of employee retention might be used as a proxy for whether to interview or hire a candidate. If employee retention is higher for some demographics than others (e.g., women leaving the technology sector more frequently than men due to facing workplace discrimination), using employee retention as the prediction target would result in systematically offering fewer positions to women.

### The Framing Trap

Once we have converted our real-world problem to a data mining problem, it is easy to forget that the system we build to solve the data mining problem exists in a bigger real-world context. Failing to consider this broader sociotechnical context, of which the machine learning system is but one component, is called the *framing trap*. If we do not consider how the outputs of our machine learning model are used in real-world decision making, and what the flow-on effects of those decisions are, we will not be able to make the broader sociotechnical system completely fair in an end-to-end sense. For example, in bail and sentencing decisions recidivism risk scores that are made by an AI system are generally given to a judge as mere recommendations, and the judge makes the final decision. The judge may be subject to *automation bias* in which they accept the algorithm’s recommendations without appropriate critique, they may ignore the algorithm’s recommendations entirely, or they may deviate from the recommendations in systematically biased ways. A fairness intervention on the AI system may thus not have the desired effect, such as provoking a response by the judge to overcompensate for the AI fairness intervention with even more biased behavior.
