The development of machine learning and data mining technologies has happened alongside the rise of the internet, social media, and the large technology companies that aim to profit from these technologies. This is not a coincidence. The “Big Tech” industry has quickly realized that it might be able to profit from any and all data it can obtain about its users or its potential future users. Given the decreasing cost of data storage, technology companies are incentivized to store all the data that they can on the people that interact with their platforms, right down to the level of clicks or individual keystrokes. Even if they do not have a plan for its use, they might say to themselves that they had better hang onto it just in case they need it later.

The catch, as you might have realized, is that all this data is not just a pile of numbers lying idly in a database somewhere. Frequently, it comes from real people. Myriad ethical issues can arise whenever personal data is stored or is used.

The potential impacts increase further as the use of data mining techniques spreads beyond commercial applications. Today, data-driven algorithms are being used to determine whether people—such as yourself—will be offered a job interview, awarded a loan, be admitted to college, receive healthcare services such as Medicaid, or even be given bail or parole. In large-scale applications the impacts can be at the scale of entire societies, such as influencing the outcomes of national elections or perpetuating patterns of racism and sexism.

A growing awareness of ethical issues around artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, and data mining technologies has led companies and organizations to develop guidelines, standards, and principles for responsible AI best practices. For example, the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM)’s Statement on Principles for Responsible Algorithmic Systems provides the following set of principles for designing algorithmic systems, including those based on AI:

* *Legitimacy and competency*: The developers of such systems (and their managers) should have the competence, expertise, and scientific and legal bases to build and deploy them, and the systems should have social legitimacy from impacted stakeholders.
* *Minimizing harm*: Developers and stakeholders should be aware of possible harms from a system and take appropriate steps to mitigate the harms.
* *Security and privacy*: It is important to keep sensitive data secure from intruders and to protect the privacy of those whose data are used.
* *Transparency*: Developers should ensure that design decisions are appropriately documented, uncertainty in algorithmic decisions is communicated, and that potential unfair biases in the system can be scrutinized externally.
* *Interpretability and explainability*: It should be possible to understand the process that an algorithmic system uses to make its decisions (*interpretability*) and to understand each decision itself (*explainability*). Rationalizations of the decisions, made after the fact without reference to the actual decision-making process, are generally insufficient.
* *Maintainability*: The proper performance of the system should be maintained throughout its life cycle, and this should be documented.
* *Contestability and auditability*: It should be possible to contest decisions made or informed by algorithmic systems and to seek redress for harmful decisions. Developers should store and preserve the information necessary to ensure that it is possible to audit the models, algorithms, data, etc. that the system uses to make its decisions.
* *Accountability and responsibility*: Organizations (and government agencies, etc.) that create and/or use algorithmic systems should be held accountable for their systems’ decisions and should be responsible for the systems, including correcting issues that arise and stopping the systems when it is necessary to do so.
* *Limiting environmental impacts*: The negative impact on the environment by algorithmic systems, for example, due to carbon emissions or energy consumption, should be monitored and restricted.

These ethical concerns are important enough that we emphasized them as early as [Chapter 1](#Ch001_3-42_B9780443158889000029.xhtml#Ch001_3-42_B9780443158889000029), “What’s it all about?” In this chapter, we will go deeper into these issues and their potential solutions. We’ll consider privacy concerns that arise from AI, machine learning, and data mining, as well as issues of fairness and bias in the behavior of algorithms. We’ll then discuss AI safety issues including the possible risk of catastrophic consequences of advanced AI. We will return to the topic of the ethics and impacts of these systems in [Chapter 15](#Ch016_571-615_B9780443158889000169.xhtml#Ch016_571-615_B9780443158889000169), “Moving on: applications and their consequences,” where we consider certain application areas of concern and the impacts of AI on our society at large.

## 7.1 Privacy

In [Chapter 1](#Ch001_3-42_B9780443158889000029.xhtml#Ch001_3-42_B9780443158889000029), “What’s it all about?” (and later, in [Chapter 4](#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054.xhtml#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054), “Algorithms: the basic methods”), we learned how supermarkets analyze their shoppers’ purchases to find patterns such as an association between buying diapers and beer (market basket analysis) and to personalize special offerings and coupons for specific customers. While customers may often enjoy receiving personalized discounts, some of these offers can be a bit *too* personal. In 2012, Andrew Pole, a statistician working for the retail firm Target, admitted to *New York Times* journalist and author Charles Duhigg that the company was attempting to identify customers who are pregnant. The goal, the statistician told Duhigg, was to convince its customers to go to Target for all their baby-related items such as diapers and bottles. In turn, this could lead busy and exhausted new parents to purchase other nonbaby-related items at Target while in the store, potentially changing their purchasing habits for years to come.

Duhigg recounted an anecdote, told to him by a Target employee, in which a father complained to a store manager that Target was inappropriately sending his teenage daughter coupons for cribs and baby clothes, which he felt might encourage her to get pregnant. Later, on the phone to the manager, the father gave an embarrassed apology. As it turned out, his daughter was already pregnant. Target had learned of her pregnancy before her father did!

Realizing that these personalized pregnancy-related coupons could be seen to be creepy, Target began to obfuscate them by mixing them together with ads for things that pregnant women would be unlikely to buy, such as lawnmowers and wine glasses. It found that its customers would use the coupons as long as they believed that they were not being targeted at them. The ad campaign was a huge success. Target’s Mom and Baby division sales increased dramatically. Its overall sales went up substantially in this time period as well, and the company’s president specifically mentioned this product category when speaking to investors. The statistician, Pole, got promoted.
