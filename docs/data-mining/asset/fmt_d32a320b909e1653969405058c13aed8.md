
To maximize accuracy in practical applications advantage can often be gained by combining multiple models. A wealth of practical experience has shown that ensembles of models are often necessary to squeeze out the last drops of predictive accuracy. [Chapter 8](#Ch009_323-345_B9780443158889000091.xhtml#Ch009_323-345_B9780443158889000091), “Ensemble learning,” introduces various popular methods of ensemble learning—bagging, boosting, and randomization, including the renowned random forest variant—before going on to show how boosting can be interpreted as a form of additive regression, a statistical model-building approach. A major drawback of most ensemble techniques is lack of interpretability but alternating decision trees and related tree-based approaches offer the prospect of high accuracy while also providing insight. Finally, we examine stacking, an intuitive method for combining a diverse set of models to maximize accuracy.

As the name of the chapter implies, [Chapter 9](#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108.xhtml#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108), “Extending instance-based and linear models,” extends instance-based learning methods and linear models. There was a resurgence of interest in linear models with the introduction of *support vector machines*, a blend of linear modeling and instance-based learning. These select a small number of critical boundary instances called “support vectors” from each class and build a linear discriminant function that separates them as widely as possible. This instance-based approach transcends the limitations of linear boundaries by making it practical to include further terms in the function that allow quadratic, cubic, and higher-order decision boundaries. The same techniques can be applied to the perceptron described in [Section 4.6](#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054_a.xhtml#s0095) to implement complex decision boundaries and also to least squares regression. An older but also very powerful technique for extending the perceptron is to connect units together into multilayer “neural networks.” We cover all these ideas in [Chapter 9](#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108.xhtml#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108), “Extending instance-based and linear models.” These ideas form the basis for deep learning methods, which we describe in depth later in [Chapter 10](#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X.xhtml#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X), “Deep learning: fundamentals,” and [Chapter 11](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121), “Advanced deep learning methods.” [Chapter 9](#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108.xhtml#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108), “Extending instance-based and linear models,” also describes classic instance-based learners, developing the simple nearest-neighbor method introduced in [Section 4.7](#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054_a.xhtml#s0120) and showing some more powerful alternatives that perform explicit generalization. The final part of the chapter extends linear regression for numeric prediction to a more sophisticated procedure that comes up with the tree representation introduced in [Section 3.3](#Ch003_67-89_B9780443158889000042.xhtml#s0020) and goes on to describe locally weighted regression, an instance-based strategy for numeric prediction.

Up to now most of the techniques described in this book have twin goals: maximizing predictive accuracy and producing comprehensible models. [Chapter 10](#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X.xhtml#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X), “Deep learning: fundamentals,” however, breaks away and focuses exclusively on maximizing modeling accuracy; it makes no attempt to provide insight by generating interpretable models. Here we enter the domain of “deep learning,” the idea of learning very complex artificial neural networks that implicitly extract increasingly more abstract representations of the underlying patterns in a dataset. We first cover the nuts and bolts of deep feedforward networks, including commonly used loss functions and activation functions, before delving into the details of training and evaluating deep networks, including hyperparameter tuning, data augmentation, and pretraining. Next we introduce a particular type of feedforward network, called a convolutional neural network, that reduces the number of parameters to be learned by weight sharing. We also describe autoencoders, which are network models for unsupervised learning, and recurrent networks, which are designed for sequential data. [Chapter 10](#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X.xhtml#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X), “Deep learning: fundamentals,” closes with some pointers to software implementations of deep learning methods.

In [Chapter 11](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121), “Advanced deep learning methods,” we consider more recent and advanced topics in the area. First we discuss methods for generating realistic data such as artificial photographic images or artworks, automatically. These generative AI techniques include generative adversarial networks (GANs), variational autoencoders (VAEs), and diffusion models. We then discuss advances in neural networks for natural language processing (NLP), beginning with word embedding methods for learning representations of dictionary words and building toward the transformer, a neural network architecture based on the concept of *attention*, which has revolutionized the field of NLP and beyond, with consequences that have already made shockwaves in our daily lives. We close this chapter with several other advanced but practically important topics, including adversarial examples that can be used to deliberately “fool” a deep neural network into making incorrect predictions, knowledge distillation methods for reducing the size of a model while retaining its performance, and deep reinforcement learning methods at the intersection of deep learning and traditional artificial intelligence, which have cracked the nut of creating an AI-based system for playing the game of Go better than the world’s best professional players.

The main focus of this book is on supervised techniques for machine learning, although we also consider unsupervised learning in the form of clustering and association rule mining. However, there are other, less conventional learning settings. In fact, we have already encountered one in [Chapter 4](#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054.xhtml#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054), “Algorithms: the basic methods”—multi-instance learning (although it can also be interpreted as a form of supervised learning with bag-based examples). [Chapter 12](#Ch013_473-484_B9780443158889000133.xhtml#Ch013_473-484_B9780443158889000133), “Beyond supervised and unsupervised learning,” covers more advanced techniques for multi-instance learning than those in [Chapter 4](#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054.xhtml#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054), “Algorithms: the basic methods.” We also look at semisupervised learning, which, by combining supervised and unsupervised learning, promises a tantalizing opportunity to exploit unlabeled data for learning more accurate classification and numeric prediction models.
