
Bayesian techniques had been used in the field of pattern recognition ([Duda and Hart, 1973](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur148)) for 20 years before they were adopted by machine learning researchers ([Langley, Iba, and Thompson, 1992](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur345)) and made to work on datasets with redundant attributes ([Langley and Sage, 1994](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur346)) and numeric attributes ([John and Langley, 1995](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur295)). The label `Naïve Bayes` is unfortunate because it is hard to use this method without feeling simpleminded. However, there is nothing naïve about its use in appropriate circumstances. The multinomial `Naïve Bayes` model, which is particularly useful for text classification, was investigated by [McCallum and Nigam (1998)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur381).

The classic paper on decision tree induction was written by [Quinlan (1986)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur441), who described the basic `ID3` procedure developed in this chapter. A comprehensive description of the method, including the improvements that are embodied in `C4.5`, appears in a classic book by [Quinlan (1993)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur443), which gives a listing of the complete `C4.5` system, written in the `C` programming language. `Prism` was developed by [Cendrowska (1987)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur87), who also introduced the contact lens dataset.

Association rules are introduced and described in the database literature rather than in the machine learning literature. Here the emphasis is very much on dealing with huge amounts of data rather than on sensitive ways of testing and evaluating algorithms on limited datasets. The algorithm introduced in this chapter is the `Apriori` method developed by Agrawal and his associates ([Agrawal, Imielinski, and Swami, 1993a](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur6), [1993b](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur7); [Agrawal and Srikant, 1994](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur8)). A survey of association-rule mining appears in an article by [Chen, Jan, and Yu (1996)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur93).

Linear regression is described in most standard statistical texts, and a particularly comprehensive treatment can be found in [Lawson and Hanson (1995)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur353). The use of linear models for classification enjoyed a great deal of popularity in the 1960s; [Nilsson (1965)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur419) is an excellent reference. He defined a `linear threshold unit` as a binary test of whether a linear function is greater or less than zero and a `linear machine` as a set of linear functions, one for each class, whose value for an unknown example was compared and the largest chosen as its predicted class. In the distant past, perceptrons fell out of favor on publication of an influential book that showed that they had fundamental limitations ([Minsky and Papert, 1969](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur396)); however, more complex systems of linear functions have enjoyed a resurgence in recent years in the form of neural networks, described in [Section 9.2](#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108.xhtml#s0050), [Chapter 10](#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X.xhtml#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X), “Deep learning: fundamentals,” and [Chapter 11](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121), “Advanced deep learning methods.” The `Winnow` algorithms were introduced by Nick Littlestone in his PhD thesis ([Littlestone, 1988](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur363), [1989](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur364)). Multiresponse linear classifiers have found application in an operation called `stacking` that combines the output of other learning algorithms, described in [Chapter 8](#Ch009_323-345_B9780443158889000091.xhtml#Ch009_323-345_B9780443158889000091), “Ensemble learning” ([Wolpert, 1992](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur573)).

[Fix and Hodges (1951)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur169) performed the first analysis of the nearest-neighbor method, and [Johns (1961)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur296) pioneered its use in classification problems. Nearest-neighbor methods gained popularity in machine learning through the work of [Aha (1992)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur9), who showed that instance-based learning can be combined with noisy exemplar pruning and attribute weighting and that the resulting methods perform well in comparison with other learning methods. We take this up again in [Chapter 9](#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108.xhtml#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108), “Extending instance-based and linear models.”

The `k`D-tree data structure was developed by [Friedman, Bentley, and Finkel (1977)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur196). Our description closely follows an explanation given by Andrew Moore in his PhD thesis ([Moore, 1991](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur400)), who, along with [Omohundro (1987)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur425), pioneered its use in machine learning. [Moore (2000)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur401) described sophisticated ways of constructing ball trees that perform well even with thousands of attributes. We took our ball tree example from lecture notes by Alexander Gray of Carnegie-Mellon University.
