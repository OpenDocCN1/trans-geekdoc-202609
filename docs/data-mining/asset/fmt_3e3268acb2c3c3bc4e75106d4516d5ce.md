
### Personalization and Communication
Personalization has become a key part of the surveillance capitalism business model. To receive personalized search results, personalized product and movie recommendations, personalized assistance from personal digital assistants, and yes, personalized advertising, we as users must submit to giving our data to Big Other. Varian, Google’s Chief Economist, sees this as a fair and reasonable trade, like giving your private information to your doctor, lawyer, or accountant. Zuboff instead sees this as exploitative. For Zuboff, rather than a reciprocal exchange the products and services become the “hooks” that lure people into a bad deal. It has become difficult to entirely opt out of the surveillance economy since those products have become integral to our society and necessary to compete in the rat race with our peers. We have become desensitized and numb to the proposition of giving up our private data to Big Other. To Zuboff this is like a Faustian pact, also known as a deal with the Devil. In making a Faustian pact we are tricked, cheated, and even though we may initially think we got a bargain we will ultimately lose out.

### Continuous Experiments
Since big data is continually harvested as it becomes available, it is generally *observational data*, that is, data that is not constructed via a rigorous causal experiment design. As such it is generally difficult to determine causality from generic big data. This motivates the pervasive use of more deliberate causal experiments to identify causal patterns by trying out different website layouts or machine learning models, etc., on different subsets of the users and measuring how this impacts metrics such as click-through rates. At Google this practice is called *A/B testing*, and it is done routinely.

Concerns arise when the experiments aim to study not only the design of a system but how it can be used to control user behavior. The infamous 2014 “Facebook mood study” by Adam Kramer et al., for example, demonstrated that it was possible to alter users’ moods by modifying the amount of emotional content in their Facebook feeds, leading to a public outcry. (To be fair, the study aimed to investigate the psychological phenomenon of emotional contagion on a social network and did not explicitly seek to develop methods of influence and control.)

Far worse, Pokémon Go, an augmented reality game created by a subsidiary of Google called Niantic in which users hunt Pokémon that virtually appear on their devices at real-world locations, demonstrated that it is possible to herd users to visit real-world locations and profit from this digitally influenced behavior via local advertising. Zuboff calls this a “surveillance capitalist’s dream come true” since it facilitates collecting real-world user behavior data, the control of user behavior in physical spaces, and the experimental study of how to control users’ behavior and profit because of this control.

## 15.12 Further Reading and Bibliographic Notes
The paper on the “illusion of progress” in classification is by [Hand (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur248), and he found that a very simple method achieves more than 90% of the classification improvement yielded by the most sophisticated scheme.

There is a substantial volume of literature that treats the topic of massive datasets, and we can only point to a few references here. [Fayyad and Smyth (1995)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur165) describe the application of data mining to voluminous data from scientific experiments. [Shafer, Agrawal, and Metha (1996)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur487) describe a parallel version of a top-down decision tree inducer. A sequential decision tree algorithm for massive disk-resident datasets has been developed by [Mehta, Agrawal, and Rissanen (1996)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur387). The technique of applying any algorithm to a large dataset by splitting it into smaller chunks and bagging or boosting the result is described by [Breiman (1999)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur65); [Frank, Holmes, Kirkby, and Hall (2002)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur181) explain the related pruning and selection scheme.

Early work on incremental work on decision trees is reported by [Utgoff (1989)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur537) and [Utgoff, Berkman, and Clouse (1997)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur538). The Hoeffding tree was introduced by [Domingos and Hulten (2000)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur139). Our description of it, including extensions and improvement, closely follows Kirkby’s (2007) PhD thesis. The MOA system is described by [Bifet, Holmes, Kirkby, and Pfahringer (2010)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur41).

Despite its importance, comparatively little seems to have been written about the general problem of incorporating metadata into practical data mining. A scheme for encoding domain knowledge into propositional rules and its use for both deduction and induction has been investigated by [Giraud-Carrier (1998)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur219). The related area of inductive logic programming, which deals with knowledge represented by first-order logic rules, is covered by [Bergadano and Gunetti (1996)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur34). Probabilistic logic learning is covered by [de Raedt (2008)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur124).
