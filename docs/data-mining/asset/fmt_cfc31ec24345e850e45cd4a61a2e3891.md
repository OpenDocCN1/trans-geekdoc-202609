
When tuning fairness hyperparameters, it is useful to first calculate the accuracy and/or the fairness measurement of the original (“typical”) machine learning model without a fairness intervention. With that information at hand, we can then threshold one of these quantities to a minimum acceptable value while optimizing the other via a grid search (or a more sophisticated hyperparameter tuning strategy such as Bayesian optimization). We can threshold the minimum accuracy (or other performance measure) to be at least, say, 95% of the accuracy of typical machine learning model while making the fairness metric as good as possible while satisfying that constraint. Alternatively, we can threshold the fairness metric (e.g., satisfying the 80% rule) while optimizing accuracy.

The prevailing “folk wisdom” is that fairness must always come at a cost of some degree of predictive performance based on the intuition in [Fig. 7.8](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#f0045). However, this is not the case. Often it is possible to obtain “*fairness for free*,” where a fairness metric is improved at no cost to predictive performance. Reasons for the fairness for free phenomenon include:

* Fairness penalties can act as a regularizer. They have the potential to reduce overfitting, hence improving generalization performance on unseen data.
* Multiple classifiers can potentially obtain same or similar numbers of errors. For example, many deep learning models with completely different weights may have essentially the same predictive performance. This is called the *Rashomon effect*. Some of those equally accurate classifiers may be fairer than others. We can therefore potentially choose between these classifiers to “gerrymander” the errors so that the system is fairer (see [Fig. 7.9](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#f0050)).

### Important Caveats to Fair AI/Data Mining

When employing fairness interventions it is essential to remember the limitations of the fair AI paradigm. A mathematical definition cannot, by itself, solve the sociotechnical problem of “fairness,” which has nontechnical dimensions and exceeds the narrowly defined AI/data mining system. Humans, including impacted stakeholders and experts from other fields such as law, the humanities, sociology, philosophy, psychology, and so on, must be part of the solution. The sociotechnical context in which an AI system is embedded must always be accounted for. The fair AI paradigm arguably oversimplifies complex sociotechnical problems to straightforward technical ones.

Even worse, fair AI technologies could potentially legitimize problematic systems by providing a veneer of ethical respectability to them. There are some systems that should never be deployed, no matter how much we intervene to make them fair. Affect-recognition systems, for example, are AI-based computer vision systems that analyze video, image, or audio data to detect emotions such as fear or anger. These systems have been deployed for applications in hiring, law enforcement, casinos, restaurants, hospitals, prisons, and beyond. As you might expect by now, many of these systems are racially biased. Some scholars have argued that the ethical issues with these systems run deeper and the surveillance they facilitate is harmful in and of itself. It has also been argued that these systems lack scientific validity, with the facial movement data they rely on being insufficient to determine emotion to the degree of accuracy required for their intended applications. In one view, correcting racial bias issues in affect-recognition systems would not be enough to make the systems acceptable for deployment in many of their intended use-cases.

Human-centered computing researcher Os Keyes and colleagues penned a scathing satire of the fair AI paradigm that serves to illustrate some of its limitations. They imagine an AI system that selects older adults with low social credit as candidates for being mulched into a high-nutrient slurry. If you have ever seen the film Soylent Green, which imagines a future in which humans are turned into food, you will understand the concept. In this scenario, an AI fairness analysis finds that White cisgender men are much more likely to be selected for mulching compared to other demographics. They perform an AI fairness intervention to correct this “bias” by *increasing* the mulching probability for all other demographics such as transgender women, Asian Americans, and so on, to match the high mulching probability of White cisgender men. They ensure that the system is adequately transparent in its decision making by publicly releasing the set of variables that were used to select “mulches”—phone and text message metadata, the number of Facebook friends, the number of birthday cards received, and so on. The system is made accountable, as the prospective mulchees can speak to a phone operator about factors of social credit the system may have overlooked that could be included in the next version of the system. The authors conclude that their system is fair, accountable, and transparent while working to solve the problem of food security, and so it must therefore be beyond all possible ethical reproach!
