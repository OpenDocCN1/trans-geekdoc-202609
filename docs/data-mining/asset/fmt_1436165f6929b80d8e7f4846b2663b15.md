
# AI Fairness and Bias in Machine Learning

This is more than just a hypothetical example. It could happen to you. According to popular job search engine site Glassdoor, on average, a company receives 250 applications for each position, out of which typically only four to six candidates receive an interview. This motivates the use of machine learning systems to automatically filter the resumes, saving time for recruiters. In 2014 US technology company Amazon developed an internal experimental machine learning tool for automatically assessing job applicants’ resumes. The system produced ratings for candidates between one and five stars, in a manner similar to how consumers rate products on the service. It was trained on 10 years of Amazon’s recruiting data. By the next year the company realized that the system was biased against women. If a resume included the word “women’s,” as in “founder of the women’s robotics team,” the candidate’s rating was downgraded. It similarly downgraded ratings from candidates who graduated from two all-women colleges. The machine learning model had learned to replicate the frequently discriminatory patterns of hiring in a male-dominated field that were naturally present in its training data.

To its credit, Amazon scrapped the system. Amazon told Reuters reporters that the tool “was never used by Amazon recruiters to evaluate candidates,” although it did not deny that recruiters had looked at its outputs. Other employers may not be as scrupulous. A 2015 study by software company Capterra estimated that 75% of recruiters and talent managers use recruiting software. It is not clear the extent to which these recruiting systems use machine learning to automatically filter the resumes but the use of such software is doubtless on the rise.

Since around the time of the Amazon recruiting tool incident, the study of *fairness and bias issues in AI and machine learning* has rapidly grown into a burgeoning area of research. There has thankfully been an increasing awareness of these issues, which have become an urgent concern as the kinds of models we have been studying in this book are now deployed in the systems that make decisions impacting each of our daily lives, both big and small, from recommendations of movies, to whether we get admitted to our dream college, to whether we receive bail or a lengthy prison sentence.

## AI Fairness in Criminal Justice

The latter scenario is one that has received high-profile scrutiny. In criminal justice, a risk assessment instrument (RAI) is an algorithmic tool for predicting a defendant’s risk of criminal activity in the future. This tool produces either numerical scores or discrete categories such as “high risk” that are assigned to defendants. RAIs are widely used to inform consequential decisions at multiple stages of the criminal justice process, including whether to incarcerate an individual before trial and/or the bond amount to avoid this and the length of their sentence. One of these systems, called Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions (COMPAS), was criticized in a study by journalists from ProPublica, an independent US newsroom. The study found that COMPAS was almost twice as likely to incorrectly predict re-offending for African Americans than for white people. Conversely, the study found COMPAS to be almost twice as likely to incorrectly predict that white people would not re-offend vs African Americans.

This study, by investigative journalist Julia Angwin and colleagues, has sparked a lot of discussion and debate. The US company that produced COMPAS, then called Northpointe (now renamed Equivant), disputed the findings, which ProPublica defended in response. Academics weighed in, with some arguing that fairness should instead be determined based on a different criterion, namely whether the risk scores are calibrated equally well in terms of predicting whether re-offending occurred between African American and white people. It can be shown mathematically that Angwin et al.’s criterion and this calibration criterion cannot both hold at the same time, due to differences in the base rate of (detected) reoffending between the two demographic groups. Regardless of how one views this complex scenario, it is clear that the use of AI algorithms in criminal justice is fraught with ethical challenges, necessitating transparency, independent audits, and robust debate.

## AI Fairness in Other Application Domains

Fairness and bias concerns can arise in almost every application of AI, machine learning, and data mining techniques in which data about human beings is used, especially when it is used to make decisions that impact the lives of those individuals.

### Credit Scoring

Decisions on whether to extend credit to an individual, and the size of the loan or credit card limit to offer, clearly fall within the “high impact” category. The creditworthiness of an individual is frequently estimated via a *credit score*, a numerical score that is typically generated by statistical models that aim to determine the likelihood that the individuals will repay a loan. Apple Card, the credit card operated by American multinational firms Apple Inc. and Goldman Sachs, came under scrutiny for possible gender discrimination after two prominent male technologists alleged that their wives received 10 to 20 times smaller credit offers, despite sharing all assets and having similar or better externally generated credit scores. The technologists David Heinemeier Hansson, creator of the popular Ruby on Rails web development platform, and Steve Wozniak, cofounder of Apple, aired their complaints on social media, where they promptly went viral.

The New York State Department of Financial Services (DFS) investigated the matter and ultimately did not find evidence of unlawful discrimination against women, concluding that men and women with similar credit characteristics received similar application outcomes from Apple Card. Nevertheless, DFS’ report criticized Apple Card for a lack of transparency to consumers due to the black-box nature of the algorithm and a policy requiring account holders to wait six months to appeal their credit terms. The report emphasized that unequal access to credit remains a systemic problem and that algorithms may perpetuate this, even while following the law. It called for a modernization of the use of credit scoring and associated laws and regulations to remedy this situation.

### Facial Recognition

Facial recognition software has also come under scrutiny for potential algorithmic bias. Researcher Joy Buolamwini was working on an undergraduate computer science class project where she was tasked with developing a peek-a-boo game when she discovered that the facial recognition software library she was using could not detect her face. Buolamwini, who is Black, needed to have her lighter-skinned roommate step in so that she could test her code and complete the project. At that time she figured that “somebody else would solve this problem.” She subsequently found that facial recognition software continued to fail to recognize her whenever she encountered it—and nobody else came along to solve the issue. She found that if she put a white facial mask over her face the software would successfully detect her.

This experience led her to perform a systematic study on algorithmic bias in facial recognition software with coauthor Timnit Gebru. Their study examined three off-the-shelf commercial systems for detecting an individual’s gender based on image data, developed by Microsoft, IBM, and Face++. The researchers found that the systems performed worse for darker-skinned subjects than lighter-skinned subjects and worse for female subjects than male subjects. Moreover, it performed worst for darker-skinned female subjects and best for lighter-skinned male subjects. The study served to draw attention to unequal performance in facial recognition software.
