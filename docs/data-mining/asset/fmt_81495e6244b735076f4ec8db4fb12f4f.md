
The CBOW model is trained via stochastic gradient descent on the log-likelihood objective function. Here, the normalization constant for the softmax requires computing `V` terms, the size of the vocabulary, which can be very large, slowing down computation. This computation can be reduced by using the *hierarchical softmax* approach, which represents the vocabulary as the leaves of a Huffman binary tree, with shorter codes (corresponding to shorter paths through the tree to a leaf node) given to frequent words. In the ideal case where the Huffman tree is perfectly balanced, this would reduce the computational complexity from `O(V)` to `O(log[2](V))`.

Like the CBOW, the skip-gram is ideally trained by maximizing the log-likelihood objective function; it also has a computational challenge in that normalizing the distributions, necessary to compute the log-likelihood and its gradient, scales linearly in the size of the vocabulary. To address this the hierarchical softmax approach can once again be used. Alternatively *negative sampling* is a simpler—and consequently more popular—approach. Instead of performing a multiclass classification of the context words `w[j]` vs. every other word in the dictionary, negative sampling performs a binary classification of each context word `w[j]` vs. a randomly chosen alternative word. In practice, several (e.g., 10) such alternative words (“*negative samples*”) are drawn, and the model is trained to distinguish between the true context words and the negative samples. Negative sampling is a simplification of a related technique called *noise-contrastive estimation* (*NCE*), which follows a similar strategy but which more properly accounts for the noise distribution. Hence, asymptotically approaches the log-likelihood objective function as the number of negative samples approaches infinity but is a bit slower and may not be necessary to achieve the goal of learning word embeddings. It is important to note that the training objectives for CBOW and the skip-gram are merely convenient proxy objectives that enable the recovery of word-embedding representations. Since we only really care about the word embeddings we obtain by the procedure, we are free to approximate the objective function so long as good embeddings are found. The authors of the original word2vec papers (Tomas Mikolov et al. 2013) found that negative sampling was sufficient for this task.

Depending on your target application, training word embeddings from scratch on your own problem-domain-specific dataset using the earlier algorithms may or may not be the best choice. There is often a trade-off between the domain-specific information contained in a domain-specific dataset and the sheer magnitude of generic internet-scale big data corpora that contain a lot of information about language and general knowledge about the world. While a model trained on the domain of interest will generally be best if you have sufficient data, in other cases it can be valuable to leverage big data. *Pretrained word embedding* models, trained on internet-scale big data, offer the power of big data and the convenience of a model that has already been trained by someone else. In practice, you could choose to either:

1. Train word embeddings from scratch on your target corpus,
2. Download pretrained word embeddings trained on generic big data and use them off-the-shelf,
3. Fine-tune pretrained embeddings on your target corpus, or
4. Learn embeddings as part of training a more sophisticated deep neural network model, potentially using a pretraining and fine-tuning approach for the embedding component of the model.

The *pretraining and fine-tuning* approach in options 3 and 4 is often effective since it leverages both generic big data and task-specific small data in a sensible way. The benefits of this approach extend beyond word embeddings to more powerful LLMs.

A key insight of the word2vec research papers was that ingesting enormous amounts of text data is crucial to the performance of language models. At the time that these methods were published (circa 2013), the simplicity of the word2vec models was crucial in scaling them to very big datasets. More recently, with improvements in computational power, particularly due to GPU and tensor processing unit (TPU) resources, and advances in deep learning architectures and algorithms, these models have been superseded by more sophisticated deep neural networks (DNNs). Powerful DNN-based language models still generally make use of word-embedding representations as components of the overall architecture. The word embeddings in a DNN can be initialized randomly or via one of the aforementioned approaches.

### Document Classification with Deep Neural Networks

Many NLP tasks can be cast as document classification. The *document categorization* task is perhaps the most obvious example, in which documents are categorized based on their content or themes, such as whether news articles are about sports or the weather. *Sentiment analysis*, which aims to predict whether the tone of a piece of text is positive or negative, such as whether a product or movie review expresses a positive opinion, can also viewed as a task that maps documents to class labels. There are many possible document classification tasks. Other examples include *language identification*, in which we must detect the language that the document is written in; *authorship ascription*, in which we aim to identify the author of a document (who might be different from person who claims to be the author); and *spam classification*, in which emails are flagged as unsolicited emails intended for marketing, phishing, fraud, or other undesirable purposes.

A key challenge in document classification is that documents are sequential in nature and thus are not explicitly in a fixed length representation, so standard supervised learning algorithms do not immediately apply. A simple—and often effective—approach to resolve this is to extract fixed-length representations of the documents before applying a standard feed-forward neural network (or, indeed, any other classifier). Classically, this was often done via count-based features, where there is a feature corresponding to each word in the dictionary. Options include *binary* features (with a one if the word occurs at least once in the document or zero otherwise), *bag-of-words* features (where the features correspond to the number of occurrences of each word in the document), and *term frequency-inverse document frequency* (TF × IDF) features. In TF × IDF we account for the fact that in the bag of words representation, commonly occurring words tend to get the highest values even though these words are often the least informative ones. This is done by down- weighting the counts of words that occur in most documents ([Section 6.3](#Ch006_209-259_B9780443158889000078.xhtml#s0060)).
