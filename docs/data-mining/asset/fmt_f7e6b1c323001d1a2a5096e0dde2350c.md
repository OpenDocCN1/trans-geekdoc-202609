
A larger follow-up study by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (`NIST`) examining 189 software algorithms supported the findings, concluding that demographic bias existed in most facial recognition systems for the tasks of (1) confirming a photo of an individual matches another photo of the person in a database and (2) determining whether a photographed individual has any match in a database. The bias in these tasks has clear and troubling implications for the use of facial recognition technologies in law enforcement surveillance contexts, where such failures are likely to contribute to systematic discriminatory patterns in policing. For example, a false-positive match can lead to an individual being subject to undeserved police scrutiny and possible arrest. The `NIST` study found higher rates of false-positive matches for images of Asian and African American faces compared to images of Caucasian faces.

## Sources of Bias

We’ve now seen several examples of data-driven algorithms that are potentially biased or unfair. But the companies that made these systems must have been greedy, thoughtless, or careless, and if *you*, talented and clever as you are, made such a system you’d easily avoid these problems. You’d simply make sure that the data was representative and reliable, and you wouldn’t use features such as gender, race, or immigration status, and everything would be fine. Right?

Don’t be so sure. Bias can creep into data-driven algorithms in subtle ways. A clear example of this was given in an online tutorial by NLP developer Robyn Speer, called “How to Make a Racist AI Without Really Trying.^(<a aria-label="noteref" href="#fn1" id="Cfn1">1</a>1)” In the tutorial, she set out to make a sentiment classifier that predicts whether a word is a positive word, like “great,” or a negative word, like “terrible.” Using these word-level predictions, this classifier could then be used to obtain a sentiment score for a document, for example, a review of a restaurant. For simplicity Speer obtained an overall sentiment score for the document by averaging the scores for each word. Thus the text “The chicken was juicy, tender, and delicious” would ideally get a high sentiment score under the model, while “The chicken was flabby and tasteless” would get a low sentiment score.

Speer began by obtaining features for her model, which in this case are *word embeddings—*feature vectors designed to represent the meaning of each word in the dictionary that is automatically obtained based on data. In this case the word embedding features were pretrained via an algorithm called `GLoVE` on a publicly available dataset of scraped webpages, the `Common Crawl`. She then obtained gold-standard sentiment labels (positive or negative) for around 7000 words from a linguistic resource available online and trained a `logistic regression` classifier on the now-labeled word embedding data.

Recall from [Chapter 4](#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054.xhtml#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054), “Algorithms: the basic methods,” that the `logistic regression` model applies a linear function to an instance, producing a number called the `log-odds` (since this number will turn out to be the logarithm of the odds that the instance is positive). `logistic regression` applies the `logit` transformation to the `log-odds` to produce a class probability between zero and one. Speer uses the `log-odds`, which is a number between negative infinity and positive infinity, as a sentiment score for a word, where larger negative numbers correspond to negative sentiment, and large positive numbers correspond to positive sentiment. We now have in hand a model that can take a piece of text as input and produce a sentiment score between plus and minus infinity that is more positive if the text expresses positive sentiment (“*The chicken was delicious*”), more negative if the text expresses negative sentiment (“*The chicken was horrible*”), and roughly zero if the text is neutral (“*The chicken was okay*.”)

According to Speer, the final step was to “behold the monstrosity that we have created!” After establishing that the model worked as intended, she applied it to the relatively neutral sentence “*Let’s go get Italian food*,” obtaining a substantially positive score of around 2.0. In contrast, “*Let’s go get Chinese food*” received a score of around 1.4, while “*Let’s go get Mexican food*” got a score of only 0.4. This is concerning—why should Mexican food be considered less positive than Italian food?

When applied to names, the system is even worse: “*My name is Emily*” (a stereotypically White name) gets a score of 2.2, while “*My name is Shaniqua*” (a stereotypically Black name) gets a score of –0.5. Further examining this result by applying the model to lists of stereotypical names belonging to several demographics, Speer found that the system gave highly positive average sentiment scores to stereotypically White names and negative sentiment scores to stereotypically Black names, with stereotypically Arab/Muslim names and Latinx names being somewhere in between these two extremes ([`Fig. 7.4`](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#f0025)).

We can all agree that the behavior of this system is hurtful and offensive. Worse, if this system were used to analyze resumes to make hiring recommendations, for example, it could easily introduce a systematic bias due to the applicants’ names. One way that this might happen is that the sentiment scores produced by the model could flag an applicant with a stereotypically Black name as having a negative attitude, and hence less employable, based on their name alone. Discrimination in hiring based on race or ethnicity is illegal in most countries, which could put the company that used this model in hot water with the law.

What went wrong here? Nothing that was done in designing this model was nefarious or in favor of one demographic group or another. Every decision made was an apparently innocuous step down the “path of least resistance” toward getting a model up and running. There were no sensitive demographic features like gender or race in the inputs to the classifier. Features were obtained via a popular model that was pretrained on a widely used dataset that is intended to be a typical and representative internet corpus. Yet, the behavior of the system is abhorrent.
