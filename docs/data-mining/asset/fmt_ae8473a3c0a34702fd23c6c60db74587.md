
While the `Seq2Seq` model’s encoder-decoder approach is intuitive and was an important step forward, a downside of the approach is that a single, fixed-length representation must represent all the information in the entire sentence, and this would be expected to become increasingly difficult as the length of the sentence increases (a fact that is borne out in empirical studies). The `RNNsearch` model improves over `Seq2Seq` by allowing its decoder to look back over the input document and decide which words it should pay the most attention to in generating the next token in the translation. This `attention mechanism` produces weights for each word in the input sentence that sum to one via a `softmax` neuron. ## 11.3 Transformer Architecture

The `NMT` models we’ve discussed so far use recurrent neural networks such as `LSTMs`. These models process the data sequentially, which is a major computational bottleneck. In `RNN` models the sequence must be processed one token at a time, which slows down processing depending on the length of the sequence, and this processing can’t easily be parallelized via a `GPU` or `TPU`. Convolutional neural networks are another alternative, and these models can be parallelized. However, `CNNs` can’t directly model long-range dependences, which are important for many `NLP` applications, including `NMT`.

To resolve this situation, researchers from Google proposed the `transformer`, an architecture that aims to avoid `RNNs` and convolution by only using attention. This enables more parallelization than `RNNs` while still capturing long-range dependencies. While their stated goal was to speed up the training of `LLMs`, the `transformer` approach also led to dramatic improvements in `NLP` performance. Though much of this improvement may be due to the larger-scale training that parallelization enables, other benefits of the architecture likely play a role, including the ability of `transformer` models to consider an entire sentence (or more) at once, and the effectiveness of attention in guiding the focus of the model to salient parts of the text. Its application has extended past `NMT` to become the state of the art in `LLMs` and their application to the majority of `NLP` tasks and beyond into other areas including computer vision. We will now discuss the components of `transformer`-based language models.

### Scaled Dot-Product Attention

As we discussed earlier, attention is a neural network technique for determining which part of some data to focus on. The `transformer` model uses a specialized version of attention, called `scaled dot-product attention`. We will view attention using an information retrieval metaphor, where the `items` of interest that we might attend to (such as the other words in a sentence) are analogous to webpages that we are searching for, and the information that determines what is currently important (e.g., the current word) is analogous to a search query. The `items` are assumed to have a vector embedding, called a `key`, which determines its relevance to the “`query`,” which also has an embedding. An item’s `key` need not be the same as its `value`, which is the vector embedding that we will use to represent it going forward, if it does indeed turn out to be salient. Then, attention can be understood as trying to “retrieve” the `values` of `items` whose `key` is relevant to the `query` by assigning relevance scores (a.k.a. `attention weights`) according to the similarity between the `key` and the `query`.
