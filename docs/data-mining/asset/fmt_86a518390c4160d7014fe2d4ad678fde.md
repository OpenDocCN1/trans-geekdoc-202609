
PPCA corresponds to factorizing a covariance matrix. Another way to reduce the number of parameters in a continuous Gaussian model is to use sparse inverse covariance models that, when combined with mixture models and EM, yield another form of clustering with correlated attributes. [Edwards (2012)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur156) provides a nice introduction to graphical modeling, including mixed models with discrete and continuous components. Unlike any other treatments he also examines graphical Gaussian models and delves further into the correspondence between a graphical model and the sparsity structure of an inverse covariance matrix. LSA was introduced by [Deerwester, Dumais, Landauer, Furnas, and Harshman (1990)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur126). pLSA has its origins in [Hofmann (1999)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur272). Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA^b) was proposed in [Blei et al. (2003)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur47). The highly effective “collapsed Gibbs sampling” approach for LDA^b was proposed by [Teh et al. (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur523), who also extended the concept to variational methods. Rather than applying LDA^b naïvely when looking for trends over time, Blei and Lafferty’s (2006) *dynamic topic models* treat the temporal evolution of topics explicitly; they examined topical trends in the journal *Science*. [Griffiths and Steyvers (2004)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur235) used Bayesian model selection to determine the number of topics in their LDA^b analysis of *Proceedings of the National Academy of Science* abstracts. [Griffiths and Steyvers (2004)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur235) and [Teh et al. (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur523) give more details on the collapsed Gibbs sampling and variational approaches to LDA^b. Hierarchical Dirichlet processes ([Teh et al., 2006](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur523)) and related techniques offer alternatives to the problem of determining the number of topics or clusters in hierarchical Bayesian models. These technically sophisticated methods are quite popular, and high-quality implementations are available online.

Logistic regression is sometimes referred to as the workhorse of applied statistics; [Hosmer and Lemeshow (2004)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur277) is a useful resource. Nelder and Wedderburn’s (1972) work led to the generalized linear modeling framework. [McCullagh (1980)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur383) developed proportional odds models for ordinal regression, which are sometimes called ordered logit models because they use the generalized logit function. [Frank and Hall (2001)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur179) showed how to adapt arbitrary machine learning techniques to ordered predictions. McCullagh and Nelder’s (1989) widely cited monograph is another good source for additional details on the framework of generalized linear models.

[Tibshirani (1996)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur525) developed the famous “Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator,” also known as the LASSO, while [Zou and Hastie (2005)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur600) developed the “elastic net” regularization approach.

Kernel logistic regression transforms a linear classifier into a nonlinear one, and probabilistic sparse kernel techniques are attractive alternatives to support vector machines. [Tipping (2001)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur530) proposed a “relevance vector machine” that manipulates priors on parameters in a way that encourages kernel weights to become zero during learning. [Lawrence, Seeger, and Herbrich (2003)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur352) proposed an “informative vector machine,” which tackles the problem with a fast, sparse Gaussian process method in the sense of [Williams and Rasmussen (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur566). [Zhu and Hastie (2005)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur599) formulated sparse kernel logistic regression as an “import vector machine” that uses greedy search methods. However, none of these approaches the popularity of Cortes and Vapnik’s (1995) support vector machines, perhaps because their objective functions are not convex, in contrast to that of a support vector machine (SVM) (and `L`[2] regularized kernel logistic regression). Convex optimization problems have a single minimum in the loss function (maximum in the likelihood). When probabilities are needed from an SVM, [Platt (1999)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur436) shows how to fit a logistic regression to the classification scores.

Some techniques for smoothing `n`-grams arise from applying prior distributions to the parameters of the model’s conditional probabilities. Others come from different perspectives, such as interpolation techniques where weighted combinations of lower-order `n`-grams are used. Good-Turing discounting ([Good, 1953](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur224)) (coinvented by Alan Turing, one of the fathers of computing) and Witten–Bell smoothing ([Witten and Bell, 1991](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur570)) are based on these ideas. [Brants and Franz (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur60) discuss the massive Google `n`-gram collections mentioned in [Section 14.5](#Ch015_516-570_B9780443158889000157_b.xhtml#s0215); they are available as a 24 GB compressed text file from the Linguistic Data Consortium.

[Rabiner and Juang (1986)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur446) and [Rabiner (1989)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur445) give a classic introduction and tutorial, respectively, on Hidden Markov Models. They have been used extensively for decades in speech recognition systems and are widely applicable to many other problems. The human genome sequencing project stretched from around 1990 to the early 2000s ([International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium, 2001](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur284); Venter, et al., 2001) and spawned a surge of activity in recognizing and modeling genes in genomes using hidden Markov models ([Burge and Karlin, 1997](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur79); [Kulp, Haussler, Rees, and Eeckman, 1996](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur336))—a particularly impressive and important application of data mining. [Murphy (2002)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur406) is an excellent source for details on how dynamic Bayesian networks extend hidden Markov models.
