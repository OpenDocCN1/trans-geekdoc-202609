
More formally, let `M(D)` be a privacy mechanism that returns the answer to a query on database `D`. Then `M(D)` is `ϵ`-differentially private if for all pairs of datasets `D[1]` and `D[2]` differing in a single element and all `S` ⊆ `Range(M)`,

`Pr(M(D[1]) ∈ S) ≤ e^(ϵ) × Pr(M(D[2]) ∈ S)`. (7.1)

In other words, if one instance in the dataset was changed, the probability of any output of the algorithm would be almost the same, up to a small multiplicative constant `e^(ϵ)`. Here, `ϵ` is the degree to which the mechanism is differentially private (lower is better).

## Techniques for Achieving Differential Privacy

Differential privacy is typically achieved by injecting random noise to the answers to the queries, with the amount of noise being calibrated to ensure that very little can be learned about any one person’s data. For example, suppose we are interested in learning the proportion of the population that engages in an embarrassing or illegal activity. You can no doubt think of a few of those. Pick your favorite one! For no particular reason, we will refer to this activity as `XXX`.

If the participants of our study are to contribute their data, they would like reassurance that the study will not reveal whether they engage in `XXX`. To help keep their secret safe, the participants are asked to flip a coin. If the coin comes up tails, they will answer the question truthfully, “yes” or “no.” If the coin comes up heads, they will instead answer randomly by flipping the coin again and responding “yes” if it is heads and “no” if it is tails. This gives them plausible deniability as a “yes” answer could have come from the coin flips instead of from their honest answer. If the coin comes up heads both times the participant would respond “yes” regardless of their data, and this happens one-quarter of the time. We then count the number of “yes” responses across the population.
