
Adversarial examples were first identified by [Szegedy et al. (2014)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur521), who also proposed the first method for constructing them, via box-constrained limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (L-BFGS) optimization. [Goodfellow et al. (2015)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur226) demonstrated that adversarial examples are not due to the nonlinearity of deep neural networks; rather, even linear models can suffer from this issue in high dimensions. They used this insight to propose a simple and fast linear approach to generating adversarial examples—the fast gradient sign method—that has proved effective and highly influential. [Papernot et al. (2016)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur427) formalized the threat model for adversarial examples and proposed more targeted methods for generating them, which exploit knowledge of the DNN architecture to perturb only a small number of features (e.g., pixels) to evade detection while potentially flipping the label to a chosen alternate label. [Kurakin et al. (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur338) showed that adversarial examples can be exploited in the physical world, when they are perceived via a camera, with important and worrisome implications for physical systems such as IoT devices and autonomous vehicles. [Carlini and Wagner (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur84) returned to optimization-based approaches, developing more effective methods that can defeat robustness measures designed to protect against these attacks, such as defensive distillation.

Knowledge distillation was first invented by [Bucilă et al. (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur74) but popularized much later by [Hinton et al. (2015)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur266), who proposed using temperature-adjusted versions of the teacher model’s predictions as “soft targets” for the student model. Deep Q-networks (DQNs) for deep reinforcement learning were proposed by [Mnih et al. (2015)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur399), who used them successfully to develop human-level agents for playing Atari games. [Silver et al. (2016)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur491) combined DQNs with Monte-Carlo tree search to develop AlphaGo, which at that stage had already defeated European Go champion Fan Hui. Silver et al. (2017a) developed AlphaGo Zero, the “tabula rasa” version of AlphaGo that learned completely from scratch, followed by AlphaZero ([Silver et al., 2017](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur492)b), which generalized AlphaGo to achieve superhuman performance in other games like Chess and Shogi.
