
One barrier to the implementation of ethical and responsible AI practices is a lack of understanding by machine learning practitioners on what ethical standards they—and the systems they are building— should aim to uphold and what the best practices are. In a step in the right direction, numerous organizations spanning government, industry, and professional societies have published documents outlining ethical AI principles that they advocate, housed within the context of cohesive ethical frameworks. These frameworks aim to provide guidelines for practitioners to follow. Organizations that have released ethical AI frameworks include Microsoft, Google, IBM, the European Commission, UNESCO, the White House, the ACM, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), the US Department of Defense (DoD), and the NIST. Fairness, accountability, and transparency are included as principles to uphold in most of these frameworks, in addition to other ethical principles such as privacy and nonmaleficence (“doing no harm”).

We began this chapter with an overview of the responsible AI principles advanced by the ACM. Note, however, that the practice of producing ethical and responsible AI guidelines has been criticized as insufficient, since without external enforcement or legal requirement, the guidelines do little to ensure that the responsible AI principles will actually be upheld by a company while potentially fending off justified scrutiny or criticism.

## Laws to Enforce Ethical Standards

While ethical frameworks and guidelines may help machine learning engineers understand what to strive for, they are typically nonbinding and not enforced. The need for laws to ensure ethical and responsible AI practices is becoming increasingly urgent. Nowhere is the gap between guideline and law more plainly stark than the 2022 Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights from the Biden administration White House’s Office of Science and Technology Policy. The Blueprint lays out five key principles to guide AI development, deployment, and maintenance: *safe and effective systems*, *algorithmic discrimination protections*, *data privacy*, *notice and explanation*, and, finally, *human alternatives*, *consideration*, *and fallback*. However, it is not legally binding. The Biden administration followed up the Blueprint with the 2023 Executive Order on the Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence, which includes a number of directives, many of which direct government agencies to perform studies on trustworthy AI. As such, although the Executive Order has little in the way of enforceable regulation or requirements that extend beyond the federal government, it will likely help to pave the way for future legislation, along with the Blueprint.

Apart from the Executive Order, as of early 2024 there were no US federal laws that specifically regulate AI. Although AI-specific laws would be preferable, general antidiscrimination laws such as Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the 1968 Fair Housing Act (FHA), and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990 can still be applicable to AI-based decision making in certain contexts such as employment and housing discrimination.

Efforts to regulate AI generally run into pushbacks from industry and from politicians aligned with industry. One of the authors of this textbook (Foulds) gave both oral and written testimony in support of an AI ethics bill in the state of Maryland in 2021 and 2023. The 2021 bill would have required algorithmic decision-making systems procured by the State to follow ethical principles including fairness and transparency and to be subject to evaluation regarding adherence to the principles. Testimony opposing the bill was given by representatives of the Maryland Tech Council (a technology industry trade organization) and Amazon, which spoke in support of AI ethics as a general principle but warned of stifling innovation and profit and wanted a (pro-industry) seat at the table; these statements helped to kill the bill. A follow-up bill was introduced in 2023 that would merely secure funds to set up a commission to investigate these types of issues and make recommendations on them. There were several favorable testimonies in support of the bill, and no testimonies were given against it. However, it still failed to become law. Efforts to get a similar bill passed continue. This example illustrates how despite the widespread support for AI ethics spanning industry and government, achieving corresponding legislation with real teeth will require strong and sustained advocacy.

## The Main Paradigms of Fair AI/Data Mining

Nontechnical AI fairness interventions are a crucial part of the process that must not be neglected. Since this is a technical book we will now focus our attention on technological interventions. These techniques are not a magic bullet and they do not avoid the need for difficult conversations and effortful collaborations between corporate representatives and impacted stakeholders. They do not shield a firm from critique once a system is deployed. But if used correctly, they can improve the fairness properties of a machine learning system, perhaps as easily as through a simple application programming interface (`API`) library call.

To implement fairness in an AI system, we need to specify what we mean by “fairness” in a concrete, mathematical way that can be implemented in a computer. This is difficult because fairness is a complex, abstract, sociotechnical construct. Nobody knows quite what it means, and any two people may have wildly different conceptions of it. Context is also important, as what might be considered “fair” in one scenario may be considered “unfair” in another. Nevertheless, we proceed. Typically we impose on our AI system a mathematical “*fairness definition*” that aims to ensure some corresponding idealized notion of fairness. At a very high level, the main approaches to defining and implementing fairness are:

*   • *Fairness metrics*, which aim to quantify fairness or bias with some function. In this approach, the learning algorithm aims to get a good “fairness score” according to the chosen fairness metric, while still predicting well.

*   • *Fair representation learning*, where we produce a new representation of the data that removes all association with “protected attributes” such as gender, race, age, etc., while capturing all other relevant information.

*   • *Causal fairness*, where we model the causal structure of the problem domain and achieve causal properties that are deemed fair. This approach aims to reckon with the causal nature that is often behind fairness and bias issues.

We can inject fairness into a machine learning model at different stages of the processing pipeline, such as:

*   • *Preprocessing:* Make the data “fair” before applying your analysis. For example, one could upsample minority groups to give them greater representation in the dataset or correct sampling errors that skewed the data in an unfair way. Fair representation learning can be understood as an implementation of this strategy, in that a fair representation is constructed before giving the processed data to a downstream model.

*   • *Inprocessing:* Ensure fairness during the learning process. We can add a “*fairness penalty*” to the model’s objective function or constrain the learning algorithm to achieve a minimum level of fairness. Adversarial learning approaches to debiasing a model based on GANs and VAEs (discussed in [Chapter 11](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121), “Advanced deep learning methods”) can sometimes be understood as inprocessing techniques, unless they are used for the purposes of fair representation learning.
