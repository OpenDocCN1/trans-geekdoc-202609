
We can start to figure out the likely reason behind the issue by noticing that the system has essentially replicated negative stereotypes that are unfortunately pervasive. The word embedding features were obtained based on a corpus of scraped webpages, the Common Crawl, and those webpages likely contain a substantial number of racist sentiments that were expressed by the people who made them. The word embeddings were influenced by those harmful statements in the corpus, which led the sentiment classifier to reflect those harmful statements in its predictions. In other words, bias in our society crept into the data and thus into the system. Even though the Common Crawl dataset was created by a nonprofit organization, with the intention of democratizing internet data for the public benefit, it still ended up leading a model that was trained on it to exhibit harmful behavior. This example shows that even with good intentions we can easily build AI systems that can do great harm unless we are extremely careful and diligent. There is no magic bullet to prevent this. It requires vigilance at every step of the model development process, and indeed, the entire life cycle of the model (as illustrated in [Chapter 1](#Ch001_3-42_B9780443158889000029.xhtml#Ch001_3-42_B9780443158889000029), “What’s it all about?,” [Fig. 1.4](#Ch001_3-42_B9780443158889000029.xhtml#f0025)).

Having seen a concrete example, we can now delve into some of the common sources of bias that impact AI systems.

## Common Sources of Bias

### Data Encodes Societal Prejudices
As we saw in the sentiment analysis example shown earlier, prejudice in our society can be encoded in the data that is used to train a machine learning model. Text data created by users may contain the full spectrum of human views and experiences, including potentially problematic content. Social media data is frequently rife with racism, sexism, and every other kind of hate, whether it is explicitly stated or present as hidden subtext. We must be cautious about letting data from such platforms determine a model’s understanding of reality.

### Data Encodes Societal (Dis)Advantages
Users’ prejudicial views and statements are the most visible way that discriminatory bias appears in data, but this barely scratches the surface of how such bias can impact the real world, thus impacting data and the algorithms trained on data. Real-world patterns of systemic and structural bias systematically advantage individuals from some demographic groups while disadvantaging others. When we look at any metric of “success” or “failure” or “merit” or “risk” based on historical data, systemic bias is frequently “baked in” to the recorded outcomes for the individuals in the dataset. Predictions of whether individuals will be successful are often used as a proxy for a decision on those individuals. For example, in credit scoring a prediction on whether an individual will repay a loan may be used as a proxy for the decision of whether to offer that person a loan. If systemic bias has impacted, for example, the ability of individuals to repay their loans, then this will be reflected in the training data for a classifier that aims to predict whether individuals will repay their loans and is used to make (or inform) a decision on whether to award a loan. Thus the overall system may make biased decisions, systematically discriminating against women in the loan offers made.

In the case of college admissions, studies have shown that individuals belonging to minoritized groups are disproportionately impacted by challenges of poverty and racism that impact academic performance such as chronic stress, access to healthcare, micro-aggressions, stereotype threat, and belongingness uncertainty. Similarly, students who identify as LGBTQ+ (lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and other marginalized gender identities) and especially nonbinary, transgender, and gender-nonconforming disproportionately suffer from discrimination, bullying, self-harm, and the additional burden of having to conceal their identities. A survey of transgender and gender nonconfirming people found that the bullying and discrimination was worst for people of color, and especially Black people.

In her book “Bandwidth Recovery,” author, professor, and faculty administrator and leader Cia Verschelden characterizes these challenges as a tax on the “cognitive bandwidth” of students that makes it difficult for them to perform to their full academic ability and that disproportionally impacts disadvantaged groups. A classifier that is trained to predict academic performance, and is used for college admissions decisions, would “bake in” these disadvantages and hence perpetuate patterns of systemic inequality in those decisions. Similar arguments can be made about machine learning models in other domains where systemic bias impacts historical outcomes that are used as proxies for decisions (e.g., in criminal justice risk assessment models for bail and sentencing decisions where inequities in policing practices impact recorded crime data, such as disparities in arrest rates for drug offenses between Black and White populations in the United States, despite both groups using illicit drugs at similar rates).

### Less Data for Minorities
By definition minority groups occupy a smaller percentage of the population than the majority group. Machine learning algorithms typically have one term in the objective function for each data point, which frequently corresponds to one individual. If a dataset collects data from each group proportionately, there will be fewer terms in the objective function for minority groups. This effectively means that the learning algorithm “cares less” about those groups unless an intervention such as cost-sensitive classification is performed. This could harm the predictive accuracy of a machine learning model for smaller demographics.

### Sampling Bias
Since we usually do not have access to data from every person, the training data for a machine learning model typically corresponds to a subset of the target population rather than to the entire population. If the dataset that is collected does not accurately represent the population of interest, the results of any analysis or machine learning model will be biased, supposing we do not perform an appropriate statistical correction for this issue. If the data sampling procedure does not randomly collect data from each member of a population with equal probability but tends to disproportionately include some individuals or groups, the resulting error is called *sampling bias*. Avoiding this issue is not straightforward.

Consider telephone surveys. In the early years after the first commercial telephone services were created in the late 1800s, only wealthy households had telephones. Even supposing that there was no *response bias*, a telephone survey, a political opinion poll, would have only considered the opinions of the wealthiest segment of society. We can compare this situation to the present day where mobile-only households without landlines are increasingly common. Traditional landline-only phone surveys would miss mobile-only households, and surveys of both landline and mobile phone populations would miss subpopulations that have neither device (e.g., the unhoused population).

The use of smartphones to collect data will always lead to a *coverage bias* in which only data from smartphone users is included. If a dataset were collected based on smartphone data, it would systematically neglect populations that are less likely to own smartphones, such as the unhoused and older adults. Similarly, if a dataset containing location data and movement patterns were collected based on data from automobiles, it would neglect the populations of pedestrians and cyclists, small children, and older adults.
