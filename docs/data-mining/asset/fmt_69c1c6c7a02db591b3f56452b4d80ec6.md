
The workplace is a front line for the impact of AI on our society. We discussed in [Chapter 7](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X), “Ethics: what are the impacts of what’s been learned?” that even after hiring AI can, and frequently does, contribute to exacerbating the power differential between employers and employees. The AI systems used by businesses are almost always designed to prioritize the business owner’s interests, such as profit, rather than employee welfare considerations. For example, shift scheduling systems, most often used by companies with low-wage employees including McDonald’s, Walmart, and Starbucks, produce wildly unpredictable schedules, with relatively short notice given to the employees. Since increasing their availability could increase their hours, it is not easy for employees to carve out time for furthering their education (a key to obtaining a higher-wage job) or spending time with their families. Gig economy workers, including ride-share and food delivery drivers as well as online labor marketplace workers on platforms such as TaskRabbit, are at the mercy of the AI algorithms that match them with jobs, set their wages, and make automated decisions that could boot them from the platforms, in many cases removing their source of income without human intervention or adequate recourse.

The problems of algorithmic management systems extend beyond gig work. In 2019 it was reported by the Verge, a US news firm, that Amazon uses AI to set a rigidly enforced performance and productivity threshold, called “the rate,” on its warehouse employees. Warnings and termination decisions were generated automatically by the system without input from human supervisors (although the supervisors were able to override the decisions). According to the report, Amazon used the system to fire at least 300 employees at a Baltimore facility, more than 10 % of the staff at that location, between August 2017 and September 2018. The practice has led to unlawful discrimination and terminations and an injury rate well above the industry average, according to a 2023 blog post by US law firm JD Supra. In response to these issues, in 2022 the California state senate passed the Warehouse Workers Protection Act, which requires companies to disclose productivity quotas to state regulators and prohibits unsafe and unrealistic quotas. Similar legislation is appearing across the United States. In the AI Now Institute’s 2023 annual report researchers highlighted how such AI-based algorithmic management systems increase the devaluation of work, unequally distribute benefits and risks, hamper collective action efforts, and surveil workers at the cost of their right to privacy. They call for increased legislation to restrict these systems and restore the balance of power between employers and employees,

## The Future of Work

There are also concerns around the impact of AI technologies on the *future of work*. Self-driving cars are poised to replace human drivers in the taxi/rideshare and trucking industries. Ever since the Industrial Revolution, automation has been a big part of manufacturing, and advances in robotics will likely see this increase, possibly at the cost of jobs for human employees. Large language models and closely related chatbot and personal assistant technologies may automate clerical and administrative work, medical decision making, etc. (IBM’s Watson AI was an early, failed attempt at this).

At least initially, low-wage workers are most at risk of being displaced by AI, with the impacts expected to disproportionately impact minoritized populations.

In the short-to-medium term workers in many fields will need to reskill to keep pace with the rapidly changing environment due to AI. In the longer term, as AI will increasingly do many of the jobs that humans currently do, at minimal cost, we as a society may need to rethink our relationship with work and decide what a future without work should look like and how a positive version of this future could be achieved. Similarly, there are concerns that AI and data mining technologies, which are most readily exploited by those who are well-resourced, are already concentrating power in the hands of large technology companies, the wealthy, and governments.

## Surveillance Capitalism

In this chapter we’ve discussed a number of ethical concerns around the use of data mining and machine learning technologies, particularly in their use by Big Tech companies to profit from data about people. We’ve also described techniques that can mitigate some of these issues, such as privacy-preserving machine learning via differentially private algorithms, or fair machine learning methods. A few of the scarier issues aside, our tacit assumption has generally been that if the Big Tech companies could simply be persuaded to take ethical and responsible AI issues seriously and make the appropriate changes everything would be fine.

In her 2018 book *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power* Soshana Zuboff, Emeritus Professor of Harvard Business School and former Faculty Associate at the Berkman Center for Internet and Society, advanced a much more pointed critique of the business models and practices of certain Big Tech companies, which she calls *surveillance capitalism*. From this perspective the economic logic that drives such tech firms is fundamentally problematic and is a threat to human rights, autonomy, and democracy. As such, the problems may run deeper than the band-aid of responsible AI technologies and practices can realistically salve or solve. Like *Big Brother* from George Orwell’s *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, the problems of surveillance capitalism come down to mass surveillance, power, and control. While Big Brother is emblematic of totalitarian control by the state by perpetrating mass surveillance, Zuboff analogously considers mass surveillance and control performed by the Big Tech industry (and startups that follow the same model), which she names “*Big Other*.”

The key points in the definition of surveillance capitalism are:

*   • It is a new kind of capitalism, heralding a new economic order.
*   • It is based on an exploitative process that takes human experience as its raw material, extracts data, makes predictions, and increases sales.
*   • Beyond simply predicting surveillance capitalism aims at control and manipulation via rewards, punishments, digital rules, nudges, and so on.

In short, surveillance capitalism is the economic logic that seeks to profit from extracting data from human experience, predicting human behavior, and ultimately controlling human behavior. According to traditional Marxist theory, power comes from controlling the *means of production*. In contrast, in the theory of surveillance capitalism power arises from controlling the “*means of behavior modification*.” The notion of surveillance capitalism is a critique of the business practices of some tech companies, with Google being the main progenitor, followed by Facebook, Microsoft, and numerous startups. It however is not a critique of the AI technologies themselves, or the use of AI/data mining to improve products and services, or of traditional industrial-era capitalist practices.
