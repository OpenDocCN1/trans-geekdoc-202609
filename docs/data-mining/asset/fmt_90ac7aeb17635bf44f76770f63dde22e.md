## Causal Fairness

In the real world, unfair outcomes happen for a reason. There are processes that systematically advantage some and disadvantage others, such as the impact of sexism on hiring outcomes. In the parlance of intersectionality theory, these processes are called *systems of oppression*. Ideally, to make an AI system fair, we ought to understand and model these systems and account for them when determining and correcting unfairness. This is difficult work, as these processes work in subtle and pernicious ways that are not always recorded digitally, and so it may be difficult to construct a model of the data and its unfairness patterns that is accurate and uncontroversial to stakeholders, who likely all have different value systems and assumptions about the world. This difficulty motivates all the other, typically easier, methodologies that we have considered thus far.

If we do decide to undertake the task of modeling all the reasons for unfairness in data and algorithms, *causal modeling* is a key tool as it allows us to understand and represent the causal relationship between the relevant variables. *Causality* hinges on the concept of an *intervention* to a system—if we were to give a drug to a patient would they be more likely to recover from their disease? In practice, we don’t always have the ability to perform such interventions; rather, the data we receive is *observational*, collected through the natural course of events without intervening. *Causal inference* is the task of trying to figure out the causal phenomena that would have occurred if we could have performed the appropriate interventions, based only on the observational data that we have. An even more difficult task, *causal discovery*, aims to construct a full causal model of the cause-and-effect relationships between all relevant variables in a problem domain.

Supposing that we can successfully model the causal structure of the problem domain, *causal fairness* methodologies intervene on the AI algorithm to achieve causal properties that are deemed fair. One such property, called *counterfactual fairness*, supposes that we could intervene on an individual’s protected attributes (e.g., their gender or race). In doing so we would be in another world where things are different from the actual world; this is called a *counterfactual world*. With a causal model in hand we could estimate how things would be different in the counterfactual world. A decision for an individual is said to be *counterfactually fair* if, in a counterfactual world where we intervened to change the individual’s protected attributes, the probabilities of the outcomes (e.g., class labels) would not be altered. If you were a man instead of a woman (or vice-versa) would the bank have offered you a loan? Or, if you were White instead of Asian (or vice-versa) would the company have offered you a job?

## Practical Guidelines for Fair Machine Learning

We’ve seen quite a few fairness definitions and methodologies but you might still be feeling quite unsure about how to design a fair machine learning system in practice. This is natural, as fairness is a complex, multistakeholder issue. Indeed, feeling uncertain about this is a good thing. As a technologist or engineer, it’s important to practice humility regarding your understanding of the nontechnical aspects of the sociotechnical context that an AI system lives in and the limitations of purely technological solutions to simply “solve” AI fairness. The last thing you should ever do is to show up as some kind of technology wizard and hand down a technological fairness intervention, as if from on-high, to “fix” an AI system without talking to anyone who is impacted by the system or examining the likely (and actual) impacts of your intervention. Well, okay, this is probably slightly better than not considering fairness and bias issues at all, but only marginally so. This is a good time to re-emphasize that it is vital to include stakeholders in the design of a fair and responsible AI/ML (machine learning) system, as we have discussed. In addition, we will go over a few things to consider in the practical design of fair machine learning systems.

### Understanding Fairness and Bias in Your Problem Domain

We have seen that many options for mathematical fairness definitions exist. These alternative fairness definitions:

*   • May encode differing goals about fairness;
*   • May make differing assumptions about the real world and ideal world;
*   • May be desirable to different stakeholders, who may have conflicting goals; and
*   • May or may not correct the underlying fairness problems (or even make them worse).

It’s important to remember that no single fairness definition is applicable in all cases! Before attempting a fairness intervention, we first need to perform a careful reflection on the fairness and bias issues in the problem domain at hand. What are the likely sources of bias in the data, and hence in the algorithm? Is there a history of prejudice or unfair practices that needs to be accounted for? We need to stop and think about:

*   • Who is being harmed: 
    *   • By larger societal processes that impact the data?
    *   • By data quality and selection issues?
