
Although challenging, the Atari games did not require much thinking ahead. Remarkably, the DeepMind researchers were able to use their approach to solve one of the long-standing problems in AI, playing the game of Go at the level of expert human players by combining DQNs with a method for thinking ahead. Go is a board game that has simple rules but is exceedingly difficult to master. Its relatively high branching factor had stymied traditional AI and ML methods. The solution, put forward in DeepMind’s AlphaGo system, was to combine deep reinforcement learning with a search strategy, `Monte-Carlo tree search` (`MCTS`). To estimate which moves are favorable, MCTS selects randomly generated moves that simulate play until the end of the game.

AlphaGo uses deep reinforcement learning to guide the move choices selected by MCTS. It was trained via self-play. AlphaGo has two neural networks: a `policy network`, which provides shortlist of promising moves, and a `value network`, which predicts the winner in a given situation. After AlphaGo defeated European champion Fan Hui in an internal match at DeepMind, in 2016 it defeated all-time great Lee Sedol, 4-1, a milestone moment for AI that was just arguably as important as the success of Deep Blue in defeating Garry Kasparov at chess in 1997. The following year, an improved version of AlphaGo, called AlphaGo Master, defeated Ke Jie, the world’s number-one player at the time, 3-0 in a three-match series.

AlphaGo was initialized using supervised learning based on expert human games and included hand-designed features. DeepMind then developed an improved method, AlphaGo Zero, which avoided both human supervision and hand-designed features and was trained completely from scratch with a blank slate. It was also simplified to use only a single DNN to evaluate the board positions, instead of both policy and value networks. AlphaGo Zero outperformed AlphaGo. It initially learned human-like play including known corner sequences, called `joseki`, but when trained further, it went on to play in a unique style different from humans. In 2017 DeepMind researchers generalized AlphaGo Zero into a system called AlphaZero, which could beat human expert players on other two-player board games like chess and shogi (“Japanese chess”).

AlphaGo and its sibling models have shown us several insights that may extend beyond these systems, in particular:

- Combining deep learning with reinforcement learning and traditional AI techniques like Monte Carlo Tree Search has proved an effective methodology for tackling difficult AI problems.
- Hand-tuned features and supervised training were initially helpful but ultimately proved limiting. There was a trend toward using raw features and training from scratch.
- Simpler models/methods were ultimately better.
- There was a trend toward generalizing the methods to multiple tasks. The best methods could solve multiple Atari games or play multiple board games.

## 11.8 Further Reading and Bibliographic Notes

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are due to [Goodfellow et al. (2014)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur227), although similar ideas were proposed much earlier by [Schmidhuber (1991)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur477). Another generative AI approach, variational autoencoders (VAEs), was proposed by [Kingma and Welling (2013)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur310). More recently, diffusion models, due to [Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur502), have become popular for generating AI art. [Rombach et al. (2022)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur462) proposed to apply diffusion models in the latent space of autoencoders, reducing model complexity while generating high-resolution images and enabling a straightforward approach to guide the generation with a natural language prompt.

Natural language processing via neural networks has a long history. The work of [Collobert et al. (2011)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur108), which argued that deep learning—where features are learned automatically from data—could replace manual feature engineering, was a milestone in showing the promise of deep learning for NLP. The word2vec models, proposed by [Mikolov et al. (2013)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur392), also represented a turning point, both due to their prominent use of vector-space embeddings for words that are ubiquitous even in today’s sophisticated deep neural network models and due to accelerating the push toward large pretrained language models that ingest as much data as possible. [Le and Mikolov (2014)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur356) extended this approach to learning document vectors, which are important for many document-level classification tasks. [Sutskever et al. (2014)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur514)’s Seq2Seq model is emblematic of the pretransformer era of deep learning for NLP, using LSTMs and an encoder-decoder architecture, in this case for neural machine translation. Convolutional networks were also popular for NLP around this time, cf. [Kim (2014)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur307). [Bahdanau (2016)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur22) improved over Seq2Seq using an attention mechanism, which paved the way for transformer models, proposed by [Vaswani et al. (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur543). Transformer-based language models revolutionized the field of NLP, quickly becoming state of the art at most NLP tasks. The GPT series of models from OpenAI, beginning with [Radford et al. (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur447), proved effective generative models of text, while BERT, due to [Devlin et al. (2019)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur129), and its successors, proved to be powerful representation learning tools with excellent performance at downstream NLP tasks.
