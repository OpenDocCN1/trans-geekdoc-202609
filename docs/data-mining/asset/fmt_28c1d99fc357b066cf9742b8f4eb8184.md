The learning rate `η`<mml:math><mml:mi>&#951;</mml:mi></mml:math>![Equation](images/F00011Xsi53.png) is a critical choice when using mini-batch–based stochastic gradient descent. Small values such as 0.001 often work well but it is common to perform a logarithmically spaced search, say in the interval \[10^(−8), 1\], followed by a finer grid or binary search.

There are, however, many heuristics for adapting learning rates by hand during training. For example, the `AlexNet` model that won the `ImageNet` 2012 challenge divides the rate by 10 when the validation error rate ceases to improve. The intuition is that a model may make good progress with a given learning rate but eventually become stuck, jumping around a local minimum in the loss function because the parameter steps are too large. Monitoring performance on the validation set is a good guide as to when the learning rate should be changed.

Second-order analysis based on a Taylor expansion of the loss to higher-order terms can also help explain why smaller rates may be desirable in the final stages of learning. If stochastic gradient descent is used it can be shown that the learning rate must be reduced for the approach to yield results consistent with batch gradient descent.

### Advanced Learning Algorithms

`SGD` is the workhorse of deep learning. Performing updates after every mini-batch, instead of after processing an entire dataset, is what makes it possible to scale deep learning to the massive datasets that it needs to achieve excellent performance. However, “scalable” (i.e., able to process a large number of data points) is not the same thing as “fast” (rapid in running time, both per-iteration and overall time to convergence). There is therefore a strong motivation to improve over it. Several variations on `SGD` have been proposed, and these have made a big impact on the application of deep learning methods.

There are two main limitations of `SGD` that these sophisticated learning methods aim to address. First, `SGD` is myopic, in the sense that it does not look ahead in any way. It just takes a step downhill in the direction of the stochastic estimate of the gradient. Each step can be noisy (potentially taking the model’s parameters in a bad direction). It could get stuck in a small local optimum or plateau. And the now steepest downhill direction may not actually always be the best direction to go in. Second, the learning rate (a.k.a. the step size, how far to “step” in the chosen direction) is the same for all dimensions, and this may not be ideal. Some dimensions may not need to be changed much at all, while others may need to change a lot, and this could vary during the learning process.

#### SGD With Momentum

As a metaphor for the dynamics of `SGD`, imagine a ball rolling down a hill. If the hill is bumpy the ball might get stuck in a hole (i.e., a local minimum), or it could stop partway down on a plateau. If the ball is not too heavy it could also go off in many arbitrary, inefficient directions, since it would tend to follow the downhill directions, which are determined by the little bumps and not by the overall shape of the hill. Now suppose that the ball is much heavier. It will build momentum as it falls, and the momentum would carry it through the holes and keep it going more directly downhill rather than sliding side to side on the bumps. As we discussed in [Section 9.2](#Ch010_347-389_B9780443158889000108.xhtml#s0050), there is a version of regular (i.e., batch) gradient descent that aims to model this process, called `gradient descent with momentum`. This is sometimes referred to as the `heavy ball method`.
