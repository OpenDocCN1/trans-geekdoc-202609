
The potential tendency of many different AI systems to seek power is an example of *instrumental convergence*. An *instrumental* goal is one that is a means to an end. Instrumental convergence occurs when multiple intelligent agents (AI or human) seek the same useful subgoal (e.g., obtaining power) to achieve each of their varied goals. This could potentially lead to harmful results. In the famous *paperclip maximizer* thought experiment by philosopher Nick Bostrom, for example, we suppose that an AI is tasked with producing as many paperclips as possible. The AI quickly determines that humans might switch it off, thus preventing it from creating paperclips, and the humans also contain atoms that are useful for creating paperclips. Its goal of maximizing the number of paperclips is in direct conflict with a world in which there are many living humans so it achieves its intermediate goal with catastrophic results.

## Artificial Superintelligence and the Threat of Rogue AI

Could an AI really exterminate all human life or enslave us all? An AI like the paperclip maximizer might not need to be more intelligent than humans, and certainly wouldn’t need to be considered “conscious,” to accomplish this if it could obtain sufficient resources. However, if humans could outsmart it, it might not succeed. An *artificial superintelligence* (`ASI`) is a hypothetical AI that is more intelligent than humans. This should not be confused with a related concept—*artificial general intelligence* (`AGI`), a hypothetical AI that is general enough to perform well over many tasks and problem domains, while not necessarily being more “intelligent” than humans. Some authors have argued that *large language models* (`LLMs`), which can solve tasks ranging from mathematics, to medicine, to law, and beyond, are already at the beginning stages of `AGI`.

If we subscribe to the *functionalist* theory of mind, which states that minds and mental states depend on the functional roles of the system that they are a part of, then the human brain is just one possible piece of hardware that could produce a mind, and we could presumably implement a mind using different hardware such as a digital computer. This would imply that, in principle, `ASI` is possible and therefore might one day be a threat to human existence and/or autonomy. The view that AI is a near-term threat to human existence is a controversial position but it has its proponents, including prominent technologists such as entrepreneur Elon Musk and the late theoretical physicist Stephen Hawking. One of the most credible voices to take this position is deep learning pioneer Geoff Hinton, who resigned (some might say retired) at age 75 from Google in 2023 to have the freedom to warn about these kinds of risks. Hinton took this view after being alarmed by the recent rapid progress of `LLMs` in their ability to acquire general knowledge and perform reasoning. A persistent theory, notably advocated by professor and science fiction author Vernor Vinge and futurist Ray Kurzweil, is that a phenomenon known as the *singularity* will occur, referring to a hypothetical scenario in which technological progress will enable its own runaway acceleration, leading to consequences that are so far beyond our current understanding that they are difficult to comprehend or foresee.

On the other hand, all technological trends to date have reached a point of diminishing returns, and `LLMs` are unlikely to be an exception to this rule. If, as most computer scientists believe, `P` ≠ `NP`<mml:math><mml:mi>P</mml:mi><mml:mo>&#8800;</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">NP</mml:mi></mml:math>![Equation](images/F00008Xsi186.png), then many AI problems are fundamentally difficult to solve and thus there are limits to what an AI might accomplish, no matter how well designed. Many AI researchers have learned from bitter experience—in struggling to achieve a few percentage points of improvement on benchmark tasks to get our work published—that progress in the field is really difficult and comes with much human blood, sweat, and tears. While the rapid advances in `LLM` technology have been unexpected and astounding, this situation has not fundamentally changed.

Moreover, even if `ASI` could be developed, an overemphasis on the importance of intelligence may be naïve. *Wired Magazine* founding editor Kevin Kelly calls this the “*thinkism fallacy*.” No matter how smart an AI is, for it to find a cure for cancer it would not only need to come up with a good idea for a cure but also perform medical trials to test its hypothesis and likely adjust its approach many times based on the data from the trials before succeeding. In 2015, renowned AI expert Andrew Ng said, “The reason I say that I don’t worry about AI turning evil is the same reason I don’t worry about overpopulation on Mars.” While much has changed since then, it is still true that there are many immediate and urgent concerns around AI that need to be addressed right now. While some concern for the more distant future may be warranted, we should not let that lead us to neglect the present. Prominent AI researcher Meredith Whittaker, who was forced out of Google for organizing against the company’s drone computer vision contracts with the US military (although Google disputes this account of events), has argued that “alarmist” views such as those expressed by Hinton have a very real cost in terms of deprioritizing AI fairness and equity issues. She expressed dismay that Hinton and others at Google warned against hypothetical future risks from AI but did not support efforts to curtain the harms due to AI that are already happening, which fall disproportionately on women, people of color, and especially women of color. We discussed AI bias and fairness issues earlier in this chapter, and in [Sections 15.10](#Ch016_571-615_B9780443158889000169.xhtml#s0085) and [15.11](#Ch016_571-615_B9780443158889000169.xhtml#s0115) we’ll expand our discussion to other such concerns at the intersection of AI and our society.
