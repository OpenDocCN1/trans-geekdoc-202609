
## Attacks on `k`-Anonymity

Even more seriously, `k`-anonymity is vulnerable to several attacks. Suppose `S` is a sensitive attribute that an adversary is trying to learn via a linkage attack. The homogeneity attack exploits a lack of diversity in the sensitive attribute. Consider a case where all `k` values of `S` for the data instances with a particular quasiidentifier `Q` have the same value `v`. Then if an individual, Alice (who is in the dataset), has quasiidentifier `Q`, the adversary can infer that `S = v` for Alice, even though they cannot determine which of the `k` instances matching `Q` corresponds to Alice. An example is given in [Table 7.2](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#t0015), where an adversary can determine that Alice has `Medical Condition` = `Eye Disease` based on her quasiidentifiers, even though they cannot determine which row her record belongs to.

Table 7.2

Example of the Homogeneity Attack. Here, although an adversary cannot determine which instance corresponds to Alice, they can infer that Alice has `Medical Condition = Eye Disease`, since every instance with the same quasiidentifiers as Alice has this medical condition.

| Explicit Identifier  || Quasiidentifiers || Sensitive Attribute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name || Spectacle Prescription || Astigmatism || Tear Production Rate || Medical Condition |
| Alice || Myope || No || Reduced || Eye Disease |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bob || Myope || No || Reduced || Eye Disease |
| Claire || Myope || No || Reduced || Eye Disease |

Credit: James Foulds

Another related attack, the background knowledge attack, exploits background knowledge on the sensitive attributes to narrow down the possibilities. For instance, suppose that Bob (who is in the dataset) matches quasiidentifiers with `k` individuals whose sensitive attribute, a medical condition, is either chest pain or obesity. Let’s say that the adversary happens to know that Bob is a personal fitness trainer, so it is extremely unlikely that Bob is obese. Then the adversary could infer that Bob has chest pain. We illustrate this scenario in [Table 7.3](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#t0020).

Table 7.3

Example of the Background Knowledge Attack.

| Explicit Identifier  || Quasiidentifiers || Sensitive Attribute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name || Spectacle Prescription || Astigmatism || Tear Production Rate || Medical Condition |
| Alice || Myope || No || Reduced || Obesity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bob || Myope || No || Reduced || Chest pain |
| Claire || Myope || No || Reduced || Obesity |
| Background knowledge: Bob is a fitness trainer, hence unlikely to be obese. |

### Differential Privacy

We have seen that `k`-anonymity has serious flaws, and it cannot ensure protection against linkage attacks, as was its goal. We’d like to instead have a methodology that has more rigorous guarantees of protection against such attacks. To do so, ideally we would like to set up our database’s security system so that if someone obtains access to it, they cannot learn anything about an individual that they could not otherwise know, even in the presence of auxiliary information. For example, in this ideal setting one could not learn which movies a person has rated unless one could already learn those ratings without having access to the movie rating database. Thus the database would not reveal any unknown private data. This ideal “nothing learned” notion of privacy has been compared to the property of public key encryption schemes such as Rivest-Shamir-Adleman (`RSA`), called semantic security, in which access to the encrypted data (“`cyphertext`”) reveals no information about the decrypted data (“`cleartext`”).

However, it turns out that this idealized notion of privacy is impossible to achieve in general. This is because auxiliary information is so pernicious that it can even expose the data of someone who is not in the dataset! Suppose, for example, we know that user Alice rates the movie “Titanic” one star lower than the average US user does. To determine Alice’s rating, we could simply compute the average star rating that US users give to “Titanic” and subtract one star from this figure. As we can see from this example, if an adversary has access to arbitrary side information it may not be possible to prevent them from making new inferences about a person based on information gleaned from our database. In this case, the inference could be made even if Alice’s data is not in our database. Our goal for privacy must therefore retreat to something more attainable.

In this example, it did not matter whether Alice’s data is in the database or not—it was exposed either way. While we could not control the risk of a privacy-breaching disclosure in an absolute sense, as it turns out, the way forward is to instead control the relative risk of a privacy-breaching disclosure for an individual who contributes their data to the database compared to if they were to leave their data out of the database. Differential privacy is a mathematical definition of privacy that takes this approach (hence the name, which refers to this difference between these two scenarios).

Differential privacy is a promise—“If you add your data to the database you will not be affected too much.” This promise holds even in the presence of auxiliary data, such as the linkage attack used against the Netflix Prize dataset. Suppose that the database is stored on a secure server, owned by someone trustworthy, called the data curator. The data curator’s goal is to make available information about the overall population, without revealing too much about any one person’s data. The data curator entertains queries from untrusted users, and they only return answers that protect the individuals’ private information.
