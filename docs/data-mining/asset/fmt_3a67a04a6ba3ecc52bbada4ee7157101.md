## Fairness Metrics

Let’s look at some of the options for fairness metrics.

### Parity-based metrics

*Parity-based metrics* aim to ensure that different demographic groups are treated similarly by the algorithm. The term usually refers to ensuring parity in the assignment of class labels to each demographic, for example, women being assigned the “credit-worthy” class label at the same proportion as men are, thus being equally likely to be recommended by the algorithm to receive an offer of a loan as men. If we assume that the different demographics are equally capable, this is an intuitive notion of fairness. It is hence widely used. Nevertheless, parity-based metrics have received a lot of criticism in the academic literature, including concerns that they might be harmful if the demographics are not actually equally capable, and that they do not prevent certain harms and abuses.

There are several ways that parity-based metrics have been implemented mathematically. Alternatively, instead of comparing the class probability distributions by computing their differences (i.e., subtraction), we can compare them by taking ratios (i.e., division). This approach is motivated by a US legal guideline for identifying discrimination. The guideline, called the `80% rule` or the `four-fifths rule`, was originally created for determining whether discriminatory practices meet a legal threshold for adverse or disparate impact in employment contexts. Disparate impact occurs when the same policies apply to everyone (e.g., the same set of hiring criteria are used, or the same classifier is applied), but the policies impact different groups substantially differently overall. This can occur even if the policies do not explicitly use the demographic membership in the decision-making process.
