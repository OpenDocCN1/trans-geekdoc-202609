A method called “collapsed Gibbs sampling” turns out to be a particularly effective alternative to variational methods for performing `LDA^b`. Consider first that the model in [Fig. 14.4B](#Ch015_516-570_B9780443158889000157.xhtml#f0025) can be expanded to that shown in [Fig. 14.4C](#Ch015_516-570_B9780443158889000157.xhtml#f0025), which was originally cast as the smoothed version of `LDA^b`. Then add another Dirichlet prior with parameters given by `Γ`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">&#915;</mml:mi></mml:math> on the topic parameters of `B`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">B</mml:mi></mml:math>, a formulation that further reduces the effects of overfitting. Standard Gibbs sampling involves iteratively sampling the hidden random variables `z[ij]`, the `θ[i]`’s and the elements of matrix `B`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">B</mml:mi></mml:math>. Collapsed Gibbs sampling is obtained by integrating out the `θ[i]`’s and `B`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">B</mml:mi></mml:math> analytically, which deals with these distributions exactly. Consequently, conditioned by the current estimates of `Γ`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">&#915;</mml:mi></mml:math>, `α`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">&#945;</mml:mi></mml:math>, and the observed words of a document corpus, the Gibbs sampler proceeds by simply iteratively updating each `z[ij]` to compute the required approximate posterior. Using either samples or variational approximations it is then relatively straightforward to obtain estimates for the `θ[i]`’s and `B`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">B</mml:mi></mml:math>.

The overall approach of using a smoothed, collapsed `LDA^b` model to extract topics from a document collection can be summarized as follows. First define a hierarchical Bayesian model for the joint distribution of documents and words following the structure of [Fig. 14.4C](#Ch015_516-570_B9780443158889000157.xhtml#f0025). We could think of there being a Bayesian `E-step` that performs approximate inference using Gibbs’ sampling to sample from the `joint posterior` over `all` topics for all documents in the model, or `P(z[ij] | w[ij], Γ, α)`<mml:math><mml:mi>P</mml:mi><mml:mo stretchy="false">(</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>z</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">ij</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub><mml:mo stretchy="false" mathvariant="normal">&#8739;</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>w</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">ij</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">&#915;</mml:mi><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">&#945;</mml:mi><mml:mo stretchy="false">)</mml:mo></mml:math>![Equation](images/F000157si130.png), where the `θ[i]`’s and `B`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">B</mml:mi></mml:math> have been integrated out. This is followed by an `M-step` that uses these samples to update the estimates of the `θ[i]`’s and `B`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">B</mml:mi></mml:math>, using update equations that are functions of `Γ`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">&#915;</mml:mi></mml:math>, `α`<mml:math><mml:mi mathvariant="bold">&#945;</mml:mi></mml:math>, and the samples. This procedure is performed within a hierarchical Bayesian model, so the updated parameters can be used to create a Bayesian predictive distribution over new words and new topics given the observed words.
