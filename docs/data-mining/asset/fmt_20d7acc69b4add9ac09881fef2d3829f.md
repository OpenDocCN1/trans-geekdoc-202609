
It is known that most complex Boolean functions require an exponential number of two-step logic gates for their representation ([Wegener, 1987](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur557)). The solution appears to be greater depth. According to [Bengio (2009)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur30), the evidence strongly suggests that “functions that can be compactly represented with a depth-`k` architecture could require a very large number of elements to be represented by a shallower architecture.”

Stochastic gradient descent methods go back at least as far as [Robbins and Monro (1951)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur459). [Bottou (2012)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur54) is an excellent source of tips and tricks for learning with stochastic gradient descent, while [Bengio (2012)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur31) gives further practical recommendations for training deep networks. Adaptive gradient methods, which adapt the learning rates for each parameter separately, have improved and accelerated DNN training for many models. These include AdaGrad ([Duchi et al., 2011](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur147)), AdaDelta ([Zeiler, 2012](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur589)), and RMSProp ([Tieleman and Hinton, 2012](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur526)), culminating in Adam ([Kingma and Ba, 2012](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur309)). On the other hand, [Zhou et al. (2020)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur597) provided a theoretical explanation of why SGD frequently generalizes better than Adam.

Bergstra and [Bengio (2012)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur31) give empirical and theoretical justification for the use of random search for hyperparameter settings. [Snoek, Larochelle, and Adams (2012)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur500) propose the use of Bayesian learning methods to infer the next hyperparameter setting to explore, and their Spearmint software package performs Bayesian optimizations of both deep network hyperparameters and general ML algorithm hyperparameters.

Good parameter initialization can be critical for the success of neural networks, as discussed in LeCun et al.’s (1998) classic work and the more recent work of [Glorot and Bengio (2010)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur220). Krizhevsky et al.’s (2012) convolutional network of ReLUs initialized weights using 0-mean isotropic Gaussian distributions with a standard deviation of 0.01, and initialized the biases to 1 for most hidden convolutional layers as well as the model’s hidden fully connected layers. They observed that this initialization accelerated the early phase of learning by providing ReLUs with positive inputs.

The origins of dropout and more details about it can be found in [Srivastava, Hinton, Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Salakhutdinov (2014)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur507). [Ioffe and Szegedy (2015)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur285) proposed batch normalization and give more details on its implementation. [Glorot and Bengio (2010)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur220) cover various weight matrix initialization heuristics and how the concepts of fan-in and fan-out can be used to justify them for networks with different kinds of activation functions.

However, it was the ImageNet challenge, with a variety of much higher resolutions, that catapulted deep learning into the spotlight in 2012 ([Russakovsky et al., 2015](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur466)). The winning entry from the University of Toronto processed the images at a resolution of 256 × 256<mml:math><mml:mn>256</mml:mn><mml:mo>&#215;</mml:mo><mml:mn>256</mml:mn></mml:math>![Equation](images/F00011Xsi152.png) pixels. Up till then, CNNs were simply incapable of processing such large volumes of imagery at such high resolutions in a reasonable amount of time. Krizhevsky et al.’s (2012) dramatic win used GPU-accelerated CNNs. This spurred a great deal of development, reflected in rapid subsequent advances in visual recognition performance on the ImageNet benchmark.

[Hinton and Salakhutdinov (2006)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur264) noted that it has been known since the 1980s that deep autoencoders, optimized through backpropagation, could be effective for nonlinear dimensionality reduction. The key limiting factors were the small size of the data sets used to train them, coupled with low computation speeds, plus the old problem of local minima. By 2006 data sets such as the MNIST digits and the 20 Newsgroups collection were large enough, and computers were fast enough, for Hinton and Salakhutdinov to present compelling results illustrating the advantages of deep autoencoders over principal component analysis. Their experimental work used generative pretraining to initialize weights to avoid problems with local minima.

[Graves et al. (2009)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur231) demonstrate how recurrent neural networks are particularly effective at handwriting recognition, while [Graves, Mohamed, and Hinton (2013)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur232) apply recurrent neural networks to speech. The form of gradient clipping presented in [Section 10.5](#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X.xhtml#s0150) was proposed by [Pascanu, Mikolov, and Bengio (2013)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur428).

The vanishing gradient problem was formally identified as a key issue for learning in deep networks by Sepp Hochreiter in his diploma thesis ([Hochreiter, 1991](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur269)). The impact in terms of the difficulty of learning long-term dependencies is discussed by [Bengio, Simard, and Frasconi (1994)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur33). Further analysis of the issue is given by [Hochreiter, Bengio, Frasconi, and Schmidhuber (2001)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur270).
