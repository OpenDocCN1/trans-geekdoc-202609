
In the media and in the public consciousness artificial intelligence is often seen as neutral and objective due to its automated nature. This is not the case. Machine learning systems are trained automatically to fit a dataset, but as we have seen in this book, much of the design process is not automated. There are many decisions that a human must make from collecting and preprocessing the data, determining the class labels, selecting algorithms, hyperparameters, and objective functions, writing code, evaluating and improving the system, and making fairness interventions. These decisions implement the values and goals of the developers. Typically, all the decisions that go into designing a machine learning system are made by data scientists, engineers, and their managers. It is unlikely that the members of such a team adequately represent the interests of the populations that the system will impact. In this scenario, the team may not be capable of ensuring that risks including bias and unfair discrimination would be adequately addressed, no matter how well intentioned they may be.

As a solution to this type of issue, the field of human-centered computing has advanced the notion of *participatory design*, a.k.a. *codesign*, in which stakeholders are engaged in design processes to ensure that their needs are met by the system. In a machine learning context, it would be wise to invite stakeholders back to monitor the behavior of the system periodically after deployment as the model and/or its data and sociotechnical context may change over time.

## Diversity and Representation in Technology

If machine learning development teams were diverse and represented a variety of backgrounds, they would be more likely to notice issues such as algorithmic bias. Unfortunately, as is well known, there is a lack of women and minoritized groups in the technology field, including AI. This is far from inevitable, as it was not always the case. Ada Lovelace wrote the first published computer program, an algorithm for Charles Babbage’s hypothetical computer, the Analytical Engine, that computes Bernoulli numbers. In the early days of computing, programming, data entry, and calculation were considered “secretarial” tasks done primarily by women. It was mainly women, who were themselves called “computers,” who maintained systems for targeting bombs during World War II. In the 1940s, a team of human computers who were Black women, led by Dorothy Vaughn, performed vital calculations for the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA), the predecessor of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), as famously depicted in the 2016 film Hidden Figures. As computers became electronic, human computers were hired as programmers, and so the six first programmers of the first general-purpose programmable electronic computer, ENIAC, completed in 1945, were all women. The important work done by human computers was generally overlooked and undercompensated and only became valued once computer programming turned into a male-dominated field. The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence in 1956, which launched AI as an academic field, was attended only by men.

The demographic disparities in AI, the technology industry, and science, technology, engineering, and math (STEM) in general arise at multiple stages of the pipeline. For example, relatively few US women receive STEM degrees, and these are primarily in the physical and life sciences while men’s are primarily in engineering. Women who receive STEM degrees are less likely to work in STEM jobs than men who receive those degrees. Possible factors that experts have cited include a lack of role models, stereotyping, a lack of family-oriented flexibility, intentional exclusion, and unintentional “like-me bias.” A report by the US Department of Commerce pointed to the gender disparity in technology as an untapped opportunity to strengthen the nation’s economic competitiveness globally. While solutions are not straightforward, in your future career when you are involved in hiring decisions *you* will have an opportunity to make a difference.

## Transparency in AI-Based Decision Making

When an AI system makes decisions that severely affect people’s lives, it’s crucial that the decision-making process is transparent so the impacted individuals can learn why the decisions were made, how they were made, and with what data. Without transparency, when the system gets it wrong the harmed parties can have no recourse to appeal against the decision and set things right. Unfortunately, statistical and machine learning systems are all too frequently considered inscrutable black boxes that are too complicated to explain yet are somehow to be trusted completely as “objective,” “neutral,” and “fair,” thereby dismissing all appeals. The humans who would appeal their decisions are frequently held to a higher standard of evidentiary proof than the algorithms are.

In one terrifying example, a Middle School teacher in Washington, D.C., Sarah Wysocki, was unjustly fired by an algorithm. Under pressure to turn around underperforming schools, the city had implemented a teacher assessment tool called IMPACT. The tool included a sophisticated metric called “value-added,” which aimed to measure the extent to which a teacher contributed “value” to students’ learning based on standardized test scores. Teachers with the lowest “value-added” scores were fired, regardless of how well they were performing on other metrics or in student evaluations, and this is what happened to Wysocki. Conversely, high-scoring teachers received bonuses.

However, as revealed in investigations by *USA Today* and the *Washington Post*, this situation led many schools in the district to cheat on behalf of their students, changing incorrect answers in standardized tests to correct ones to artificially inflate the scores. Wysocki alleges that the elementary school that many of her students had attended the previous year had engaged in this practice. Those students had previously been scored as “advanced reading level,” yet they came into her classroom struggling to read at all. Wysocki had stellar student evaluations, high scores on all other metrics, and the backing of her principal, who described her as “creative,” “visionary,” and “motivating.” The progress in reading ability her students were making had been celebrated within the school. But when she appealed her firing, her claims were dismissed, with the board determining that investigating claims of cheating was beyond the scope of the panel. Within days of her firing, Wysocki was hired by a school in nearby Virginia, one that was well resourced and did not fire teachers based on test scores. A poor school’s loss of a good teacher was a wealthy school’s gain.

In response to transparency issues the field of *explainable AI* has arisen, which aims to develop techniques for looking inside machine learning “black boxes” to explain the models and their decisions to users in an understandable way. One popular strategy, called *knowledge distillation*, is to fit a simple and understandable model (e.g., a linear model or a decision tree) to the predictions of the complicated model of interest (e.g., a deep neural network), thereby capturing the essence of its behavior in a simpler form. Research on explainable AI has been spurred in part by the EU’s data privacy law, the GDPR. Among other provisions, the GDPR requires businesses that use personal user data for decision making to explain those decisions at the users’ request, although scholars have debated whether this amounts to a “right to explanation” in the sense of explainable AI. In the United States, some states have adopted similar laws, such as the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA).
