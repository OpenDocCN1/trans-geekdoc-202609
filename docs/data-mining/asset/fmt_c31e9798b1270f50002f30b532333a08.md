
While Waymo and Cruise robotaxis had not caused any (human) deaths as of the time of the hearing, they had malfunctioned and stopped, causing traffic flow issues. A Waymo self-driving car killed a dog in San Francisco early in 2023. Shortly after the hearing a Cruise vehicle was involved in a crash with the fire truck in which a passenger sustained minor injuries. Later in 2023 a Cruise AV ran over a pedestrian, dragged her 20 feet while attempting to pull over, and stopped with its rear tire on top of her leg until first responders could extricate her with the jaws of life. The incident occurred after a human-driven vehicle hit the pedestrian and knocked her into the AV’s path. The CPUC alleged that Cruise misled them and the public by withholding the fact that their AV had dragged the passenger. In the wake of this incident and others the California Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) suspended Cruise’s AV permits, Cruise voluntarily suspended its robotaxi operations across the United States, and the company’s CEO, Kyle Vogt, resigned. At the time of writing the situation is in flux and the long-term implications of these events are unclear.

The safety record of self-driving cars has been mixed. According to watchdog website tesladeaths.com, as of August 2023 there have been 41 deaths from crashes involving a vehicle where Tesla Autopilot was engaged at least 30 seconds earlier, across 36 separate incidents. As is standard for recording automobile death statistics, this figure includes cases where Tesla was deemed not at fault. On the other hand, a 2017 report from the US National Transportation Safety Board, which was investigating an earlier Tesla Autopilot-related death, concluded that for Tesla cars the crash rate dropped by 40% after Autopilot was installed.

Since self-driving cars travel at high speeds among other vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists, it may not ever be possible to make them perfectly safe. In cases where an accident is unavoidable the AI system may need to make decisions that determine who is likely to be harmed or killed between its passengers and other road users. In such a case should the AI prioritize the safety of its own passengers or aim to minimize the number of deaths overall? This situation is sometimes likened to the *trolley problem* from moral philosophy and psychology. The trolley problem is a thought experiment in which a runaway tram will hit and kill one or more people if left to continue on its railway track but an operator could decide to switch it to a different track, where it would hit and kill a different set of people. While some philosophers have emphasized the similarity between accident avoidance in self-driving cars and the trolley problem, others have pointed out substantial differences including:
*   • The role of moral and legal responsibility. With real-world self-driving cars one needs to consider *who* is responsible for making these life or death decisions and what their legal and moral responsibilities are, while this is abstracted out of the trolley problem.
*   • There may be potentially unlimited set of considerations factoring into the decision, instead of a small, predefined list of considerations in the trolley problem (e.g., age, whether the person is a war criminal).
*   • Temporal aspects differ. The accident management algorithm is designed in advance then later executed in real time.
*   • With self-driving cars there is the necessity of risk estimation and reasoning under uncertainty that is not present in the traditional trolley problem.

Whether or not we view such decisions as instances of the trolley problem, solutions are far from easy. Self-interest plays a complicating role. A 2016 study performed on the Mechanical Turk crowdsourcing platform by economist Jean-François Bonnefon and colleagues found that although participants in principle preferred AVs to implement *utilitarian ethics* that prioritized minimizing deaths, even at the expense of their passengers, and would like others to buy such systems, they themselves preferred to ride in AVs that prioritize protecting their passengers at all costs. They were overall against regulation enforcing the utilitarian approach and would be less likely to buy such a vehicle. The study’s authors concluded that enforcing the utilitarian approach could slow the adoption of self-driving cars, which might ironically increase the overall number of vehicular deaths, supposing that the self-driving vehicles are safer than human-driven ones.

Other ethical concerns around self-driving cars include:
*   • *Cybersecurity*. A hacker could take control of a self-driving car and use it as a weapon.
*   • *Privacy*. The destinations visited and in-vehicle video or audio data would likely need to be recorded. Since AVs are internet connected, this information could be hacked.
*   • *Liability*. Who is at fault when a self-driving car crashes?
*   • *Loss of jobs for professional drivers*. This would impact professions such as taxi drivers, truck drivers, chauffeurs, and postal workers.

## Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems
*Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems* (*LAWS*) are weapons that can locate, select, and kill human targets without human supervision. These systems already exist. Israel’s Harop missile is a “loitering munition” with a 10-foot wingspan and a 50-pound warhead. It searches for up to 6 hours for a target that meets a particular set of criteria (e.g., “looks like a tank”). The Kargu quadcopter, made by Turkish manufacturer STM, can carry up to 1.5 kg of explosives and is capable of selecting and tracking targets based on images using facial recognition. Not all *automated* systems are *autonomous*. Drones that are remotely piloted by human operators, such as General Atomics’ Reaper and Predator drones used by the US military, are not autonomous weapons systems although they could potentially be made autonomous in the near future.

Killer robots that roam on two or four legs, akin to those seen in the Terminator movies, can be built using existing technology. US company Boston Dynamics builds bipedal and quadrupedal robots and has received US military funding, although it states in its ethical principles documents that it will not authorize or partner with those who wish to weaponize its robots. Turning its robots into weapons, however, is as simple as taping a gun to one of them. In 2021 a US art installation by art collective MSCHF, titled “Spot’s Rampage,” attached a paintball gun to Boston Dynamics’ four-legged “Spot” robot and allowed exhibit visitors to remotely control the robot. Boston Dynamics was naturally unimpressed with the project, calling it “provocative” in a statement released on Twitter and threatening to void the robot’s warranty if the exhibition went ahead.

There is a growing movement to ban LAWS. The International Committee for Robot Arms Control (ICRAC), a Non-Government Organization (NGO), called for a discussion on the prohibition of LAWS as early as 2009. In a 2023 United Nations policy brief titled “A New Agenda for Peace” UN Secretary-General António Guterres called for a legally binding prohibition on LAWS that function without human control or oversight, with regulation on all other autonomous weapons system, to be instituted by 2026.
