This method goes by the name of Naïve Bayes, because it’s based on Bayes’ rule and “naïvely” assumes independence—it is only valid to multiply probabilities when the events are independent. The assumption that attributes are independent (given the class) in real life certainly is a simplistic one. But despite the disparaging name, Naïve Bayes works very well when tested on actual datasets, particularly when combined with some of the attribute selection procedures introduced in [Chapter 6](#Ch006_209-259_B9780443158889000078.xhtml#Ch006_209-259_B9780443158889000078), “Preparation: data preprocessing and exploratory data analysis,” that eliminate redundant, and hence non-independent, attributes.

Things go badly awry in Naïve Bayes if a particular attribute value does not occur in the training set in conjunction with every class value. Suppose that in the training data the attribute value `outlook` = `sunny` was always associated with the outcome `no`. Then the probability of `outlook` = `sunny` given a `yes`, `P(outlook = sunny|yes)`<mml:math><mml:mi>P</mml:mi><mml:mo stretchy="false">(</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">outlook</mml:mi><mml:mo>=</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="italic">sunny|yes</mml:mi><mml:mo stretchy="false">)</mml:mo><mml:mo>,</mml:mo></mml:math>![Equation](images/F000054si13.png) would be zero, and because the other probabilities are multiplied by this the final probability of `yes` in the earlier example would be zero no matter how large they were. Probabilities that are zero hold a veto over the other ones. This is not a good idea. But the bug is easily fixed by minor adjustments to the method of calculating probabilities from frequencies.

For example, the upper part of [Table 4.2](#Ch004_91-164_B9780443158889000054.xhtml#t0015) shows that for `play = yes`, `outlook` is `sunny` for two examples, `overcast` for four, and `rainy` for three, and the lower part gives these events probabilities of 2/9, 4/9, and 3/9, respectively. Instead, we could add 1 to each numerator, and compensate by adding 3 to the denominator, giving probabilities of 3/12, 5/12, and 4/12, respectively. This will ensure that an attribute value that occurs zero times receives a probability which is nonzero, albeit small. The strategy of adding 1 to each count is a standard technique called the `Laplace estimator` after the great 18th-century French mathematician Pierre Laplace.
