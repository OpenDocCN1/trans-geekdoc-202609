
Randomized response is applicable to discrete data. In practice, we frequently want to privatize numeric data. For example, a common use-case of differential privacy is to privatize a machine learning model’s parameters so the model can be disclosed without risk of it being reverse-engineered to expose any information about the individuals who contributed their data for training the system. The trick to doing this is to add just enough noise to the data to obscure any one person’s data point.

where each `Y[i]` is independently drawn from a Laplace distribution `Lap(Δf/ϵ)`.

The `Laplace mechanism` is `ϵ`-differentially private. We can choose a target value of `ϵ`, and the previous equation will tell us how much noise to add to achieve that level of privacy. Of course, if the sensitivity is too high, we must add too much noise to achieve a reasonable degree of differential privacy, ruining any information we might glean from the output of our privatized query.

Another option is to add noise from a normal distribution, in a method called the `Gaussian mechanism`. The amount of noise to add depends on the `l2`-sensitivity, which is defined similarly to the `l1`-sensitivity but is computed based on the `L2`-norm (Euclidean distance). Compared to the `Laplace mechanism`, the `Gaussian mechanism` allows less noise to be added, and the noise requirements scale more gracefully in the number of queries performed. However, it comes at a cost. The definition of differential privacy must be relaxed to allow a “fudge factor” `δ` that represents a small probability that the privacy guarantee can be violated and information be released, potentially “in the clear.” This relaxed privacy definition is sometimes called (![ent](images/epsi_1013.png),δ)-differential privacy, which is pronounced as “`epsilon-delta differential privacy`,” and it is also sometimes referred to as `approximate differential privacy`, or simply “`differential privacy`,” thus expanding the definition to include this fudge factor.
