
Much of what we know about AI fairness and bias issues from real systems was revealed by journalists. Fairness issues in criminal justice risk assessments were exposed by investigative journalists Angwin et al. (2016), who used a freedom of information request to obtain data on the COMPAS system. [Dastin (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur121) described the case of Amazon’s internal recruiting tool that was biased against women. Researchers have also weighed in and [Bulamwini and Gebru (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur75) analyzed bias at the intersection of gender and skin tone in facial recognition systems, further validated in follow-up work from NIST by [Grother et al. (2019)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur238). Many other detailed examples of problems with AI-based decisions were given by [O’Neill (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur426). The ACM’s statement on principles for responsible algorithmic systems, including AI-based systems, was published by [ACM Technology Policy Office (2022)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur5). In a blog post, [Speer (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur505) provided a compelling example of how bias issues can arise unintentionally in *natural language processing* (*NLP*) systems. Common sources of AI bias are illustrated in depth by Barocas and Selbst (2016). [Noble (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur421) gives a detailed account of representation issues in the output of algorithms, focusing on Google search.

The main fair AI paradigm, in which fairness metrics are enforced in a learning algorithm, is due to [Dwork et al. (2012)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur153). The authors proposed the individual fairness metric and considered and critiqued differential parity fairness. The 80% rule, a legal criterion for discrimination with a parity flavor and that can be used for fair AI, was codified by the US Equal Employment Opportunities Commission in C.F.R. 29.1607 (EEOC, 1978), and corresponding machine learning algorithms were developed by [Zafar et al. (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur586). The equalized odds and equality of opportunity fairness definitions, which aim to address some of the issues with parity-based fairness, were proposed by [Hardt et al. (2016)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur250). While authors including Hardt et al. and [Simoiu et al. (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur495) criticized parity-based fairness metrics, [Foulds and Pan (2020)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur175) discussed both sides of the argument and defended the concept of parity-based fairness. [Pleiss et al. (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur437) showed a key limitation of equalized odds fairness, namely that it is incompatible with calibrated probability estimates. The incompatibility and resulting trade-offs between fairness definitions were earlier analyzed by [Kleinberg et al. (2016)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur316). Fair representation learning was proposed by [Zemel et al. (2013)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur591). A canonical example of the use of adversarial learning for fair representations was developed by [Beutel et al. (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur39). A blog post by [Tonk (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur533), which applies the methods of [Louppe et al. (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur369) to fairness, is also recommended reading on this topic. [Kusner et al. (2017)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur340) developed the first, and most prominent, causal fairness method, counterfactual fairness.

The consideration of fairness at the intersection of protected groups can be motivated by intersectionality, a framework for analyzing systemic patterns of injustices and how they interact with overlapping protected demographic dimensions, due to [Crenshaw (1989)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur115). [Kearns et al. (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur302) proposed subgroup fairness, which aims to ensure fairness at the intersection of multiple protected groups, and was not informed by intersectionality theory but rather aims to prevent subset targeting, a.k.a. fairness gerrymandering. [Foulds et al. (2020)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur176); [Islam et al. (2023)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur286) critiqued subgroup fairness from the perspective of intersectionality and proposed the first definition that was explicitly motivated by intersectionality. [Hebert-Johnson et al. (2018)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur258) proposed multicalibration, another fairness definition for overlapping protected groups that focuses on calibrated probability estimates.

An important strand of work critiques the limitations of the AI fairness paradigm, and this work is often insightful for how one ought to use (or not use) AI fairness interventions in practice. Blogdett et al. (2020) provided a critical review of fair NLP research, which went beyond summarizing the papers to pointing to the serious deficiencies in that subfield, and in fair ML more generally in terms of addressing the broader context of who has been harmed by a system and what a fair system should actually do. [Selbst et al. (2019)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur485) shone a spotlight on the importance of accounting for the sociotechnical context that an AI system is embedded in and the pitfalls that can arise because of it, such as the framing trap. [Keyes et al. (2019)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur305) critiqued the shortcomings of the dominant fairness, accountability, and transparency (FAT) framework with a scathing satirical example. [Islam et al. (2021)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur287) showed that despite the conventional wisdom in the field, it is frequently possible, and indeed easy, to obtain some degree of “fairness for free” without a loss of predictive performance. The AI Now Report is an annual publication that provides a critical perspective on the state of the field, and it highlights problems in real-world deployed systems as well as limitations of the technologies including fair AI methods. Although it’s always valuable to look at the most recent edition, the 2019 AI Now Report is one that has stood the test of time ([Crawford et al., 2019](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur114)).

For an overview of the main issues in AI safety, including the alignment problem, see [Hendrycks (2021)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur262). Krakanova (2020) explains the problem of specification gaming, and her website maintains an extensive and frequently updated list of examples of it.^(<a aria-label="noteref" href="#fn3" id="Cfn3">3</a>3) [Bostrom (2012)](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur53) is a classic paper on the dangers of advanced AI and how it might go rogue. In an interview, Meredith Whittaker provides a counterpoint to the current alarmism around possible existential threats from AI and reminds us that there are more pressing issues that we perhaps ought to focus on instead ([Chan, 2023](#References_705-736_B978044315888900025X.xhtml#fur89)).
