Deep learning utilizes high-capacity architectures, which are susceptible to overfitting even when data is plentiful, and early stopping is standard practice even when other methods to reduce overfitting are employed, such as regularization and dropout. This is done by monitoring learning curves that plot the `average` `loss` for the training and validation sets as a function of `epoch`. The key is to find the point at which the validation set `average` `loss` begins to deteriorate.

People often use one of the standard `loss` function formulations for neural network outputs simply because it is already integrated into software tools. However, the underlying goal of learning may be different—to minimize the classification error, or perhaps to optimize some combination of precision and recall. In these cases it is important to monitor the true evaluation metric as well as the `average` `loss`, to gain a clearer idea as to whether the model is overfitting the training set. Furthermore, it can be instructive to determine whether the model can classify the data perfectly by adding more capacity and stopping at the appropriate point.

While early stopping is an effective tool for preventing overfitting, a downside is that it muddies the relationship between the capacity of the model and the tendency of the system to overfit. If the model is trained to convergence then increasing model capacity (e.g., increasing the number of layers, or number of neurons per layer) increases the propensity to overfit and reduces the propensity to underfit. In contrast, with early stopping we can no longer be sure that these interventions will have the desired effect on mitigating over- or underfitting, complicating the hyperparameter selection process. Some experts eschew this technique for that reason.

## Unsupervised Pretraining
Unsupervised pretraining can be an effective way to both initialize and regularize a feedforward network, especially when the volume of labeled data is small relative to the model’s capacity. The general idea is to model the distribution of unlabeled data using a method that allows the `parameters` of the learned model to be somehow transferred to the network, or used to initialize or regularize it. We will return to the subject of unsupervised learning in [Section 10.4](#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X.xhtml#s0140) and [Appendix D.3](#FigD1_687-703_B9780443158889000248.xhtml#s0040), and we will discuss an important variation of it, `self-supervised learning`, in [Section 11.2](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#s0030). However, the use of activation functions such as `ReLUs` that improve gradient flow in deep networks, along with good parameter initialization techniques, mitigates the need for sophisticated pretraining methods.

## Data Augmentation and Synthetic Transformations
Data augmentation can be critical for best results. As [Table 10.1](#Ch011_391-432_B978044315888900011X.xhtml#t0010) illustrates, augmenting even a large data set with transformed training data can increase performance significantly—and not just for deep architectures. A simple transformation for visual problems is simply to jiggle the image. If the object to be classified can be cropped out of a larger image, random bounding boxes can be placed around it, adding small translations in the vertical and horizontal directions. By reducing the cropped image larger displacements can be applied. Other translations such as rotation, scale change, and shearing are appropriate as well. In fact, there is a hierarchy of rigid transformations that increase in complexity as `parameters` are added. One augmentation strategy is to apply them to the original image and then crop a patch of given size out of the distorted result.

## Residual Connections
The trend toward deeper and deeper neural networks brings challenges in training. As we backpropagate the `gradients` in a very deep network, going backward through the layers from the output toward the input, the layers are further removed from the output and have smaller direct impacts on the `loss`. Consequently, their `gradients` typically get smaller, making it harder to learn them efficiently. This is called the `vanishing gradient` problem.

In an ideal world adding more layers would do no harm to training set performance. If some of the layers were unnecessary for prediction the model could simply make those extraneous layers be `identity mappings`; the output they produce is the same as the input; thus the network mimics a shallower one. However, in practice additional layers may hurt performance, even on the training set, since vanishing `gradients` make the model harder to train. It may well fail to learn even the simple `identity mappings` that would resolve this issue. Since the power and promise of neural networks is in deep models trained on big data, this impediment to training deep networks is particularly painful.

`Residual networks` (`ResNets`) are an architectural strategy that aims to address vanishing `gradients` and make it easier to learn `identity mappings`; they have been crucial to progress in fitting deeper networks than were previously feasible. They enabled the authors of the research, Kaiming He and colleagues from Microsoft Research, to win multiple computer vision contests in 2015. The key idea in residual networks is the `residual connection`—a connection between neurons that skips over a block of layers and outputs the identity mapping. This makes it easier to learn `identity mappings`, since it is already there by default and can be easily obtained by setting the `weights` of other (direct) connections to zero. Residual connections are an instance of the more general class of `skip connections`, a.k.a. `shortcut connections`, that skip over one or more layers and may or may not output the identity. Skip connections, including residual connections, also create shortened paths to the output layer, thereby mitigating vanishing `gradients`.

A block of layers with a residual connection around it is called a `residual module`. A `deep residual network` stacks multiple residual modules on each other to build the overall network. Following the original `ResNet` model in 2015, it is common for `CNN` models in computer vision (discussed later) to stack residual modules that each contain one or more of the typical sequences of layers in `CNN`s: `convolution`, followed by `batch normalization`, followed by a `ReLU` layer. Residual connections are also used in the `transformer` architecture (discussed in the next chapter).
