
where `pos`![Equation](images/F000121si142.png) is the position in the document, `i`![Equation](images/F000121si143.png) is the dimension in the positional encoding, and `n`![Equation](images/F000121si144.png) is a scaling constant that the authors set to 10,000. The result is that each dimension of the positional encoding is a sinusoid, and the wavelengths of the sinusoids follow a geometric progression varying between `2π`![Equation](images/F000121si145.png) and `2π n`![Equation](images/F000121si146.png). While other functions, or learned positional embeddings, could be used, the sinusoidal approach works well in practice, and it can extrapolate beyond the length of the sequences seen in the training data. Other authors, such as those of the `GPT` models, have used learned positional embeddings instead.

## 11.4 Transformer-Based Language Models

We can now combine all of the components we described to create language models using the transformer architecture.

### Decoder-Only Architecture

Suppose we want to build a transformer-based language model to predict sequences of words. Such a model is trained to optimize the log-likelihood of each of the words `w[i]`![Equation](images/F000121si147.png) in the text corpus given the previous words in the context window of size `k`![Equation](images/F000121si148.png):

`L = ∑[i] log P(w[i] | w[i-1], w[i-2], …, w[i-k])`.![Equation](images/F000121si149.png)

Each word is represented by an input embedding plus a positional encoding vector. The representations are fed into a stack of `N`![Equation](images/F000121si150.png) transformer blocks, using masked multihead attention to ensure that at each step of the sequence, only previous tokens are used to predict next tokens. In the final layer, the next word `w[i]`![Equation](images/F000121si151.png) is predicted using the last layer’s hidden state vector `h[N]`![Equation](images/F000121si152.png) and the input embeddings `V[e]`![Equation](images/F000121si153.png) via `softmax(h[N] V[e]^T)`![Equation](images/F000121si154.png) ([Fig. 11.11](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#f0060)). The model we have just described is sometimes called a `decoder-only` transformer architecture because there is no encoder stage, unlike some of the models we will describe later. It is also an `autoregressive model`, a term borrowed from time series analysis that means the future (here, later words in the sequence) is predicted based on the past (here, previous words in the sequence).

### Generative Pretrained Transformers

The `GPT` series of models from OpenAI are large transformer-based language models. They are variants of the decoder-only architecture. Since they focus on predicting next tokens given previous ones, they excel at generating new text data. ChatGPT, a chatbot based on `GPT`, has been fine-tuned via a process called `reinforcement learning from human feedback`, to follow instructions, or “prompts,” given in natural language form. It can provide startlingly human-like and sophisticated responses. The models can also be fine-tuned to solve other tasks, which was a popular approach with the earlier `GPT` and `GPT-2` models. With more recent `GPT` models, users are instead encouraged to perform “`zero-shot`,” `one-shot`, or “`few-shot`” learning, whereby models can sometimes solve specialized tasks without fine-tuning by instead providing the task to solve, possibly with a small number of examples, in the prompt itself and relying on the system to interpret the prompt and solve the task based on its knowledge obtained from its large natural language training corpora.

The main modification to the decoder-only architecture in the `GPT` models is to address supervised learning tasks. The model is first trained as a language model, predicting next tokens given previous ones, which is viewed as a pretraining step. The models are then fine-tuned with additional training to perform supervised NLP prediction tasks including reading comprehension tests, summarization, translation, question answering, and so on.
