
# Fairness Considerations

Parity-based approaches to fairness are appropriate in scenarios where the differences in outcome/class label distributions in the dataset are due to unfair reasons, such as unfair societal processes like racism or sexism, prejudice on behalf of human annotators that systematically bias the labeling process, or other issues with the data that have harmful and unequal impact across protected demographics such as toxic language in social media, unrepresentative datasets, and so on. They are also valuable when the goal is to promote equality (understood as an intrinsically desirable outcome), perform affirmative action, mitigate harmful stereotypes, increase diversity in the pool of selected individuals, or prevent legal liability under the EEOC guidelines. Achieving these goals may come at the cost of predictive accuracy but if the labels are considered unfair anyway then we may be willing to accept some degree of trade-off between accuracy and fairness.

On the other hand, in scenarios where demographic differences in the distribution of labels in the data are accurate, legitimate, and occur for reasons that are considered justifiable, then parity-based metrics would not be appropriate. In such scenarios, where both society and data are considered to be on a level playing field, a parity-based fairness intervention would unnecessarily skew the behavior of the AI system, with likely harm to prediction accuracy, and perhaps introducing a new fairness issue in the altered behavior of the system. If the dataset has disparity in the class labels, then achieving parity is incompatible with predicting the class labels accurately, that is, the “correct” classifier is ruled out. Another fairness definition, called `equalized odds`, avoids this issue. Instead of ensuring parity of the distribution of class labels between demographic groups, equalized odds ensures that the classifier has equal error rates between demographic groups:

`P(ŷ=1|s=1,y) = P(ŷ=1|s=0,y), y ∈ {0,1}`

![Equation](images/F00008Xsi117.png)(7.14)

Equalized odds reward accurate classification and prevent the system from only performing well for the majority demographic at the expense of minority groups. A relaxed version of this definition, called `equality of opportunity`, only enforces parity for the individuals belonging to the “deserving” class label. For example, the people who can truly pay back their loan (`y = 1`) will have the same probability of being correctly classified as such individuals, regardless of their gender:

`P(ŷ=1|s=1,y=1) = P(ŷ=1|s=0,y=1)`

![Equation](images/F00008Xsi119.png)(7.15)

While equalized odds and equality of opportunity are compatible with the “correct” classifier, they have another issue along the same lines—they may be incompatible with correctly calibrated probability estimates. In other words, when the classifier says that a particular instance has, say, an 80% chance of class `y = 1`, the actual class probability may be something else, for example, 60%. Here, although the classifier is both fair (according to the definition) and accurate (in terms of the binary classification accuracy percentage), achieving fairness has still come at a cost in terms of its predictive behavior. The practical impact is that we can no longer take its class probability estimates at face value. A lack of calibration can also be a fairness issue if the probabilities are calibrated for one demographic group but not another. A human decision maker may be more skeptical of the model’s predictions for the group that does not have calibration.

Another limitation of parity-based fairness methods is that they only ensure parity for the protected groups that are specified and not subgroups of them. This could potentially be exploited for deliberate harm, in a phenomenon known as `subset targeting` or `fairness gerrymandering`. Suppose, for instance, an AI system could allocate coupons for discounts at an expensive clothing store with an equal proportion to Black people and White people, yet only gave the coupons to those Black people who could not afford to shop at the store and thus would never use them. Parity-based fairness fails to prevent this injustice (at least when race is the only protected attribute).

A closely related issue occurs when subgroups at the intersection of several protected groups are unfairly impacted. In the 1976 employment discrimination lawsuit DeGraffenreid vs General Motors, plaintiffs alleged that General Motors’ seniority-based layoffs unfairly harmed Black women, who the company had never hired prior to 1964 and all of whom were fired under the policy. The company was able to successfully defend against the suit because the incident did not meet the standard of either race discrimination or sex/gender discrimination when these dimensions where considered alone, and Title VII law did not identify Black women (or any other intersecting subgroups of legally protected categories) as a group protected by law.

This case, and others like it, led legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw to coin and develop `intersectionality`, a theoretical framework for analyzing how injustices (and advantages) occur and are potentially magnified at the intersection of multiple protected dimensions including gender, race, nationality, sexual orientation, socioeconomic class, and disability. Intersectionality posits that these injustices occur because unfair societal processes, termed `systems of oppression`, interact in interlocking ways that affect the lived experience of individuals belonging to the intersection of multiple such protected attributes. Systems of oppression include sexism, racism, the prison-industrial complex, and the school-to-prison pipeline. To learn more, Crenshaw’s TED talk, available to watch on YouTube, is a good place to start.
