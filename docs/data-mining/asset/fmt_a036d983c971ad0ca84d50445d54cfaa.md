
Many difficulties arise. One is scarcity of data. Users are loath to demonstrate several iterations of a task; they think the agent should immediately catch on to what they are doing. Whereas a data miner would consider a 100-instance dataset miniscule, users bridle at the prospect of demonstrating a task even half a dozen times. A second difficulty is the plethora of attributes. The computer desktop environment has hundreds of features that any given action might depend upon. This means that small datasets are overwhelmingly likely to contain attributes that are apparently highly predictive but nevertheless irrelevant, and specialized statistical tests are needed to compare alternative hypotheses. A third is that the iterative, improvement-driven development style that characterizes data mining applications fails. It is impossible *in principle* to create a fixed training and testing corpus for an interactive problem such as programming by demonstration because each improvement in the agent alters the test data by affecting how users react to it. A fourth is that existing application programs provide limited access to application and user data; often the raw material on which successful operation depends is inaccessible, buried deep within the application program.

Machine learning is already widely used at work. Text and Web mining is bringing the techniques in this book into our own lives as we read our email and surf the Web. As for the future, it will be stranger than we can imagine. The spreading computing infrastructure will offer untold opportunities for learning. Machine learning will be in there, behind the scenes, playing a role that will turn out to be foundational.

## 15.10 Machine Learning Technologies and Applications of Concern

In [Chapter 7](#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X.xhtml#Ch007_261-315_B978044315888900008X), “Ethics: what are the impacts of what’s been learned?,” we discussed concerns around applications of machine learning in hiring, criminal justice, credit scoring, and facial recognition, mostly revolving around fairness and bias issues. Here we’ll consider several other applications and technologies in which different ethical issues arise.

### Deepfakes

Generative deep learning technologies have now made it possible to generate or manipulate photos, video, audio, and text in a realistic manner, as we discussed in [Chapter 11](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121), “Advanced deep learning methods.” These technologies are rapidly improving, and they will have a profound impact on our society. A *deepfake* is an artificially created or altered piece of media that is achieved via deep learning technology. These technologies have many benefits such as creating beautiful artworks, enhancing educational content or films, or even making funny memes to share on the internet. They may be able to help people with some forms of paralysis such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) to speak again in a close approximation of their own voice. However, the potential harms of these technologies are severe. This is not merely hypothetical, as some of these harms have already occurred. In 2019 a deepfake app called DeepNude was released that modified images of women to “undress” them, creating realistic nude images. After widespread public outrage the app’s creators took it down. In a tweet the creators of the app wrote “The world is not yet ready for DeepNude.”

In the wake of the 2018 school shooting at Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School in Parkland, Florida, Emma González, a student survivor, posted a compelling video in which she ripped up a sheet of paper with a bullseye target on it. To counter her activism as a proponent of gun-control, deepfake images and videos were made that fraudulently showed her instead ripping up the constitution of the United States. Despite the low quality of the images and video, many people were fooled into thinking that they were real. While fake or misleading content is not a new problem, generative AI has made the production of such content cheaper, faster, more scalable, and more realistic.

Experts have cited many potential harms to individuals, organizations, and society from deepfakes, including:
* Identity theft
* Blackmail
* Pornography (cf., the DeepNude example)
* Reputational sabotage to scuttle employment or romantic opportunities of rivals
* Political reputational sabotage to discredit opponents
* Falsifying politically inflammatory acts such as police violence or terrorism to stoke anger, violence, or panic, stoke social divisions, or even start a war (cf., the Parkland shooting example)
* Creating “fake news”
* Manipulating elections
* Damaging trust in institutions
* Undermining diplomatic relations between nations
* Disinformation and cyber-espionage
* Discrediting journalists and news outlets
* Obfuscating the truth by claiming that legitimate videos are deepfakes.

### AI Art

The capability of generative deep learning models such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) and diffusion models to create artistic works has opened up both exciting possibilities and ethical dilemmas (see [Section 11.1](#Ch012_433-471_B9780443158889000121.xhtml#s0010) for more information on these models). These “*AI art*” models can create realistic and artistically impressive synthetic artworks resembling paintings, drawings, photographs, and more. The systems can be directed via natural language prompts describing the desired image. *Style transfer* models such as *CycleGAN* can automatically convert artworks into the artistic style of another artist, change the season where a scene takes place, or produce a drawing based on a photograph. Commercial AI art software such as Dall-E, CrAIyon, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion has put the power of AI art into the hands of consumers.

However, the impact on human artists and their livelihoods looms large. In 2022 an AI-generated artwork won first place at the Colorado State Fair Fine Arts Competition, in the “digital arts/digitally-manipulated photograph” category, raising the ire of some artists. The piece, developed by digital artist Jason M. Allen using Midjourney and titled “Théâtre D’opéra Spatial” (“Space Opera Theater” in French), combined Victorian and futuristic imagery ([Fig. 15.3](#Ch016_571-615_B9780443158889000169.xhtml#f0020)). The use of Midjourney was declared in the submission. One of the judges said that he had missed this fact when judging it but he stood by the result, calling it a “beautiful piece.” On the other side of the debate one Twitter user wrote “This sucks for the exact same reason we don’t let robots participate in the Olympics.”
