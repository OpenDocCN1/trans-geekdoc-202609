
# Surveillance Capitalism

## The Rise of Surveillance Capitalism: Aware Home vs Nest

To illustrate the rise of surveillance capitalism, Zuboff contrasts an early vision of the smart home and ubiquitous computing with the current state of affairs in those areas. The Aware Home was a research project begun in 2000 at Georgia Tech, which envisioned a symbiosis of humans and home via context-aware sensors. Crucially, the researchers made the working assumption that *the data and knowledge arising from the system would belong entirely to the people who live in the house*. As such, the home would keep its ancient role as a private sanctuary, protected from outside observation or influence. In the year 2000 this assumption was in line with most people’s expectations and was considered uncontroversial. Fast-forward to 2018, when Nest, a subsidiary of Alphabet, was merged with Google. Like the Aware home the Nest Thermostat uses sensors in the home to learn about its environment and the home’s inhabitants. But unlike the Aware Home data and knowledge acquired from the system are *uploaded to Google’s servers and hence shared with third parties for analytics*. Since Nest takes little responsibility for the use of the data by those third parties, users would need to read many other privacy policies and license agreements to understand what might happen to their data. A study from the University of London found that a user of a single Nest Thermostat would need to read almost 1000 contracts to understand how their data are being used! So what went wrong between the year 2000 and 2018? You guessed it—the rise of surveillance capitalism.

### Data Extraction and Analysis

As we know well by now, “Big Other” harvests data from many sources including online transactions, a growing array of sensors including drones, wearable devices, smart homes, smart phones, and so on, as well as government and corporate databases, and surveillance cameras. Further, everyday actions are increasingly digitized and hence recorded and stored. Your every click, social media post, email, text message, search query, and more can be captured to learn about you. This data stream is sometimes called the *digital exhaust*.

As the saying goes, “If it’s free, you are the product.” For example, Google’s users are not its customers. Rather, its customers are advertisers, to whom it sells ads via the Google AdWords platform, which performs an algorithmic auction process. Since well-targeted ads are more valuable, Google is motivated to store every scrap of information it can on its users to improve ad targeting and thus increase profits. This also incentivizes aggressive data collection strategies—whenever there is a legal gray area on whether data collection is allowed, Big Other companies will take data first, ask permission later, and when the protests and finally lawsuits inevitably come, pay the resulting fines or settlements at a small fraction of the resulting profits. This brings to mind Facebook’s former internal motto, attributed to Mark Zuckerberg: “Move fast and break things.” For instance, not only do Google Street View’s vans take photos of peoples’ homes without asking for permission but they also had scanners that were scraping data from private Wi-Fi networks, including those of residential Internet users. A lawsuit from 38 US states resulted in a $7 million settlement and forced Google to police its employees over privacy issues. At the time of writing overseas lawsuits are ongoing.

As you are now well familiar with, after collecting all this data machine learning is then brought to bear to analyze the data, particularly to predict behavior, which Zuboff calls a “*behavioral futures market*.” Ubiquitous data collection for behavioral prediction, feeding into a surveillance capitalism business model, has become the default model for Silicon Valley startups.

### Monitoring and Contracts

The increasing computer mediation and digitization of all aspects of life enables Big Other to increase their control by imposing contracts in a more fine-grained and constraining manner and monitor and enforce them digitally. If someone misses a car payment the vehicle can cease to operate and report its location for pickup. Auto insurance companies can monitor their customers’ driving to determine whether to pay out claims. Smartphone location tracking data can be used to check whether gig economy workers have completed their assigned tasks and automatically refuse payment for the work.

Contracts, which are essentially promises and obligations, are central to the functioning of our society. If contracts are enforced automatically, it removes the necessity to, or possibility of, developing trust.

Zuboff has concerns that Big Other is removing contracts from the social sphere and turning them into automated processes. Surveillance capitalists have stepped into the role of contract enforcer, occupying a power typically held by the state, which in a democratic society was legitimately chosen by the people to wield that power. According to Zuboff, Big Other has seized that power unilaterally, without legitimate authority to do so, and mechanically enforced it, without oversight and in many cases without detection. She sees the enforcement of rewards and punishments by Big Other as “a new kind of invisible hand” that overthrows the rule of law and could even be understood as a “coup des gens” (overthrow of the people).

Zuboff compares the automatic monitoring and enforcement of contracts to the *panopticon*, a theoretical prison design proposed by philosopher Jeremy Bentham in the late 1700 s that is frequently used as metaphor for modern-day surveillance in the workplace. The use as a metaphor is associated with philosopher Michel Foucault. In the panopticon there is a central guard tower, with cells around the periphery and blinds that prevent prisoners from observing the guards ([Fig. 15.5](#Ch016_571-615_B9780443158889000169.xhtml#f0030)). The design makes it so that the guards can watch any or all of the prisoners, and the prisoners cannot tell whether or not they are being watched at any given moment. Hence the prisoners must behave as if they are always watched. This behavior modification, called “*anticipatory conformity*,” is the main goal of the system. Zuboff sees the automaticity that surveillance capitalism strives for as even worse than the panopticon, in which the prisoners at least had a choice on whether to conform. With continual monitoring and automatic enforcement of contracts, even the option not to conform is removed.
