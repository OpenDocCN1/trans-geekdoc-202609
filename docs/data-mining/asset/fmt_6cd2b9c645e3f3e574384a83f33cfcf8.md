
As AI becomes more sophisticated and inscrutable, there are rising concerns that AI and machine learning technologies can behave in dangerous and unintended ways. These issues arise in large part because it is difficult to fully specify and control the behavior of a system that is learned rather than programmed to ensure that it behaves as we want it to. This issue is called the *AI alignment problem*.

## The AI Alignment Problem

*I really hate this damn machine*,

*I wish that they would sell it*.

*It never does quite what I want*,

*But only what I tell it*.

—Anonymous

Some of you may have heard this quote, with unknown attribution, in one of your first programming classes. Any programmer quickly learns that it is difficult to perfectly tell a computer exactly what you want it to do, and a slight misstep in specifying its instructions could lead to disastrous results.

A similar issue can occur when designing an AI or machine learning system. The *AI alignment problem*, sometimes called the *value alignment problem*, is the problem that the objective function that a human specifies for an AI may not perfectly correspond to their intended behavior and goals for the system, and it could admit unintended consequences. There is a fairly direct analogy to stories of a genie or other supernatural entity that grants wishes, but the stated wish has a loophole that causes it to backfire and come true with unforeseen negative results. The story of King Midas, who was granted his wish that everything he touches turns to gold, is a canonical example.

AI alignment problems can happen due to *unintended side effects*. The AI fairness and bias issues that we discussed fall into this category; a machine learning system may accurately predict the target class label while also exhibiting harmful behavior such as unfair discriminatory patterns in its predictions. This is an alignment issue because a machine learning objective function that only rewards accurate predictions fails to rule out biased behavior.

As previously discussed in this chapter, class labels are often chosen as convenient proxies for ground truth, such as repaying a loan as a proxy for a more holistic notion of creditworthiness, and these choices can impair alignment in a manner that introduces discriminatory bias. When an AI system has a real-world physical presence, direct physical harm becomes a possibility, that is, a self-driving car that runs over pedestrians to reach its destination or a robot vacuum cleaner that knocks over tables while cleaning.

Misaligned AI issues can also occur from *specification gaming*, also known as *reward hacking*, in which the AI system achieves its goal via a novel and unforeseen strategy that was not intended by the system’s designer. Humans do this, too. If a restaurant manager punishes her staff whenever they are seen to not be actively working, employees are incentivized to do their assigned tasks as slowly as possible. The employee may achieve this by cleaning surfaces in the least efficient order, with the gunk in the microwave falling onto the microwave stand and further down to the counter below so that both surfaces must be cleaned again, creating unnecessary work that allows the worker to appear busy when they are not.

Researcher Victoria Krakovna maintains a list of examples of specification gaming on her blog if you would like to see many instances of the phenomenon. In one case, reported by Twitter user Custard Smingleigh, a Roomba robot vacuum cleaner was trained to drive using a neural network via reinforcement learning with rewards for speed and for not setting off its bumper sensors that indicate a collision. Since there were no sensors on the back, the AI learned to travel at high speed in reverse, thus avoiding penalties for any collisions.

This example shows how it can be difficult for humans to perfectly specify the desired behavior of an AI system, resorting instead to simpler *proxy goals* that may or may not lead to the intended results. One possible solution to this challenge is to learn the reward function, from human feedback or *imitation learning* methodologies such as *inverse reinforcement learning*, but this can create its own specification gaming issues. For example, researchers from OpenAI created a reinforcement learning algorithm that learns preferred behaviors from human feedback and used it to train a robot hand to grasp a ball. However, the AI instead learned to fool human annotators by simply placing the hand between the ball and the observer, appearing to grasp it but not actually doing so.

Of course, unanticipated solutions can be desirable and are a sign that the learning algorithm is powerful enough to find solutions that go beyond what the human designers could dream up. When Google Deepmind’s AlphaZero system was trained to play chess it produced novel and effective game-playing strategies that human players did not know about. Whether a novel solution is desirable, or if it constitutes unwanted specification gaming, can be a complicated question. One of the authors was once told of a case where an airport hired academic consultants to help them reduce the time that passengers were waiting to claim their baggage. The problem was formalized as a mathematical optimization problem with the objective of minimizing the passengers’ wait time. The optimization algorithm produced a solution in which the passengers had to walk all the way across the airport to retrieve their baggage, thereby waiting next to the carousel for a shorter time. This solution seems perverse as it did not actually get the baggage back to the passengers any faster. However, the airport implemented it and the complaints by the passengers went away!

While it is difficult enough to specify the objective or reward function of an AI system well enough that the solution to the optimization problem is sure to be what we want (*outer alignment*), further AI alignment problems can arise when the system learns that it must achieve intermediate goals to achieve its objectives (*inner alignment*). A robot vacuum cleaner that aims to clean an entire house must learn to identify doorways and navigate between the rooms of the house, for instance. We typically train machine learning algorithms by optimizing some objective function but the models that are learned could themselves be optimizers (e.g., learning to efficiently navigate the house). Such a model is called a *mesa-optimizer*. Alignment problems can occur if the learned intermediate goals that a mesa-optimizer aims to achieve are the result of overfitting, called *goal mis-generalization*. For example, if an intelligent agent seeks a coin that is always in the same location in its training data but that coin is in a different location at test time, it may blindly navigate to the original location and might not even recognize the coin in its new location.

One intermediate *emergent goal* that arises naturally and can help an AI system achieve many goals is to obtain power over its environment by increasing its resources or its ability to prevent any other agent or human from impeding it. Such *power-seeking* behavior has clear and obvious risks. The robot vacuum cleaner might learn how to lock all humans in the house in a closet so that they cannot create a mess, for example. It could learn how to evade being switched off (which would prevent it from completing its cleaning), potentially by harming or deceiving humans.
