
It follows that

$$\begin{aligned}
D_f(h(W)\|h(Z)) &= \mathbb{E}_{X'\sim Q}f\left(\frac{P(X')}{Q(X')}\right) \\
&\overset{(a)}{=} \mathbb{E}_{X'\sim Q}f\left(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q(\cdot\mid X')}\left[\frac{P(X,X')}{Q(X,X')}\right]\right) \\
&\overset{(b)}{\leq} \mathbb{E}_{X'\sim Q}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q(\cdot\mid X')}f\left(\frac{P(X,X')}{Q(X,X')}\right) \\
&\overset{(c)}{=} \mathbb{E}_{X'\sim Q}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q(\cdot\mid X')}f\left(\frac{P(X)}{Q(X)}\right) \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{[X,X']\sim Q}f\left(\frac{P(X)}{Q(X)}\right)=D_f(W\|Z).
\end{aligned}$$

Note that (B.2) implies (a); (b) follows from Jensen’s inequality, and (c) can be obtained from (B.1).

## B.2 Examples of $f$-divergence

In the following, we list useful examples of $f$-divergence encountered in the main text. However, it is worth noting that different $f(t)$ can lead to the same divergence, as shown by the following simple fact.

**Proposition B.5** The $f$-divergence with $f(t)$ is the same as the $f$-divergence with $\tilde f(t)=f(t)+\beta(1-t)$ for any $\beta\in\mathbb{R}$.

We will choose only one $f(t)$ for each divergence in the example, although one can choose different $f(t)$ for convenience. The examples are all strictly convex at $t=1$, and thus $D_f(P\|Q)=0$ if and only if $P=Q$.

### KL-divergence

With $f(t)=t\ln t$, we obtain the KL-divergence (Kullback–Leibler divergence) as follows

$$\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)=\int_{\Omega}\ln\left(\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\right)p(z)\,d\mu(z).$$

KL-divergence can be unbounded.

Given random variables $[X,X']$, with probability measure $P$, their mutual information is defined as

$$I(X,X')=\mathrm{KL}(P(X,X')\|P(X)P(X')).$$

Mutual information is often used to measure the independence of the random variables $X$ and $X'$. If the random variables are independent, then the mutual information is zero. In the general case, mutual information can be unbounded.

### $\chi^2$-divergence

With $f(t) = (t - 1)^2$, we obtain $\chi^2$-divergence as follows:

$$\chi^2(P\|Q) = \int_{\Omega} \left(\frac{p(z)-q(z)}{q(z)}\right)^2 q(z)\,d\mu(z) = \int_{\Omega} \left(\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\right)^2 q(z)\,d\mu(z) - 1.$$

The $\chi^2$-divergence can be unbounded.

### Squared Hellinger distance

With $f(t) = (1 - \sqrt{t})^2$, we obtain squared Hellinger distance as follows:

$$H(P\|Q)^2 = \int_{\Omega} \left(\sqrt{p(z)} - \sqrt{q(z)}\right)^2 d\mu(z) = 2 - 2 \int_{\Omega} \sqrt{p(z)q(z)}\,d\mu(z).$$

Hellinger distance is always between $[0,2]$.

### $\alpha$-divergence

KL-divergence, $\chi^2$-divergence, and Hellinger divergence all belong to the more general family of $\alpha$-divergence for $\alpha \ge 0$. It is defined with $f(t) = (1-\alpha)^{-1}(t - t^{\alpha})$ for $\alpha > 0$:

$$D_{\alpha}(P\|Q) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \left(1 - \int_{\Omega} \left(\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\right)^{\alpha} q(z)\,d\mu(z)\right). \quad (B.3)$$

We have

- $D_1(P\|Q) = \mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)$: when $\alpha \to 1$, we have $f(t) \to t \ln t$.
- $D_2(P\|Q) = \chi^2(P\|Q)$.
- $D_{0.5}(P\|Q) = H(P\|Q)^2$.

When $\alpha < 1$, $\alpha$-divergence is bounded, and when $\alpha \ge 1$, $\alpha$-divergence is unbounded.

We can also write $\alpha$-divergence differently as follows:

$$D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(P\|Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \ln[1 + (\alpha - 1)D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)] = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \ln \int_{\Omega} \left(\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\right)^{\alpha - 1} p(z)\,d\mu(z).$$

The quantity $D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(\cdot)$ is often referred to as Rényi entropy, which is also nonnegative. When $\alpha = 1$, we use the convention that

$$D_{1}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(P\|Q) = \lim_{\alpha \to 1} D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(P\|Q) = \mathrm{KL}(P\|Q). \quad (B.4)$$

One important property of $\alpha$-divergence is that it is convenient to estimate the $\alpha$-divergence of product distributions. This property can be used to obtain lower bounds for statistical estimation.

**Theorem B.6** Let $P^n$ and $Q^n$ be the product distribution of $n$ iid samples from $P$ and $Q$ respectively. Then

$$D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(P^n\|Q^n)=n\cdot D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(P\|Q).$$

For $\alpha=1$, this becomes

$$\mathrm{KL}(P^n\|Q^n)=n\cdot\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q).$$

### $L_1$ norm and TV-norm

With $f(t)=|t-1|$, we obtain the $L_1$ norm between two measures as

$$\|P-Q\|_1=\int_{\Omega}|p(z)-q(z)|\,d\mu(z).$$

The quantity is always bounded between $[0,2]$. We can also define the TV-norm between two measures as

$$\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac{1}{2}\|P-Q\|_1=1-\int_{\Omega}\min(p(z),q(z))\,d\mu(z),$$

which is always between $[0,1]$.

The TV-norm can also be defined equivalently as follows.

**Proposition B.7**

$$\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\sup_A|P(A)-Q(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{g:\,\|g\|_{\infty}\le 1}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P}g(Z)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q}g(Z)\right],$$

*where $A$ is over all measurable sets, and $g$ is over all measurable functions with bounded $L_\infty$ norm.*

## B.3 Basic Inequalities

Many $f$-divergence inequalities can be found in Sason and Verdú (2016). In the following, we will only present some of the inequalities that are used in the book.

In many applications, it is useful to bound the TV-norm of two distributions in terms of other divergences. The following inequality is a straightforward application of data-processing inequality.

**Lemma B.8** *We have*

$$\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2\le\frac{1}{c_f}D_f(P\|Q),$$

*where $c_f$ is defined below with $p(s)=(1-s)q+sp$:*

$$c_f=\min_{p,q\in[0,1]}\int_0^1\left[\frac{1}{q}f''\left(\frac{p(s)}{q}\right)+\frac{1}{1-q}f''\left(\frac{1-p(s)}{1-q}\right)\right](1-s)\,ds.$$

**Proof** Given any measurable set $A$, let $p=P(A)$ and $q=Q(A)$. By the data processing inequality, with $h(z)=\mathbb{1}(z\in A)$, we have

$$\begin{aligned}
D_f(P\|Q) &\ge q f(p/q) + (1-q) f((1-p)/(1-q)) \\
&= \left. q f\left(1 + \frac{s(p-q)}{q}\right) + (1-q) f\left(1 + \frac{s(q-p)}{1-q}\right) \right|_{s=1} \\
&= g(0) + g'(0) + \int_0^1 g''(s)(1-s)\,ds \\
&= (p-q)^2 \int_0^1 \left[\frac{1}{q} f''\left(\frac{p(s)}{q}\right) + \frac{1}{1-q} f''\left(\frac{1-p(s)}{1-q}\right)\right](1-s)\,ds \\
&\ge c_f (p-q)^2,
\end{aligned}$$

where we have used Taylor expansion, and $g(0)=g'(0)=0$. Therefore,

$$\frac{1}{c_f}D_f(P\|Q) \ge \sup_A (P(A)-Q(A))^2 = \|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2.$$

This proves the desired bound.

This result implies the following inequalities. The bound of TV-norm in KL-divergence is often referred to as Pinsker's inequality.

**Theorem B.9** We have

$$\begin{aligned}
\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2 &\le \frac{1}{4}\chi^2(P\|Q), \\
\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2 &\le \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q), \\
\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2 &\le H(P\|Q)^2.
\end{aligned}$$

**Proof** Consider $\alpha$-divergence with $\alpha=1,2$. Let $f(t)=(1-\alpha)^{-1}(t-t^\alpha)$, and $f''(t)=\alpha t^{\alpha-2}$. Let

$$c(p,q)=\int_0^1\left[q^{1-\alpha}p(s)^{\alpha-2}+(1-q)^{1-\alpha}(1-p(s))^{\alpha-2}\right](1-s)\,ds.$$

There exists $p_0\in(0,1)$ such that (for $\alpha=1,2$)

$$c(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\left[q^{1-\alpha}p_0^{\alpha-2}+(1-q)^{1-\alpha}(1-p_0)^{\alpha-2}\right]\ge 2.$$

We can now apply Lemma B.8 with $c_f = \alpha \min_{p,q} c(p,q)$ and $c(p,q)\ge 2\alpha$ to obtain the result. This implies the first two inequalities of the theorem. For the third inequality, we may still estimate a lower bound of $c(p,q)$ as defined. However, the following derivation is more direct:

$$\begin{aligned}
\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2 &= \left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|p(z)-q(z)|\,d\mu(z)\right]^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{4}\left[\int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{p(z)}-\sqrt{q(z)}|\cdot|\sqrt{p(z)}+\sqrt{q(z)}|\,d\mu(z)\right]^2 \\
&\le \frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{p(z)}-\sqrt{q(z)}|^2\,d\mu(z)\cdot\int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{p(z)}+\sqrt{q(z)}|^2\,d\mu(z) \\
&\le \frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{p(z)}-\sqrt{q(z)}|^2\,d\mu(z)\cdot\int_{\Omega}2[p(z)+q(z)]\,d\mu(z) \\
&= \int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{p(z)}-\sqrt{q(z)}|^2\,d\mu(z) = H(P\|Q)^2.
\end{aligned}$$

The first inequality used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. The second inequality used $(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})^2\le 2(a+b)$. The last two equations used the definition of Hellinger distance and the fact that each density $p(z)$ and $q(z)$ integrates to 1.

We can also derive an upper bound of Hellinger distance by the square root of TV norm. However, in general, this bound is not tight when the norms are small.

**Proposition B.10** We have

$$H(P\|Q)^2 \le 2\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.$$

**Proof** We note that

$$\begin{aligned}
H(P\|Q)^2 &= \int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{p(z)}-\sqrt{q(z)}|^2\,d\mu(z) \\
&\le \int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{p(z)}-\sqrt{q(z)}|\cdot|\sqrt{p(z)}+\sqrt{q(z)}|\,d\mu(z) \\
&= \int_{\Omega}|p(z)-q(z)|\,d\mu(z).
\end{aligned}$$

This implies the result.

The following result is also useful.

**Proposition B.11** Let $\rho=\sup_z\ln(p(z)/q(z))$. Then

$$H(P\|Q)^2 \le \mathrm{KL}(P\|Q) \le (3+\rho)H(P\|Q)^2.$$

**Proof** Let $\kappa=\sup_{t\in[0,\infty)} f_{\mathrm{KL}}(t)/f_H(t)$. Then by using the fact that $f_{\mathrm{KL}}(t)/f_H(t)$ is an increasing function of $t\in[0,\infty)$, we obtain

$$\kappa \le \frac{f_{\mathrm{KL}}(\exp(\rho))}{f_H(\exp(\rho))}.$$

Moreover, since $p(z)/q(z)\le\exp(\rho)$ with $\rho\ge0$, and when $\rho\in(0,\infty)$, we have

$$\kappa \le \frac{f_{\mathrm{KL}}(\exp(\rho))}{f_H(\exp(\rho))} \le 2.5+\rho.$$

We now have

$$\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q}f_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(z)/q(z)) \le \kappa\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q}f_H(p(z)/q(z)) = \kappa H(P\|Q)^2,$$

which implies the desired bounds.

It is also relatively easy to obtain bounds of $\alpha$-divergences for different values of $\alpha$. In general, if $\alpha < \alpha'$, then we can bound $\alpha$-divergence by $\alpha'$-divergence as follows. It is a direct consequence of Jensen's inequality.

**Proposition B.12** If $0 < \alpha < \alpha'$, then

$$D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(P\|Q) \le D_{\alpha'}^{\mathrm{R\acute{e}}}(P\|Q),$$

where the convention of (B.4) is adopted for $\alpha=1$ or $\alpha'=1$.

**Proof** We consider the case of $1 < \alpha \le \alpha'$. Then Jensen's inequality implies that

$$\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\right)^{\alpha-1}p(z)\,d\mu(z)\right]^{(\alpha'-1)/(\alpha-1)} \le \int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\right)^{\alpha'-1}p(z)\,d\mu(z).$$

By taking the logarithm and dividing each side by $\alpha'-1$, we obtain the desired bound. Similarly, we can prove the case of $0 < \alpha \le \alpha' < 1$. By combining the two cases, and taking limit at $\alpha\to1$ or $\alpha'\to1$, we obtain the desired inequality for all $0 < \alpha \le \alpha'$.

Theorem B.9 is useful to bound the product distributions for TV-norm via $\alpha$-divergence. Due to Proposition B.12, it is beneficial to use $\alpha<1$. In particular, we have the following result using Hellinger distance.

**Theorem B.13** Let $P^n$ and $Q^n$ be the product distribution of $n$ iid samples from $P$ and $Q$ respectively. We have

$$\|P^n-Q^n\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2 \le H(P^n\|Q^n)^2 = 2 - 2\left(1 - \frac12 H(P\|Q)^2\right)^n.$$

**Proof** From Theorem B.6 with $\alpha=0.5$, we obtain

$$\ln\left(1 - \frac12 H(P^n\|Q^n)^2\right) = n\ln\left(1 - \frac12 H(P\|Q)^2\right).$$

It follows that

$$H(P^n\|Q^n)^2 = 2 - 2\left(1 - \frac12 H(P\|Q)^2\right)^n.$$

Together with Theorem B.9, we obtain the desired bound.
