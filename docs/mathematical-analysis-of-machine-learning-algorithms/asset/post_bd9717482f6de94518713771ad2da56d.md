
For the approximate regularized ERM method in (6.1), we have with probability at least \(1-\delta\),

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}L(\hat w,X,Y)
\le \inf_{w\in\mathcal W}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}L(w,X,Y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_2^2\right]
+\epsilon'+\frac{2\gamma^2}{\lambda n}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal D}\|\psi(X)\|_2^2
+M\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{n}}.
$$

### 6.5 Local Rademacher Complexity Analysis

The technique to prove McDiarmid’s inequality is called the *Martingale method*, and it can derive concentration inequalities with convergence rates of \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\). As shown in Example 6.24, it is possible to obtain \(O(1/n)\) expected convergence result using Offset Rademacher complexity. However, the rate with respect to concentration is still \(1/\sqrt{n}\). In order to improve the analysis, we need to establish concentration inequalities with faster convergence rates. It is possible to prove faster convergence rates with the Martingale method by deriving Bernstein-style concentration inequalities. However, more refined forms of Bernstein-style concentration inequalities are needed to analyze empirical processes, and those refined forms are referred to as *Talagrand’s concentration inequality* (Talagrand, 1995, 1996b). We state the following version of Talagrand’s inequality by Bousquet (2002).

**Theorem 6.33** (Bousquet, 2002). Consider iid random variables \((Z_1,\ldots,Z_n)\sim\mathcal D^n\). Let \(\zeta\) be a real-valued function of \((Z_1,\ldots,Z_n)\). Moreover, for each \(k\in[n]\), let \(\zeta_k\) be a real-valued function of \((Z_1,\ldots,Z_{k-1},Z_{k+1},\ldots,Z_n)\) so that

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\zeta-\zeta_k]\le \zeta.
$$

Assume that for each \(k\), there exists a function \(\zeta'_k\) of \((Z_1,\ldots,Z_n)\) such that

$$
\zeta'_k\le \zeta-\zeta_k\le M,\qquad \mathbb{E}_{Z_k}\zeta'_k\ge0,\qquad \zeta'_k\le uM.
$$

We have for all \(t\ge0\),

$$
\Pr\left[\zeta\ge\mathbb{E}\zeta+\sqrt{2((1+u)M\,\mathbb{E}\zeta+n\sigma^2)t}+\frac{tM}{3}\right]\le e^{-t},
$$

where \(\sigma^2\ge n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Z_k}(\zeta'_k)^2\).

Theorem 6.33 is a Bernstein-style concentration inequality, which can be compared to the additive Chernoff-style concentration inequality of Theorem 6.16. We can apply Theorem 6.33 to empirical processes, and obtain the following counterpart of Corollary 6.19. A similar (two-sided) uniform convergence result can be found in Bousquet (2002).

**Corollary 6.34.** Consider a real-valued function class \(\mathcal F=\{f:\mathcal Z\to\mathbb R\}\). Let \(\mathcal D\) be a distribution on \(\mathcal Z\). Assume that there exists \(M,\sigma>0\), so that for all \(f\in\mathcal F\),

$$
\sigma^2\ge \mathrm{Var}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}[f(Z)],\qquad
\sup_{z'\in\mathcal Z}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}f(Z)-f(z')\right]\le M.
$$

Let \(\mathcal S_n=\{Z_1,\ldots,Z_n\}\) be \(n\) independent random variables from \(\mathcal D\). Then with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over \(\mathcal S_n\), for all \(f\in\mathcal F\),

$$
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}f(Z)-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(Z_i)
\le \epsilon_n(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)+\sqrt{\frac{(4M\epsilon_n(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)+2\sigma^2)\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}
+\frac{M\ln(1/\delta)}{3n}
$$

$$
\le 2\epsilon_n(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}
+\frac{4M\ln(1/\delta)}{3n},
$$

where \(\epsilon_n(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\) is as in Definition 6.1.

**Proof.** Let

$$
\zeta=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left[n\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}f(Z)-\sum_{i=1}^n f(Z_i)\right],
$$

and

$$
\zeta_k=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left[(n-1)\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}f(Z)-\sum_{i\ne k}f(Z_i)\right].
$$

Assume that \(\zeta_k\) is achieved at \(f_k\), and \(\zeta\) at \(f_0\). Then

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\zeta-\zeta_k]\le\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}f_0(Z)-f_0(Z_k)\right]=\zeta.
$$

Let

$$
\zeta'_k=\left[n\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}f_k(Z)-\sum_{i=1}^n f_k(Z_i)\right]-\zeta_k,
$$

then

$$
\zeta'_k\le\zeta-\zeta_k\le\left[\mathbb{E}_Z f_0(Z)-f_0(Z_k)\right]\le M.
$$

Moreover, since \(Z_k\) is independent of \(f_k\), we have

$$
\mathbb{E}_{Z_k}\zeta'_k=\mathbb{E}_{Z_k}\left[\mathbb{E}_Z f_k(Z)-f_k(Z_k)\right]=0,
$$

$$
\mathbb{E}_{Z_k}(\zeta'_k)^2=\mathbb{E}_{Z_k}\left[\mathbb{E}_Z f_k(Z)-f_k(Z_k)\right]^2\le\sigma^2.
$$

By taking \(u=1\) in Theorem 6.33, we obtain the first desired bound. The second bound is a consequence of

$$
\sqrt{\frac{(4M\epsilon_n(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)+2\sigma^2)\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}
\le \sqrt{\frac{4M\epsilon_n(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}
$$

$$
\le \epsilon_n(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)+\frac{M\ln(1/\delta)}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}.
$$

The first inequality used \(\sqrt{a+b}\le\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}\), and the second inequality used \(\sqrt{4ab}\le a+b\).

To illustrate its consequences, we introduce the following definition of rate function. In general, we expect the uniform convergence complexity \(\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)\) to grow sublinearly in \(r'\) (see examples later), which implies that the rate function \(\bar r\) is well defined (see Figure 6.2).

**Definition 6.35.** Given \(\mathcal D\) and \(\mathcal F\), and considering a localization function \(h:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R\) such that \(b_0=\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}h(f)>-\infty\), define localized function class \(\mathcal F^h(b)=\{f\in\mathcal F: h(f)\le b\}\) for all \(b>b_0\). For any \(\alpha>0\), the rate function with respect to localization \(h\) is defined as

$$
\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)=\sup\left\{r: r\le\inf_{r'>\max(r,\alpha b_0)}\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)\right\}.
$$

We note that the requirement of \(r'>\alpha b_0\) in Definition 6.35 is only to make sure that \(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha)\) is always non-empty, and thus \(\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)\) is well defined.

**Example 6.36.** In Bartlett et al. (2005), the definition of the localization function is \(h(f)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal D}[f(Z)^2]\). The localized function class \(\mathcal F(b,\mathcal D)\) is \(\{f\in\mathcal F: \mathbb{E}[f(Z)^2]\le b\}\), with \(b_0\ge0\).

In the analysis of ERM, it is natural to employ the same local function class as in Bartlett et al. (2005); the more general definition given in Definition 6.35 simplifies some calculations of \(\bar r_n(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\) using the offset uniform convergence complexity, as shown in Proposition 6.40. This leads to a result similar to Example 6.10, which employs offset uniform convergence.

Next we state the following simple property of the rate function.

**Proposition 6.37.** The rate function in Definition 6.35 is always nonnegative.

**Proof.** Note that \(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha)\ne\varnothing\) when \(r'>\max(0,\alpha b_0)\). Since \(\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)\) is always nonnegative, with \(r=0\), we have \(r\le\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)\) when \(r'>\max(r,\alpha b_0)\).

**Proposition 6.38.** For all \(\alpha>0\) and \(b>\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}h(f)\), we have

$$
\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(b),\mathcal D)\le\max\left(\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D),\alpha b\right).
$$

**Proof.** Note that \(\mathcal F^h(b)\) is non-empty. Let \(\hat r=\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\). The definition implies that

$$
\hat r\ge\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(\hat r/\alpha),\mathcal D).
$$

If \(b\le\hat r/\alpha\), then

$$
\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(b),\mathcal D)\le\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(\hat r/\alpha),\mathcal D)\le\hat r
=\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D).
$$

Otherwise, let \(r'=\alpha b>\hat r=\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\). By the definition of \(\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\), we have

$$
\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(b),\mathcal D)=\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)\le r'=\alpha b.
$$

By combining the two situations, we obtain the desired bound. \(\square\)

**Example 6.39.** Let \(h(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[f(Z)^2]\) and assume that \(b_0=\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}h(f)=0\). If

$$
\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(b),\mathcal D)\le\frac{\tilde c_1}{\sqrt n}b^{q/2}
$$

for some \(0<q<1\), then we obtain

$$
\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\le\left(\frac{\alpha\tilde c_1^2}{n}\right)^{1/(2-q)}.
$$

As we will see, the convergence rate of ERM under variance condition is determined by \(\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\), and this leads to a rate of convergence faster than \(O(1/\sqrt n)\).

**Proposition 6.40.** Let \(\mathcal F=\{\phi(w,Z):w\in\Omega\}\). Consider a localization function \(h(w)\) and the corresponding offset function \(h'(w,\mathcal S_n)=0.5\alpha h(w)\). Then

$$
\bar r_n^h(\alpha,\mathcal F,\mathcal D)\le\max\left(2\epsilon_n^{h'}(\mathcal F,\mathcal D),\ \alpha\inf_{w\in\Omega}h(w)\right).
$$

**Proof.** Let \(b_0=\inf_{w\in\Omega}h(w)\). Consider any \(r\ge0\) such that

$$
r\le\inf_{r'>\max(r,\alpha b_0)}\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D).
$$

For any \(\phi(w,\cdot)\in\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha,\mathcal D)\), we know by the definition of localized function class that

$$
h'(w,\mathcal S_n)=0.5\alpha h(w)\le0.5r'.
$$

It follows that

$$
\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)\le\epsilon_n^{h'}(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)+0.5r'\le\epsilon_n^{h'}(\mathcal F,\mathcal D)+0.5r'.
$$

This means that for all \(r'>\max(r,\alpha b_0)\), the condition

$$
r\le\epsilon_n(\mathcal F^h(r'/\alpha),\mathcal D)
$$

holds.
