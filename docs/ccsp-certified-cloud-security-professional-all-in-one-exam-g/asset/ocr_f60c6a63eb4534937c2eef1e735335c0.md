> Many system staff will want to simply disable components of a system that are nonsensical rather than actually removing them completely from a system. Although this will serve to protect a system to a greater degree than if these components were left operational, it also leaves a system more at risk than totally removing them would. If these components are left in place, the possibility exists that they will be inadvertently adverted or made operational at a later date by systems staff or with administrative access to a system. In this scenario, the systems are assumed to not be using these components, additional security measures, such as monitoring, may not be enabled, leaving a system increasingly vulnerable and without oversight of any potential compromises.

Once all nonsensical components have been removed from the host, the components left be locked down and restricted as much as possible to further reduce the number of possible avenues of exploit and vulnerabilities. Role-based access should be employed to ensure that only users with appropriate have access to a system, and once they have access, they are only allowed the minimum level needed to do their jobs. Servers should be configured to only allow secure access methods to ensure network sniffing cannot expose credentials or sensitive information, and network access should be restricted to only those appropriate networks, hosts, or protocols to reach a server. By restricting access at the network level, you can minimize the possible exposure to risks and threats to a specific set of networks or where additional monitoring can be employed, rather than opening broad network access. At no time should administrative or privileged access be directly allowed on a server from an external source, nor should the use of shared credentials or access be allowed where it is not possible to trace back the actual activity to an individual.

Even when a server is properly built, configured, and hardened to minimize risks, efforts are only valid at the specific time in which they are implemented and successfully passing scanning requirements. Operations and software implementations are constantly responding to evolving threats and exploits, and the process of enhancements and updates is continuous. Therefore, a strong patching and oversight program is the ongoing security of a server. Systems staff should be actively and continuously monitoring patches and exploit announcements from vendors to evaluate the risk and impact of each to their servers and the unique configuration circumstances of their implementations. Those patches are particularly pertinent to their servers or the configuration of their servers should be utilized as soon as testing and validation are possible within their environments.
