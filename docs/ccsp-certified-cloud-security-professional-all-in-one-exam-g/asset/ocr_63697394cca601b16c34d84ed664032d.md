The first step in patch management is being aware of the patches available. Most vendors offer notification mechanisms or programs to send out notices to system maintainers or security personnel that a new patch has been released, with information as to what is contained within. This information will help an organization in setting priorities for implementation. If the organization has a well-established patching cycle that is done on a regular basis, many patches will be added to this, without requiring additional efforts. However, in the case of security patches that have already known or verified exploits, patching might require more emergency or ad hoc cycles to get them in place more immediately. With emergency patching, an organization should have a well-documented and established procedure for testing and notification.

Once an organization is aware of patches being available for their systems or software, the next step is to acquire the patches. Depending on the vendor and software implementation, patches can come in a variety of forms. Many times they are downloadable files in various formats that personnel will use to perform the patching. This can be a ZIP file, a proprietary format, or a binary executable. In instances, patching utilities go directly to the vendors' systems and download the patches directly for implementation. However, this is not possible with many security setups, and overall should be avoided if possible, as it will require a system to have outbound network connectivity to the Internet. Systems that are out the control of the organization or cloud provider must ensure the patch file that has been downloaded matches and validates what the vendor has officially provided.

After the patch has been evaluated for implementation and the software or script obtained, the next step is the actual implementation of the patch. This should always be done on a nonproduction environment and thoroughly tested before being introduced with production systems. Depending on the type of it, diligence may not always be possible, but to the extent that it is, should deploy patches across multiple systems. When these types of utilities are used, it is imperative to monitor the successful completion and implementation of patching to ensure that no hosts or appliances fail to install them. With cloud environments, many times patches are done by building new baseline for virtual images or devices, or then reimaging to new baseline with the new patches installed and tested. Either method is fine and dependent on the particular configurations and needs of the system, so long as policies and practices are established and documented.

After patches have been installed, the last (and very vital) step is to validate and verify the patches. Depending on the nature of the patches, this can be done by a variety of ways. Care and attention should always be paid to the documentation out by the software vendor that accompanies the patches. Many times scripts or instructions will be provided for verifying the patches. At the very least, the process for installing the patches should provide success or error responses back to the person or utility implementing them. Once patches have been successfully installed and verified, it is imperative that the hosted software and applications are also tested to ensure they are still performing as expected.
