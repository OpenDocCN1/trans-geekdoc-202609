
# Understanding Business Requirements

Three big concepts are crucial to determining the business requirements for BCDR, whether implemented with a traditional data center model or a cloud hosting model:

- Recovery point objective (RPO): The RPO is defined as the amount of data a company would need to maintain and recover in order to function at a level acceptable to management. This may or may not be a restoration to full operating capacity, depending on what management deems crucial and essential.
- Recovery time objective (RTO): The RTO is a measurement of the amount of time it would take to recover operations in the event of a disaster to the point where management's objectives for BCDR are met.
- Recovery service level (RSL): The RSL measures the percentage of the total, typical production service level that needs to be restored to meet BCDR objectives in the case of a failure.

> EX TIP Be sure to know the difference between these three concepts and to recognize them by their acronyms.

# Understanding Risks

With BCDR plan, there are two sets of risks—those that require the execution of the plan in the first place, and those that are realized as a result of the plan itself.

Many risks could require the execution of the BCDR plan, regardless of the solution the company has opted to take. These risks include the following:

- Natural disasters (earthquakes, hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, and so on)
- Terrorists attacks, acts of war, or purposeful damage
- Equipment failures
- Utility disruptions and failures
- Data center or service provider failures or neglect

Apart from the risks that can lead to the initiation of a BCDR plan, there are also risks associated with the plan that need to be considered and understood:

- **Change in location**: Although cloud services are normally accessed over broad networking, a change in geographic hosting location in a BCDR situation can cause network latency or performance issues. This can impact both the user and customer perspectives when one comes to the system or application, as well as the business's ability to update and maintain data, especially if large data updates or transfers are required. Latency can also implement timing issues between servers and clients, especially with many security and encryption systems that rely heavily on time syncing to ensure validity or timeout processes.
- **Maintaining redundancy**: With a BCDR plan, a second location will need to be maintained to some extent, depending on the model used and the status and design of the failover site. Both sites will need additional staffing and oversight to ensure they are compatible and maintained to the same level in the event of an unforeseen emergency without notice.
- **Failover mechanism**: In a seamless transition to occur between the primary and failover sites, there must be a mechanism in place to facilitate the transfer of services and connectivity to the failover site. This can be done through networking changes, DNS changes, global load balancers, and a variety of other approaches. The mechanism used can involve caching and timeouts that impact the transition period between sites.
- **Bringing services online**: Whenever a BCDR solution is declared, a primary issue or concern is the speed at which services can be brought online and ready at the failover site. With a cloud solution, this typically will be quicker than a traditional failover site because a cloud provider can take advantage of rapid elasticity and self-service models. If the images and data are properly maintained at the failover site, services online can be brought quickly.

# Chapter 4: Cloud Platform and Infrastructure Security

## Define Scope
The first step in formulation is to ensure security concerns are an intrinsic part of the plan, rather than trying to retrofit the plan after it is developed.

## Gather Requirements
Requirements taking into account the RPO and RTO objectives we previously discussed. This determines what needs to be included in the plan as well gives a sense of what types and models are necessary.

## Analyze
This step is where the requirements and scope are combined to form the objectives and roadmap for the actual plan design. This step involves a thorough analysis of the current production hosting location for the system or application, a decomposition of components that need to be replicated for a BCDR situation, and the areas of risk associated with that.

## Assess Risk
In any IT system, regardless of hosting circumstances or providers, risk assessments are an ongoing and continuous process to ensure security compliance and regulatory requirements. Here's a list of the main risks that are assessed:

- Load capacity of the BCDR site Can handle the needed load to run the application or system, and is that capacity readily and easily available? Can the BCDR site handle the level of network bandwidth required for the production services and the user community accessing them?
- Migration of services Can the BCDR site handle the bandwidth and requirements to get the production services mirrored and configured in a timely manner? If there are huge data sets, will incremental copies be performed regularly versus doing to a single large transfer at the time when services are down? How many services are on standby and can be rapidly deployed and expanded when needed?
