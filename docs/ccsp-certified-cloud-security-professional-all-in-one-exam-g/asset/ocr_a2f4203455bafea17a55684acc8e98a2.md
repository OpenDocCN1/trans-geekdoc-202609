# Design and Plan Security Controls

In order to ensure a secure security policy and overall governance, the Cloud Security Professional should concentrate on several different area, as detailed in this section.

# Physical and environmental Protection

Physical and environmental protection relates to any and all physical devices and infrastructure components. While the access and technologies used with a cloud infrastructure offer a unique set of services to customers, underneath it is all a data center model, albeit in cases on a much larger scale. However, because a cloud by definition is a system that is accessible over broad networking, such as the Internet, physical protections must also extend to those systems that are used to access the cloud.

The physical assets in the data center includes servers, cooling units, power distribution units, networking gear, physical racks, and miles of cabling, as well as the actual facilities and the auxiliary systems located on the premises, such as battery backups, power cords, generators, fuel tanks, and the surrounding periphery. Outside the data center property there are still more physical devices and infrastructure that are important to the Cloud Security Professional. These include the power and network conducts that the data center connects to, as well as the endpoints of access for the users and customers, such as mobile devices, workstations, laptops, tablets, and any other client systems.

From a physical perspective, the approach to security is no different than in any other system, with defense in layers being the guiding principle. From the outside of a data center building, you will have typical security measures such as fences, cameras, lights, vehicle access control and barriers, as well as personnel stationed at various locations. Access to the interior of the buildings should be tightly controlled by personnel, doors, key card access, identity provisioning, and other aspects that are similar to how you would gain access to an IT system, including multifactor authentication, in the form of badges, codes, biometrics, and so on. Once, access to the actual floor space where the systems resides should be further controlled and restricted by the use of cages and sectioning, as well as employing different levels of access control and monitoring. In some cases, there may be contractual or regulatory requirements to have different types of systems segmented from others in physical cages, depending on the type of system, the jurisdictions that have authority over it, and the type of data that it stores and consumes.
