# Log Management

Throughout an environment, logs are generated in many places and in different ways. Servers generate system logs and application logs; same with network devices, storage devices, and many various security devices such as IDS and IPS. A very important aspect to consider as a data center is configured and brought in is the logging capabilities of the physical assets. The Cloud Security Professional need a thorough understanding of what logging is enabled by the vendor, as well as what logging capabilities exist beyond the default. In many instances, logging by default will be at a more information level or warning level, rather than capturing all events that are important or relevant to the particular organization or system.

A great deal of exposure and availability in a cloud environment will depend on what service service model is used. This also extends to how responsibility for collecting and managing log data. With an IaaS service model, most log collection and maintenance will be the responsibility of the cloud customer as far as virtual machines and application logs are considered. The SLA between the cloud customer and the cloud provider need to clearly spell what logs are available in an IaaS environment, who has access to them and the responsibility to collect them, who will be responsible for maintaining and archiving them, as well as what retention policy. With a PaaS implementation, the cloud provider will need to collect the operating system logs and possibly logs from the application as well, depending how implemented and what application frameworks are used. Therefore, the SLA will need to clearly define how logs will be collected and to the cloud customer and what degree support is available with such efforts. With a SaaS implementation, all logs will be exposed by the cloud provider via SLA requirements. With many PaaS implementations, logging to some degree is exposed via the application itself to administrators or account managers of the application, but those logs might be limited to a set of user functions or just high-level events. Another detail or more detailed will also need to be part of the SLA, though with SaaS implementation, the cloud provider will be exposing to very little cloud data unless they have APIs already designed to do.

Another key of log management is the retention of log files. The exact duration and type of retention will be dictated by regulatory requirements, which will set the minimum standards, and may not be expanded by organizational policy. Log retention will typically be for a period of time, depending on the type of data of the system stores and the sensitivity level of it. On top of the overall time required, retention policies usually also dictate the type of storage and accessibility of it. For example, the log retention policy may be for one year, where 30 days’ worth of logs will be immediately accessible at all times, and the remainder kept on cheaper and slower storage, or possibly written to tape and stored in long-term archival systems.
