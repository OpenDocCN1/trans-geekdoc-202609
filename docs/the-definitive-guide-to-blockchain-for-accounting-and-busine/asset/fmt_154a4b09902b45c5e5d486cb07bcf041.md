
This digital ledger keeps detailed records of each and every transaction in the order they occurred. Multiple, real-time distributed copies of this ledger make it impossible for any one participant to alter the records, *ex post*. Even though any participant could alter the ledger under their control, and change a past transaction, their ledger would now be out of sync with the ledgers held by other participants, and hence invalid. This is what leads to the key advantage of blockchain – immutability of records. That is, once a transaction is recorded and accepted by a majority of participants, the record of the transaction is permanent and cannot be altered by any one participant or a small group of participants. Thus, the blockchain records and stores all transactions in the data-ledger, and all of these records are accessible to any participant.

The blockchain ledger is immutable and non-amendable. In other words, it is write-only or append-only. Participants are allowed to add new records of transactions but not allowed to modify or alter existing ones. When a transaction has to be reversed, either because it was entered in error or was not executed, a separate entry has to be made. Thus the original transaction details are preserved forever, and so is the subsequent reversal. This form of appending ensures that the record shows that the transaction occurred initially, and was reversed on a later date. Whereas allowing modification and alteration would lead to deletion of the original transaction. It is important to understand the nuance, in a blockchain all participants even at later date would be able to distinguish between events which led to a transaction that was subsequently reversed and those non-events that never led to any transactions being recorded.

To summarize, the key terminology and nomenclature in blockchain may have slightly different meaning or nuance than in its common usage. A ***participant*** or a ***member*** is any individual or organization that shares the same goal or interest with other participants and they collectively form a ***network***. The network is formed and maintained by the members for a certain purpose, the rules and protocol in the network are governed by the members who have a shared goal or interest. Every action of the members, such as joining the network, requests to other members, accepting of another member’s request, exchange of goods or money and leaving the network, are called ***transactions***. Each and every transaction is recorded in a data ***ledger***. The data-ledger is shared real time with all participants, hence multiple identical copies of the data-ledger is preserved. Every new transaction has to be accepted, then recorded in the ledger and the ledger shared with all participants. The difference in meaning and their implications will be discussed in the next section.

## 3.3. ONE WORD, TWO MEANINGS AND OTHER MISCONCEPTIONS

As noted earlier, “transaction” in blockchain lingo is not the same as what we define as transaction in normal accounting and business usage. From a blockchain perspective, every interaction a participant has within the blockchain ecosystem is defined as transactions, only a fraction of those would qualify as a transaction in an accounting context. Knowing the difference between the two will be a useful skill as business processes and dealings increasingly transition to a blockchain domain. While some insinuate that blockchain will replace the accounting profession, it hardly seems so. Quite to the contrary, blockchain might actually make certain accounting skills and knowledge more relevant.[³](#CH003.html#fn3) While, blockchain surely would have effect on some accounting procedures and processes, it will also create the need for new skill-sets to be developed within the context of accounting and business. For example, the skill and knowledge to identify “transactions” from blockchain which ought to be recorded as an accounting transaction will continue to be relevant. This culling of “transactions” has to be both valid and comprehensive, in that all and only those transactions that have a bearing in an accounting context have to be selected, and none others. The skill and ability to do so will require a grasp of pertinent and current accounting standards. For example, ordering of goods from a supplier in a blockchain context is a “transaction,” but it is not recorded as such in accounting.

The data-ledger is a digital record of all transactions in a blockchain. As previously discussed, this ledger is shared between each participant of the networks. Each participant maintains an up to date copy of the ledger. This feature makes it impossible for any one member to alter the records in the ledger stored with them, since they will be unable to alter the records in ledgers held by other participants. Without getting the approval of the other members, the alterations made will not be recognized as being valid. This makes it impossible for any one member to alter their version of data-records, leading to the immutability feature of the blockchain.

In contrast, ledger in an accounting context is a book containing all the accounts in which information from accounting “journal entries” are posted as debits and credits. Again, while using the identical word, the meaning is very different. There is no notion of “debits” or “credits” in the data-ledger, it is merely a compilation of all transaction regardless of whether or not it has an impact on the financial statement. Whereas a ledger in accounting context maintains a running total of account balances. Each accounting transaction is recorded in at least two ledger accounts, using the terminology of “debits” and “credits.” This process will continue to persist even when blockchain is used. In that sense, the data-ledger of a blockchain is not a replacement or a substitute for the accounting ledger. They do not undertake the same function, hence are likely to coexist.

In blockchain terminology, an asset is anything of value that members are willing to exchange in the network. It could be currency, property, cryptocurrency, pictures, records, company shares, etc. This is not consistent with the accounting definition of asset. An asset as defined in accounting has to meet certain criteria defined by FASB or IFRS. Assets, in an accounting context, are resources owned by a business which has the potential of generating future cash flow for the company, and its value can be objectively monetized. For example, a patent or copyright of intellectual property can be termed as an asset in a blockchain, but not for accounting purposes. One such blockchain application is described in a later chapter, wherein the musicians create, share and transact in original music through blockchain. The content that is traded is termed an “asset” in blockchain terminology but will not be termed as an asset for accounting purposes as it does not meet the necessary criteria required for recognition as an asset, that is, objective valuation.

A very common misconception, promulgated mostly by non-accounting computer scientist, is that blockchain is a triple-entry bookkeeping system.[⁴](#CH003.html#fn4) The term does have a *zing,* and of course “triple” is better and more secure than “double,” it easily conveys that blockchain technology is an improvement over current accounting processes. While that may arguably be so, the use of term “triple-entry” to describe the accounting implication for blockchain is incorrect. It is naively assumed that accounting transactions between two parties are mirror images of each other, that is, if one company has a revenue other must have an expense; and if one party records accounts receivable the other party must record an account payable.
