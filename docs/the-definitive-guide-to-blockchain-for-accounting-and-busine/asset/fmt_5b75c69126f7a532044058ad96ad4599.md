
There are significant challenges to current methods of supply chain tracking. Properly knowing the supply chain history requires intense individual effort and communication with suppliers and their suppliers, and immense computational work to accurately identify all relevant elements. Supply chain participants are understandably reluctant to share sensitive data with entities that they may not know or trust, and participants are required to cooperate with what would be to them, unknown entities that they may be reluctant to trust. Responsible supply chain traceability may be seen as an essential business function with respect to the regulatory affairs or shareholder relations aspects of a business, but to other departments, may not be seen as essential to core business and meriting corporate resources. The traditional methods of supply chain audit and analysis depend on a significant paper trail, and paper certification provides neither strong traceability nor freedom from corruption. For these reasons, the present system of mineral supply chain tracking needs a new solution.

In 2014, in response to Dodd-Frank, downstream manufacturing companies took up the monumental task of attempting to track the origins of the materials in their supply chain. They spent over 700 million dollars tracking their products upstream in an attempt to identify the smelters and refiners supplying them. It was an expensive and lengthy process.

## 9.7. BLOCKCHAIN SOLUTION

As we can see the mineral supply chain is full of complex problems that have multifaceted causes. Blockchain, paired with careful system design, provides us with a way to address these problems. Blockchain has four key characteristics that make it suitable to address these increasing regulatory challenges.

The first two characteristics are the tools of our trade. They allow us to create a system that can keep track of the history, or provenance of the materials we are transacting and ensure that record is trustworthy:

**1.** Blockchain can *tokenize* key due diligence data on tracked minerals, submitted by all participating supply chain actors. These tokens are unique digital assets that resist tampering and duplication.

**2.** Blockchain maintains an *immutable* of transactions. This guards against the changing of documents, which is the most common vector for corruption and supply chain risk.

The other two characteristics provide the incentives for every supply chain actor to use our well-designed tools. They are how we can turn our system from an interesting tool for companies who know each other well into a global infrastructure that can address some of the deepest problems the mineral supply chain faces:

**3.** Blockchain can create self-governed *incentive systems*, to make it beneficial for supply chain actors to participate in the system.

**4.** Blockchain allows parties who do not trust each other to *transact without a trusted third party*, eliminating the need to trust a single company with supply chain data.

The success of our mineral blockchain tracking system depends completely on designing the tools and the incentives properly.

### 9.7.1. Recording the History of Supply Chain

Blockchain enables us to create digital assets such as tokens that cannot be duplicated or altered. We leverage this trait to limit the number of tokens available, and this uniqueness allows the representation of real world assets such as minerals or metals. Once we have a token representation of our minerals, we can link due diligence data to them and keep track of the history of who held them, giving us a powerful tool for supply chain security.

Tokens can be created that are either fungible or non-fungible depending on the assets we would like to tokenize. There are many ways we can make use of this digital uniqueness to ensure responsibility in our supply chain, each with strengths and weaknesses depending on the specific problem to be addressed.

Let’s start by looking at fungible tokens. With fungible tokens we only keep track of the quantity we have; every token we purchase can be merged into a larger pile of tokens making them all indistinguishable from each other. Fungible tokens are the most commonly created type of token in the blockchain world. They are easy to handle, like a currency, can be stored in a wallet as a number, and moved around like cash. Metals are also fungible so it seems intuitive that a fungible token would work for tracking responsible materials.

### 9.7.2. Fungible Tokens

Consider a company that wants to produce responsible gold for investment purposes. They purchase all their gold from one large-scale mine that links to one large-scale smelter, they have their production practices audited and show this information on a website or in a database. They process the gold into 1kg bars and put the bars into a vault. For each kilo bar in the vault they create a fungible, digital token on a blockchain which are then sold on the open market. The gold kilo token would contain a link back to the website or database, showing the percentage of responsible gold contained in that token.

There are many advantages to this approach. These are listed as follows:

*   Fungible tokens are easy to manage because we do not need a physical identifier linking the token to the metal. We know all the metal is in a vault, and it all comes from one source, so it doesn’t matter which bar is which.
*   Investors can move into and out of gold positions fluidly with minimal transaction fees.
*   Implementation is straightforward – there are few processes that would have to be changed, and only one company needs to know how to use the blockchain. One company can decide to do this unilaterally without the buy-in from every member of the supply chain.

There are some limitations as well, these are:

*   The tokens are fungible only because we control all the aspects of responsibility off the supply chain and keep the gold from these processes separate from the rest of the market. It works well when we have *one vault* storing all our gold coming from only known sources and audited supply chains. If, however, multiple companies each create their own vault and their own fungible token, these tokens could not be mixed without losing the information about which vaults held our gold. This makes such a system difficult to scale.
*   We need to ensure good data is provided to the blockchain. This example assumes the gold in the vault is responsible and the company is providing some data on it. This is possible when the gold is purchased directly from a mine with rigorous, documented processes in a very short supply chain, but most of the gold supply in the world does not meet these criteria, making this approach impossible to scale.
*   Fungible tokens may represent responsibly sourced metals, but they are still very valuable digital assets that can be transferred at will between wallets without needing a middleman. Ironically, this means the tokens can be used to launder money easily and we will need to build in KYC into such a system to address this.

If we want to have a greater level of detail over which metals come from where, we can use non-fungible tokens that represent metal in our supply chain. Non-fungible tokens can be created to represent specific shipments of metal ores or ingots, effectively acting as a digital twin that can be used to demonstrate who owns it. Each token can contain a link to data that demonstrates the responsibility of the shipment that it is linked to.
