
Who gets to see the data that is attached to our tokens? This is a key question that will likely give many companies pause before joining a blockchain traceability program. Some companies’ business models are based on arbitrage, and supply chain transparency may cause them to lose business. Other companies may have legitimate anti-trust concerns about being in communication with their deeper tier suppliers or cooperating with their competitors for supply chain responsibility. For most companies these concerns will hold much more sway than the economic incentives of the system.

To illustrate, imagine a company making $20m per year trading minerals. It will not matter whether the blockchain system costs them $50,000 or pays them $50,000 if it threatens their core business of $20m per year. They will only join if it means a secured access to the global mineral market of the future, and only if their data is secure.

The sensitivity of supply chain data implies a guiding principle: all data in our traceability system should be under the control of the data owner and nobody else. This principle is known as data *self-sovereignty*. It means the owner of a blockchain token is the only one who has access to see the data about the minerals the token represents. No other company or government actor would be able to access that supply chain data. The token holder would be solely responsible for sharing their responsibility data at their own discretion. By holding to the principle of data self-sovereignty, we can overcome the strongest disincentive to companies becoming blockchain-registered responsible supply chain participants.

### 9.8.3. Immutability of Data
Most corruption happens with money and payments and is hidden by changing documents after the fact. If records of mineral sales, provenance and due diligence are stored permanently and irrevocably along with our mineral traceability tokens, it closes the largest avenues for corruption and the illicit practices that grow from it. No supply chain actor wants to pay bribes to get business done. The promise of unchangeable records acts as a strong positive incentive to join for companies who want to be responsible. In the long run the industry demands for transparency in the mineral supply chain will only increase, and companies that benefit from corruption will be pushed out of the market, due to ill-reputation and their failure to engage in responsible tracking system. Immutable records are better for every actor in the supply chain but are especially useful for helping large-scale mines maintain their social contract to operate in countries with weaker institutional oversight.

In July 2017, Acacia Mines, a subsidiary of Barrick Gold Corporation, was presented with a $190 billion bill for unpaid taxes and fines from the government of Tanzania for operating illegally and under-declaring the value of their gold exports.[^(20)](#CH009.html#fn20) This bill was more than the $162 billion GDP of the country in 2017. Ultimately the situation was resolved with Barrick agreeing to pay $300 million and the government taking a 16% stake in Acacia Mines. Having an immutable record would have resolved this issue before it began.

### 9.8.4. Ensuring Credible Information
Our token traceability system is only as useful as the data that is entered. If tokens are created for minerals with falsified due diligence reports, the credibility of the system will be damaged. If the data in our system is used to give companies access to the world market for their minerals, then companies have a strong incentive to have a positive standing. If mine, smelter, or other supply chain participant with shady practices must choose between cleaning up their practices and lying that they have, they will likely go with whichever is easier. Therefore, to incentivize good data in the blockchain we need to increase the difficulty to fake data.

The first thing we can do is require participating companies to undergo third-party audits and attach a scan of their reports to the non-fungible tokens. This is a good start, but how can we improve it? A third-party compliance audit may be paid for by the company undergoing the audit resulting in a conflict of interest in the final report. It results in a single point of failure in our system.

A more robust solution would be to create a standard set of data to be collected and signed off on by multiple parties at multiple points in the supply chain. Some data, such as shipment amounts and numbers, grade and location might be collected automatically from enterprise management software or internet of things devices in the production facilities. Automation makes it more difficult to fake data increasing trust.

By forming a standard set of data to be collected, we can then score companies based on the practices they employ, like a social reputation score. Companies who know their suppliers, tag and track their shipments, have good relationships to civil society, are transparent about worker wages, and send and receive all payments electronically might get a higher social reputation score than companies with fewer such practices. If customers prefer suppliers with a higher social reputation score, it could act as an incentive for companies to improve their practices and invest in data collection and tracking.

### 9.8.5. Incentivizing Incident Reporting
Once companies are part of our system, we can have a degree of confidence in their practices from the fact that they are willing to participate in a transparency program. Nevertheless, transparency programs and responsibility policies might not mitigate all supply chain risks. Even a mine–transport–trader–smelter group who implements a robust and blockchain-backed responsibility program could experience problematic incidents. For example:
*   A truck might be robbed, and illegal taxes collected by a conflict group.
*   Workers in a more remote mine site may bring children to work in the mine and hide them during scheduled inspections.
*   Workers may dispose of hazardous waste improperly.

To address and reduce incidents of illicit behavior, we first need these incidents reported and these reports attached to our tokens. We need to identify a group that is in a position to witness the incident, identify how to make reporting easy, identify incentives that would encourage them to make the report, and then identify how to prevent false reports.

Incidents of child labor are difficult to identify. A mine may put children in school on the day of inspection and then revert when the inspectors leave. First let’s identify a group that can witness these incidents. We know incidents like this happen because inspectors are not the only group of workers who visit mine sites. Often mining engineers, geologists or other workers will visit these sites and see incidents that they cannot act on. This is our identified group that is in a position to witness the incidents. Now, how can we make reporting easy for this group? Mining engineers and geologists are often well-paid and have access to technology. Perhaps a simple reporting app on a smart phone would suffice.

Next, how can we then incentivize them to make a report? Engineers and geologists have a code of ethics. Knowing that a report would make a difference might be enough to encourage them to participate. Perhaps we could take funds and give them to an NGO that works to end child labor with each report. On the other side they might be concerned for their safety as a whistleblower, so having a certain level of anonymity might also be important to encourage participation. Perhaps only the NGO would have access to the identity of who made the report.
