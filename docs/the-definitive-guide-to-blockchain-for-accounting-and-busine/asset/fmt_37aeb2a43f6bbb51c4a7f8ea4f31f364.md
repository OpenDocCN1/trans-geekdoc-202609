
*countries all over the world are still in the early stages of determining how and whether to regulate crypto. Uncertainty about what the rules in any particular country are makes a determination of which country’s rules apply even more difficult.[³](#CH010.html#fn3)*

Absent a solitary worldwide regulatory framework, which is both unlikely and unwise, innovators need to plan for potential frictions between jurisdictions.

Digital asset regulatory frameworks need to be properly aligned with investor expectations, as the geographic locations of ecosystem participants often differ. Hester Peirce acknowledged this issue in Singapore, by stating

*If the investors, the platforms on which they are transacting, and the companies in which they are investing are all operating in one country, the investor knows which country’s rules apply, but things get more complicated when multiple jurisdictions are involved.*

Taking note of these challenges, digital securities entrepreneurs believe their products and services can offer a viable solution to the regulatory quagmire that is likely to accelerate as Web 3.0 approaches.

## 10.5. DIGITAL SECURITIES – MIGRATING TO WEB 3.0

The concept of a more trustworthy, equitable and open web was envisioned long ago, however, proper implementation required major advancements across computing disciplines over many decades. Luckily, recent breakthroughs in artificial intelligence, distributed systems and cloud computing, among other computing fields, have brought us closer to Web 3.0. The impending transformation of the way value is created, stored, transferred and redefined cannot be overstated. However, one must first understand the principles of the next web to truly grasp the coming paradigm shift.

The foundation of Web 3.0 is a “Semantic Web,” where internet data is linked together in a machine-readable format. According to the World Wide Web Consortium, a web of linked data would enable computers to do more useful work and to develop systems that can support trusted interactions over the network.[⁴](#CH010.html#fn4) As such, internet data would be sharable and reusable across application, venture and communal boundaries. Another tenet of Web 3.0 is the creation of a Stateful Internet or

*an internet that can handle data, and rules for changing the state of that data, in the way the internet handles messaging today – more or less decentralized – and that allows private stateful networks to interoperate with each other and the public network through standard protocols.[⁵](#CH010.html#fn5)*

Blockchain technology provides a medium for network participants to hold and transfer state over the internet, without the assistance of centralized service providers. A stateful internet and semantic web would give rise to a new digital economy, one that operates across multisided marketplaces online. DLT systems, smart contracts and security tokens will enable compliant interoperability between our current financial system and the digital value systems of tomorrow.

Security tokens or digital securities are designed to represent ownership in assets and/or entities. Digital securities offer the same advantages as conventional ERC20 tokens in terms of simplicity, efficiency and liquidity, but they also have the added benefit of programatically embedded regulatory compliance. By ensuring proper adherence to identity, jurisdiction, and asset category restrictions, digital securities have an opportunity to become a globally standardized mechanism by which regulated finance operates on Web 3.0.

Digital securities offer robust supervisory controls, making them an ideal vehicle for managing a multitude of regulatory jurisdictions. Governing laws and regulations are embedded in the protocol layer, providing adaptability and flexibility. A coded framework of preventive and detective controls can mitigate impacts of risk, leading to decreased compliance costs. As coded frameworks reduce compliance expenses, they will help streamline the facilitation of cross-border transactions between DLT systems. Smart contracts can certify strict adherence to all applicable regulations while executing any corporate action, such as voting, dividends, share-buy-backs and trading. The resulting decrease in governance-related frictions can allow for the creation of trustless businesses.

Through a combination of web platforms, DLT systems, smart contracts and blockchain-based assets, autonomous economic models and enterprise structures are finally made possible. Investors and market participants can find counterparties, discover prices, and trade digital instruments that represent ownership and economic rights. Organizational governance and decision-making can be formalized, automated and imposed using software.

By serving as a hybrid of investment instruments, agreements, contracts, and vehicles, digital securities can ensure the proper migration of today’s financial laws and regulations to the new digital economy. As such, security token usage is projected to grow significantly over the coming decades.

## 10.6. PROJECTED GROWTH OF SECURITY TOKENS

When classifying digital securities, we find it best to divide security tokens into two main categories, traditional security tokens and asset-backed security tokens (ABSTs). Traditional security tokens are digital assets issued during a security token offering (STO), a private funding method we examined in the Curzio Research chapter. These blockchain-based assets exist independently of the physical world, meaning they live and trade “on-chain.” By law, traditional security tokens must abide by the rules and regulations of each issuer, investor and subsequent holder.

As of 2019, most of the economic value in the digital securities industry has come from traditional security tokens used for startup financing. Chain Partners, a South Korean blockchain research and advisory firm, estimates that $5.1 billion of the current $102 billion total addressable startup fundraising market can be captured by security tokens in the future ([Fig. 10.1](#CH010.html#fig1)).

ABSTs are digital assets created through the tokenization of pre-existing financial/real assets; the tokens are “on-chain” representations of assets that exist “off-ledger.” ABSTs are backed by and derive value from the underlying real-world assets, drawing conceptual parallels to asset-backed securities (ABS) from legacy finance.

ABSs are created through the powerful and innovative process of securitization. As defined by Columbia University Professor Dr Suresh Sundaresan, securitization is

*a framework in which some illiquid assets of a corporation or a financial institution are transformed into a package of securities backed by these assets, through careful packaging, credit enhancements, liquidity enhancements, and structuring. (Sundaresan, 1997)*

The flexibility of securitization has revolutionized the investable world, offering opportunities to both issuers and investors.
