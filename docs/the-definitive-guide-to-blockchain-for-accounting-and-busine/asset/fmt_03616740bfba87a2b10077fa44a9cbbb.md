
*   *Australian Modern Slavery Act (2018)*[^(14)](#CH009.html#fn14) Australia’s Modern Slavery Act, like the UK’s requires businesses and other organizations (including government departments) above a certain size (consolidated revenue of A$100 million) to report annually on the risks of modern slavery in their operations and supply chains, and the action they have taken to assess and address those risks, and the effectiveness of their response.

*   *French Corporate Duty of Vigilance Law (2017)*[^(15)](#CH009.html#fn15) Companies subject to this law, their subsidiaries, and companies under their control, must establish mechanisms to prevent human rights violations and environmental impacts throughout their chain of production. These mechanisms must be reported each year as part of a “vigilance plan.” The vigilance plan should identify and analyze the risks of human rights violation or environmental harms, identify actions to mitigate or prevent the most serious violations, and discuss the effectiveness of those actions.

The evolution of global obligation to analyze, action, measure effectiveness and report on measures taken to address the risk of modern slavery in the supply chain have somewhat paralleled the activity suggested or required by conflict minerals regulation. In both cases, reporting requirements or guidelines imply corporate understanding of the components of their supply chain, and thus imply a transparency to the participants in the supply chain that to this date are not present. Regulations noted above, even the most current ones, are strong in guidance but weak in consequence. The Independent Review of the Modern Slavery Act Section 54 concerning transparency in supply chains notes:

*\[…\] there is a general agreement between businesses and civil society that a lack of enforcement and penalties, as well as confusion surrounding reporting obligations, are core reasons for poor-quality statements and the estimated lack of compliance from over a third of eligible firms.[^(16)](#CH009.html#fn16)*

### 9.4.3. Ramifications of Changing Political Dynamics

In 2017, the US House approved an amendment to a spending bill and that amendment cut off funding to “implement, administer, or enforce” the Dodd-Frank Conflict Minerals Rule. What had commenced as a recognition by Congress of the significant problem of illegal activity in the global provision of minerals was virtually rescinded in less than a decade.

However, industry recognizes the significance of the intent of the regulations. Some large companies voluntarily comply with the previous legislation, having put the processes and controls in place. For example, one large multinational food company, Nestlé, implemented their own Nestlé Responsible Sourcing Standard[^(17)](#CH009.html#fn17) in July 2018. The standard defines expected duties, practices and reporting by third-party suppliers and establishes milestones for improvement. Another global technology company, Intel, writes in 2019,

*While we do not procure direct, our suppliers acquire and use minerals from multiple sources worldwide. Intel is committed to the responsible sourcing of minerals – sourcing done in an ethical and sustainable manner that safeguards the human rights of everyone in our global supply chain …. Prompted by growing concerns about child labor and additional human rights violations beyond the scope of Dodd-Frank, Intel has evolved its responsible minerals program and related due diligence practices to address minerals originating from CAHRAs.[^(18)](#CH009.html#fn18)*

## 9.5. MONEY LAUNDERING THROUGH MINERAL SOURCING

Money laundering is the act of disguising the source or true nature of money obtained through illegal means.[^(19)](#CH009.html#fn19) The money to be “laundered” may come from any number of illegal activities, including illegal arms sales, smuggling, drug trafficking, prostitution, embezzlement, insider trading, bribery, computer fraud schemes or, in the context of this discussion, illegal mining. Money obtained in these ways is considered “dirty,” as it would appear suspicious if large quantities of money simply appeared in an individual’s bank account. This money is “cleaned,” by passing through several “legitimate” businesses before being deposited, thus obscuring its true origin. With the origin obscured, the “cleaned” money will appear to have been derived from legal activities, with the result that financial institutions handle it without suspicion.

There is considerable incentive for illegal activity in gold: gold is fungible (one sample of gold appears identical to any other sample of gold); gold is of high value per mass making it relatively easy to smuggle; and gold is universally traded and exchanged. A recent example of the extent of the use of gold to launder money or gold laundering appeared in the Americas in 2016. Three ex-employees of an American metal refining company along with accomplices from several South American countries purchased rough gold from illegal gold mines owned by drug traffickers in Peru. The gold was smelted into doré bars which were then transported via Ecuador, Bolivia, Colombia and other Latin American countries by air to a refinery in the United States. The refined gold of course had the same composition as gold refined from any other source, save it was illegal gold mined at the expense of forced labor and possibly child labor from illegal drug cartel-owned mines hidden in the Amazon forest. These traders succeeded in laundering $3.6 billion worth of gold between 2013 and 2016 before being arrested.

Traditional sourcing methods are hard-pressed to identify the presence or absence of illegal gold in a refined bar. New methods are needed to assure to identify the “responsibly obtained” minerals and to distinguish them from those obtained illicitly, under duress, or as a component of illegal financial activity.

## 9.6. TRACEABILITY REQUIREMENTS

The OECD Due Diligence Guidance recommends that companies generate several types of information, the specificities of which depending upon the placement of the source along the processes in the mineral supply chain. This data should be gathered for locations of mineral origin as well as transit. Specifically, the guidance recommends obtaining data for minerals:

*   originating from or transported via a conflict-affected or high-risk area;
*   originating from a country having limited known reserves; or
*   originating from a country through which CAHRA minerals are known to transit.

Supplier data should be gathered for companies having shareholder or other interests in companies that supply or have supplied minerals from or operate in a CAHRA origin or transit country.

Trader data should be gathered, recording all payments given for export, import and re-export, as well as all taxes and payments made to government and private security officials. This data is not the conventional data required in a simple supplier–client business exchange, and gathering this data calls for new innovation to assure confidentiality while maintaining transparency.
