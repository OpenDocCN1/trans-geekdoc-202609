
A component manufacturer will know from which of hundreds of metal traders they sourced their minerals from, but is unlikely (for business confidentiality reasons among others) to have any idea which of tens of thousands of providers of local mineral transport were used to get the mineral to which trading houses for amassing and transporting then to which smelters for processing. These logistical challenges are indeed complex.

According to Dynda A. Thomas, partner with Squire Patton Boggs,[²](#CH009.html#fn2)

*There are roughly 400 smelters and refiners of the processed conflict minerals world-wide. Because of that limited number of processors, industry is able to focus directly on requiring them to comply with recognized due diligence standards for conflict minerals sourced from the Democratic Republic of Congo and adjoining countries. So, most companies have not undertaken extraordinary efforts to determine the particular smelters and refiners of the processed conflict minerals in their products. Instead most have opted to identify all the possible smelters and refiners in all their supply chains.*

## 9.4. EVOLVING REGULATION ON TRACEABILITY OF MINERALS

The individual sectors of the mining supply chain have historically been regulated by national governments. Mines have been regulated by mining rules, smelters by industry-specific health, safety and environment laws, etc. Until recently the mining supply chain has not been the focus of international regulation on process and consequence. The current decade is witnessing the implementation of regulation that directly impacts the mineral supply chain. This activity has been driven by rising government and consumer awareness of unethical practices that are being used in the provision of mineral resources.

### 9.4.1. Conflict Minerals Regulations

Businesses involved in the mineral supply chain in 2019 need to know their suppliers. For certain minerals, namely tungsten, tin, tantalum and gold (the 3TG minerals) they need to know their suppliers specifically because they are regulated to do so. These minerals are mined in some countries using child labor and forced labor. The proceeds of this mining frequently support illegal activities and/or military insurgents, and the initial conflict mineral regulations were meant to impact the purchase of such illegal minerals and reduce human rights abuse. Reporting obligations for enterprises in the mineral supply chain have evolved in seven years since the first Act.

*   *The Dodd-Frank Act (2012)*[³](#CH009.html#fn3) was the first regulation to apply the concept of corporate supply chain responsibility to the mineral supply chain for 3TG minerals. The Act requested documentation however did not prescribe penalties for non-compliance.
*   *The OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Supply Chains*[⁴](#CH009.html#fn4) of Minerals from Conflict-Affected and High-Risk Areas (CAHRAs) issued in 2011 and amended in 2012 recommended measures to integrate due diligence into corporate management systems.
*   *The Chinese GSRM (2014)* expressed guidance in social responsibility and sustainable development for Chinese companies operating abroad.
*   *The Chinese Due Diligence Guidelines for Responsible Mineral Supply Chains (2015)*[⁵](#CH009.html#fn5) provided guidance for Chinese companies to identify, prevent and mitigate their risks of contributing to conflict, serious human rights abuses and risks of serious misconduct.
*   *The third edition OECD Due Diligence Guidance (2016)*[⁶](#CH009.html#fn6) articulated recommendations on supply chain due diligence and described a duty to report.
*   *The EU Conflict Minerals Regulation (2017)*[⁷](#CH009.html#fn7) goes a step further by addressing the concept of corporate social liability and the risk of harm. Corporations are now seen to have a social liability within their sphere of influence for the social, ecological and economic consequences of the company’s activities. Reporting needs to be transparent to enable others to monitor and verify how a company is meeting its duties and trying to mitigate problems. The EU Conflict Minerals Regulation does envisage future consequences for non-compliance. The bar has been raised for reporting in the mineral supply chain.
*   *The Mosi-oa-Tunya Declaration*[⁸](#CH009.html#fn8) was adopted on September 13, 2018 at the International Conference on Artisanal and Small-scale Mining and Quarrying (ASM18) in Livingstone, Zambia. This declaration called on government and international private sector to ensure that production and sourcing practices do not contribute to adverse human rights or to conflict and its financing.
*   *EU Non-binding Guidelines for identifying CAHRAs*[⁹](#CH009.html#fn9) Commission Recommendation (EU) 2018/1149 was published on August 10, 2018. These guidelines will aid companies understand the definition of CAHRAs as described in the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation. In addition to providing suggested open source resources for companies to use in identifying CAHRAs the guidelines provide a series of “red flag” topics. Red flags are situations that “give rise to a reasonable suspicion that the minerals may contribute to conflict or serious abuses associated with their extraction, transport or trade.”

The overall objective of these regulations has been to initiate the establishment of secure, transparent and verifiable supply chains of minerals and to ensure responsible practices, while not contributing to armed conflict and associated human rights abuse.

### 9.4.2. Modern Slavery and Human Rights Regulations

In the past seven years, two international organizations, three governments and one US state have introduced regulations or directives concerning the assessment of modern slavery and human rights abuse in the workforce. In their protocols, industry is required to perform risk assessment of their direct business as well as that of their suppliers, and to report on those assessments annually.

*   *The California Transparency in Supply Chains Act*[^(10)](#CH009.html#fn10) went into effect in 2012. It requires medium and larger manufacturing and retail businesses generating global revenue exceeding $100 million to disclose their initiatives to remove human trafficking and slavery from their direct supply chains. The intent of the law is to provide consumers with information on which companies responsibly manage their supply chains.
*   *The EU Directive on Non-Financial Disclosures (2014)*[^(11)](#CH009.html#fn11) are non-compulsory guidelines intended to help companies disclose information on various non-financial aspects of their operations in their annual reports. Companies have flexibility in what they report, on subjects including policies, risks and due diligence implemented and on issues such as anti-corruption and bribery issues, environmental, social and employee matters.
*   *The United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs)*[^(12)](#CH009.html#fn12) were endorsed by the UN Human Rights Council in June 2011. They provided the first global standard for addressing the risk of adverse impacts on human rights linked to business activity, The UNGPs identify three fundamentals: the state duty to protect human rights; the corporate responsibility to respect human rights; and access to remedy for victims of business-related abuses
*   *The UK Modern Slavery Act (2015)*[^(13)](#CH009.html#fn13) makes companies accountable for slavery and labor abuses occurring along their whole chain of operations. Any business – or part of a business – that has a global turnover of £36m or more and supplies goods or services in the UK must produce and publish an annual slavery and trafficking statement. The statement must set out what steps the organization has taken to ensure there is no slavery in any part of its business, including its supply chains.
