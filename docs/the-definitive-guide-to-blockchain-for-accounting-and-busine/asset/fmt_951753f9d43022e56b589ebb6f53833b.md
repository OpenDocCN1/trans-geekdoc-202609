## 11.2. CRYPTOTAX BUSINESS MODEL

As the popularity of cryptoassets and its widespread use increases worldwide, regulators and taxing bodies all over the world are developing and issuing regulatory guidelines on taxation laws. 2019 was a milestone year for cryptotaxation, as regulators around the world adjusted their tax policies and issued updated guidance on the tax treatment of cryptoassets. The complexity and variation in these guidelines are creating new business opportunities for accountants and tax experts. While the United States and the United Kingdom recently issued more thorough guidelines in late 2019, countries like South Korea are in the process of developing the regulations and intend to release those in 2020.

In 2014, the Internal Revenue Services (IRS) in the United States was one of the first regulatory bodies to publish a comprehensive guidance on tax treatment of cryptocurrencies.[¹](#CH011.html#fn1) Early on, the IRS recognized the dual purpose of cryptocurrencies – to pay for goods or to be held as investment. Though these types of currencies operate as “real money” they do not have legal tender status in any jurisdiction. Hence for tax purposes, cryptocurrency was treated as property and generally the tax laws governing the acquisition and disposal of property were applicable to cryptocurrencies. The 2019 tax form for individuals in the United States (Form 1040) includes a direct question on cryptoassets (see [Fig. 11.1](#CH011.html#fig1)). New IRS guidance issued in October 2019, includes tax treatment on “hard forks” and “airdrops.” They illustrate the complexity of the treatment through hypothetical examples.

In early 2020 a blockchain tech firm, Lukka, unveiled a “do-it-yourself” tax tool to help individuals and businesses calculate their tax obligation to the IRS. The tool, developed in collaboration with [CPA.com](http://CPA.com), was released ahead of the tax filing season and in the wake of the recent IRS guideline to help taxpayers better understand the underlying tax laws on cryptoinvestments.

Likewise, in December 2019, the UK also released its cryptotax guideline[²](#CH011.html#fn2) on cryptoexchanges and cryptomining activities among others. The guideline maintains that cryptocurrencies, like Bitcoin, will not be considered money but will be categorized as commodity, but leaves open the possibility of reconsideration as the industry evolves, noting:

*The cryptoassets sector is fast-moving and developing all the time. The terminology, types of coins, tokens and transactions can vary. The tax treatment of cryptoassets continues to develop due to the evolving nature of the underlying technology and the areas in which cryptoassets are used. As such, HMRC will look at the facts of each case and apply the relevant tax provisions according to what has actually taken place (rather than by reference to terminology).[³](#CH011.html#fn3)*

There are many types of tax nuances based on whether the crypto was mined or acquired in an exchange. Additionally, while sometimes these are taxed as income, there are situations when only capital gains are taxed. Additionally, the taxable value of the cryptocurrency varies depending on the situation. The various activities of obtaining cryptoassets are identified as:

*   Buying and selling of tokens.
*   Exchanging tokens for other assets.
*   Mining.
*   Providing goods or services in return for cryptoassets.

Additionally, the tax treatment of these activities depends on the party involved and the purpose of the transaction, and is likely to incur one or more of the following taxes:

*   Capital gains tax.
*   Corporation tax.
*   Income tax.
*   National insurance contributions.
*   Stamp taxes.
*   Value Added Tax, or VAT.

The complexities and various possibilities with significant economic consequences for tax payers make ancillary services of cryptotax profitable and growing. Additionally, the variation in taxing regulations across jurisdictions would lead to tax advisory services for better tax management for high net worth individuals by exploiting these differences. Moreover, corporations might use these vehicles to create “virtual” tax havens. Thus, the role of accountants and tax experts are likely to grow, and not diminish, as a consequence.
