
Finally, how can we prevent false reports? We could try collecting some automated metadata such as date and time of a visit, or we could reduce the impact of individual reports so that a single report won’t be as damaging to a company. Perhaps the reports don’t automatically get assigned to shipments from the mine in question until the NGO is able to confirm them.

An implementation of the above may proceed as follows. We need to start with money. We identify a company sending miners and geologists to mine sites. These mine sites are all tracked on our token-based traceability system we outlined above. The company locks $20,000 in a smart contract. Once the money is locked in, the company’s engineers and geologists are able to use a simple reporting app to make a report whenever they visit a production site. Limiting reporting to people appointed by the company contributing the money is one way to reduce the incidence of false reports.

The report should be very simple, either stating the worker saw an incident of child labor, or that they did not. Either way, submitting the report transfers $10 from the smart contract to a non-profit tasked with following up on these incidents to clean up the supply chain.

The company locking in the money, as well as the engineers and geologists will be able to see an aggregation of how many reports have been made at that mine site, with anonymous data. After a certain number of incidents are reported, the NGO makes a surprise visit to the mine to verify the reports. If child labor is seen, the NGO adds a red flag on the blockchain to all shipments coming from the site.

With our above proposal you may have noticed a heavy use of uncertain words such as “perhaps” and “try.” Incident reporting is not a solved problem. Incidents happen because of complex dynamics with a lot of moving parts, including the individual motivations of workers, local culture and attitudes which may vary from region to region, economic stability in the region, and people in the position to report incidents being afraid of repercussions for doing so. Implementing a blockchain-powered system to address incident reporting may have a mix of positive outcomes and unintended consequences that are very difficult to predict and at this point we should expect solutions to be experimental.

## 9.9. CONCLUSION

With any industry disruption there will be winners and losers. The danger is that large established international mining companies and manufacturing brands win at the expense of poorer miners and local cottage industry. This does not have to be the case.

By designing the system with an equal playing field in mind, we have the potential to make it so that the winners are everyone who wants to participate in a responsible mineral supply chain, and the losers are armed groups sourcing using illicit practices.

We are seeing a significant shift in public awareness and governmental oversight of the mining supply chain, and with this shift has come the need for a safer, more transparent, accurate and detailed, reliable and cost-effective means to assure provenance. Blockchain technology can and will provide that solution.
