
It is unreasonable to expect every mine to adopt a blockchain traceability system overnight. How can we deal with smelters using material from a combination of blockchain-tracked and non-blockchain sources? In this case, we can create non-fungible tokens at the smelter level, linked to due diligence data that the smelter has performed on their mines. These tokens would not give the same level of visibility as the tokens generated at the mine but would allow customers the choice of what to purchase.

In [Fig. 9.1](#CH009.html#fig1), we can see a smelter that has purchased 10 tons of material but only nine from blockchain-tracked mines. After processing, their customer wishes to purchase nine tons of blockchain-tracked metal. The smelter then sends nine tons of metal that has been mixed together from both sources, however by including the nine tons of blockchain-tracked certificates, the customer is able to know their money went to the blockchain-registered sources.

### 9.7.6. Greenwashing
It is important that whether all a smelter’s input mines are using blockchain or not, that they are all responsible. We do not want blockchain-based mineral traceability to be a vector for greenwashing. It is not enough for a smelter to purchase 50% from responsible blockchain-registered sources, and the other 50% from known conflict sources, selling their blockchain traceability tokens to downstream customers who care about traceability, and anonymous material to everyone else. This could provide a false sense of confidence to downstream companies that they are not the ones contributing to illicit activities, reduce pressure on negative actors in the supply chain, and ultimately make on-the-ground impacts worse than not using blockchain at all.

To avoid this, it is important that the smelters continue to perform their due diligence on their input mines as the OECD guidelines require and encourage more and more mines to register on the blockchain over time. Blockchain tokens should be created at the smelter level with the associated due diligence the smelter has done to account for any material from sources not already blockchain-enabled.

In the example above, the smelter purchased 10 tons of material, nine of which came with non-fungible tokens that allow us to trace all the way back to the mine of origin. For the one ton outstanding, a token should be created at the smelter level, linked to the due diligence the smelter has performed. Customers purchasing that material would be aware the token has not come from the mine of origin but would still have some level of trust and information on the material, and leverage for pushing the mines to adopt traceability going forward.

## 9.8. INCENTIVIZING PARTICIPATION IN BLOCKCHAIN
The best-designed mineral traceability system only tracks minerals if companies use it, and they will only use it if they see how they benefit. If the benefits outweigh the costs, companies will join and continue to participate. If not, the system becomes a hypothetical proof of concept.

One of the core features of blockchain is the ability to create systems and exchange value based on a shared set of rules. These rules are enforced by computers running the blockchain system, so no single user has power to change them. By setting these rules properly we can make sure every user in our traceability system sees a benefit from participating.

### 9.8.1. Incentives for Companies
The more participants in a system, the harder it is to set rules that incentivize everyone. Rule-setting in a supply chain system can get very complex, but we can start with two simple rules:

**1.** If a participant provides value to the system, they get adequate compensation or some form of value for participating.

**2.** If a participant derives value out of the system, they must pay for it, or create value to the system.

If a company provides data to the blockchain, that is valuable. They should be rewarded in some way, such as money, increased reputation or better access to customers. If a company is using the blockchain for compliance they should pay for it in some way, such as by paying money, or setting up computers to help run the blockchain system.

We’ve already seen a slate of different actors in the supply chain. Let’s return to our earlier example of the gold supply chain to see possible benefits each participant could receive from using a blockchain traceability system, and of examples rules we might include to support that.

Governments in regions where gold is located may wish to control the extraction and transaction of gold within their borders, to prevent smuggling, funding of conflict groups, tax evasion, and human rights abuses. Governments may wish to require individuals and companies to sell through a government-endorsed channel that is registered with blockchain tracking. To encourage governments to adopt our system we can require country-specific licenses to transact with gold on our system as well as automated tax collection.

![image](images/C09-UFIG14.jpg)

Mines provide due diligence data to access the world market. Large-Scale Mining operations (LSM) undergo expensive audits and collect due diligence data to assure market players to purchase from them, and mines would like the cost of providing this data reduced. Artisanal Miners (ASM) generally cannot provide this data and would benefit from having legal channels through which to sell. This would provide this data for them that would be enforced by local government. In addition to the reputational incentive, we may also provide ASM with benefits such as better prices, money to build up infrastructure in their community, and digital payments to make compliance easier and more beneficial than those obtained through illegal sales.

![image](images/C09-UFIG15.jpg)

Transporters often have many documents they must produce to legally ship material. These documents might take days to obtain and keep sorted. By associating these documents with our shipment tokens, we provide an efficiency benefit.

Security is also a large concern with transporters, with high value shipments at risk of being robbed. We might include rules that delay recording data about transport on the token until after shipment arrives.

![image](images/C09-UFIG16.jpg)

Traders, smelters and manufacturers all have their own compliance needs as well as customers asking for supply chain information, and a need to access the world market. They all provide some value to the system whenever they trade tokens or upload relevant data, and they all benefit from receiving compliance information from the system. The balance of how much they benefit versus how much they contribute is still an open question that will likely be answered over time on a case-by-case basis.

![image](images/C09-UFIG17.jpg)

Finished product manufacturers often are the brand names who benefit the most from the supply chain transparency provided by the system. These actors would have detailed supply chain information and in return they should pay the most for it.

![image](images/C09-UFIG18.jpg)

Recyclers are the end point in the supply chain; however, they are also a point where gold starts over, feeding material into the smelters. Recyclers will provide data to our blockchain system showing the information they have on where their inputs came from, and as such should have a benefit to providing this data, in the form of a better reputation, or reduced costs of compliance.
