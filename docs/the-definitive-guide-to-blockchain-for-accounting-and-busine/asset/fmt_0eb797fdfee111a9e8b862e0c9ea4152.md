
New-age businesses have been started that use digital assets as collaterals for loans denominated in US $s. This is beneficial for individuals who have most of their personal wealth invested in digital currency. It is also applicable to international small and medium enterprises (SMEs) who may not be able to get traditional loans in US $s, since large banks mostly deal with larger foreign corporations.

The cryptolending organization grants short-term cash loans, denominated in US $s, while holding on to many forms of cryptocurrency (such as Bitcoin and Litecoin) as security toward repayment. Typically, the interest rate is about 8%. Also, to safeguard against the volatility of cryptoassets, typical loan to value ratio is about 35–50%. Moreover, when the loan to value ratio exceeds 70%, the borrower is required to transfer additional collateral, or payoff part of the loan to maintain the loan to value ratio under 70%.

At the origination of the loan, the lender requires the borrower to transfer digital assets to a secured wallet maintained with a third party. The custodian of the collateralized asset generates a unique wallet address for each borrower. The borrowers transfer their cryptoasset to their respective wallet through the blockchain. The borrowers have to be assured that the digital assets they are transferring will be secured and returned to them once the loan is paid off. The third-party securer of the digitized asset, would move the digitized asset offline into a vaulted storage system, known as “Cold Storage.” Digitized assets stored in a “hot wallet” are available for transactions, however, those stored in “cold storage” are not. As an independent custodian of digital assets, such organizations have to maintain sophisticated internal control system to ensure safe keeping of the digital assets in their custody. They do so primarily through authorization and segregation of duties. Access to “cold storage” would require multifactor authentication and “tiered access controls” restrict access to employees. Collateral for each loan is stored separately, in different “cold storage,” access to each protected by a unique private key. Moreover, the hardware is geographically dispersed as a precaution against physical threat, following best practices of disaster recovery protocol.

After the loan is initiated and the collateral transferred to the “cold storage,” the lender continues to monitor real-time value of the collateral. An automated alert, or a margin call, is sent to the borrower should the loan to value ratio were to exceed 0.7. On the receipt of the margin call, the borrower is asked to transfer additional digital assets to their cold storage wallet to restore the loan to value ratio. Upon receipt of a margin call, the borrower could potentially pay off all or part of the loan. In instances when the borrower does not respond to the margin call, the lender liquidates the collateral to fulfill the borrower’s obligation and transfers the remaining collateral back to the borrower.

In early 2018, when the value of digital currency was rapidly declining, risk management protocols for the lenders were triggered. As the loan to value ratio exceeded 0.7, email warnings were dispatched. The borrowers were notified of the impending margin call and asked to transfer additional collateral or payoff the loan. In the experience of one such lending company, most customers chose to add additional collateral to rebalance their loan to value ratio. However, some customers, anxious of the market turbulence, chose to pay off their loan early.

The rapid development of collateralizing cryptoassets has been facilitated through the blockchain technology. Digitized assets can easily be transferred to the lender and the entire loan transaction could occur in absence of a physical meeting between the borrower and the lender. This allows the lender to efficiently reach markets which otherwise would be prohibitively costly. Furthermore, it allows the cryptoborrowers the flexibility to locate the cheapest source of capital. The transfer, storage, regular monitoring and eventual return of the collateral is automated and monitored algorithmically. The internal controls around the process are adaptations of traditional controls, such as authorization, authentication and segregation of duties, in a digitized environment.

Traditional risks of collateralized lending are mitigated in cryptolending. As discussed earlier, one of the systemic risks is for lenders to ensure that the collateralized asset is secured and the borrower cannot sell or use it as a collateral toward another loan. The blockchain, by design, does not allow people to “double-spend” or pledge the same asset twice. Once they sent their collateral to the “secured wallet” of the lender, they no longer have access to those assets, until the loan is paid off. The other prevalent risk that lenders bear is that of sudden and permanent decline in value of the collateral. Real-time algorithmic monitoring of the collateral value and the requirement of a conservative loan to value ratio limits the lender’s exposure to volatile markets. Moreover, geographical location and physical monitoring of the collateral is no longer a concern, allowing individuals and businesses all over the world access to loans in US $s, without having to go through elaborate and time-consuming process of applying for loans at institutional banks.

## 11.4. APPLICATION IN AUTOMOBILE INDUSTRY

The used car market is rife with “lemons” and the mantra always has been “buyer beware.” An unsophisticated buyer often doesn’t know the condition of the car, whether it has been in accidents, whether regular maintenance were undertaken on a regular basis, have there been major failures or repairs, etc. The used car market is rife with uncertainties and no one really trusts the used car salesman. This inherent lack of trust between the buyer and seller, typical in this market, presents an opportunity for blockchain-based solution.

For an automobile, there are many parties involved through its lifecycle: the original manufacturer, the original dealer, mechanics, insurance agents, previous owners, Division of Motor Vehicles, etc. Moreover, there are easy opportunities for manipulation and forgery. The seller could easily manipulate the speedometer, or lie about regular maintenance, or major forthcoming repairs. A blockchain solution is proposed, connecting various stakeholders into a distributed ledger system as shown in [Fig. 11.2](#CH011.html#fig2).

An application called “Car Dossier”[⁴](#CH011.html#fn4) provides a digital dossier based on blockchain that stores all the relevant information about the car from its manufacturing onwards. It includes ownership details, records of accidents, major repairs, regular maintenance, insurance coverage, registration documents, etc., by connecting different parties into a unified system.

Another interesting blockchain solution being developed by Ernst and Young (EY) is to distribute ownership of cars to multiple owners. With the rising costs of owning, maintaining and parking the car, millennials living in big cities usually forgo owning a car. Typically a car is used only for a fraction of the day, creating idle capacity. EY is creating a blockchain-based mobility platform named Tesseract.[⁵](#CH011.html#fn5) The platform facilitates recordkeeping for multiple ownership of the car and distributes the cost of ownership in a transparent and verifiable manner. In the Tesseract platform, the vehicles and trips are digitally logged on the blockchain. Transactions are automatically settled via smart contracts between owners, operators and third-party service providers. The ownership of the car can be flexible and fractional across multiple owners.
