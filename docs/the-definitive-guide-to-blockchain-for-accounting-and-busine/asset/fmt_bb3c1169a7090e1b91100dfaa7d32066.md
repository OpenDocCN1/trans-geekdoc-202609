
In this chapter, we provide a non-technical understanding of the above definition and also eradicate few popular myths about the efficacy of blockchain. For while blockchain is a valuable technology with many potential applications in business and accounting, it can’t cure all problems. It will not make traditional accounting, legal or supply chain professions obsolete. Quite to the contrary, by bringing efficiencies and reducing transactions costs, it will enable many micro-transactions which in fact would require more of the traditional services.

The original problem that blockchain solved was that of transfer of money between strangers in a secure, trusted and non-reversible fashion without going through an intermediary, such as a financial institution. This feature when extended, allows blockchain to enable money or asset transfer between unknown parties who do not necessarily trust each other. Clearly this mechanism works as the market capitalization for both Bitcoin and Ethereum are in billions of dollars, with the market capitalization of Bitcoin reaching close $300 billion and that of Ethereum to $200 billion (see [Fig. 3.1](#CH003.html#fig1)). It does so in a trusted way which is faster and costs much less than traditional alternatives. These features of the new technology give it promise and the power to disrupt traditional means of transferring assets and record-keeping.

## 3.2. PARTICIPANTS AND TRANSACTIONS

The individuals or organizations engaged in a blockchain are called the participants. The participants comprise the network. Individuals or organizations decide to become part of the group that is formed with a common goal. This common goal could be any shared interest, such as transfer of money, maintaining the integrity of a supply chain for a particular industry, provenance of minerals or agricultural products. Additional examples of shared goals are presented in later chapters which discuss those applications in greater detail.

All participants of a blockchain network are equals, with no single participant having more rights or power than any other. Since the network has no single powerful participant or a “central figure” the network is not centralized, and is termed a *decentralized* network. All participants have similar access and power. Any participant in the network could communicate with any other, making use of a seamless and direct peer-to-peer communication. This peer-to-peer communication enables direct transfer of assets, currency or records between participants without having to go through a “central” figure or an intermediary. Since each participant benefits from the network they are motivated to maintain the integrity and proper functioning of the network. In a subsequent chapter, we will discuss the economic incentives of the participants to be honest and act in good faith to preserve and protect the network.

Actions taken by the participants of the network are called “transactions.” If a participant joins the network, it is a transaction. When a participant requisitions to transfer funds or assets to another participant, it is a transaction. When the other participant accepts the funds, it is a separate transaction. Interestingly, it is also a transaction when a participant leaves the network. In general, any record of the activities of the network is labeled as “transaction.”

The term “transaction” in blockchain nomenclature is over-generalized than its meaning and usage in traditional accounting and business vocabulary. In business education, we define transaction as a business event with an unrelated third party having a monetary consequence. In accounting terminology, transaction is even more precisely and narrowly defined as having an impact on the financial statements, and thereby subject to meeting certain criteria promulgated by the accounting bodies, like the FASB or IFRS.

Blockchain has the capacity to record an infinite number of transactions. The transactions take place ***only*** between the participants of the network. The blockchain will not record transactions or dealings that a member of the blockchain has with a non-member, since it does not affect the ecosystem. Each and every transaction is recorded in a data *ledger*. The term “ledger” merely denotes a book, a register or an archive of records. In the instance of blockchain, the ledger is the digital version of a traditional register.

The ledger in blockchain is digital, hence multiple identical copies of the ledger can be easily reproduced and shared with all the participants of the blockchain. This property of being able to maintain multiple identical copies of the ledger at different locations gives blockchain its key advantage over traditional record-keeping system. In traditional record-keeping with physical records, there is a unique ledger or the record book which was kept at a central and secure location. The copies of this ledger aren’t as reliable as they may not be updated in real time, hence could be incomplete. This need for a unique, secure and reliable ledger required centralization. The digitization of the ledger obviated the need of such a central figure or an intermediary.

To fully appreciate the advantage of this feature, consider the traditional system with a bank or financial institution acting as the central figure. The bank is the steward of the only ledger, which recorded all transactions. The bank is the single entity that owns, records, controls and governs the accountholders records of transactions. Thus, there is only one single copy of the individual’s record of transactions. There is only one set of books. Likewise, when we communicate with our friends and family through Facebook, all the communication goes through Facebook’s servers, hence Facebook has the ownership of all information and content passing through its servers. Blockchain technology eliminates the role of the central figure, and instead distributes that power equally to each and every participant. The data-ledger is held at each participant node, thereby removing any one party’s control over the data.
