
Businesses need to be aware of the behavior of the participants in their particular supply chains in order to be able to assure responsible sourcing, however that knowledge can be hampered by a need to protect business confidentiality, by a lack of knowledge of the participants more than one transaction removed from the company, and until recently by a lack of concern with events outside the walls of the company. A recent increase in regulatory request, consumer interest and potential legal liability have moved enterprises to expand the role of product sourcing beyond a simple interaction with their immediate suppliers. A company’s procurement service that once would have been limited to such immediate needs as identifying reliable suppliers, evaluating product quality and ensuring timely delivery, now needs to seek assistance from other departments within the company to manage new purchasing subjects such as ensuring regulatory compliance, understanding and avoiding harmful business risk, and even previously unimagined challenges such as avoiding participation in illegal activity, money laundering and human slavery – in other words, in providing due diligence; due diligence being the care that a reasonable person exercises to avoid harm to other persons or their property.

The scope of these purchasing activities is expanding from the evaluation of one’s immediate suppliers, to now considering the full supply chain and assuring due diligence from suppliers several steps preceding the immediate supplier, that is, to entities with whom the business may have no formal relationship or contact.

## 9.2. COMPLEXITY OF MINERAL SUPPLY CHAIN

The mineral supply chain consists of several sequential processes or links. Each process involves multiple participants, and multiple activities. It is evident that performing due diligence in the mineral supply chain is not easy. A good example of the complexity of the mineral supply chain can be seen in the following graphic illustration of the gold supply chain.

![image](images/C09-UFIG1.jpg)

The gold supply chain consists of a series of general processes or links, involving: tens of countries where the mineral awaits; millions of miners, both artisanal and industrial; thousands of local traders and exporters; hundreds of smelters in different countries; thousands of traders of finished raw material; thousands of component manufacturers; tens of thousands of manufacturers of the finished product; and hundreds of thousands of different finished products to be sold to the final consumers.

![image](images/C09-UFIG2.jpg)

The first process in the gold supply chain is the government of the country where the gold is located. Each country regulates the ability to mine and ownership of mines, as well as the human, environmental, safety and political conditions under which mining can occur. Countries may cooperate with international regulation and guidance or may govern independently. Countries, just like individuals, can be responsible or corrupt, laissez-faire or interventionist, nationalistic or global in their behaviors, and this variability means that some countries are much less effective than others in assuring responsible activities in the gold supply chain. Countries also vary in their permission or prohibition of artisanal mining, individual land ownership, and mining rights.

![image](images/C09-UFIG3.jpg)

To get the gold ore out of the ground requires miners. Estimates say 100 million people depend on artisanal mining. About seven million people are employed in large-scale industrial mining (LSM) worldwide. Artisanal and Small-Scale Mining (ASM) occurs in approximately 80 countries. ASM production supply accounts for 20% of mined gold. A typical ASM mining process includes digging the gold ore, transporting the ore (carried in bags or by wheelbarrow) to where it is manually crushed, washed and filtered. The filtered sediment is processed chemically to yield an amalgam that is then sold to local traders. LSM extraction of gold typically involves machine crushing and milling the rock to a powder or slurry, then vat or heap leaching using sodium cyanide to yield impure gold.

![image](images/C09-UFIG4.jpg)

There are a variety of forms of transportation involved in moving gold ore from the mine site to the smelter. In the instance of ASM the gold amalgam needs to be assembled in sufficient quantity to be exported to smelters. This involves transport by a variety of means: from bicycle and motor scooter, to truck and trailer, and to private airplane. Road transport may be subject to arbitrary taxation by local government officials or extortion by illegal groups. Air transport may even be untraceable (see Section 9.5).

![image](images/C09-UFIG5.jpg)

Local traders may purchase gold amalgam from miners and sell it in turn to large traders, or miners may act as a cooperative and sell to large traders directly. Large traders may then smelt the gold themselves or may ship the amalgam to a smelter. Other participants in the process between the miner and the smelter may include mine supervisors, local tribal authority, government officials, police and others, each of whom can perform their work legally or illegally.

![image](images/C09-UFIG6.jpg)

The smelter is considered the midpoint in the mineral supply chain. Smelters receive not only mineral ore from LSM and ASM but also recycled material coming from a variety of sources. In a smelter, gold is released from its natural mineral state by melting and then reacting with chemicals. The resulting doré gold is a semi-pure alloy of gold and silver that can vary in purity, so it is subsequently then transported to refineries, where further treatment can bring the purity of the gold to nearly 100%.

![image](images/C09-UFIG7.jpg)

A component manufacturer will purchase gold from metal trader who has purchased the processed gold from a smelter or a refiner. The component manufacturer may need only minute amounts of gold for each component but will be making millions of components. Gold is a very good electrical conductor, it does not easily corrode in air, and is ductile. For these reasons, gold is the preferred material used to make small electrical connectors, circuit traces, integrated circuits, transistors and memory chips.

![image](images/C09-UFIG8.jpg)

Finished product manufacturers are the final immediate process in our gold supply chain. Our modern electronics are complex assemblages of dozens of small components, each of which contains gold. In fact, virtually any electronic appliance that has a circuit board, from old TVs and VCRs to GPS systems and solar panels, will contain gold.

![image](images/C09-UFIG9.jpg)

The final process in the gold supply chain is recycling. Thirty-five to 40 smartphones (without batteries) contain roughly 1 gram of gold, the same quantity that appears in a ton of lower grade gold ore in the ground. Recycled gold appears at the midpoint of the chain at the smelter, where it is reprocessed along with new ore to make doré gold.

Regulation and guidelines have so far focused their attention on the most apparent participants in the supply chain for consumer goods, namely, the finished product manufacturers, yet the same motivation for regulation (assurance of safe process and prevention of unwanted consequence) can be applied the length of the supply chain. Given the complexity, tools are needed to provide transparency and consistency to all processes in a mineral supply chain while maintaining business confidentiality and encouraging fair business competition.
