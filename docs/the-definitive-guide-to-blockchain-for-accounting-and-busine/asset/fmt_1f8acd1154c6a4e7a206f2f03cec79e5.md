
From a purchaser’s perspective, institutional real estate investments require large up-front costs, most of which are financed by local lenders. This limits the ability of lesser known purchasers to access lower-cost global capital markets for borrowing. Likewise, from the lender’s perspective, they are limited to regional markets which restrict their ability to transfer risk and seek out potentially higher performing asset classes for diversification. Regionally concentrated lenders can modulate loan exposure through DLT systems, offering pieces of these cash flows to a global pool of investors by way of security tokens. The subsequent token buyer would benefit from real estate exposure outside of their domicile, while the original lender can benefit from an alternative method of liquidity.

If executed properly, tokenization has the potential to increase the liquidity level of certain institutional real estate investments to that of “Traded REITs.” A movement of such magnitude along Resource Real Estate, LLC’s “Illiquid-Liquid Spectrum” would bring additional investors, increase market competitiveness and lower middlemen commissions, leading to more efficient asset pricing, higher profits for direct participants, and further reductions in institutional friction. The real estate investment trust (REIT) structure has made a profound impact on global real estate markets since its creation in 1960. Originating in the United States, REITs offered investors a vehicle to have exposure to large-scale, diverse and liquid portfolios of income-producing real estate assets. Sixty years after its introduction, countries around the world continue to pass laws adopting REITs for the first time, demonstrating current opportunities for unlocking value with creative structures in real estate markets around the world.

We believe that digital securities can provide an innovative framework for creating transformative real estate investment vehicles and instruments, leveraging technological advancements to track real estate market data. By providing real-time data to market participants, investors would have access to information needed to transact confidently. This would draw more actors to real estate markets, further increasing the amount of market data. The resulting feedback loop between buyers, sellers, industry actors and data points would lead to a more efficient price discovery process.

Digital securities are currently best suited for real estate markets with strong informational technology infrastructure, open data initiatives, and future-focused governments and businesses. Developed nations best meet these criteria, especially those that already have utilized the REIT structure for years, as digital securities would offer an innovative structuring to unlock further value. As such, we have come up with a multistep proposal for using DLT systems and asset tokenization to increase data transparency and investment liquidity within the Japanese real estate market.

## 10.9. THE JAPANESE REAL ESTATE MARKET

In this section we provide a brief overview of the Japanese real estate market. We first describe the economics and the size of the market. We then discuss some areas of deficiency and inefficiency in that market, which could be improved upon.

### 10.9.1. Market Size and Investment Environment

Behind the United States and China but ahead of the UK, India and Germany, the Japanese economy is currently the third largest in the world, and is expected to hold this position well into the 2020s per nominal gross domestic product (GDP) forecasts by Nomura Research Institute, Ltd. (NRI) ([Fig. 10.6](#CH010.html#fig6)), making Japan an ideal target for capital deployment over the coming decade.

As reported by Sumitomo Mitsui Trust Research Institute (SMTRI), the market cap of real estate private funds that invested in Japanese domestic real estate totaled 17.7 trillion JPY (about 160 billion USD) in early 2019. When factoring in Japanese real estate investment trusts (J-REITs), which accounted for 17.9 trillion yen, the Japanese real estate market had nearly 36 trillion yen (about 340 billion USD) of assets under management. These results are illustrated in [Fig. 10.7](#CH010.html#fig7) found in SMTRI’s 27th “Survey on Private Real Estate Funds.” Note that while the portion of private funds has remained relatively steady over the years, the majority of growth in assets under management has been fueled by J-REITs.

When compared to other major real estate markets, Japan offers relatively high spreads to investors. According to Jones Lang LaSalle (JLL), prime office buildings in Tokyo had an average yield gap of 2.9% in 2019, which is higher than the average yield gap of both London (2.5%) and NYC (1.25%). As a result, foreign funds searching for yield have deployed a record amount of capital into Japanese real estate over the past several years: in the year 2017 alone, overseas firms spent 1.1 trillion yen on acquisitions. In fact, according to SMTRI’s survey, the relative attractiveness of Japan’s yield gap was the top reason for foreign investors choosing Japan ([Fig. 10.8](#CH010.html#fig8)).

Foreign investors, such as institutional real estate giants KKR and Blackstone, are also attracted to Japan’s ultra-low interest rate environment, which is set by the Bank of Japan. In an effort to promote growth, the Bank of Japan instituted policies that facilitate cheap financing markets, fueling real estate demand. Interestingly, some banks even pay businesses to borrow from them by issuing negative interest-bearing loans. These accommodative monetary policy conditions have prompted significant deployment of sovereign capital into Japanese real estate over the past decade, as displayed in [Fig. 10.9](#CH010.html#fig9), where the bottom bar shows the percentage of SMTRI survey respondents that managed capital in Japan from overseas.

### 10.9.2. Japanese Real Estate Market Pain Points[^(10)](#CH010.html#fn10)

With the increasing likelihood of further easing of rates by the Bank of Japan, the competitive landscape for property acquisitions has intensified. Funds with an average investment period of 5+ years dominate this market, as the average length of new real estate funds launched in 2019 is now 9.1 years. The increased length in fund duration coincides with decreased liquidity, as investor capital is used to purchase and maintain the underlying real estate assets.

Japanese real estate securitization involves major players, such as GK-TK (GodoKaisha-TokumeiKumiai) and TMK (TokuteiMokutekiKaisha). Securitized products draw significant investment interests from global institutions looking to manage balance sheet risk and diversify cash flows. As institutional investors require higher yields than individuals, the recently inflated real estate prices make it difficult to form deals, particularly in metropolitan areas. In fact, when surveyed by SMTRI, nearly 25% of foreign investors chose “rising real estate prices” as a reason for not investing in the Japanese market ([Fig. 10.10](#CH010.html#fig10)).
