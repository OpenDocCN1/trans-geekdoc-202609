
While in the real world with real currency, everyone insists on getting the actual currency note and no one, I know of, would accept a photocopy. Just imagine, if we were allowed to make purchases using a photocopy of a $100 bill. Each one of us would be infinitely rich!! We could buy whatever we want as long as we had a good copier. However, in the virtual world, with virtual currency, the currency transfer was a copy and not the original, hence for Bitcoin to work the inventors had to resolve the problem of double-spending. That is, even though the transaction is happening through a copy and not the original, once the Bitcoin has been spent, the buyer will not be allowed to spend it again.

Consequently, for cryptocurrency to be successful the problem of “double-spending” needed to be solved. In traditional methods, the trusted third party will maintain the records and prevent double-spending. Digital signatures provide part of the solution, but the main benefits are lost if a trusted third party is still required to prevent double-spending. The computer scientists promoting the idea of a digital currency that does not rely on a “trusted third party” had to find an alternative solution to enable all participants the ability to validate each and every transaction on a real-time basis. That is, they needed the digital form of Yap money discussed earlier. Basically, everyone in the virtual community would know the wealth everyone possesses and be aware of all the transactions that occur in the cyber-domain using Bitcoin.

The solution proposed by Nakamoto relied on a peer-to-peer distributed solution, that later came to be known as blockchain. Peer to peer is a decentralized communication model where each party has the same capabilities and any party can initiate a session. In computer lingo, peer-to-peer network allows each node to function both as a client and as a server. One of the earliest popular applications of peer-to-peer model was the music sharing application, Napster. This architecture allows for massive parallel computing environments. In a blockchain, the peer-to-peer distributed network would timestamp each and every transaction, thereby maintaining a chronological order of transactions. The validity check for transactions is delegated to multiple users and computers. Therefore, rather than relying on the honesty and integrity of the “trusted third party,” the system now relies on the honesty and objectivity of the individual nodes verifying each transaction. Nakamoto asserted that the “system is secure as long as honest nodes collectively control more CPU power than any cooperating group of attacker nodes.” We will elaborate on this concept in a subsequent chapter.

The challenge really is that we want a mechanism to enable the seller to know that the buyer has the resources (or Bitcoins) to pay for the goods. Let’s understand the problem through a simple example. Say, my wife and I are on a vacation abroad and one morning she says she is completely out of local currency and needs to convert $500 from the bank. Later that day, I want to know how much of the foreign currency she still has remaining in her purse before I make a purchase. Rather than asking her or look through her purse, which will surely upset her, I decide to recall all the purchases she made during the day and subtract that amount from $500. If I know the amount of money she had at the start of the day and have a comprehensive list of all her spending, I can infer how much she still has left with her.

Nakamoto’s solution was similar. For each and every buyer, if we know all the Bitcoins they obtained in the past, and have a comprehensive list of all the times the buyer spent the Bitcoins, we will know how much is left and whether the buyer has the amount of Bitcoins to transfer to the seller. In that sense, it is the earliest transaction that counts. Once we have undisputed record of the earliest transactions, the future attempts to double-spend does not matter. Basically, to be certain about the absence of a transaction is to be cognizant of all the transactions. In the traditional setting, it was the role of the trusted third party to provide and validate that information.

In the Bitcoin model, in the absence of the trusted third party, each and every transaction with Bitcoin has to be publicly announced. Moreover, multiple comprehensive records of all transactions have to be maintained with multiple parties, so that no one party can change the records without the knowledge and approval of the others. Since it is only the earliest transactions that are valid, hence matter, we only need a way for all the participants to agree on a singular history of the order in which the transactions were received or executed. For the seller to be assured that the payment is valid, the majority of the participants in the system must recognize this particular transaction to be the first one received to lay a claim on the existing resources of the buyer. Once this particular seller’s transaction is recognized to be the first one, the transfer of resources is permanent and cannot be altered. This feature is defined as the *immutability*. The records are secured and permanent because they are immutable, that is once recorded these cannot be subsequently altered.

## 2.5. BLOCKCHAIN INTRODUCED

Extending the above concept to the digital domain, the participants are replaced by “nodes,” denoting a computing unit. The transactions are stored in a block, and the series of blocks comprise the blockchain. In order to ensure that the node reporting the transaction is legitimate, they have to undertake a *proof of work*.[⁶](#CH002.html#fn6) This is much like how we humans have to sometimes prove to the computer that we are not robots, by identifying traffic lights in a series of pictures! Once a transaction is validated and recorded by one of the nodes it is transmitted to all the nodes in the network. Each node maintains identical copies of the validated transactions, which are called *blocks*. The nodes start working on the next set of transactions and repeat the process. Briefly, these steps are summarized as[⁷](#CH002.html#fn7):

*   New transactions are broadcast to all nodes.
*   Each node collects new transactions into a block.
*   Each node works on finding a difficult proof of work for its block.
*   When a node finds a proof of work, it broadcasts the block to all nodes.
*   Nodes accept the block only if all transactions in it are valid and not already spent.
*   Nodes express their acceptance of the block by working on creating the next block in the chain.

The last bullet is non-trivial and needs further elaboration. Suppose at a meeting, a challenging problem is being discussed. There is a lot of conversation, but finally someone makes a great suggestion, people nod their head and move on to the next issue. The conclusion of discussion for a topic and continuation to the next topic is an implicit acknowledgment of the consensus of the final statement made on the previous topic. The agreement in blockchain follows the same principle. Once the nodes accept a solution of the *proof of work* submitted by another node and have validated all the transactions in the new block sent along with the proof of work, they start working on the next block using the *hash* of the accepted block as the previous hash.[⁸](#CH002.html#fn8)
