
# Collateralized Borrowing

## Historical Origins

Throughout the ages, people all over the world, have occasionally found themselves with a common problem – need for fast cash. A common solution to this problem emerged from ancient China to ancient Greece – collateralized borrowing. Collateralized borrowing, or pawning as it is commonly known, involves temporary exchange of any non-monetary asset for cash which is expected to be reversed within a specified time. That is, the borrower surrenders something of value to the lender for cash, later the borrower regains possession of the item once the loan is repaid. In instances when the borrower is unable to pay back their loan, the lender obtains ownership of the collateral and can sell the collateral and recover the loan amount plus a nominal profit. This mechanism of obtaining short-term cash, without necessarily disposing off assets, is not just for the poor. Famously, in the fourteenth century King Edward II of England pawned royal family jewels to help finance the war against France, and Queen Isabella of Spain is said to have borrowed against her jewelry to fund Christopher Columbus’s expedition to the New World.

## Modern Consumer Loans

The concept of collateralized borrowing has been adapted for contemporary needs and modern technology. It now impacts most adults in the U.S. Mortgage and auto loans are two of the most common form of collateralized borrowing. At the outset, the lender remits cash that is used toward the purchase of the house or the car. Subsequently, the borrower makes periodic payments, exceeding the interest amount, toward paying off the loan. The borrower, in the meanwhile, gains the right of use of the asset. If the borrower sells the asset, the lender has the first right toward the proceeds from the sale to satisfy any outstanding loan balance. Often, the lender holds the title to the property until the loan is paid in full, thereby preventing a sale of the collateralized asset without their knowledge. In instances when the borrower becomes delinquent or is unable to pay the loan, the lender seizes the asset and forecloses on the loan. The lender then sells off the asset to recover the unpaid balance.

## Margin Investing

Brokerage firms have adapted collaterailized financing in the investing domain by allowing investment on margin. Investment on margin allows an investor to invest an amount greater than their liquid assets through obtaining a loan using securities in their accounts as collateral. For example, if an individual has $10,000 to invest in the market, she buys securities worth $10,000 and uses those securities as collateral to borrow an additional amount, say $8,000, which she also invests. Thus, with a seed capital of $10,000 the individual can purchase $18,000 of securities. Now assume her investment gains 5% over a span of time when the loan has accrued 1% interest. In her original investment of $10,000 the investor would have made a modest $500 (or 5% on their investment), but through margin she nets $820 ($900 of return less $80 in interest), or 8.2% on their investment of $10,000. If her investment increases in value at a rate exceeding the interest on the loan, she earns a higher rate of return than the underlying market. Leverage makes a good deal even better. However, when market declines and the investment loses value, she suffers larger losses since she still has to make the interest payment. Moreover, when the investment loses value the lender may, as a preventive measure to secure their loan value, sell some of the investment to reduce the amount of loan outstanding.

## Business Financing

Businesses also use collateral in their financing activities. Two traditional methods are through factoring of accounts receivables and pledging of inventory. Most businesses sell on credit to the customers, generating revenue but not cash at the time of sale. Cash is subsequently collected when the money is received from the customer. In the meantime, the company has accounts receivables, which is a promise of future cash inflow. A fast-growing business often faces cash flow problems due to the delay in collecting on the accounts receivables. A business in need of immediate cash could approach a lender and borrow cash, selling its receivables at a discount to compensate the lender for interest charges as well as the risk of non-payment. The lender then collects on the invoices, and remits the excess to the business after the principal balance and accrued interest on the loan is fully paid off. The benefit of factoring is that the business obtains the use of cash without applying or qualifying for a loan, thereby bypassing traditional banking channels. Likewise, inventory can be pledged as collateral to obtain a commercial loan. If the borrower defaults on the loan conditions, the lender may liquidate the inventory to meet the terms of the loan. The lender may also regularly and periodically inspect the pledged inventory to ensure that the pledged inventory is not obsolete or otherwise diminished in value.

## Benefits

Collateralized loan is popular because it provides benefits to both the lender and the borrower. The pledged assets, or collateralized assets, gives lenders a level of reassurance should the borrower default. This mitigates the risk for the lender, enabling the lender to charge a lower interest rate than they otherwise would. For the borrowers, they get the use of cash without having to liquidate their asset, thereby maintaining the right to use the asset as well as benefit from the potential appreciation of value of the asset. Additionally, it allows the borrower to avert the tax consequences that may arise from the sale of the asset, such as paying taxes on capital gains.

## Risks

The lender takes on the risk of not recovering the principal and interest on the loan, should the borrower default on payments. Collateralized loan helps mitigate this risk, by providing the value of underlying assets as a form of guarantee toward future payments. However, some risks remain and are based on the quality of the collateral. These are:

*   As the collateralized asset acts as a guarantee toward the loan, a drop in value of the asset poses risk for the lender. For example, say a lender grants a $100 loan based on a collateralized asset with market value of $100. Suppose the asset, pledged as collateral, declines in value to $90, the borrower may simply forgo the asset and not repay the loan, with the lender absorbing a $10 loss. To prevent against such occurrences, lenders typically require collateral of greater value than the loan amount. This is known as “Loan to value ratio,” which typically is around 0.8. In other words, the collateralized asset is 125% (1/0.8) of the loan amount. Having a lower loan to value ratio provides greater protection to the lender. The higher the volatility of the asset value, the lower the loan to value ratio is expected to be.
*   The lender wants to prevent the borrower from selling, or otherwise disposing off, the collateralized asset without the lender’s knowledge or consent. In traditional pawnshops, the lender typically takes physical custody of the asset, prohibiting the sale of the asset. In mortgages and auto loans, the lender retains the title to the assets, thereby precluding sale of the asset without their knowledge.
*   Lenders typically prevent the borrower from using the same asset as collateral toward multiple loans. Technically, that would yield a greater loan to value ratio than is acceptable to any of the lenders. Moreover, in case of default by the borrower, the sale of collateralized asset may not be sufficient to cover all the loan balances, resulting in losses for some or all the lenders.
