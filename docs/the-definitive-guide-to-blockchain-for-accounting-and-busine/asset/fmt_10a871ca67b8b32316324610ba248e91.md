## 10.2. FINANCIAL TRANSFORMATION SPARKED BY BLOCKCHAIN

A `blockchain` is a distributed database structure that records transactions on a digital ledger, providing users with a secure and transparent history of events. In our ever increasing digital world, where countless amounts of data are vulnerable to alteration and theft, a `blockchain`’s composition provides enhanced security and assurance to its users.

As noted in previous chapters, `blockchain` security is built on the concepts of consensus and tamper-resistance. Consensus is achieved when network devices confirm both the reliability of transactions and the true state of a system. Once transactions are confirmed, the records of a `blockchain` are considered tamper-resistant, meaning they cannot be altered without an overriding consensus. Moreover, cryptography plays a key role in `blockchain` data security. Cryptographic hashing algorithms help protect the ledger’s transaction data by generating encryption keys to encrypt and decrypt pieces of information. Asymmetric cryptography, or public key cryptography (`PKC`), is used to secure digital asset wallets, ensuring that only the intended users have access.

In contrast with symmetric encryption algorithms, which use the same key to encrypt and decrypt data, `PKC` algorithms use one key for encryption (`public key`) and another key for decryption (`private key`). Digital asset wallet addresses are generated using `public key`s, allowing them to be safely shared with others. `Private key`s are used for digital signature creation and transaction verification; transactions are verified and added to the ledger after confirming the hash within the digital signature. Asymmetric cryptography systems are widely used throughout the digital assets industry to enhance transaction security, as asset transfers can only be authorized using the `private key` associated with its corresponding digital wallet.

When combining these features, the integrity of a `blockchain`’s data is upheld, offering a medium to transform financial products into cryptographically-secured digital tokens; these digital assets can be issued and transferred on `DLT` networks. `DLT` systems are modernizing financial services infrastructure and related processes, by creating shared data structures (ledgers) of electronic records that are authenticated through multiparty consensus mechanisms. As such, digital asset transactions are recorded in real time on an authoritative ledger that is readily available to appropriate network users. As discussed in detail in a later chapter, the `DLT` network model can eliminate or facilitate the reporting and auditing process to reduce costs and provide greater assurance. Financial instruments and related procedures are further transformed when integrating `smart contracts` into `DLT` systems.

## 10.3. SMART CONTRACT STANDARDS EXPEDITE DIGITAL ASSET FUNCTIONS

As mentioned in earlier chapters, `smart contracts` are protocols that digitally facilitate the performance of previously agreed upon terms and procedures. Transactions executed through `smart contracts` are conditional, as the underlying code follows “`if, then`” logic statements, meaning if a requirement is satisfied, then an action occurs. `Smart contract` activity is recorded on a distributed ledger, making all transactions trackable and tamper-resistant. The unique characteristics of `smart contracts` assure reliable transaction execution without third parties, resulting in reduced operating and transaction costs.

`Smart contracts` are conceptually similar to “stored procedures” used in traditional database structures when automating software. However, `DLT` `smart contracts` can automate business processes across distinct entities, while network rules offer guaranteed execution and outcomes are verifiable and auditable for all system participants. Likewise, the distributed computing platform “`Ethereum`” offers a turing complete scripting language, providing functionality for `smart contract` implementation using built-in standards.

`Ethereum` Request for Comments (`ERC`) standards describe the requisite set of functions for tokens, enabling `smart contracts` and related applications to communicate and execute functions in a predictable manner. The `ERC20` standard provides a framework for the systemized creation of fungible tokens (`ERC20` tokens), as well as balance tracking and transfer functional capabilities. By leveraging `Ethereum`’s robust platform, `ERC20` tokens offer businesses a simple mechanism for creating their own digital assets. This novel functionality sparked a digital asset fundraising boom, which brought unconventional challenges to global financial regulation.

## 10.4. HARMONIZING FINANCIAL REGULATION ACROSS JURISDICTIONS

Major innovations in technology, such as the internet, coincide with a deeper integration of individual markets within the global financial system. In 1987, former `SEC` Chairman Charles Cox took note of this trend while testifying before Congress, stating

*as a result of a number of factors, including technological advances and the removal of restrictions on foreign participation by many of the world’s securities markets, internationalization is more than a developing trend, it is a present day reality.[¹](#CH010.html#fn1)*

The advancements in technology also bring about new challenges in cross-border regulation, as made evident by the recent innovations in `blockchain` and digital assets.

Regulation of businesses based in other jurisdictions requires local lawmakers to reflect on how their rules will apply to entities that would like to access their capital markets. In some instances, this simply requires both foreign and domestic companies to adhere to the same laws. However, regulators realize that this approach might not always be appropriate, leading to certain exemptions for foreign issuers and investors. This flexibility allows locales to reap the economic rewards of global enterprise, while at the same time protecting its citizens from potential frauds and abuses.

Between 2014 and mid-2018, over $11 billion was raised through digital assets. Mostly prompted by `Ethereum`’s `ERC20` standard, this global phenomenon demonstrated the effectiveness of fundraising with `DLT` and `smart contracts`. However, most of the utility tokens created over this time period were issued through non-compliant initial coin offerings (`ICOs`). According to Satis Group, 78% of `ICOs` were identified scams, 7% failed or had gone dead, and only 15% went on to trade on an exchange.[²](#CH010.html#fn2) As such, innovators and policymakers recognized the apparent need for increased digital asset regulation.
