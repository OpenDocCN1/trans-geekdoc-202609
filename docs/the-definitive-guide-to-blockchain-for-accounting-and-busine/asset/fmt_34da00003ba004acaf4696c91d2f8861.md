
If digital securities serve as the vehicle for intermediation between transacting parties, our proposed network of DLT systems can provide further visibility into data related to underlying Japanese properties. Upon initial issuance, property information, such as land title, capitalization and occupancy rates, ownership and tenant history, etc., can be tracked in real time throughout the lifecycle of each digital security. This information is typically scattered across multiple organizations and presented in differing formats; the data can now be standardized and made readily available on web-based platforms using DLT systems.

We envision network users initially being those that are not in the top tier of global real estate investment management, particularly those real estate managers that struggle to get above the initial capital deployment threshold. The main motivation for network participants would be access to crowdsourced real estate market information that is more valuable in the aggregate, than the individual pieces each actor would accumulate on their own. One might consider this network to be the next web’s version of a business ecosystem, which Harvard Professor James F. Moore (1996) defined as:
*an economic community supported by a foundation of interacting organizations and individuals – the organisms of the business world. The economic community produces goods and services of value to customers, who are themselves members of the ecosystem. The member organisms also include suppliers, lead producers, competitors, and other stakeholders. Over time, they coevolve their capabilities and roles, and tend to align themselves with the directions set by one or more central companies. Those companies holding leadership roles may change over time, but the function of ecosystem leader is valued by the community because it enables members to move toward shared visions to align their investments, and to find mutually supportive roles.*

The value of our proposed ecosystem would rise exponentially as Web 3.0 technologies are further integrated into business processes and everyday society, creating an unparalleled trove of real estate market data.

Institutional real estate firms would eventually be incentivized to join this network, once the business environment radically evolves. As per Tom Gruber, Co-founder and CTO of Intraspect Software, “Globalization is eliminating the traditional advantages of the large corporation: access to capital, access to markets, and economies of scale.”[^(16)](#CH010.html#fn16) In a similar manner, we believe that DLT ecosystems, smart contracts, security tokens and other Web 3.0 technologies can transform the methods and incentives surrounding data transparency in the Japanese real estate market today, and eventually the global real estate industry of tomorrow.

Yuto Ohigashi of JLL believes “there are two keys to increasing transparency: changing the market mechanism through government initiatives, and having private market players be conscious of transparency in their business activities.”[^(17)](#CH010.html#fn17) Japanese open data initiatives are incentivizing the creation of DLT systems that can serve as a standardized network for aggregating and sharing real estate data, while digital securities will soon track Japanese real estate market transactions, which we believe would generate access to further international investment.

### 10.10.2. Open Access
JLL’s 2018 survey found that real estate investors gravitated toward markets that were open and transparent. Their research found that “Highly Transparent” cities account for 70% of direct real estate investment across the 158 cities covered, and nearly three-quarters of cross-border investment. Investors and property developers are increasingly requiring more information related to city-level dynamics. As made evident by [Fig. 10.13](#CH010.html#fig13), a meaningful correlation was found between a cities’ perceived real estate transparency and its total transaction volume.

Tokyo had a transparency score of 1.98, ranking at 50th among global cities. Even though Japan’s largest metropolitan area fell within the second transparency tier, it was still impressively ranked a top 3 global city by real estate transaction volume. In order to see the necessary uptick in real estate transaction volume, Japanese real estate managers will likely need to capture a larger share of the international investment community. According to Savills World Research, there was a sharp decrease in the percentage of cross-border investment volumes between 2017 and the third quarter of 2018 ([Fig. 10.14](#CH010.html#fig14)).

In December 2018, Japan saw a year-over-year decrease from international investments amounting to over 2 trillion yen, which was the largest percentage decrease since the 2008 Financial Crisis. This data validates SMTRI’s survey results, where real estate investment managers sought an “expansion of the categories of investors to invest in funds.” The tokenization of Japanese real estate can allow for the creation of diverse products that would attract alternative investors worldwide.

As discussed earlier, security tokens are an ideal vehicle for facilitating seamless cross-border transactions, due to their programmable nature. Tokenized Japanese real estate can provide a coded framework for preventive and detective asset controls, which would help mitigate jurisdictional regulatory concerns. According to JLL’s 2018 Index, Japan ranked 31st in “Governance in Listed Vehicles,” which was a contributing factor to the decrease in international investment. If digital securities became a preferred vehicle for listings, governance could be standardized and automated. International investors would have a more convenient mechanism for participating in governance actions, leading to a corresponding increase in the deployment of cross-border capital; a globally diverse investor pool would enhance market liquidity.

### 10.10.3. Enhanced Liquidity
As noted in the previous section, nearly half of Japan’s real estate Assets Under Management are locked up in the private market, resulting in a large liquidity premium. The liquidity premium is primarily due to high transaction costs, which are composed of a wide range of market frictions: large bid-ask spreads, decreased market depth, high property search costs and brokerage fees, all of which increase market complexity.

Security tokens can increase liquidity within Japan’s private real estate market by enhancing characteristics of the underlying investments. The current model is heavily reliant on intermediaries, such as real estate bankers and brokers, and the buyer base for these complex assets is limited. By providing an asset standardization framework, digital securities can reduce analysis and search costs. When supply and demand frictions are smoothed, transactions can occur more frequently, leading to increased price transparency.
