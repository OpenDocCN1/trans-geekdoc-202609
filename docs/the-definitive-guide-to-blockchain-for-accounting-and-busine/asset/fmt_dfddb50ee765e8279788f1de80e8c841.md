
Some of the previous limitations can be addressed through the use of a non-fungible token. It is a bit like passing a baton from person to person in a relay race. At the end it will have the fingerprints of everyone who held it. To illustrate, let’s expand the above example by starting at a gold mine. Our responsible gold mine wants to sell responsibly sourced gold to their customers. They extract gold, melt it into “doré” bars that are weighed, and a non-fungible “doré” token is created to represent each bar. The doré token contains a unique ID, which is put on a tag and attached to the doré bar, as well as a link to a database of due diligence data. As the doré bars are passed from owner to owner, the associated non-fungible tokens are also passed along to each new owner. When the doré bars reaches the refiner, it is processed into 1kg gold bars and the tokens are frozen so they can no longer be traded. We issue new tokens for the new, refined gold kilo bars that contain a unique ID and a link to the old token. The gold kilo bars are put into a vault as before, and people purchasing or trading the new gold token have a record of its supply chain history.

Non-fungible tokens have advantages over fungible tokens, these are:

* Non-fungible tokens allow us to represent individual units of material (the doré bars and the kilo bars). Having this extra granularity of data means we are not limited to a single mineral source or vault.
* Because the individual shipments are represented, we can also record who held them and where they were produced. This allows us to track minerals from the point of production, all the way down the supply chain.
* The tokens are linked to specific bars or shipments, making them more difficult to use for money laundering.

However, these also impose new challenges as follows:

* For the tokens to track the metal, we need to *link the metal to the non-fungible tokens*. Tagging presents a challenge whenever the material is processed or changes form.
* Because we are tracking materials along a supply chain, *we need all companies along the supply chain to participate*, making it more challenging to implement a project. These companies will likely need to adjust their internal processes to keep track of these blockchain-tracked shipments, meaning additional work and cost.
* *We still need to ensure that good data is provided to the blockchain*. Our non-fungible tokens are only as useful as the data they are based on.

Non-fungible tokens are an intuitive way of tracking material shipments because in many ways they act like the physical materials they represent. As long as a company holds a token, they can prove the history of the associated metal.

Moreover, an additional improvement could be made for compliance purposes. After a company sells their products and transfers the associated non-fungible token, they still need to show that they used to own it, and that it was responsibly sourced. If we were using a regular traditional database, this would not be an issue at all. We would keep a record of the tokens we possessed, and their history and the compliance records can be prepared. Since we need this record secured and immutable, we need to store the information on the blockchain.

There are many ways to approach this, but one simple possibility is to put in a rule that non-fungible tokens can only be traded once and then they are frozen forever. Holding a non-fungible token allows one to create a new token that can also be traded once. This is a bit like passing a ball of yarn from person to person, with everyone still holding the thread after they’ve passed the ball.

### 9.7.4. Operationalizing Tokenization

One of the primary challenges is to link the metal to the tokens. This is the number one question people ask when they think about supply chain solutions. How do you make sure the materials you are tracking match the records, certificates or tokens on the blockchain? When we say this, there are two functions we need to be able to perform:

**1.** If we have a shipment of material, we should be able to locate the blockchain token that shows us the record of ownership and associated due diligence data.

**2.** If we have a token we want to be able to identify and verify the shipment it relates to.

To accomplish the first task, we need to have a tag with a unique identifier that links to the blockchain. To accomplish the second, we need information about the material stored with the token, including identifying marks, last known location, and total mass and grade tracked by the token.

One illustration of the implementation is to consider an ingot of tin. On the ingot is a sticker with a QR code. We scan the QR code and an app calls up our non-fungible token showing us all the previous owners. Another illustration is that we have a token for a pallet of tin ingots displayed in an app. Along with the token, the app has information about which warehouse the tin is stored in, how many ingots in the pallet, a unique identifier and a photo of what it looks like. We can then go to the warehouse and locate the pallet.

The method we use to link the material with the token can vary depending on which problems we are trying to solve. For example, we may have concerns that minerals from illicit sources are being mixed in with our shipments before the minerals are processed into metal. Minerals look like piles of rocks so if this is a concern, we might weigh the shipment before and after transport and spray the minerals with an identifying compound such as a rare radioactive isotope.

We may have concerns that tracked metal ingots in our supply chain are being lost, and that companies are replacing the ingots with fakes that have the same ID number. If this is our concern, we might take high resolution photographs of the ingots that show fine detailed cracks and lines in the metal to prove the bar is genuine. The important thing is that the token tracking system be flexible enough to accommodate different identification methods. This will ensure the system can adapt to the different challenges of different minerals at different stages of the supply chain.

### 9.7.5. Mass Processing of Minerals

Minerals and metals are raw materials, not end products by themselves. They need to be processed to be useful. Ores need to be smelted into metal. Metals need to be refined and alloyed to become useful in our end products. Most smelters and refiners use a continuous process where ores from many sources are processed together. These processes make batch-by-batch tracing unfeasible. The world of the future may be one where all these processes are handled batch-by-batch, but for the time being, we need another way to trace beyond these processing points in the supply chain.

In order to overcome these points of processing, we can use a mass–balance approach. The concept of mass–balance traceability is that at processing points we track volumes of material going into the process and the volumes coming out, rather than tracking the metals themselves.

Consider a smelter purchases five one-ton shipments of tin. One ton from mine A and four from mine B. These mines are both tracked, and the smelter receives tokens from both. After processing we create a new token representing the tin ingots that is linked to all the old tokens. In this way, we consider all the tin to be 20% from mine A and 80% from mine B, with a link to the due diligence information from both mines. This also allows us to claim that the money paid for the ingots is traceable, 20% to mine A and 80% to mine B.
