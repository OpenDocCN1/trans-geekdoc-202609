
Trade takes place on the basis of trust. In ancient days, under barter system, two people will exchange goods in a face-to-face transaction. Each party will assess the quality of the item being traded as there was little or no recourse of return. Later, with the advent of money, the exchange would be facilitated through currency, and there was no need for the two parties to find something of value with the other person to consummate the exchange.

In traditional retail trade, the exchange of money with goods would occur almost simultaneously. If the payment is made in cash, there is no need for a trusted third party to protect the interest of the buyer and seller. However, even in those trivial situations, the party with the higher risk of reneging is usually required to keep their end of the bargain before receiving. For example, when we go through a fast food drive-through, we order the food, we pay cash and soon thereafter we pick up our order. The time lag between payment of cash and getting our order is minimal. However, as you may have noticed when using the drive-through the customer always pays prior to collecting their order. That is, the task of collection of payment is always done prior to the task of fulfillment of the order. Why is this the case? Bluntly, because the merchant does not trust the customer. The merchants believe that there is a non-zero probability that some customers will speed away with their order before paying, if the tasks were reversed, that is the merchandise was given prior to collecting cash. In that case the merchant will have no recourse. If you are wondering, why does the customer pay cash prior to collecting her order? That’s because the customer has a recourse. The restaurant has a building and simply cannot run away! Now it has happened to all of us, sometimes the order isn’t properly filled and some items are missing. In those cases, as customers we do have a recourse, and we inevitably go into the restaurant to complain and get the items we paid for. Likewise, for the same reasons, many gas stations in the United States require the customer to prepay a deposit or provide their credit card prior to filling up gas.

The prior situation is completely reversed when you go to a nice sit-down restaurant. There the customer is served food prior to paying for those. Since one is sitting down within the premises of the restaurant, it is perhaps difficult and a bit embarrassing to leave without paying the bill. The restaurant knows that and hence evaluates the risk of non-payment to be low. In these situations, the restaurant is implicitly trusting the diner, to fulfill their payment obligations after they have had their meal.

In global trade, however, especially for those conducted over the internet, the buyer and seller do not meet face to face. They transact the exchange remotely, and the goods, services or money are exchanged without physical interaction between the buyer and the seller. In these situations, either party requires protection that the other party would fulfill their obligation on the trade. When two honest people cannot trust each other because they have never met, interacted or know each other, both of them need a trusted third party to act as an intermediary to facilitate the exchange. The trusted third party provides implicit assurance to the transacting parties that the terms of the trade would be upheld, and if either party is dissatisfied with the trade, they could reverse it with minimal cost. For this service, the trusted third party collects a small fee for each transaction they underwrite.

The trusted third party can provide that assurance because it has access to the records of the parties and can assure the seller that the buyer has sufficient resources to pay for the purchase. Since the seller cannot directly observe the bank accounts or wealth of the buyer, they require the service of the financial intermediary. What if the wealth of the buyer was public knowledge, which each seller could independently verify, on a real-time basis, whether or not the buyer has the resources to make the purchase, will we still need the trusted third party to act as an intermediary?

Public record of personal wealth is not a new or a modern concept. Even in ancient civilizations, without much modern comfort or trade, people still realized that they needed some measurement of wealth through which everyone can agree that one has the ability to pay and purchase goods or services, in which case the wealth transfers from buyer to the seller. One of the classic cases of development of money, that economists love, is the case of a remote, tiny island called Yap. Yap is located in the Pacific Ocean, east of Philippines and north of Papua New Guinea. Yap had no form of precious metals to convert to currency, but they found limestone deposits on another island, named Palau, hundreds of miles away. Centuries back, the Yap people carved the limestone deposits into giant wheels (see [Fig. 2.2](#CH002.html#fig2)), and they started using these discs as the representation of wealth, or money in our lingo.[⁴](#CH002.html#fn4) The stones are called Rai and the stones are considered extremely valuable in the island of Yap. Since the Rai was difficult to produce and transport, only a few were produced, which stabilized the value of Rai for a long time. The last one was produced in 1931 and since then these stones have generally retained their value in the Yap economy.

The Yap people started using the stone for currency, to purchase provisions and even for dowry. However, unlike paper currency, these stones were heavy, some weighing more than a car – a bit problematic to be carrying these around to exchange. Hence, the concept of money and wealth became more abstract. When an exchange takes place in Yap, the stone isn’t moved, except everyone in the island knows that the stone has a new owner. In fact, everyone in the village knows the current owners of the stones. The noteworthy feature of the stone currency is that it could change ownership without even as much as a mark placed on the stone to indicate the exchange, and the stone wheel remains undisturbed in the former owner’s premises. Nobel Laureate Milton Friedman compared the Yap money with the gold standard.[⁵](#CH002.html#fn5)

## 2.4. CRYPTOCURRENCY – BASIC FEATURES AND CHALLENGES

A purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash would allow online payments to be sent directly from one party to another without going through a financial institution. Unlike real cash, cryptocurrency is stored on the users’ computer or smartphone and not in their wallet. When a buyer makes a purchase through cryptocurrency, they do not physically hand the cash or any tangible object, instead commits to the resource over the internet, akin to transmitting a file.

There is a basic distinction between transferring a computer file versus transferring a physical object. Once I give you cash or another physical object, I no longer have possession of it and cannot give the same item to another individual. However, when I transfer a computer file, say a pdf of this chapter to my editor I am sending her a copy, I still have the original on my computer. Moreover, nothing restricts me from sending the same file to another individual. Extend this example to the domain of cryptocurrency. Transferring cryptocurrency is like transferring a computer file, we are sending a copy and not the original. Therefore we could send the same file to another person. This is called *double-spending*.
