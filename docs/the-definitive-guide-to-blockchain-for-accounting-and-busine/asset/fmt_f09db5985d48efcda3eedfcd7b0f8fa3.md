
In addition to provenance, the agriculture industry could benefit from insurance, crowd-funding and other similar applications on blockchain. For example, agricultural insurance built through smart contracts could link to key weather data provided by government satellites and make immediate payout in instances of drought or flooding. The process of designing, verifying, implementing and enforcing smart contracts for agricultural insurance is in developmental phase with a few pilot implementations as evidence of proof-of concept. These pilot programs have been implemented in Southeast Asia and the Indian sub-continent. However, despite the benefits, the rate of adoption of these products in the rural and illiterate communities remain low.

Land registration of agricultural land in developing nations is another significant application of blockchain technology. In the impoverished regions of the world, illiteracy compounded with low life expectancy, often lead to misappropriation of land. The United Nations Development Program is working in India to make land registry more reliable. While still at a pilot stage, the social implications of the success of this project cannot be overstated.

## 11.8. CONCLUSION
This chapter provided a brief overview of interesting applications of blockchain technology across many industries, from agriculture to insurance and collateralized borrowing. Blockchain’s key feature is that of providing a platform to enable secure, verified digital transactions in real time. Proper harnessing of this feature would lead to cost savings through efficient operations, simultaneously increasing convenience of the users and their trust in the system and records.

The purpose of this chapter was to demonstrate wide ranging applicability and adoption of this revolutionary technology. Additionally, the purpose was to highlight the opportunities in accounting, banking, finance and taxation that this technology creates. As the technology enables microtransactions, more and more transaction would occur, creating demand for accounting and finance professionals. As can be seen through the examples, each one of these is a demonstration of increased demand for highly skilled accounting and finance professionals to help implement and execute these innovations.

## 12.1. INTRODUCTION
The advent of blockchain and distributed ledger technology (BC/DLT) ushers in a whole new asset class and potentially a new methodology and practice for auditing and financial reporting. As outlined throughout this book, this technology is new and different. This technology is disruptive not just to the markets and business processes it impacts, but also to internal controls and the financial reporting process of businesses. Risks, assertions, information systems, business processes and internal controls are all very different in the BC/DLT environment and will need to be addressed with different methods and tools.

This chapter will compare and contrast some of the similarities and profound differences between BC/DLT technology and conventional central database type business systems currently in use in most businesses. Blockchain may well be the “gateway” disruption which will encourage us to replace antiquated assurance methodologies and practices in favor of a much more comprehensive, automated approach. For example, periodic sampling used widely today in auditing, must give way to population-based auditing and monitoring. Blockchains are by design automated networks which leverage analytics and cryptology extensively to facilitate reporting and assurance. Conventional database assurance and reporting often use periodic non-statistical sampling methods simply because it is cost-effective. The effort and resources needed to do population-based auditing or statistical sampling using our current auditing procedures is prohibitive. We need to recognize that even without the advent of BC/DLT our current auditing practice needs to change to adequately address the new and existing risks to operational and financial reporting.

The current methodology and frameworks (i.e., COSO) undergirding auditing are sound and sustainable, but the current audit practice methods are woefully inadequate! Progress is being made in areas such as population monitoring with automated analytics however the adoption rate is ever so slow. This slow adoption rate of automation in auditing and reporting is not just the result of technical challenges, but rather it reflects cultural resistance to change because of the profitable infrastructures which supports the current auditing profession. Auditing fees are often billed based on “bodies and hours” discouraging the use of automation by firms. Complex manual sampling methodologies and practice guidelines are entrenched and encourage antiquated manual auditing methods. Regulators and standard setters do not adequately encourage automated audit practices, holding auditing firms accountable only for the predominately manual processes they have adopted. The technology of analytics and automation has been around for decades and their adoption in re-defining operational and marketing processes has been rapid. Financial services, internet retail, social media advertising and others have embraced structured and unstructured data analytics rapidly while auditing and financial reporting have lagged in leveraging these methods and tools.
