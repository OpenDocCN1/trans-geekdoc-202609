With the recent emergence of a multidisciplinary scholarship on the biases and discriminations of algorithmic systems, the interplay between 'the social' and 'the technical' has become more visible than in the past. One example is the recent book by the information science scholar Safiya Umoja Noble, Algorithms of Oppression (2018), which illustrates how Google Search results tend to reproduce racial and gender stereotypes. Far from being merely 'technical' the unstable socio-technical arrangement of algorithmic systems, web content providers and crowds of googling users on the platform contributes to the discriminatory social representations of African Americans. According to Noble, more than neutrally divided country, the (socio-)technical arrangement of Google Search amplifies and reifies the commodification of black reifies the commodification of black women’s bodies.

I believe that it should be sociology’s job to explain and why and under what circumstances algorithmic systems may behave this way. The theoretical toolkit of ethology mobilized by Rahwan and colleagues (2019) in a quite simple reason: machine learning tools are eminently social animals. They learn from the social – datified, quantified and transformed into computationally processable probabilistic relations among people, objects and information – and then manipulate it, by drawing information. While Rahwan et al. are right in putting forward the ‘scientific study of intelligent machines, not as engineering artefacts, but as class of actors with particular behavioural patterns and ecology’ (2019: 477), their analytical framework focuses on ‘evolutionary’ and ‘environmental’ dimensions only, downplaying the cornerstone of anthropological and sociological explanations, that is culture. Here I argue that in order to understand the causes and implications of algorithmic behaviour, it is necessary to first comprehend how culture enters the code of algorithmic systems, and how it is shaped by algorithms in turn.
