The habitus-driven algorithmic distinctions separating the relevant from the irrelevant, the visible from the invisible, significantly reduce users' digital options, pushing them to interact with the tailored filtered content. (2) Nudging. A high IA and a weak CA generate an unbalanced interaction where the user is oriented by her own inclinations or will. Having a minimal algorithmic awareness, the user barely notices that a technological force is exerted on her. The machine behaves according to encoded logics partially independent of the ones she is guiding, either because of a lack of local training, or due to deliberate design choices. In the first case, repeated negative feedback on the part of the user — for instance, a skipped recommendation or removed ad — can somehow adjust the machine habitus and produce a gradual realignment — as in the case of the aforementioned 'cold start' problem (Celma 2010). Otherwise, and until then, the system opaqueges the user's actions, potentially inducing some sort of behavioural transformation. Here I use the term 'nudge' to indicate algorithmic outputs capable of deviating users from their habitual conduct or way of thinking. Machine learning systems ordinarily nudge platforms through micro-targeted ads, notifications and recommendations, often giving rise to forms of manipulation, regimentation and dataified control (Kellogg, Valentine and Christen 2020; Christen 2020; Darmody and Zwick 2020). The nudge-based exercise of computational authority is particularly effective when users are workers rather than consumers — as in the case of Uber drivers, who cannot simply ignore algorithmic control, since the murky algorithm is the boss (Rosenblatt and Stark 2016). Nudge after nudge, the machine ‘might end changing the user’s immediate actions (e.g. whether to share a news article, or watch a video) but also long-term attitudes and beliefs, simply by controlling the exposure of a user to certain types of content' (Burr, Cristianini and Ladyman 2018: 745). After a prolonged trip into the ‘rabbit hole’ of YouTube’s videos, we might re-emerge ‘radicalized’, since ‘our tastes adjust’ to more and extreme content – argued Tufkci (2018) in the New York Times. Still, algorithmic nudging is not necessarily bad for the user: in fact, it could also represent a chance to burst the pre-digital cultural bubble produced by the habitus’ amor fati – that is, the love for the familiar resulting from the interiorization of social structures (Lizardardo 2013). While this social inertia is technologically reinforced by aligned machines assisting users, misaligned relations have ‘the potential to detach the formation of state from class-related socialisation’ (Webster 2019: 2) – for instance, by nudging a working-class consumer toward the accumulation of a middle-class cultural capital, consisting of ‘legitimate’ movies, books or records (Bourdieu 1984).

We are heading towards the end of this chapter, and it is time to zoom out with the imaginary camera. The interacting users of the previous section should now look like thousands of dots connected by thin lines – such as Figure 4. From this panoramic point of view, it is easier to see how digital platforms ‘may be likened to fields in Bourdusian sense’ (Forcade and Jones 2020; see also Levina and Arriaga 2014).

Inspired by the philosopher Cassirer, Bourdieu saw the real as relational: any element of social life will be defined by the relational systems within which Bourdieu called these systems ‘fields’ (1983, 1989a, 1993, 2002). The value of a novel is inseparable from the historically contingent configuration of the literary field; a ‘legitimate’ US speech in 2020 would have been ‘unimaginable’ according to the common sense (or *doxa*) of the same field; my academic career is evaluated each year in relation to the accomplishments of other scholars; ultimately, every individual and organisational endeavour is structurally entangled with the historically determined relational dynamics.

According to Bourdieu, these are essentially ‘battlefields’ (Bourdieu and Wacquant 1992: 17), spaces of conflict and competition among agents investing economic, social and cultural capital to improve their relative positions of power and status (Bourdieu 1986). Like games, fields have-granted rules, such as the academic ‘publish or perish’ and the neoliberal ‘get rich or die trying’. Like players, social agents possess a practical ‘sense of the game’, are organized in teams, and are committed to winning. They internalized the rules but can still somehow improvise (Bourdieu and Wacquant 1992: 22; Schirato and Roberts 2018: 156).

Digital platforms deliberately encapsulate the fields of social life (Helmond 2015; Cotter 2019; A. Mackenzie 2019; van Dijk 2013). Our social capital is roughly indicated by the number of friends we have on Facebook or followers on Instagram; the symbolic capital of restaurants, hotels and museums is crystallized in the popularity rankings and user-generated reviews featured on TripAdvisor, Yelp;; LinkedIn quantifies users’ professional status and network, while the metrics assigned to scholars in ResearchGate gamify scientific production. We have intuitively experienced how this process of platforms works, and how pervasive it is — to the delight of digital ethnographers and computational social scientists (Salgik 2018).
