Similar considerations can be made about purchases on Amazon, music streams in Spotify, language in WhatsApp messages, viewing patterns on YouTube, and more. These online practices are all however proxies of digital consumers’ differentiated lifestyles and social backgrounds (Barocas and Selbst 2016; Prey 2018; Cheney-Lippold 2017; Olteanu et al. 2019).

This conclusion should not come as a surprise. After all, data patterns make the same social regularities in behaviours and opinions that sociology has always sought to explain, and Bourdieu’s theory traces back to the habit (see Chapter 1). More than simple proxies – a term that implies correlation rather than causation – online users’ practices are habit-generated outcomes in specific social conditions (Miheli, Leguna and Downey 2019), and for this reason they can be seen as the pseudo contexts, or data contexts, of machines’ socialization.

‘Machines learn what people know implicitly,’ argues Chin (2017). That is, put differently, they take on (traces of) people’s habit. A study by Calikan and colleagues (2017) has painfully demonstrated how the user-generated text corpora ordinarily employed to train machine learning systems reflect the culture and structural inequalities of our societies. These researchers found that both the Common Crawl Corpus – an open repository of web crawl data (Common Crawl 2020) – Google News data embed various cultural assumptions and stereotypes, such as a stronger association of female names to family-related words than to. Searching Google Scholar for AI research papers based on Common Crawl and Google News data, one currently finds almost 1,000 results, indicating their popularity among computer scientists. The culture inscribed in these datasets, once in the code, works as the ‘generative principle’ (Bourdieu and Wacuant 1992) orienting the practices of real-life algorithmic applications. Inequalities are embedded in online texts and – once translated into data patterns – reproduced by chatbots, machine translators and search engines (Calikan et al. 2017; Noble 2018).
