Torpignattara. Families get bigger, apartments smaller, while population density grows exponentially. The share of inhabitants with a university degree drops by two thirds, and house prices follow a similar trend. In Torpignattara there are fewer cinemas, theatres and libraries per inhabitant than in Parioli, where residents are richer and healthier (Lelo, Monni and Tomassi 2019). The poorer neighbourhood – ‘city of thousands’, as it has been called (Desiati 2017) – sees forms of social solidarity and belonging. ‘conditions of existence’ (Bourdieu 1977) vary dramatically between these two areas of Rome, so do the cultural schemes and practical reason interiorized and reproduced by their inhabitants – whether human or not.

IAQOS is quite different from the AI systems that we ordinarily encounter in our digital peregrinations. These have commercial goods, such as sorting products in optimal ways, maximizing revenues through subtle nudging techniques, and spying on us. IAQ creators, Oriana and Salvatore, compared these ordinary platform algorithms to battery chickens, enslaved for the sake of production. In their immaterial cages, the placeless infrastructures of mobile apps and digital platforms, there is no neighbourhood, no café, no school. How can they be socialized then? Where? Into what culture? By whom?

This chapter will show that, when applied to the ordering of the social world, all machine learning systems are socialized, based on data contexts that bear the imprint of socially located cultural realities. Input data are not the only source of the culture in the code. In fact, the culturally informed choices and goals of those in developing and programming algorithms are inscribed in their design features, as a sort of deus in machina. However, in the age of deep learning and platform data, machine creators have partly lost control of their own technological products. AI systems are embedded in dynamic feedback loops within defied social environments, whose systemic outcomes are often hard to forecast. Their artificial cognition can be extremely complex and difficult to explain from the outside. As a result, biases and cultural inclinations derived from the social world are technologically spread by automated systems, contrary to their widely supposed neutrality.
