Critical research has mainly dealt with ‘social power’ of algorithms as a one-way effect (Beer 2017), with the risk of putting forward forms of technological determinism – implicit, for instance, in most of the literature about filter bubbles (Bruns 2019: 24). Yet, recent studies show that the outputs of autonomous machines are actively negotiated and problematic by individuals (Velkova and Kaun 2019). Automated music recommendations or micro-targeted ads are not always effective at orienting the taste and consumption of platform users (Siles et al. 2020; Ruckenstein and Granrath 2020; Bucher 2017). Algorithms do not unidirectionally shape our dataied society. Rather, they intervene within it, taking part in situated-material interactions involving both human and non-human agents (Law 1990; D. Mackenzie 2019; Burr, Cristianini and Ladyman 2018; Orlowski 2007; Rose and Jones 2005). Hence, the content of ‘algorithmic culture’ (Striphas 2015) is the emergent outcome of techno-social interaction dynamics. From this point of view, deciding whether or not to click on a recommended book on Amazon represents an instance of human–machine interaction – which is, of course, heavily engineered to serve the commercial goals of platforms. Nonetheless, in this digitally mediated exchange, both the machine learning algorithm and I maintain relative margins of freedom. My reaction to recommendations will be immediately measured differently in our next encounter, also based on feedback. On my end, I will perhaps discover new authors and titles thanks to this particular algorithm, or – as often happens – ignore its automated suggestions.

Paradoxically, since machine learning systems adapt their behaviour probabilistically based on input data, the social outcomes of multiple interactions with users are difficult to predict a priori (Rahwan et al. 2019; Burr, Cristianini and Ladyman 2018; Mackenzie 2015).
