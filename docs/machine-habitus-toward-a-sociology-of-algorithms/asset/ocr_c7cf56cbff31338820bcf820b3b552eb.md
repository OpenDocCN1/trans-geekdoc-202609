Similarly, the fact that black defendants evaluated by COMPAS ‘were 45 percent more likely to be assigned higher risk scores than white defendants’ (Larson et al. 2016) can be traced back to the inclusion of problematic indicators in the software’s simple predictive model. The COMPAS score is based on a 137-point questionnaire administered to people at the time of arrest. The answers to the questions are fed into a linear equation of the type you solved in high school. Severe criminogenic needs, risk factors, are identified. These include ‘educational-vocational-financial debts and achievement skills’, ‘antisocial and prociminal associates’, and ‘familial-marital dysfunctional relationship’. All of these measures are out of poverty. It’s positively Kafkaesque.(Broussard 2018: 156)

Algorithms’ design features are only one source of bias – the easiest to correct, and one that machine creators are clearly responsible for (Roselli, Matthews and Talagala 2019; Dans London 2017; Pedreschi et al. 2018). What stands out in the computer science literature is that deliberate or unconscious actions, intentions and assumptions crystallized as deus in machina account for only a minor part of the possible errors and mishaviours of platform algorithms and AI (Campolo et al. 2017). When a machine learning system becomes racist, sexist or classist, another category of bias is often involved, broadly classed ‘data bias’ (Baeza-Yates 2018; Olteanu et al. 2019; Buolamini and Gebru 2018) ‘training data bias’ (Danks and London 2017). This encompasses the unfair inclinations and flaws inductively acquired from machine trainers (Cavazos et al. 2020; Caliskan, Bryson and Narayanan 2013).
