Similarly, doing cultural sociology in a platform society inevitably entails the – more or less aware – encounter with populations of autonomous agents driven by an ‘algorithmic culture’ (Hallinan and Striphas 2016). Comparable considerations can be made for social science research on topics as diverse as consumption, finance, communication, religion, education, journalism, political behaviour, cities, marketing, sexuality, organizations, and more. As nicely summed up by Kitchin: ‘there is a need to focus on critical and empirical attention on algorithms and the work that they give their increasing importance in shaping social and economic life’ (2017: 14).

For sure, the sociological unveiling of artificial agents – of their encoded goals, dispositions, practices, effects and trajectories – presents significant methodological challenges. While scientists know perfectly how to investigate humans, generally lack the competences and instruments for studying algorithmic systems (Rieder 2017). Things are further complicated by the black-boxed character of technological artefacts (Burrell 2016). Latour and Woolgar reflect on this as early as in Laboratory Life: ‘Once an item of apparatus or a set of gestures is established in the laboratory, it becomes very difficult to effect the traces into a sociological object. The cost of revealing sociological factors [...] is a reflection of the importance of the black box activities of the past’ (1986:259–60). Nevertheless, almost two decades of critical algorithm studies have demonstrated that retransforming algorithms into sociological objects is possible (e.g. Rieder 2017; Kitchin 2017; Geiger 2017). Building on this multidisciplinary literature, here I outline four complementary research directions for a (cultural) sociology of algorithms. Yet, this research strategy is probably too demanding for projects that mainly focus on the code. Following machine creators essentially serves to understand how code is designed and socialized.

A second, more straightforward option is following the users of algorithmic systems, in order to see their practices and reactions (see Table 3). While for understanding users of algorithmic systems, this methodological choice does not allow us to reliably grasp dispositional aspects (Lizardo 2017).

A third approach consists of following the medium meaning the environment where users and machines interact. This is also the motto of the domain of research known as ‘digital methods’, which studies social phenomena online (Rogers 2013). Following the medium serves to track digital infrastructure, focusing on its affordances (see Table 3). Often based on computational methods, this research direction is best suited for studying how techno-social fields evolve.

Lastly, a fourth and certainly less established research direction is to follow the algorithm. This is best illustrated by the analysis of recommended music on YouTube presented at the end of Chapter 3, quantitatively tracing associations. Several other recent papers have taken similar methodological pathways in order to study automated systems (see Table 3).

When this is not the case, for highly personalized ads and recommendations, dedicated data collection tools allow to track machine behaviour anyway – for instance, the Tracking Exposed project (2019), currently working on Facebook, YouTube and Pornhub. In particular, the computational status of artificial social agents should not prevent researchers from following them using qualitative methods (e.g. Geiger 2017), and eventually analysing their outputs in order to explore how they ‘see’ the entangled users (Jacobsen 2020).

## Beyond a sociology of algorithms
