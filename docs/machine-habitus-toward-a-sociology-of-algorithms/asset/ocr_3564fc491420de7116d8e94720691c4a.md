Besides my technologically deterministic speculations,
Crawford and colleagues (2019) from the AI New Institute
at New York University have recently highlighted other, more
pressing and worrisome as issues. As their 2019 report show,
machine learning-based technologies are increasingly used to
hire and monitor employees, determine citizens’ benefits and
social services, with the concrete risk of widening extent
inequalities and power asymmetries. Tech companies such
as Amazon, Microsoft and Google are ‘fighting’ to be in line
massive government contracts to grow the use of AI for
tacking and surveillance’ (Crawford et al. 2019: 11–12).

The public and private rush toward automation technol-
gies is particularly visible in the US (Eubanks 2018). In
October 2020, the city of Phoenix saw the first fully driverless
ridesaring service open to the general public – meaning that
residents can now call Uber-like cars with no one in the
front seat (Krafcik 2020). No less advanced and dystopian
 implementations have taken place in countries like Israel,
Russia and China, as well as in Europe (AlgorithmWatch
2020). Military and policing machine learning applications,
including killer robots, drive massive national investments
worldwide (Pasquale 2020). Behind the responsible facade of
AI ethics themes, corporations and governments continue
to invest in face recognition systems and biased algorithmic
tools (Crawford et al. 2019: 20–1; La Quadrature du Net
2020; AlgorithmWatch2020).
