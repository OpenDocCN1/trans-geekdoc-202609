First, in both cases, the primary socialization process builds on the agent's inner predispositions (Berger and Luckmann 1966). For humans, these being a biological and genetic traits – despite this a debated topic in sociology (Mills and Tropf 2020; Bliss 2018). In the case of machine learning algorithms, these predispositions are architectural and cultural instead; they derive from design choices and, therefore, from the *deus in machina*. This is also true in the case of advanced AI systems: for instance, the hyperparameters of artificial neural networks are decided priori by the machine creators (Kelleher 2019: 80).

Second, both primary socialization and preliminary machine training are supervised by ‘significant others’ (Berger and Luckmann 1966: 66), who control, filter and mediate the experiences by the learning agent. For children, these are normally the parents or other members of the family (Lahir 2019). For machine learning systems, machine creators play a surrogate role – by dictating goals, conducting an A/B testing experiments and, ultimately, deciding whether and when the training phase can be regarded as successful and complete. A socialized machine, quite like a child, not only absorbs a socially conditioned perspective on the social world, but does so ‘in the idiosyncratic coloration’ by the individuals in charge of its primary socialization (Berger and Luckmann 1966: 151).

Third, the worldviews acquired during the primary socialization process aim to be general and transable as possible (Berger and Luckmann 1966: 152), for humans as well as for artificial social agents. Supervised machine learning systems are commonly trained on large global data contexts, in order to learn how to ‘generalize’ – that is, perform well also in unseen data contexts (Mackenzie 2015: 439).
