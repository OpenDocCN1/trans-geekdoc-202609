Observe that $p$ is the estimated expected value and $SE_{\bar{X}}$ is the estimated standard error based on the simulation of taking sample proportions for random samples of size $n = 100$. Compare this value with the standard deviation for the entire bowl, discovered earlier. It is one-tenth the size! This is not a coincidence: the standard error of $X$ is equal to the standard deviation of the population (the bowl) divided by the square root of the sample size. In the case of sample proportions, the standard error of $X$ can also be determined using the formula:

```
$SE(\bar{X}) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$
```

where $p$ is the population proportion and $n$ is the size of our sample. This formula shows that the standard error is inversely proportional to the square root of the sample size: as the sample size increases, the standard error decreases. In our example, the standard error is

```
$SE(\bar{X}) = \sqrt{\frac{0.375 \cdot (1 - 0.375)}{100}} = 0.0484$
```

p <- 0.375
sqrt((1-p)/100)

### 7.3.5 Summary

Let's look at what we have learned about the sampling distribution of the proportion:

- 1. The mean of all the sample proportions will be exactly the same as the population proportion.
- 2. The standard deviation of the sample proportions, also called the standard error, is inversely proportional to the square root of the sample size: the larger the size used to calculate sample proportions, the closer the sample proportions will be from the population proportion, on average.
- 3. As long as the random samples are large enough, the sampling distribution of the sample proportion, or simply the distribution of $X$, will approximate the normal distribution. This is true for sample proportions regardless of the structure of the underlying population; regardless of how many red and white balls are in the bowl, or whether you are performing any other experiment that deals with sample proportions.
