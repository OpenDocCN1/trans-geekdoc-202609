Since life expectancy is always a positive value, we do not provide any interpretation to the intercept in this example. The $b_1$ coefficient is the slope of the regression line; for any country, if the life expectancy were to increase by about one year, we would expect an associated reduction of the fertility rate by about 0.137 units.

## 10.1.2 The Model

As we did in Chapters 8 on confidence intervals and hypothesis testing, we present this problem in the context of a population and associated parameters of interest. We collect a random sample from this population and use it to estimate these parameters. We assume that this population has a response variable (Y), an explanatory variable (X), and there is a statistical relationship between these variables, given by the linear model

## 10.1 The simple regression model

$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot X + \epsilon,$

$\beta_0$ is the population intercept and $\beta_1$ is the population slope. The statistical part of this relationship is given by $\epsilon$, a random variable called the error term. We make additional assumptions about the distribution of the error term. The expected value of the error term is zero, and the assumed standard deviation is equal to a positive constant $\sigma$. We review the meaning of these quantities. If you were to take a large number of observations, the error terms to be greater than zero and sometimes less than zero, but on average, equal to zero. Similarly, some error will be very close to zero and others far from zero, but on average, we would expect them roughly $\sigma$ units away from zero. Recall the square of the standard deviation is called the variance, $Var(\epsilon) = \sigma^2$. The variance of the error term is equal to $\sigma^2$ regardless of $X$. This property is called homoskedasticity or constancy of variance. It will be useful later in our analysis.
