# 7.3.4 Sampling variation: Standard deviation and standard error

Another relevant characteristic observed in Figure 7.13 is how the amount of dispersion or *sampling variation* changes when the sample size changes. While all the histograms have a similar bell-shaped configuration and are centered at the same value, observe when...

+   - the sample size is $n = 25$ (left histogram) the observed sample proportions are about low as 0.1 and as high as 0.65.
- the sample size is $n = 50$ (middle histogram) the observed sample proportions are about low as 0.15 and as high as 0.55.
- the sample size is $n = 100$ (right histogram) the observed sample proportions are about low as 0.20 and as high as 0.55.

As the sample size $n$ increases from 25 to 50 to 100, the variation of the sampling distribution decreases. Thus, the values are clustered more and more tightly around the center of the distribution. In other words, the histogram on Figure 7.13 is more spread out than the one in the middle, which in turn is more spread out than the one on the right.

We know that the center of the distribution is the expected value of $ar{X}$, which is the population proportion. From this, we can quantify this by calculating how far the sample proportions are, on average, from the population proportion. A well-known statistical measurement to quantify dispersion is the *standard deviation*. We discuss how it works before we continue with the sampling variation problem.
