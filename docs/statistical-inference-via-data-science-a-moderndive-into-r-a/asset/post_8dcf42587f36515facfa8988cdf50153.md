## 9.9 Hypothesis Testing

It turns out from New York City in 2023 Alaska flies to seven airports on the West Coast region of the US while Hawaiian only flies to HNL (Honolulu). Given the clear difference in distance from New York City to the West Coast versus New York to Honolulu, it is not surprising that we observe different (statistically significantly different, in fact) air times in flights.

This is a clear example of needing to do more than a simple exploratory data analysis using data visualization and descriptive statistics to get an appropriate conclusion. This is why we highly recommend you perform an EDA of any sample data before running statistical inference methods like confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

### 9.7.3 Problems with p-values

On top of the many misunderstandings about hypothesis testing and p-values we listed in Section 9.5, another unfortunate consequence of the expanded use of p-values and hypothesis testing is a phenomenon known as “p-hacking.” P-hacking is the act of “cherry-picking” only results that are “statistically significant” while dismissing those that are not, even at the expense of the scientific ideas. There are lots of articles written regarding misunderstandings and the problems with p-values. We encourage you to check some of them:

- 1. Misuse of p-values⁴
- 2. What a nerdy debate about p-values shows about science and how to fix it⁵
- 3. Statisticians issue warning over misuse of P values⁶
- 4. You Cannot Trust What You Read About Nutrition⁷
- 5. A Litany of Problems with p-values⁸

Such issues are so problematic that the American Statistical Association (ASA) put out a statement in 2016 titled, “The ASA Statement on Statistical Significance and P-Values,”⁹ with six principles governing the proper use and interpretation of p-values. The ASA released guidance on p-values to improve the conduct and interpretation of quantitative science and to inform the growing emphasis on reproducibility of science.

We as authors prefer the use of confidence intervals for statistical inference, since in our opinion they are much prone to large misinterpretation. However, many fields still use p-values for statistical inference and this is one reason for including them in this text. We encourage you to learn more about “p-hacking” as well as its implication for science.

## 10 Inference for Regression

In this chapter, we revisit the regression model. Having armed ourselves with an understanding of confidence intervals from Chapter 8, we will now study inference for regression.

### 10.1 The linear regression model

We briefly review the regression model introduced in Chapter 5. To understand the relationship between two variables, we use a linear regression model. If the relationship between the explanatory variable $x$ and the dependent variable $y$ is linear, then

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1x + \epsilon$$

where $\beta_0$ is the intercept, $\beta_1$ is the slope, and $\epsilon$ is the error term. In this chapter, we will use linear regression to estimate $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$.

Below is the summary of the two variables. Observe that there are 183 observations without missing values.

| fert_rate | 183 | numeric | 0.9 | 1.6 | 2.0 | 3.2 | 6.6 | 1.15 |

Above we the summary of the two variables. Using simple linear regression between the response variable ($y$) and the explanatory variable ($x$), the regression line is:

$$\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1x_i$$

where $\hat{y}_i$ is the predicted value of $y$ for the $i$th observation, $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ are the estimates of the intercept and slope, respectively. To estimate these coefficients, we use the method of least squares, which minimizes the sum of squared residuals:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

To estimate these coefficients, we use the $lm()$ function in:

```r
simple_model_10 <- simple_data_10 %>%
  lm(fert_rate ~ life_exp)
```
