# 7.4 Second activity: Chocolate-covered almonds

By looking at the weights in the frame almonds_sample, observe $X_1 = 3.0$ grams, $X_2 = 3.9$ grams, $X_3 = 4.0$ grams, and so on. If you view the entire frame, for example running View(almonds_sample) in R, you could check $X_{23} = 3.3$, $X_{24} = 4.4$, and $X_{25} = 3.6$, so the sample mean would be

```
$$\bar{X} = \frac{3.0 + 3.9 + 4.0 + \dots + 3.3 + 4.4 + 3.6}{25} = 3.67$
```

In R:

```
almonds_sample |>>
sumize(sample_mean_weight = mean(weight))
```

```
# A tibble: 1 x 2
replicate sample_mean_weight
<int>dbl
1 3.67
```

So, once this sample was observed, the random variable $ar{X}$ was realized as $ar{X} = 3.67$, the **sample mean** was 3.67 grams. Note that the possible values that $ar{X}$ can take are all the possible sample means from samples of 25 almonds from the bowl. The chances of getting these sample means are determined by the configuration of almond weights in the bowl.

When $ar{X}$ is constructed as the sample mean of a given random sample, the sampling distribution of the sample mean is precisely the distribution of $ar{X}$. In this context, recall what we are interested in determining:

+   1. The center of the *distribution* of $ar{X}$
2. The effect of *sampling variation* in the *distribution* of $ar{X}$ and the effect of the sample size on *sampling variation*
3. The shape of the *distribution* of $ar{X}$

As we did dealing with sample proportions, we use simulations again to produce good approximations of the distribution of $ar{}$, the sample mean weight of almonds. We also work with samples of size 25, 50, and 100 to learn about changes in sample variation when the sample size changes.

This is the process we follow:

+   * we generate 1000 samples,
* calculate the sample means of almond weights, and
* use them to draw histograms.
