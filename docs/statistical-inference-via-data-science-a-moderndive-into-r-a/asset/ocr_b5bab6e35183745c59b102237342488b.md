# 10.2 Theory-based inference for simple linear regression

The population parameters $eta_0$ and $eta_1$ are constants. Similarly, the duration of the $i$th eruption, $x_i$, is known and also constant. Therefore, the expression $eta_0 + eta_1 \cdot x_i$ is a constant. By contrast, $\epsilon_i$ is a normally distributed random variable.

The response $y_i$ (the waiting time for the $i$th eruption to the next) is sum of the constant $eta_0 + eta_1 \cdot x_i$ and the normally distributed random variable $\epsilon_i$. Based on properties of random variables and the normal distribution, we can state that $y_i$ is also a normal distribution random variable with mean equal to $eta_0 + eta_1 \cdot x_i$ and standard deviation equal to $\sigma:

$y_i \sim Normal(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i, \sigma)$

for $i = 1, \dots, n$. Since $\epsilon_i$ and $\epsilon_j$ are independent, $y_i$ and $y_j$ are also independent for any $i \neq j$.

In addition, to Subsection 10.1.5, the least-squares estimator $b_1$ is a linear combination of the random variables $y_1, \dots, y_n$. So $b_1$ is also a random variable! What does this mean? The coefficient for the slope from a *particular sample* of $n$ pairs of duration and waiting times. If we collected a different sample of $n$ pairs, the coefficient for the slope would be different due to *sampling variation*.

Say we hypothetically collect many random samples of duration and waiting times, and using the least-squares method compute the slope $b_1$ for each of these samples. These slopes would form the sampling distribution of $b_1$, which we discussed in Subsection 7.3.4 in the context of sample proportions. What would we learn is that, because $y_1, \dots, y_n$ are normally distributed, $b_1$ is also normally distributed linear combination of random variables. After some calculations that go beyond the scope of this book take into account properties of the expected value and standard deviation of the responses $y_1, \dots, y_n$, it can be shown that:

$b_1 \sim Normal(\beta_1, \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i - \bar{x})^2}})$

That is, $b_1$ is normally distributed with expected value $eta_1$ and standard deviation equal to the expression above (inside the parentheses and after the comma). Similarly, $b_0$ is a linear combination of $y_1, \dots, y_n$ and using properties of the expected value and standard deviation of the responses, we get:

$b_0 \sim Normal(\beta_0, \sigma \sqrt{\frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i - \bar{x})^2}})$
