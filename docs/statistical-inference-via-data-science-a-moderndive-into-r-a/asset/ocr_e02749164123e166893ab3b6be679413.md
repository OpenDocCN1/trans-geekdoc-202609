We recall that due to the Central Limit Theorem described in Subsection 7.3, the sample mean weight of almonds, $ar{X}$, is approximately normally distributed with expected value equal to $\mu$ and standard deviation equal to $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Since the population standard deviation is unknown, we use the standard deviation, $s$, to calculate the standard error $s/\sqrt{n}$. As presented in Subsection 8.1.4, the t-test statistic

$t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{s\sqrt{n}}$

follows a $t$ distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom. Of course, we do not know what $\mu$ is. But since we assume that the null hypothesis is true, we can use this value to obtain the t-test statistic as shown in the code below. Table 9.1 presents these values.

```
almonds_sample_100 |>
summarize(x_bar = mean(weight),
s = sd(weight),
n = length(weight),
t = (x_bar - 3.6)/(s/sqrt(n)))
```

# TABLE 9.1: Sample mean, standard deviation, size, and the t-test statistic

| x_bar | s | n | t |
|---|---|---|--|
| 3.68 | 0.362 | 100 | 2.26 |

The value of $t = 2.26$ is the sample mean standardized that the claim $\mu = 3.6$ grams corresponds to the center of the $t$ distribution ($t = 0$), and the sample mean observed, $ar{x} = 0.36$, corresponds to the $t$ test statistic ($t = 2.26$).

Assuming that the null hypothesis is true ($\mu = 3.6$ grams) how likely is it to observe a sample as extreme as or more extreme than `almonds_sample_100`? Correspondingly, how likely is it to observe a mean as extreme as more extreme than $\bar{x} = 0.36$? Or even, how likely is it to observe a test statistic as $t = 2.26$ units or away from the center of the $t$ distribution?

Because this is a two-sided test, we care about extreme values that are 2.26 away in either direction of the distribution. The shaded regions on both tails of the $t$ distribution in Figure 9.1 represent the probability of these extreme values.
