
Also, a more recent study focusing solely on the impact of large language modeling (LLM) provided remarkable insights. The study focused on more than 1,016 occupations, 19,265 job tasks, and 2,087 daily work activities and applied both expert assessment and LLM approaches to assess the consequences of LLM for each occupation. The study concluded that knowledge-intensive industries are among those most affected by the emergence of generative artificial intelligence (AI), especially those with routine cognitive tasks such as quantitative data collection and analyses, and technical and scientific services (Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock, [2023](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000012)). In other words, it remains clear that social scientific research, foundational and applied, conducted by academics or practitioners is equally likely to be affected by ET. This is corroborated by other analyses (Cotton, Cotton & Shipway, [2023](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000007)). Evaluation as a form of knowledge production applying social science methods is no exception.

Writ large, no profession, or industry, is left unaffected by digital ETs. It is not a question of `whether` an industry and its professions will be affected. It is a question of `how`. According to the aforementioned McKinsey study, the public and social sectors are among those that will be affected the most ([2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000033)). These are the sectors within which evaluation activities prevail. It was documented not too long ago that organizations within these sectors are also vastly dominant procurers of evaluation services in the United States of America (U.S.) and elsewhere ([Kinarsky, 2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000021); Lemire, Fierro, Kinarsky, Fujita-Conrads & Christie, [2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000028)).

It remains debatable to what extent evaluation can be considered a profession in its own right ([Picciotto, 2011](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000053)) and its practice demarcates an industry (Nielsen, Lemire & Christie, [2018a](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000039)). However, it is clear that it is a form of expert knowledge, which is mostly procured, contracted, and delivered in the context of market conditions (Nielsen, Lemire & Christie, [2018a,](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000039) [2018b](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000040)). Here, we understand the market in broad terms, often involving public service organizations as a procurer, partner, or provider of evaluation services.

Procurers demand evaluation services in competition with adjacent forms of knowledge production ([Nielsen & Hunter, 2013](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000045)). [Nielsen et al. (2018c)](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000041) demarcated this form of expert knowledge as “evaluation services \[that\] share an evaluative purpose and are offered through a set of activities informed by specialist evaluative knowledge” (pp. 21–22).

When gauging the interface between ETs on the one hand and evaluation practice on the other hand, one cannot ignore the market forces shaping this interface. Effectively, market dynamics shape `whether` we are evaluating (or demanding other services), `how` we evaluate (ex-ante, ongoing, ex-post – and sometimes with what methods), and `what` we evaluate (the characteristics of the evaluand)*.* The contributions to this anthology are suggestive that ETs already are, or are likely to become, part and parcel of `what` and `how` evaluators evaluate.

However, we must look at its implications from a market perspective. What are the consequences of emerging technology for evaluation as an industry?

In this chapter, I seek to answer this overarching question. More specifically, the research questions are: (1) will ETs affect evaluation as an industry; and (2) if so, how will ETs affect evaluation practice?

To do so, I review the relevant literature on the evaluation market and relate this to the emerging technologies as presented previously by Bruce and her colleagues ([Chapter 2](#c2.xhtml#c2), this volume) and York and Bamberger ([Chapter 3](#c3.xhtml#c3), this volume). I will then relate the findings to a wider discussion on the practice of evaluation.

## Emerging Technologies and Evaluation

In this volume, Bruce, Gandhi and Vandelanotte ([2025](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000004)) and [York and Bamberger (2025)](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000062) provide an overview of the different emerging technologies relevant for evaluation practice. They describe what they are, their potential uses, and pitfalls. I refer to these chapters for further analysis of the extant technologies.

The premise of the anthology, and this article, is that these technologies for data capture, data storage, and data processing will have profound consequences for evaluation practice.

## Characteristics of the Evaluation Market and Its Industry

Research on the commercial aspects of evaluation is scarce. This is paradoxical given that most evaluation practice is carried out under contracted agreements and thus is part of the evaluation market. Nielsen, Lemire and Christie ([2018c](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000041)) mapped the available literature as part of a special issue of New Directions for Evaluation (NDE), which was entirely dedicated to the topic (Nielsen, Lemire & Christie, [2018a](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000039)). They observed a dearth of empirical research on the evaluation market in general. This included major markets such as the United States (U.S.), Canada, United Kingdom (U.K.), and elsewhere.

In their NDE volume, various contributors offered analyses of the largest segments of evaluation commissioners in the U.S., federal government (Lemire, Fierro, Kinarsky, Fujita-Conrads & Christie, [2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000028)), and philanthropies ([Kinarsky, 2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000021)). Peck offered an in-depth analysis of the large evaluation providers in the U.S. market and how they operate ([2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000021)). Hwalek and Straub surveyed the segment of smaller evaluation providers and described how they operate ([2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000018)). Lahey, Elliott and Heath offered an analysis of the Canadian evaluation marketplace ([2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000021)). They focused mainly on the Federal segment. Davies, Morris and Fox also provided a similar analysis of the evaluation market in the U.K. ([2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000010)). Elsewhere, Nielsen and his colleagues analyzed the Danish evaluation market (Nielsen, Lemire & Christie, [2018d](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000042); [Nielsen & Winther, 2014](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000044)). Based on these empirical analyses, Lemire, Nielsen and Christie collated findings and offered a number of conclusions as to the nature of the evaluation market and its actors in general ([2018](#c13.xhtml#re_TNFUK_13_AREV_C013-CIT000028)). I shall return to these in short order.
