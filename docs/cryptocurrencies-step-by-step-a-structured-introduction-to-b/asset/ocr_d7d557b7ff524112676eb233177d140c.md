**Commodity-backed stablecoins** are another type, tied to physical assets like gold or oil. These stablecoins, such as **Paxos Gold (PAXG)**, allow users to own and trade assets traditionally considered safe havens in financial markets. For instance, one PAXG token represents ownership of one ounce of gold stored in secure vaults. Commodity-backed stablecoins enable investors to access physical assets without the logistical challenges of storage and transportation while benefiting from blockchain’s speed and transparency.

A third type is **cryptocurrency-backed stablecoins**, which use other cryptocurrencies as collateral. Examples include **DAI**, issued by the MakerDAO protocol. To maintain its peg to the US dollar, DAI requires users to deposit a higher value of collateral in cryptocurrencies like Ethereum. If the collateral value drops significantly, the system automatically liquidates it to maintain the peg. This over-collateralization ensures stability but comes with the risk of losing collateral during market downturns.

The most innovative yet challenging category is **algorithmic stablecoins**, which maintain their value using smart contracts and supply-demand mechanisms rather than physical or digital reserves. An example is **TerraUSD (UST)**, which previously relied on its native token, LUNA, to stabilize its price through an automated mint-and-burn process. However, the collapse of UST in 2022 highlighted the vulnerabilities of algorithmic models, especially during extreme market conditions. Despite these risks, developers continue to experiment with algorithmic stablecoins, seeking more robust designs.

Stablecoins serve various purposes within the crypto ecosystem and beyond. One of their primary uses is as a **medium of exchange**. Due to their stability, stablecoins are ideal for transactions, especially in regions where local currencies are volatile or depreciating. For example, businesses in countries experiencing hyperinflation often use stablecoins as an alternative to holding rapidly devaluing national currencies. They also provide a means for individuals in such economies to store value and access international markets.

In **decentralized finance (DeFi)**, stablecoins have a foundational role. Many DeFi platforms use stablecoins for lending, borrowing, and liquidity provision. For instance, users can deposit stablecoins into lending protocols like **Aave** or **Compound** to earn interest or use them as collateral for loans. Their stability makes them the preferred choice for DeFi applications, as volatile cryptocurrencies could introduce significant risks to these systems.

Stablecoins also enable **cross-border transactions** with lower fees and faster settlement times than traditional banking systems. Migrant workers, for example, use stablecoins to send remittances to their families without incurring high fees
