A major advantage of DEXs is their **permissionless nature**. Users can trade without creating accounts, undergoing KYC checks, or relying on a central authority. This appeals to those who value privacy and want to maintain full control over their assets. On DEXs, traders retain their private keys, ensuring they are the sole custodians of their funds. However, this also means that if a user loses access to their wallet or private keys, their funds cannot be recovered.

DEXs excel in providing access to a wide variety of tokens, including newly launched or less-known cryptocurrencies. Unlike CEXs, which often require rigorous listing processes, DEXs allow any token that meets their protocol’s standards to be traded. This openness fosters innovation but also introduces risks. Many tokens listed on DEXs lack sufficient vetting, increasing the chances of encountering scams or low-liquidity projects.

One key difference between CEXs and DEXs is the way trades are executed. CEXs use **order books**, where buyers and sellers place bids and offers that the exchange matches. DEXs, on the other hand, use **automated market makers (AMMs)**. AMMs rely on liquidity pools—pools of tokens provided by users—to facilitate trades. Instead of matching orders, trades are executed against these pools, with prices determined by algorithms based on supply and demand. This system ensures constant liquidity but can lead to **slippage**, where the actual trade price deviates from the expected price due to large orders or low pool reserves.

Liquidity is a challenge for many DEXs. While top-tier platforms like Uniswap have robust liquidity pools, smaller DEXs often struggle to attract enough users to maintain competitive pricing. This contrasts with CEXs, which often provide deep liquidity through institutional partnerships and market-making services. For traders handling large volumes or seeking the best prices, CEXs may offer a more reliable experience.

Security is where DEXs shine. Since funds are stored in user wallets rather than on centralized servers, the risk of exchange-wide hacks is virtually eliminated. However, DEXs are not immune to risks. Vulnerabilities in smart contracts have been exploited in the past, leading to significant losses. For example, the **DAO hack** on Ethereum in 2016 demonstrated how a flaw in a smart contract could be exploited. Users must exercise caution and ensure they interact with reputable DEXs and audited smart contracts.

DEXs also face challenges related to **network fees** and transaction speed. Because trades occur on the blockchain, users must pay gas fees for each transaction. On networks like Ethereum, gas fees can be prohibitively high during periods of congestion. Layer 2 solutions and alternative blockchains, such as **Arbitrum, Optimism, and Binance Smart Chain**, are helping to reduce these costs and improve transaction times.
