# Centralized vs. Decentralized Exchanges

Crypto exchanges are platforms where users buy, sell, and trade cryptocurrencies. These platforms come in two main forms: **centralized exchanges (CEXs)** and **decentralized exchanges (DEXs)**. Each serves a different purpose, with unique features, advantages, and risks. Understanding how they work helps users choose the best option based on their needs and goals.

**Centralized exchanges** operate as intermediaries between buyers and sellers. They are managed by organizations that oversee the trading process, provide liquidity, and secure user funds. Some of the largest CEXs, like **Binance**, **Coinbase**, and **Kraken**, dominate the market because of their ease of use, speed, and wide range of services. These exchanges often support fiat-to-crypto trading, allowing users to deposit traditional currencies like USD or EUR and exchange them for cryptocurrencies.

One of the biggest advantages of CEXs is their **user-friendly interface**. For newcomers, CEXs provide a straightforward way to start trading. Most platforms offer features like market charts, live order books, and one-click buying or selling. Additionally, they handle transaction matching and settlement internally, meaning trades are executed quickly. Advanced features, such as margin trading and futures contracts, make CEXs appealing to experienced traders as well.

CEXs also provide **custodial services**, holding user funds in centralized wallets. This is convenient because users don’t have to manage their own private keys. However, this convenience comes with risks. By storing funds on a centralized server, CEXs become attractive targets for hackers. High-profile incidents, such as the **Mt. Gox hack** in 2014 and the **Bitfinex breach** in 2016, resulted in the loss of hundreds of millions of dollars. To mitigate this risk, many exchanges now use **cold storage** for the majority of funds, multi-signature wallets, and insurance policies.

Regulation is another key aspect of CEXs. Most operate under strict compliance with local laws, requiring users to complete **know your customer (KYC)** and anti-money laundering (AML) verification processes. While this fosters trust and helps prevent fraud, it compromises user privacy, as personal information is stored on centralized servers. For those who prioritize anonymity, this can be a significant drawback.
