**Fake initial coin offerings** and token sales are another avenue for fraud. Scammers create convincing websites, whitepapers, and marketing campaigns to mimic legitimate projects, tricking investors into sending funds for non-existent tokens. During the ICO boom of 2017, many fraudulent projects raised millions before disappearing. Even today, scammers exploit the hype around new blockchain technologies like **NFTs** and **metaverse tokens** to launch fake sales.

**Malicious smart contracts** pose a more technical but equally dangerous risk. Some smart contracts are intentionally coded to include backdoors or malicious functions. For example, contracts may allow developers to withdraw funds without users' consent or restrict token holders from selling their assets. These scams exploit the trust users place in blockchain's immutability and transparency. Conducting audits or relying on platforms with strong vetting processes can mitigate this risk.

**Impersonation scams** are widespread on social media platforms like Twitter and Telegram. Scammers pose as influencers, project teams, or customer support representatives, often asking users to send cryptocurrency for a fake giveaway or assistance. These scams prey on trust and urgency, leading victims to act hastily. Official channels and verified accounts are the only reliable sources for interacting with legitimate projects.

Scammers also exploit the **complexity of crypto wallets**. For example, some scam wallets display fake balances or generate addresses controlled by the scammer. When users send funds to or from these wallets, they unknowingly transfer them to the scammer's account. Open-source, well-reviewed wallets reduce the risk of such scams.

**Exchange hacks** are less frequent today but remain a significant concern. Centralized exchanges hold vast amounts of user funds, making them attractive targets for hackers. Incidents like the **Mt. Gox hack**, where over 850,000 Bitcoin were stolen, and the more recent **KuCoin breach**, highlight the importance of using reputable exchanges with strong security measures. While some exchanges reimburse users after hacks, others do not, leaving victims with substantial losses.

In addition to outright scams, the crypto space suffers from **exaggerated claims and misinformation**. Developers and promoters may mislead investors about a project's capabilities, partnerships, or progress. While these claims may not constitute fraud legally, they create unrealistic expectations, leading to disappointment and financial losses. Projects with transparent development roadmaps and regular audits are more trustworthy.
