CBDCs offer several advantages over traditional fiat currencies and payment systems. First, they improve **financial inclusion** by providing access to digital payments for unbanked and underbanked populations. In developing countries, where access to banking services is limited, a CBDC could enable individuals to store and transfer money using a mobile phone without the need for a traditional bank account.

Second, CBDCs enhance the efficiency and speed of **domestic and cross-border payments**. Traditional cross-border payments are often slow and costly due to intermediaries and differing regulations. A CBDC could streamline this process by enabling direct transfers between central banks or designated institutions. Projects like the **mBridge initiative**, a collaboration between the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and several central banks, are exploring cross-border CBDC solutions.

Third, CBDCs provide governments with better tools to enforce **monetary policy** and track economic activity. With a digital currency, central banks can implement targeted measures such as direct stimulus payments or programmable money, where funds are restricted for specific purposes or timeframes. This level of control could help governments respond more effectively to economic crises or changes in consumer behavior.

However, CBDCs also present significant challenges and risks. One major concern is **privacy**. Unlike cash, which allows for anonymous transactions, CBDCs create a digital trail that could be monitored by central banks and governments. While this oversight helps combat financial crimes, it raises questions about surveillance and individual freedoms. Striking the right balance between transparency and privacy will be a critical challenge for CBDC adoption.

Another challenge is the potential impact on the **banking system**. If individuals and businesses move their deposits from commercial banks to central banks in favor of CBDCs, it could disrupt traditional banking models. Commercial banks rely on customer deposits to fund loans and maintain liquidity. A large-scale shift to CBDCs might force banks to seek alternative funding sources, potentially increasing borrowing costs.

Technical infrastructure is another hurdle. Developing a secure, scalable, and user-friendly CBDC system requires significant investment in technology and cybersecurity. Central banks must protect against cyberattacks, counterfeiting, and fraud while ensuring that the system remains accessible to all users, including those with limited digital literacy or access to technology.

Some central banks are exploring **blockchain technology** as the foundation for CBDCs, while others prefer centralized systems. Blockchain offers transparency and immutability, but it also comes with scalability and energy-efficiency concerns. For example, the **Bahamas’ Sand Dollar**, one of the first fully operational CBDCs, uses a centralized system to facilitate quick and low-cost payments across the islands. This approach prioritizes efficiency over decentralization.
