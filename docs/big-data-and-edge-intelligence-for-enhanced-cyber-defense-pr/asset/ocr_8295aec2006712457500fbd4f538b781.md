# ##6.1 INTRODUCTION

In the early hours of May 12, 2017, the world was shaken by a catastrophic cyber-attack launched by WannaCry ransomware [1, 2], a notorious self-propagating malware. The epicenter of this digital onslaught was the British National Health System (NHS) hospital network, where the impact was not short of calamity. Reports estimated that a staggering toll of 50,000 NHS victims fell victim to this malicious assault, bringing healthcare services to the brink of chaos [1]. Yet, the pernicious influence of this ransomware extended beyond the borders of the United Kingdom, rapidly infiltrating networks in over 150 countries across the globe. WannaCry ransomware and its typically gain entry into systems via vulnerabilities of the networks or systems [2]. The malicious behavior of these threats chillingly apparent once they begin to compromise the integrity of a system's file structure. It is at this pivotal juncture that the importance of an intrusion detection system (IDS) becomes abundantly clear.

An IDS, referred to as IDS, serves as the second line of defense against cyber threats, and it plays a crucial role in mitigating the consequences of such attacks [2]. This role comes into sharp focus when the primary line of defense, typically a firewall, proves inadequate in detecting or countering a threat. The WannaCry incident and others let underscore the critical necessity of IDS within the field of networked systems.

In essence, when firewalls fail in their protective duties, IDS step forward as vigilant sentinels [3]. They possess the capacity to detect anomalous activities, identify suspicious patterns, and trigger alerts upon detecting potential threats to the network. This capability is particularly pivotal in safeguarding the integrity, confidentiality, and availability of hosts within a network of interconnected systems.
