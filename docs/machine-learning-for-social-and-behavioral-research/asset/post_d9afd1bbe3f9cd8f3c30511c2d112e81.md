## 4.6 Bayesian Regression

The use of Bayesian estimation is becoming increasingly popular in the social and behavioral sciences. As such, researchers often wonder whether the same types of methods that they have utilized in Frequentist estimation also exist in the Bayesian framework. A full description of Bayesian estimation is well beyond the scope of this book. Instead we present a simple applied example of how lasso regression can be applied in a similar manner using Bayesian estimation.

In Bayesian estimation, results similar to ordinary least squares regression can be achieved by using a noninformative (diffuse) normal prior on the regression coefficients. Extending this to ridge and lasso regularization, the ridge has been shown to be equivalent to the use of a more restrictive normal distribution prior, while the lasso is equivalent to a Laplace distribution prior (Tibshirani, 1996). The lasso estimate is the mode of the posterior while the ridge is the mean. To perform variable selection, it is generally preferable to use sparse extensions of these two methods. In contrast to the comparison of testing 100 values of $\Lambda$ in ridge or lasso regression, in Bayesian regularization it is preferable to use a hierarchical representation of a sparse prior, which only requires running one model that implicitly selects an ideal degree of prior variance. Hierarchical Bayesian currently includes methods such as the Bayesian adaptive lasso, the spike and slab prior, and the horseshoe. We provide a brief application of the horseshoe prior. For more detail, see Van Erp, Oberki, and Mulder (2018).

As a simple application with a subset of 150 observations from the ECLS-dataset, we applied the horseshoe prior with the rsnatarn package, using the specification of Piroönen and Vehtari (2016). This was assessed across four chains, with the posterior distributions displayed in Figure 4.11.

In Figure 4.11, the only regression coefficients for math and knowledge have posterior distributions away from 0. These are the only two parameters that have median estimates away from 0 (rounded to the nearest decimal). Using 95% credible intervals (the Bayesian counterpart to confidence intervals) to select which parameters are nonzero induces more uncertainty into which estimates are nonzero, additional noise parameters that do not include 0. This allows for more subjectivity and theoretical input into which variables are *selected* in terms of performing variable selection, through placing different types of priors on each of the regression coefficients.

Bay regularization can also be used in scenarios where uncertainty exists as to whether a parameter should be included, allowing the user to fit a more complex model, placing priors on parameters that are not of interest, resulting in a more holistic modeling procedure that does not necessarily have to be performed in a series of several steps, removing or adding variables based on the previous model's results.

Although we get results similar to that of the lasso in Frequentist estimation, because we used a smaller subset of the data to speed the computational time (Bayesian estimation typically takes longer with increasing samples), it is necessary to remember that variable selection can depend heavily on the sample size. Larger sample sizes generally result in larger numbers of selected variables.

One of the main benefits of the Bayesian framework is the flexibility it allows users to specify models that reflect not only their theoretical ideas, but also a combination of different aims. For instance, the different forms of regularization discussed earlier, such as the lasso, group lasso, and the lasso for interactions, can also be specified using Bayesian regularization. This requires manual specification, however, the principles described in applying Bayesian regularization to linear regression scale to more complex scenarios such as multilevel or structural equation modeling. Our main point in detailing Bayesian regularization is that the use of Bayesian estimation in statistics and machine learning is becoming more popular and an awareness of the similarities and distinctions of approaches (regularization) across estimation frameworks will be increasingly important.

## 4.7 Summary

In this chapter detailed the application of regularization to regression models, with variable selection as a primary focus. The most commonly applied forms of regularized regression include both the ridge and lasso, which serve different purposes in their application. We explained a number of extensions, including the elastic net, the group lasso, hierarchical lasso, and Bayesian forms of regularization. The most important component of applying regularization is for researchers to clear in their research aims and limitations to their data, as each form of regularization applies to specific facets of these two considerations. While our focus in this chapter was on the application of regularization to univariate outcomes, we discuss the extension of regularization to multivariate outcomes in Chapter 8, with modeling measurement error (Chapter 7) as a specific consideration.

### 4.7.2 Computational Time and Resources

The runtime for the algorithms detailed in this chapter vary greatly. On the quick end, the application of regularized regression with the glmnet package takes only a matter of seconds, even when testing the default of 10 penalties. This amount of time can increase as the number of predictors increases, but it rarely longer than a minutes. On the slow end is the hierarchical lasso, with models often taking on the order of hours to fit a small number of penalties (i.e., 10). The authors have tested this method (as implemented in multiple packages) across a variety of empirical problems, and on more than one occasion been unable to get models to finish, as the runtimes were on the order of days. In between regularized regression and the hierarchical lasso lie stability selection and the forms of the group lasso, with both methods being closer to regularized regression in run times. Bayesian estimation almost takes longer, but there are a number of front-end packages that have been developed that require less programming and model specification, have built-in functions to implement shrinkage priors, and drastically cut the runtimes. Both the brms (Bürkner, 2017) and stanarm (Goodrich, Gabry, Ali, & Brilleman, 2022) packages for implementing Bayesian regression. Both packages recommend the use of the horseshoe prior for regularization.
