```
ridge = \text{OLS} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_{j}^2, \quad (4.4)
```

where $\lambda$ is the penalty that controls the amount of shrinkage. Note that when $\lambda = 0$, Equation 4.3 reduces to ordinary least squares regression. As $\lambda$ increases, the $eta_j$ coefficients are shrunk toward 0. The counterpart to ridge lasso regression, is defined as

```
lasso = \text{OLS} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_{j}|. \quad (4.5)
```

In Equation 4.5 the absolute value of each $eta_j$ coefficient is multiplied by a parameter, $\lambda$. It quantifies the influence of the lasso penalty on the overall model fit—as $\lambda$ increases, a steeper penalty is incurred for each parameter, which results in greater shrinkage of the coefficient sizes. It is common to test a range of $\lambda$ values, combined with cross-validation, to examine what the most appropriate degree of regularization is for a given dataset.

In contrast to the lasso, the ridge penalty takes the sum of the squared coefficients. Whereas the lasso will push the all the way to 0 (as any non zero beta will contribute to the penalty term), the ridge penalty will instead shrink coefficients, but not necessarily all the way to 0, as the squaring operation means small betas incur negligible penalties. Both methods have benefits and drawbacks (see James et al., 2013), and can be seen as reflecting diverging views on the nature of the underlying association (i.e., whether the coefficients are sparse). One benefit of regularization is that it better handles multicollinearity among all predictors.

In effort to combine the variable selection aspects of the lasso with the ability to handle multicollinearity from ridge regularization, Zou and Hastie (2005) proposed the elastic net (enet) regularization. Through the use of a mixing parameter, $\alpha$, the elastic net combines both ridge and lasso

```
enet = \text{OLS} + (1-\alpha)\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_{j}^2 + \alpha\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_{j}|. \quad (4.6)
```
