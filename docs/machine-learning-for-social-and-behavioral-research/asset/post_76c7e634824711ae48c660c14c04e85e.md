In some research scenarios there is little flexibility with regard to description. One example is the case of a single predictor—there are a limited number of options for modeling this single relationship. At the other extreme when the number of predictors is extremely large. For example, if the predictors represent a large battery of depression scales, with each scale including 10 to 30 items, there is likely to be a large number of small effects. If the sample size is large, a majority of these coefficients are likely to be significant and contribute to prediction. As an example, Mottus and Rozgonjuk (2019) found that increasing the number of personality items included to predict age out-of-sample predictive performance, such that 300 items predicted better than 120, which predicted better than 30, which improved upon just using Big Five domains. In an example as this, though each of the 300 individual relationships can be examined, and interpreted, one must take into account the effects of the other items that contribute to the prediction of Y, making description in a linear model less clear. On the other hand, summed scores could be created for each scale, thus simplifying the number of parameters in the linear model. However, just as in the above example, this always results in a decline in performance, making it unlikely that both prediction and explanation can be maximized with one method. This highlights that there is often flexibility not only with the algorithms that are tested, but also with the predictors included, and whether summaries of the predictors are used (e.g., summed scores), with each choice having consequences for both prediction and description.

Often, the distinction between description and explanation is less than perfect. A statistical method that exemplifies this is structural equation models (SEMs). Discussed further in Chapters 7 through 10, SEMs involve the specification of regression equations between latent and observed variables, while often imposing a number of weak and strong constraints. The imposition of constraints implies causal relationships between variables (Bollen & Pearson, 2013). As a result, using SEMs favoring explanation over description given the requirements of imposing specific constraints into the model.

The final point we make with respect to the second principle is that research often does not involve a single study or paper, but a series of steps, with each step encompassing multiple goals. As an example, in relation to machine learning, this may involve first assessing which variables contribute to the prediction of Y (falling under both description and prediction), and then in follow-up studies, assessing these selected variables across multiple time points to ascertain causal relations with Y. Further, we can include prediction as a part of these follow-up studies to determine the magnitude of effect for Y. Notably, the neglect of incorporating aspects of prediction into explanatory research is one of the causes of the replication crisis (e.g., Yankoni & Westfall, 2017). The benefit of combining explanation and prediction is well described in Shmueli (2010): “The consequence of including predictive modeling and testing alongside exploratory modeling is losing the ability to test the relevance of existing theories and to discover new causal mechanisms.”

## 2.5 Principle #3: Labeling a Study as Exploratory or Confirmatory Is Too Simplistic

Often, the terms exploratory or confirmatory are used to provide a metascription of the research conducted and to conduct research that lies on the continuum between exploratory and confirmatory. Although this continuum is not difficult to understand, at the very least, most researchers agree on the research that falls at the end points. Fully confirmatory research entails full theoretical justification for study design and the analyses performed (these studies are often experimental in nature), whereas exploratory research lacks theoretical justification and is described as the “kitchen sink” approach. However, difficulties arise in assessing research: theoretical justification for variable inclusion, not what types of relationships are examined; partial inclusion of nonoretically justified variables; transforming a variable-based sample distribution; and studies with little theoretical justification, to name a few. As described above, this becomes increasingly difficult as the number of variables increase and as the algorithm options expand.

Machine learning is often described as the kitchen sink approach: just throw everything in and see what comes out. This is often depicted as the miners of the 1800s holding up a golden nugget, data mining. In practice, researchers often use theoretical justification for the sample and variables analyzed, and may even hypothesize on the presence of specific interactions. However, there are no hypotheses for every variable included, which variables will be most important, and the degree of prediction error. Obviously, these characteristics do not just justify a label of confirmatory research, and because there is some theoretical justification, it is not entirely exploratory research either. But how much confirmatory (or less exploratory) is this than the kitchen sink approach?

Instead of labeling an analytical approach according to this continuum, we instead advocate for fully describing the theoretical justification for each aspect of the analysis and approach. As an example, the predictors included based on theory? If a subset is, which ones? There are advantages and disadvantages to different degrees of theoretical specification. For instance, only including justified predictors can increase one’s confidence in the generalizability, whereas justified predictors offer an opportunity to discover associations. This has important consequences for the resulting conclusions, while not neatly fitting into a dichotomy between exploratory and confirmatory.

This dichotomy is typically applied to algorithm as well, with linear regression being a confirmatory (causal) approach, and algorithms, such as random forests, purely exploratory. This becomes particularly difficult for models that are not supervised in nature, such as certain text mining.

### 2.5.1 Model Size

The question of assessing the degree of confirmation becomes more difficult in the case of complex models, such as SEMs, dynamical systems, and network models. Simply put, the simplicity of stating and testing hypotheses in traditional experimental designs does not translate to complex models. As the number of variables increases, and the models grow in size, it becomes increasingly difficult to specify hypotheses that account for every aspect of the model. Simply stating a one-line hypothesis and one-line alternative hypothesis no longer holds. With larger models comes more ways to wrong, thus rejecting a hypothesis. As a result, conformity comes to mean something else in these situations, taken as a straw man argument. Instead, by saying confirmatory really mean a minimum degree of specification—not exploratory. This difficulty is a contributing factor for why there is a dearth of complex models in social and behavioral publications. This leads to the specification of many simple relationships. For instance, Lieberman and Cunningham (2009) found that a randomly issue of the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology had an average of 93 statistical tests performed per paper.

An additional contributing factor is an explicit bias against complexity—thus there is a preference for parsimony. One hallmark of this viewpoint comes from Box (1976):

> Since all models are wrong the scientist cannot obtain a "correct" one by excessive elaboration. On the contrary William of Ockham he seek an economical description of natural phenomena. Just as the ability to devise simple but evocative models is the signature of the greatest scientist so-elaboration and overparameterization is often the mark of mediocrity. (p. 792)

Selecting the correct model is indeed extremely difficult and there are a number of competing factors that influence model selection (see Chapter 3); however, this is in contrast to more recent emphasis on embracing complexity (e.g., Fried & Robinaugh, 2020). If you think of brain functioning as an infinitely complex set of interactions with Brodmann, 1994, the question becomes, how can we understand various subspecies without the use of extremely complex algorithms or models?
