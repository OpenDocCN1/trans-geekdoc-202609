Although there is some variability in the parameter estimates across the three models, with x4 denoted as a significant predictor in the second sample of 100, for the most part, our conclusions stay the same. However, when we add variable selection to the process, our conclusions can vary greatly. Adding backselection selection to choose which variables should be in the model (using the Akaike information criterion to choose a final model), we get additional uncertainty. In the first sample, variables x4 and x5 are chosen, in the second sample x3, x4, and x6, in the full sample x2 and x4. As we can see, it is the act of variable selection and dichotomous cutting that induce instability in the resulting models, not just the use of binary splits for decision trees.

Although there have been many suggestions for overcoming the instability of decision trees, the method that has generated the most research is that of bootstrap aggregating (bagging; Breiman, 1996a). The general idea is that of creating one tree, many (hundreds or thousands) are created on bootstrapped samples taken from the original dataset. This topic be the focus of the following chapter, along with extensions of this concept. The main drawback is that the creation of a host of trees makes the results more stable, we no longer have a single structure to interpret. Because the goal of many research projects is the creation of a single, interpretable tree structure, our focus for the rest of the chapter is only on methods that address stability, while resulting in a single tree.
