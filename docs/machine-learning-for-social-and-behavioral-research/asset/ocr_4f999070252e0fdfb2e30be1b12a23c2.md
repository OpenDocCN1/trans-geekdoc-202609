In the second stage, $\lambda$ ranged from 3,500 to 6,500 in steps of 30. The optimal penalty value was chosen based on the BIC-500, a measure of model fit proposed by Grimm et al. (2022) and represents the value of the Bayesian information criterion for the model if the sample size was 500. This was proposed to balance model fit and the penalty for each parameter in the BIC, which is small for high sample sizes like we have in the ECLS-K data. We applied the same two-stage approach to determine an optimal value of $\lambda$ when examining the effects to the slope.

The optimal $\lambda$ for the intercept was 5,000, which shrunk all of the effects to zero with the exception of father's education and poverty status. These effects were retained while the effects to the random slope were penalized to determine an optimal value of $\lambda$ for these effects. In the first stage, $\lambda$ values ranged from 0 to 8,000 in steps of 250. In the second stage we examined $\lambda$ values between 2,500 and 7,000 in steps of 50. The $\lambda$ value with the lowest BIC-500 was 700 with no effects to the slope retained. This is fairly common in mixed-effects models because there is much less power to detect effects on random slopes compared to random intercepts. Thus, it may make sense to have different criteria to determine which effects to retain when predicting random slopes versus random intercepts, particularly if the goal is to retain parameters of a given effect size.

The lasso mixed-effects model retained two effects on the random intercept (*father's education and poverty status*) and no effects on the random slope when using the BIC-500 as the selection criterion. That certain effects were retained, a mixed-effects model was fit using the lme package with only father's education and poverty status as predictors of the random intercept. This approach follows the relaxed lasso (Meinshaus, 2007), and lets us retain the natural scale...; however, we subtracted 1 from father's education and poverty status, so a value of 0 was observed. Father's education and poverty status had a positive effect on the intercept (children above the federal poverty line had higher intercepts than those below the federal poverty line).
