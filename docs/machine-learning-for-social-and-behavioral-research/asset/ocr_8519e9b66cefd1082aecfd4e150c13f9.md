# 11.3 Basic Text Mining

Text data can contain a lot of information. Before we conduct more complex analysis, we first show how we can mine some simple, but useful, information from text data. The teaching evaluation data do not include direct information about the gender of the professors. In practical data analysis, such information can be very useful. Although the gender of individual professors can be identified indirectly using the narrative comments because student often uses words that reflect the gender of a professor. For example, a comment from one student was given:

> My favorite Professor by far. He is very helpful and willing to help out the best he can. He will not just give you answers however, but help guide you so that you can learn it on your own. I recommend taking him for whatever math courses you can. He requires a lot of work, but it pays off in the run!

In the comment, the student used pronouns “he” four times and “him” once. This clearly indicates that the professor is male even though the direct sex information was not collected.

As another example, see the comment below from another student on a different professor. In the comment, there are gender pronouns “she” (six times) and “her” (one time). Therefore, one can confidently assume that the professor is female.

> She is one of the best voice teachers I have ever had. She may not be able to sing like she used to, but she sure how the voice works. And she knows that everyone is different so she doesn't use the same methods with everyone. I love her!!!

Certainly, if we use one comment to identify the gender of a professor, we can have a lot of uncertainty especially when gender pronouns were not used often. One the other hand, if multiple students used the same gender pronouns to describe a professor, the results are cross-validated and can be more accurate.
