### 12.6.7 Latent Space Model

Another class of network models is constructed around latent attributes rather than the observed attributes in ERGM (Snijders, 2011). One such model is the latent Euclidean space model proposed by Hoff, Raftery, and Handcock (2002). In the model, each node is assumed to be associated with a latent position in a Euclidean space. The distance of two nodes in the latent space is related to how likely they are connected in the network. $z$ be a vector of latent positions of node $i$ in the latent space. For nodes $i$ and $j$, the Euclidean distance between them is:

```latex
$d(i, j) = \sqrt{(z_i - z_j)(z_i - z_j)} = \sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^{D}(z_{id} - z_{jd})^2} \quad (12.7)$
```

where the symbol $(\cdot)$ is the transpose of the matrix or vector, $D$ is the dimension of the Euclidean space, $z_i = (z_{i1}, z_{i2}, \dots, z_{iD})$ and $z_j = (z_{j1}, z_{j2}, \dots, z_{jD})$ are the latent positions of nodes $i$ and $j$, respectively. With this distance function, the latent space model can be written as

```latex
$$\begin{cases} a_{ij} & \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_{ij}) \\ \text{logit}(p_{ij}) &= \alpha + \beta h'_{ij} - d_{ij} \end{cases} \quad (12.8)$$
```

where $\alpha$ is an intercept, $h_{ij}$ is a vector of covariates and $\beta$ is the associated coefficients of the covariates. The covariates can be related to the nodes/or edges of the network. The coefficient for $d_{ij}$ is fixed as $-1$. Therefore, the closer two nodes are in the latent space, the higher the probability for them to be related after controlling the covariates in the model.

To fit the latent space model, we need to find the dimension of the latent space. One way to determine it is to compare models with different dimensions. For example, for the latent space data, we fit the model with $D=1$:

```latex
$$\begin{cases} a_{ij} & \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_{ij}) \\ \text{logit}(p_{ij}) &= \alpha + \beta h'_{ij} - \sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^{D}(z_{id} - z_{jd})^2} \end{cases}$$
```

The BIC for the model is plotted in Figure 12.12. Clearly, the model with two dimensions has the smallest BIC. This means that the friendship can be best explained using a two-dimensional latent space.

## Summary

In this chapter, we introduced the basic methods for network analysis. Network data are different from traditional data in that nodes are naturally interrelated. Network visualization is a critical component, and should be the first step in network analysis. Although one can extract information from a network and use it in a statistical model, many network modeling methods are available. For example, exponential random graph models and latent space models are currently widely used.

### 12.7. Further Reading

- This chapter provides only a limited sample of the literature on social network analysis. For a more complete treatment of network analysis, one can consult the review paper by Snijders (2011) and books as Carrington, Scott, and Wasserman (2005); Scott (2017); Wasserman and Faust (1994). Many newly developed methods, such as network analysis with mediation Che et al. (2020); Liu et al. (2018) were not discussed in this chapter.
- The analysis in this chapter has focused on a binary, undirected social network. However, the methods can be easily adapted for valued, directed networks. Furthermore, network analysis is not limited to social networks, but can be extended to many other networks such as psychological networks (Fried et al., 2017) and biological networks (Barabási, Gulbache, & Loscalco, 2011).
- The chapter only discussed cross-sectional networks. Longitudinal networks and associated analytical methods are available such as the temporal ERGM (Hanneke & Xing, 2007; Krivitsky & Handcock, 2014), the stochastic actor-oriented models (Snijders, van Bunt, & Steglich, 2010), and the dynamic network analysis (Sewell & Chen, 2015).
