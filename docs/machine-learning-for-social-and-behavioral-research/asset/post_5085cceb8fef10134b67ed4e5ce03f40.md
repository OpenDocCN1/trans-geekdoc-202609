# 10.3 Structural Equation Model Trees

In Chapter 9 we discussed the pairing of mixed models and decision trees as a mechanism for identifying groups in longitudinal data. In this section we provide an overview of pairing decision trees with SEM in the form of SEM trees (Brandmaier et al., 2013) and forests (Brandmaier et al., 2016). These two methods are within the same family of other multivariate methods developed as an integration between psychometrics and decision trees (Breiman et al., 1984), including the use of unconstrained covariance matrices as outcomes (Miller et al., 2016) and nonlinear mixed-effects models (Stegmann et al., 2018), among others. As SEM can be a general family of models, namely, including a number of models for longitudinal data, SEM trees can be used in similar ways to those methods described in Chapter 9. For the purpose of this chapter, we just focus on the use of SEM for cross-sectional data.

An additional component of decision trees, outside of identifying nonlinear associations, is that observations are placed into terminal nodes, with each terminal node forming a subgroup. Given this, decision trees have also been used based on values of the predictors. Conceptualized as a form of exploratory multiple grouping method (Jacobucci et al., 2017), SEM trees provide a structural equation model as outcome with using decision trees to identify a grouping structure that improves the fit of the model. This grouping structure can be the result of one or more dichotomous splits on one predictor or predictors, multiple dichotomous splits form step functions between the predictors in the tree and the outcome criteria, which is the deviance in the case of SEM trees. The use of step functions allows decision trees and SEM trees to fit highly nonlinear structures. However, the use of single decision trees results in the drawbacks of less optimal prediction and instability of the structure. Just as decision trees have been generalized to random forests (Breiman, 2001a), fitting hundreds or thousands of trees using a subset of predictors for each tree, the same has occurred for generalizing SEM to SEM forests. Although an interpretable tree structure is lost, the effects of predictors across the hundreds of trees can be condensed into variable importance metrics, allowing researchers to gauge the relative influence of each variable.

# 10.3.2 Algorithm Details

The SEM trees algorithm is implemented in the semtree package (Brandmaier et al., 2022) in R. Prior to running SEM trees, researchers need to first fit the outcome model, which can take any form of SEM. The SEM can be tested and paired with semree using either the lavaan (Rossel, 2012) or (Boker et al., 2011) packages. We note, however, that semree was originally designed for OpenMx and in practice better than OpenMx compared to lavaan.

A number of tuning parameters are available for the SEM trees algorithm. Although we term these options parameters, we do not mean that researchers should test multiple values of each. Instead, the tests should be based on best practices and theoretical considerations given the nature of heterogeneity. One of the options is the splitting criterion (method = in semree.control()). The default, naive, compares all possible split values across the predictor,, the cutoff that maximizes the likelihood ratio. Cross-validation (method = "cross-validation") defaults to 5-fold CV splits the data into partitions, testing the improvement in fit due to cutoff on a holdout set of observations. In practice, this procedure can be extremely intensive from a computational standpoint. The procedure that we recommend in most scenarios is the fair criterion. This method proceeds in two steps: The first step is to split the sample into two, using the first half of the sample to estimate the improvement in fit associated with each cutoff, associating a single best cutoff for predictor. In the second step, the second half of the sample is used to test the best cutoff for each predictor. This procedure for selecting cutoffs overcomes some of the bias of decision decision for selecting cutoffs from predictors with more response options (possible cutoffs). Given this, we recommend using the fair criterion.

The second tuning parameter to consider setting is the minimum sample size. It is important to note that an SEM is estimated in each node, and depending on the complexity of the specified SEM, this could lead to testing complex SEMs with inadequate sample sizes. Therefore, we recommend setting a minimum sample size for each node (min.N = in semree.control()), with 100 being a reasonable default for most SEMs.
