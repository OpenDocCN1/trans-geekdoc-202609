## 1.2 Why Do We Need Machine Learning?

There have been reviews of machine learning and calls for its increased use in most area of social and behavioral research, such as clinical psychology (e.g., Dwyer, Falkai, & Koutsouterlis, 2018), political science (e.g., Grimm, 2015), economics (Athey, 2018), and sociology (McFarland, Lewis, & Goldberg, 2016). The natural question is why? The easiest explanation is the increased availability of large datasets, which are facilitated by new collection methodologies, openly available dataset repositories, and distributed laboratories (see Moshontz et al., 2018). While data represents one piece of the pie, an equally important rationale for the use of machine learning is the increasing support for the idea that many associations in the social and behavioral sciences are more complex than the models previously considered. Complexity can mean many things, but researchers have discovered complexity in the number of variables (e.g., Kendlier, 2019), the nature of modeled associations (i.e., interactive and nonlinear effects; Van Doorn, Verhoef, & Bijmjolt, 2007), heterogeneous (e.g., Ram & Grimm, 2009), or the existence of dynamic changes over time (e.g., Cole, Bendezu, Ram, & Chow, 2017). Each chapter in this book either lays the groundwork for assessing complex associations or directly addresses at least one of these regarding complexity.

### 1.2.1 Machine Learning Thesis

We believe that part of the motivation for machine learning lies in the thesis that psychological phenomena are extremely complex, possibly infinitely complex (Lin, Tegmark, & Rosnik, 2017; Jolly & Chang, 2019), requiring large numbers of variables, each likely producing small effects (Götz, Gosling, & Rentrow, 2021). Thus, the thinking is that complex algorithms must improve our modeling of data. This has undoubtedly been the case in a large number of published articles, there has concurrently with a surprising number of studies that have found equal predictive performance for linear models relative to machine learning algorithms that search for nonlinear and interactive effects (see Christodoulou et al., 2019; Jacobucci et al., 2021). In contrast to the notion that these studies disprove our complexity thesis, we instead view these studies as informative for elucidating various limitations regarding study design. For example, the predictors and/or outcome may contain too much measurement error (Jacobucci & Grimm, 2020), to be represented as latent variables and not to assess unique entities (the idea behind constitutive versus; Kendall, 2017), have limitations regarding the number and predictors which were included, contain a sample size that was too small, among many others. Our point here is that this area of research is too new to jump to premature conclusions. Instead, of blaming the algorithm or the underlying thesis, we believe that more work is needed to understand how study design contributes to predictive performance. Despite supporting the complexity thesis above, it is simultaneously necessary to prevent the proffered of what could be termed a naive complexity — simply put, for example, studies that assess various environmental factors for understanding depression will likely not assess important genetic contributors. This can be referred to as integrative pluralism (Mitchell, 2009) and requires assessing the rules for combining various cases across multiple ontological levels.

## 1.3 How Is This Book Different?

The authors all have backgrounds in psychology and have worked with a wide variety of datasets coming from multiple areas of social and behavioral research. This means the following distinctions from most machine learning books:

- A focus on datasets with more variables than sample size. Our research domains rarely see datasets of this form.
- Greater focus on multivariate outcomes and specifically on imposing structure on the outcome variables. Each author is an expert in latent variable modeling and this expertise is infused into multiple book chapters.
- Greater focus on dealing with measurement error which is incorporated into multiple chapters. Simply put, most of the data that we analyze have non-negligible measurement error which can have a large effect on our ability to accurately model associations in the data.
- Additional detail on the replication, stability, and reporting of results to increase the chances that the findings are published. Our goal is that readers of the book can take what they learn and apply it to their own data instead of the aim of publishing their findings.

Additionally, instead of attempting to cover machine learning comprehensively, we take a narrower focus in primarily detailing regularization, decision trees, and ensembles of decision trees. We describe each of these in Chapters 4 through 6, and discuss how these algorithms have been combined with latent variable models in Chapters 8 through 10. By focusing on these algorithms, we omit other commonly used families of algorithms, most notably vector machines and deep learning. Support vector machines have a long history of being applied in neuroimaging data, whereas deep learning has become the state-of-the-art for image recognition and natural language processing. While we expect features of this book to include deep learning, we opted to omit both deep learning and support vector machines due to their lack of interpretation and absence in being integrated with latent variable models. Readers are referred to James et al. (2013) for an accessible introduction to support vector machines and Cholelet and Allaire (2018) for an introduction to deep learning. One may question why we primarily detail algorithms that have not changed, to a large extent, since the 1980s, but to put it simply, little progress has been made in prediction performance in more standard datasets (e.g., Hand, 2006). Similar things could be said in the context of variable selection (cf. Hastie, Tibshirani, and Tibshirani, 2020).

### 1.3.1 Prerequisites for the Book

Our goal in writing this book was to provide a background for understanding published machine learning papers or reference for researchers to publish journal articles using machine learning. With this goal, we wrote the book to be used as a standalone reference or to be used as part of a graduate machine learning course. Thus, the first part of the book requires readers to have at least a graduate level course or be familiar with that level of material. Chapter 4 specifically covers regression but does not provide a background on linear regression and only a small detail regarding the use of regression for alternative outcome types. Chapters 7 through 10 focus heavily on the use of latent variables. Chapter 7 provides a background on the use of factor analysis, but it is relatively brief. If readers wish to supplement this material, we recommend Beaujean (2014). Further, these chapters focus on longitudinal data. For further detail on the use of growth curves or multilevel models we recommend Grimm, Ram, and Estabrook (2016). We do not expect readers to have background in either text mining (Chapter 11) or social network analysis (Chapter 12), so these chapters were written at an introductory level.

## 1.4 Definitions

### 1.4.1 Model vs. Algorithm

One of the confusing pieces of terminology in the realm of machine learning is the use of *model* or *algorithm* when referring to specific aspects of the analysis. To put it simply, the *algorithm* refers to the type of method (e.g., linear regression, random forest), whereas the term *model* refers to the results of fitting an algorithm to data. Thus, fitting an *algorithm* to data yields the *model*, and a model can take data as input and provide predictions. Linear regression is an algorithm. After fitting this algorithm to data, we get a model, such as: $\hat{y}_i = 1.3 + 0.3 \cdot x_{ij}$. The same thing occurs for more advanced algorithms as the resulting model can take data as input and generate a predicted value for each observation. The fitting process typically requires a higher computational burden, and the relationship between input and output is more complex, but the procedure across algorithms is similar.

### 1.4.2 Prediction

To define what we mean by prediction, we first define what we mean by the *terms*. In the simplest case, we can use a single sample and fit a linear regression model, $y = x\beta + e$, deriving estimates for each of the unknown coefficients, comprising the vector $\beta$. With this, we now have estimates that can be used to create predictions for the observations in this sample, $\hat{y} = x\hat{\beta}$, where $\hat{y}$ is a vector of predictions for our sample. Additionally, we follow Shmueli (2010) in assigning the term *predictive model* to the regression equation that produces $\beta$. More specifically, we can generally refer to the predictive model as $f(x)$, differentiating this from $f(x)$, as $f(x)$ is estimated from the data $x$ and $y$, whereas $f(x)$ refers to a general function, such as a linear regression. Because we are using the same sample to estimate $\beta$ and $\hat{y}$, there will be some form of positive bias in our predictive performance, such as $R^2$. Instead, we can use separate datasets to estimate $\hat{\beta}$ as we do to test $\hat{y}$.
