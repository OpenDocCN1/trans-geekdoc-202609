As SEM can be a general family of models, namely, including a number of models for longitudinal data, SEM trees can be used in similar ways to those methods described in Chapter 9. For the purpose of this chapter, we just focus on the use of SEM for cross-sectional data.

An additional component of decision trees, outside of identifying nonlinear associations, is that observations are placed into terminal nodes, with each terminal node forming a subgroup. Given this, decision trees have also been used based on values of the predictors. Conceptualized as a form of exploratory multiple grouping method (Jacobucci et al., 2017), SEM trees provide a structural equation model as outcome with using decision trees to identify a grouping structure that improves the fit of the model. This grouping structure can be the result of one or more dichotomous splits on one predictor or predictors, multiple dichotomous splits form step functions between the predictors in the tree and the outcome criteria, which is the deviance in the case of SEM trees. The use of step functions allows decision trees and SEM trees to fit highly nonlinear structures. However, the use of single decision trees results in the drawbacks of less optimal prediction and instability of the structure. Just as decision trees have been generalized to random forests (Breiman, 2001a), fitting hundreds or thousands of trees using a subset of predictors for each tree, the same has occurred for generalizing SEM to SEM forests. Although an interpretable tree structure is lost, the effects of predictors across the hundreds of trees can be condensed into variable importance metrics, allowing researchers to gauge the relative influence of each variable.
