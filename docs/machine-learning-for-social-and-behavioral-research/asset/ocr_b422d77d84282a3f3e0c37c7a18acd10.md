In contrast to the notion that these studies disprove our complexity thesis, we instead view these studies as informative for elucidating various limitations regarding study design. For example, the predictors and/or outcome may contain too much measurement error (Jacobucci & Grimm, 2020), to be represented as latent variables and not to assess unique entities (the idea behind constitutive versus; Kendall, 2017), have limitations regarding the number and predictors which were included, contain a sample size that was too small, among many others. Our point here is that this area of research is too new to jump to premature conclusions. Instead, of blaming the algorithm or the underlying thesis, we believe that more work is needed to understand how study design contributes to predictive performance. Despite supporting the complexity thesis above, it is simultaneously necessary to prevent the proffered of what could be termed a naive complexity — simply put, for example, studies that assess various environmental factors for understanding depression will likely not assess important genetic contributors. This can be referred to as integrative pluralism (Mitchell, 2009) and requires assessing the rules for combining various cases across multiple ontological levels.

# 1.3 How Is This Book Different?

The authors all have backgrounds in psychology and have worked with a wide variety of datasets coming from multiple areas of social and behavioral research. This means the following distinctions from most machine learning books:

+   - A focus on datasets with more variables than sample size. Our research domains rarely see datasets of this form.
- Greater focus on multivariate outcomes and specifically on imposing structure on the outcome variables. Each author is an expert in latent variable modeling and this expertise is infused into multiple book chapters.
- Greater focus on dealing with measurement error which is incorporated into multiple chapters. Simply put, most of the data that we analyze have non-negligible measurement error which can have a large effect on our ability to accurately model associations in the data.
