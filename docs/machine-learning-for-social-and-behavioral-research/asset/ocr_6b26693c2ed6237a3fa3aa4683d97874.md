In contrast to a model that includes paths between all variables, thus fitting covariance (correlation) matrix perfectly, often involves the placement of constraints in the model based on theory, often taking the forming of removing paths between variables (i.e., setting these parameters to 0). One of the most common models is a mediation model, examples of which are displayed in Figure 2.5.

In this figure, the left panel depicts a full mediation model. More, though, that this is a saturated model — every variable has a relationship (without worry about the direction of the arrows) with every other variable. Saturated models have no misfit. In contrast, the right panel has one arrow removed between X and Y, representing a constraint that the direct relationship between X and Y is 0. There can still be a indirect relationship between X and Y, although an indirect path through M. Our purpose in describing both models is that they represent different theoretical ideas, which can be generalized to models containing more variables, and different forms of relationships (e.g., nonlinear).

This forming of modeling requires a higher degree of specification because each path needs to have a justification, and the model as a whole must relate to a specific hypothesis. However, a large number of variables can be related to a specific hypothesis. This increased flexibility allows not only for the input of theory, but also for balancing the specification of theoretical relationships with uncertainty as the types of relationships in other parts of the model. The default label assigned to the statistical analysis of numbers of variables is exploration, as inputting theory into the model is rarely in practice.
