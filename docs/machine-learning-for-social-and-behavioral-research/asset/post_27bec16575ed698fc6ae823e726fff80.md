## 3.6.1 Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curves

The ROC curve is designed to use a collection of continuous data to derive a cutoff (threshold) that maximizes a balance between two continuous metrics. In the context of classification, this involves plotting both sensitivity and specificity (the false positive rate or 1 - specificity in practice) across the range of possible cutoffs, e.g., what is the sensitivity and specificity for a cutoff of 0.1, 0.5, and all up to 0.99. It is important to note that in our above discussion of deriving optimal cutoffs, these plots only display one metric, while the ROC curve denotes the impact of cutoffs on two metrics, sensitivity and specificity. An additional use of this is to calculate the area under the curve (AUC), which gives us a threshold-free metric for characterizing our prediction performance. The AUC can be characterized as the probability that a randomly drawn positive case has a higher probability than a randomly drawn negative case (Faw, 2006). Values close to 1 indicate thresholds that result in a near 1 value for sensitivity and specificity, meaning that on balance, we are predicting both classes well. Similar to the previous characterization of alternative performance metrics, it is desirable to achieve values greater than 0.8.

In our example, we achieve a value of 0.78, below what we would hope to achieve. Our model better separated outcome classes; we would expect to see a curve closer to the top left of the curve plane, indicating that both a high value of sensitivity and specificity (1–FPR) can be achieved with this model. The individual line in the ROC curve denotes the actual values across different cutoffs. In our results, we see that using a high cutoff results in an extremely low FPR (we classify those without depression well), while an opposite effect occurs for a low cutoff with our model classifying well. It is unlikely that a researcher wishes to use either a low or high cutoff, as there is typically a large decrement with extreme cutoffs, instead preferring something near 0.5. In some cases, particularly with hard-to-sample populations (those with a history of suicide attempts), the use of low cutoffs may be justified.

## 3.7 Imbalanced Outcomes

With binary outcomes, it is common to have some degree of imbalance, and in some settings, particularly in clinical research, the level of imbalance (i.e., < 10% positive cases) can cause a host of problems. With imbalanced outcomes we will detail changes that can be made to k-fold CV, whether sampling-based methods improve our prediction, and alternative performance metrics are more attune to assessing specifically the positive cases.

The first strategy developed that we will discuss for imbalanced outcomes is the stratified k-fold CV. For an example, we can think of attempting to predict which participants have a history of attempting suicide. Since this is typically a rare event, less than 10% of the sample is likely to have a “Yes” response. Given this, splitting the sample into 5 or 10 different partitions could lead to partitions with less than 10% just by chance. Stratification involves prearranging the distribution of the outcome in each partition of the data to ensure roughly equal distribution of the outcome. Results have been mixed in the assessment of the efficacy of stratified k-fold CV, with some research indicating positive effects in classification settings (Kohavi, 1995), as well as no improvements in the regression setting (Breiman & Spector, 1992).

### 3.7.1 Sampling

A number of strategies have been developed to sample from either the positive or negative class, or both, to create new distributions that are more balanced. The simplest are random oversampling (ROS) and random undersampling (RUS). While ROS randomly samples from the minority case to produce an equal distribution of positive and negative cases, RUS randomly removes majority cases to produce an equal distribution. A more sophisticated approach that has a large degree of study is the synthetic oversampling technique (SMOTE; Chawla, Bowyer, Hall, & Kegelmeyer, 2002). SMOTE is similar to ROS, but instead of duplicating positive cases, SMOTE creates artificial minority cases by using the k-nearest neighbors for a given case. Specifically, for a particular case i, the SMOTE algorithm finds k similar cases and generates synthetic cases with predictor values that represent a blend of the k-nearest neighbors.

Among the simple sampling strategies, ROS is preferred over RUS (e.g., Batista, Prati, & Mauro, 2004; Ba, Maki, & Mazurowski, 2018). Further, SMOTE has been generalized into a large number of variants, while additional strategies have also been developed to generate synthetic cases. A recent study of these strategies found that any form of oversampling performs better than no sampling, with the best performance attributed to recent generalizations of SMOTE. The majority of the studies that have evaluated sampling methods have used benchmark datasets from computer science, making the generalizability of the findings to social and behavioral research questionable. Two recent papers focused on evaluating these strategies in simulated data that are in line with social and behavioral research (Goerbergh et al., 2022; Jacobucci & Li, 2022). While Goerbergh et al. (2022) found detrimental effects of sampling strategies with respect to calibration (defined below) and did not lead to better AUC values than no sampling, Jacobucci and Li (2022) found only slight benefits to the use of sampling strategies for both the AUC and AUPRC, and of the sampling strategies, ROS performed as well if not better across conditions than SMOTE. Note in Jacobucci and Li (2022) is that they also tested the random over sampling examples (ROS; Menardi & Torelli, 2014) which is implemented in the caret. package performs along with SMOTE. Notably, that ROS performs poorly in some conditions and is not a recommended approach.
