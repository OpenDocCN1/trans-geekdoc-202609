# 2.5.1 Model Size

The question of assessing the degree of confirmation becomes more difficult in the case of complex models, such as SEMs, dynamical systems, and network models. Simply put, the simplicity of stating and testing hypotheses in traditional experimental designs does not translate to complex models. As the number of variables increases, and the models grow in size, it becomes increasingly difficult to specify hypotheses that account for every aspect of the model. Simply stating a one-line hypothesis and one-line alternative hypothesis no longer holds. With larger models comes more ways to wrong, thus rejecting a hypothesis. As a result, conformity comes to mean something else in these situations, taken as a straw man argument. Instead, by saying confirmatory really mean a minimum degree of specification—not exploratory. As models grow, this becomes more and more torture, until the word confir ceases to mean anything.

This difficulty may be a contributing factor for why there is a dearth of complex models in social and behavioral publications. This leads to the specification of many simple relationships. For instance Lieberman and Cunningham (2009) found that a randomly issue of the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology had an average of 93 statistical tests performed per paper.

An additional contributing factor is an explicit bias against complexity—thus there is a preference for parsimony. One hallmark of this viewpoint comes from Box (1976):

> Since all models are wrong the scientist cannot obtain a "correct" one by excessive elaboration. On the contrary William of Ockham he seek an economical description of natural phenomena. Just as the ability to devise simple but evocative models is the signature of the greatest scientist so-elaboration and overparameterization is often the mark of mediocrity. (p. 792)

Selecting the correct model is indeed extremely difficult and there are a number of competing factors that influence the model selection (see Chapter 3); however, this is in contrast to more recent emphasis on embracing complexity (e.g., Fried & Robinaugh, 2020).
