In Figure 4.3, both the ridge and lasso estimates are displayed with the amount of penalty decreasing from left to right on the X-axis. Starting from the far right in each figure, the OLS estimates ($\lambda=0$), the penalty increases (moving left), each of the parameter is gradually shrunk towards 0. In comparing lasso estimates to ridge estimates, note that the lasso sets parameters to 0 at differing rates, with some set to 0 immediately (small penalties). In the right panel of Figure 4.3, three parameters exhibit differing behavior and aren't set to 0 until the penalty becomes much larger. With the ridge parameter trajectories, none of the parameters reaches 0 until they all do at the largest penalty (most programs stop increasing $\lambda$ once all parameters are set to 0).

Note that this is, a couple of parameters are not shrunk towards 0 until larger penalties are used. Additionally, in some cases with regularization, penalized parameters will increase with larger penalties. In both of these scenarios, this occurs when there are parameters that have a more influential effect on the outcome. As the less influential effects are shrunk, more influential effects are actually increased. The point of this is that there is not always a uniform effect of increasing the penalty on all of the parameters.

To better understand why of ridge and lasso regularization results in different effects on the parameters as the penalties are increased, it is helpful to display the equivalent configurations of Equations 4.4 and 4.5. For ridge regression, this is $\beta_1^2+\beta_2^2+...+\beta_p^2 \le s$ and for the lasso, $|\beta_1|+\beta_2|+...|\beta_p| \le s$, where s is such a constraint. As a result, in contrast to thinking of either the ridge or lasso as penalizing coefficients, we can think of placing constraints on the summation of the squared (ridge) or absolute value (lasso) of the regression coefficients. Thinking of this in our way, as decrease the sum of all coefficients is required to become smaller, thus pulling each parameter towards 0 at differing rates, depending on each predictor's relationship with other predictors as the outcome. To visualize this, Figure 4.4 depicts the constraints of Equations 4.4 and 4.5 using two predictors.

The left side of Figure 4.4 contains the constraints of ridge regularization, where squaring of parameter estimates results in a circular constraint region. In the right side, summing the absolute value of each lasso regularization results in a diamond-shaped constraint region. Additionally, $\beta$ represents the OLS parameter estimates, while the ellipses centered around $\beta$ are regions of constant residual sum of squares. In both of these figures, we can imagine the parameters to be less than 1. Decreasing the value of $s$ results in smaller constraint regions, and thus moves each parameter closer to 0.
