#  7.4.1 Indexes of Reliability

We provide a brief overview of indexes of reliability. For a more detailed coverage, see McNish (2018). As displayed in Equation 7.2, the definition of reliability is intuitive. In practice, however we do not have access to T, therefore we must estimate reliability solely with X. In psychological research, Cronbach's alpha (Cronbach, 1951) is by far the most common for calculating reliability based on a single administration.³Cronbach's alpha can be defined as

```
$$\alpha = \frac{k}{k-1} \left(1 - \frac{\sum s_i^2}{s_X^2}\right)$
```

where k is the number of items, $s_i^2$ is the variance of each item $i$, and $s_X^2$ is the sum of all variances and covariances for all the items. In line with Equation 7.2, we can characterize Cronbach's alpha as the proportion of variance in a scale attributable to a common source. In Calculating Cronbach's alpha for the 12 Grit items, we got a value of 0.84 (95% CI = 0.82, 0.85). This represents a good reliability estimate,⁴ it is worth delving into the assumptions and limitations regarding Cronbach's alpha. Two of the most important limitations of Cronbach's alpha are the assumption of tau equivalence and unidimensionality (among others, see McNish, 2018). This essentially means that there is only a single latent variable that underlies the scale, and each item is equally representative of that single latent variable. In practice these assumptions are unlikely to hold. To provide alternatives to Cronbach's alpha, we first need to provide background on the use of factor analysis.
