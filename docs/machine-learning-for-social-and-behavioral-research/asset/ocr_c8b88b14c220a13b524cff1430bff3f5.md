# 2.3.1 Complexity

Brandmaier et al. (2017) also highlight an additional question researchers utilizing machine learning must answer: What degree of complexity I wish to interpret? Despite this, it is common for researchers to seek simple results. This may be exemplified in the desire to identify a small number of large effects upon many interrelated variables. Not only is this unlikely to occur in practice, particularly in social and behavioral data where the variables are all related to one another to at least a small degree (e.g., the crud factor; Meeb, 1990), but forcing this structure upon the results comes at a price (detailed in Chapter 3). A second way that this manifests itself is in the desire to select a final model, treating the results as if they reflect the true model. Not only does this strategy negate the fact that other models evidence fit are only minutes worse, deserve these models largely in functional form (and thus their explanations as to the underlying relationships), but it also budgeons nuance in understanding the underlying relationships.

We borrow the term explanatory pluralism (e.g., Kendler, 2005) to describe the common phenomenon of observing multiple algorithms exhibiting identical performance along with very little differentiation in the importance of predictor variables. In fact for this latter piece, it is a common experience to remove the most important predictor from the model and not see an evident change in predictor performance. We use the term explanatory pluralism to point out that there are multiple explanations for the functional form between predictors and an outcome, as well as which variables contribute to the prediction of the outcome. This is obviously not always the case, but is more common than not predictors come from interrelated constructs. Observing such results makes the study's original questions and hypotheses exceedingly difficult.

In contrast to most statistical texts that contain empirical examples that are, for the most, relatively neat, we primarily use datasets that were collected as a part of large studies, containing many interrelated variables, muddying the simplicity to the modeling and interpretation.
