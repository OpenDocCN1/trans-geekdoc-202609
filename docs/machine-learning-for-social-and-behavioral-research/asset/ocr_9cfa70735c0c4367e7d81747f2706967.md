# 6.4 Gradient Boosting

Similar to random forests, gradient boosting (also known as gradient machines or gradient boosting trees) is a method that combines the use of hundreds or thousands of individual decision trees in an attempt to overcome the problems with a single decision tree. However, in contrast to bagging and random forests, where each tree is created independently on a bootstrapped sample, boosting creates a sequence of trees based on weighted forms of the data. Developed originally for classification in the form of the AdaBoost algorithm (Freund & Shapire, 1995; 1997); the idea behind the boosting algorithm has also been extended to many other statistical problems (see Buhmann & Hothorn, 2007 for more details). Most notably, Friedman, Hastie, and Tibshirani (2000), as well as Friedman (2001), developed highly adaptable algorithms that can be applied to a list of different loss functions. This algorithm has also been extended to multivariate outcomes (Miller, Lubke, McArtour, & Bergheman, 2016). The basic idea of this algorithmic framework, originally termed gradient boosting machines by Friedman (2001), now more frequently referred to as simply boosting, is to fit an additive combination of weak learner (weak in the sense that its predictive performance is usually substandard), most often a decision tree with few (potentially one) partitions of the data, to the gradient (residuals) of the prior combination of weak learners.
