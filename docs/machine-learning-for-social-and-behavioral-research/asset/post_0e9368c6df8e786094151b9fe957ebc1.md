## 4.5.4 Group Regularization

Sometimes in social and behavioral research our predictors are not completely independent from one another. Instead, there are groups of variables, for example, five items purported to measure one factor of personality, a set of demographic variables, In this case we may not want to select individual predictors but instead select groups of predictors. The use of group lasso could be portrayed as a less counterpart to hierarchical regression. This refers to hierarchical entry of single sets of predictors. This is distinguished from what has been termed multilevel, or mixed effects. This later form of modeling is about the inclusion of random effects, opposed to solely fixed effects to account for variability by clustering. Hierarchical regression is a more form of modeling where predictors are entered based on a prior sequence. For a scenario where the numbers of variables preclude strong specification, Yuan and Lin (2007) proposed the group lasso

$grouplasso = \arg\min_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^{P} x_{ij}\beta_j)^2 + \lambda \sum_{g=1}^{G} \sqrt{p_g}\|\beta_g\|_2$

where $p_g$ accounts for the varying sizes of groups and $\beta_g$ is the vector of all $\beta$ coefficients in group $g$. In the group lasso, the lasso penalty at the group level, instead of individual parameters being set to 0, entire groups can be pushed to 0. An alternative form of group regularization is for scenarios where researchers are not interested in only selecting groups, but also selecting variables within those groups. When they wish to penalize at the group level, researchers have the option to use the group lasso, with some sparse extensions (Huang, Breheny, & Ma, 2012). One, the sparse group lasso (srplasso; Hastie, & Tibirani, 2010), has the same group lasso as Equation 4.5, but adds a penalty for each individual predictor

$srplasso = \arg\min_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^{P} x_{ij}\beta_j)^2 + \lambda_1 \sum_{g=1}^{G} \sqrt{p_g}\|\beta_g\|_2 + \lambda_2\|\beta_j\|_1$

where $\lambda_1$ represents the group penalization and $\lambda_2$ is for the individual $\beta_j$ coefficients. This model produces sparsity at two levels, ideal for incorporating group information into variable selection.

## Regularized Regression

Across 20 variables, there are four groups of variables: demographics, items measuring conscientiousness, openness, and neuroticism. We specify a hierarchical sequence of five models:

- $Model_0: agree = \beta_0$
- $Model_1: agree = \beta_0 + A*demo$
- $Model_2: agree = \beta_0 + A*demo + B*const$
- $Model_3: agree = \beta_0 + A*demo + B*const + C*open$
- $Model_4: agree = \beta_0 + A*demo + B*const + C*open + D*neur$

where $a$ is a score of agreeableness, demo refers to a set of gender (0 male; 1 female) and three dummy variables for age, const are five items measuring conscientiousness, open are five items measuring openness. Each predictor set addition is based on a specific theoretical relationship. For instance, in the first model, we test whether the addition of demographic variables contributes a significant degree to the prediction of agreeableness and above a null model. In the second model, we similarly test the addition of conscientiousness items and above demographic variables. In this model sequence, we can estimate the increase in $\Delta R^2$ solely due to the addition of a set of variables, resulting in

$R^2(Model_4) = \Delta R^2(Model_4) + \Delta R^2(Model_3) + \Delta R^2(Model_2) + \Delta R^2(Model_1)$
