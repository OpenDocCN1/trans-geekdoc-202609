
where X and Z are known design matrices, $\beta$ is a matrix of fixed-effects parameters, $\Phi$ is the variance-covariance matrix for the random effects, $\sigma^2$ is the observation residual variance, $n$ is the sample size and $I$ is an identity matrix. In the lasso mixed-effects models,

```
$$f_{lasso} = -ZLL\beta + \lambda\sum_{p=1}^P |\beta_p| \quad (9.6)
```

where $\lambda$ is the penalty and $\Sigma_{p=1}^P$ is the sum of absolute values of the $P$ fixed-effects parameter estimates. As the penalty, $\lambda$, increases, the fixed-effects estimates shrink and will eventually be set to 0. If the penalty is 0, then the fixed-effects parameters are not penalized and the estimates are equal to those obtained through a standard mixed-effects modeling program. The value of $\lambda$ is varied from a low value to a high value, and an optimal value is often chosen through cross-validation (i.e., k-fold cross-validation) or through an index of model fit that accounts for model complexity (e.g., Bayesian information criterion).

## Software

There are multiple packages for incorporating regularization into mixed-effects models. Specific to our goals is the method developed by Scheldorfer, Bühlmann, and van Geer (2011) and implemented in the R package lmasso (Scheldorfer, 2011). For generalized linear mixed-effects models, there exists an extension (Scheldorfer, Meier, &hlmann, 2014) of the method developed by Scheldorfer et al. (2011) and implemented in the glmlasso package (Scheldorfer, 2012). We note that Groll and Tutz (2014) developed a similar approach, which is implemented in the glmLasso package (Groll, 2017), however, the fixed effects are penalized in this approach to random effects.

## 9.5 Illustrative Example

Longitudinal data are analyzed from the ECLS-K. Our goal is to determine whether this set of easily collected variables measured at the beginning of kindergarten can account for differences in the change trajectories for reading abilities assessed during the first two years of school.

The outcomes of interest are the reading theta scores based on the public use dataset. These scores are estimated ability based on the questions asked and answered by the children. These scores ranged -2.644 to 2.122 for the children assessed in kindergarten through first grade. Observing reading trajectories from a 1% random sample of children are contained in Figure 9.2. From this figure, we see that the trajectories are fairly linear over time, with large between-child differences in performance at the beginning of kindergarten and the rate of change over time.

To begin, a linear mixed-effects model was fit to the reading scores with the time metric beginning September 1, 1998, which approximated the first day of school in kindergarten, with the intercept centered at this day. This linear mixed-effects model can be written as

```
$y_i = (\beta_0 + u_0) + (\beta_1 + u_{ij})(x_{ij} - 0.25) + e_i$
```

where $\beta_0$ is the fixed effect for the intercept with $u_0$ as its associated random effect, $\beta_1$ is the fixed effect for the slope with $u_{ij}$ as its random effect, $x_{ij}$ is the timing metric (beginning September 1, 1998), and $e_i$ is the time-dependent residual. The random effects are assumed to follow a multivariate normal distribution with zero means, $u_{ij} \sim N(0, \Sigma)$. The time-dependent residual is assumed to be normally distributed with zero mean and variance $\sigma_e^2$.

As a basis for comparison, the linear mixed-effects model in Equation 9.7 was estimated using the nlme package. The -2LL was 33856.9. We retain this information for now, and discussing results from machine learning techniques. The fixed effect for the intercept was -0.89 and the fixed effect for the slope was 1.03, suggesting that, on average, children had a score of -0.89 in kindergarten and 1.03 per year. The rate of change is substantial given the random-effects parameters. The standard deviation of the random intercept was 0.52 and the standard deviation of the random slope was 0.16. Thus, the average yearly change represented approximately two standard deviations of between-child differences. The intercept and slope were negatively associated with a correlation of -0.56. Finally, the standard deviation of the residual was 0.24.
