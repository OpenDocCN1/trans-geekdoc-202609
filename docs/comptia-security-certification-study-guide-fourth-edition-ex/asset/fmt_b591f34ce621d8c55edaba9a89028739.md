
# Supply Chain Assessment

*Supply chain assessment* involves identifying any risks within the supply chain and mitigating those risks. For example, the assessment might evaluate what to do if there is a shortage of raw materials or if there is a fire at the manufacturing plant. All such threats could present risk to your business.

## Testing

When assessing threats against your organization’s assets, it is important to perform different types of tests. You could perform a *vulnerability test*, also known as a *vulnerability scan*, to test whether your systems are vulnerable to different exploits and weaknesses. You could also perform a more active type of test known as *penetration testing*, where the tester tries to bypass security controls and gain access to a system. You will learn more about these types of tests in [Chapter 20](#ch20.xhtml#ch20).

There are a number of other forms of tests that can be performed that do not relate directly to technology. For example, your organization should perform fire drills on a regular basis. You could also test your business processes by testing supplier delivery flexibility and stock levels by testing rush orders. If you are in the service industry, you could test customer service by using mystery shoppers.

## Change Management

After testing your systems with either a vulnerability scan or a penetration test, you may discover that you need to make changes to the configuration of the systems in order to make them more secure. When making changes to the system configuration, be sure to follow the change management process that your organization has set out. This process typically involves applying the change to test systems first, backing up the production system before making the change, applying the change, and then verifying the proper operation of the system after the change.

The key to change management is the documentation. For example, when planning for changes, you should document the desired changes, document what to do if the changes do not go as planned (this is called the *rollback plan*), and then summarize the results of the change operation.

## Identify Mitigation Techniques

Once you have identified the threats and prioritized them, you know which threat solutions to focus on, or at least how to reduce the risk of the threat occurring. This is known as *mitigating* the threat. Mitigating the threat typically involves spending money on a solution that implements a security control to protect the asset from the risk. You can implement fault-tolerant technologies, firewalls, encryption, or access control systems, to name a few.

## Evaluate Residual Risks

Once you have implemented solutions to mitigate the threats, reevaluate the asset and identify any threats that may still exist. The remaining threats are known as *residual risk.* It is critical to express this residual risk to management so that they can decide if they are willing to accept that residual risk or want to implement additional security solutions.

# Tools to Help Analyze Risk

You can use a number of tools to help assess, analyze, and make decisions with regard to risk analysis and risk assessment. In this section you will learn about those risk tools and concepts.

## Risk Matrix/Heat Map

A risk matrix, also known as a heat map, is a tool used to assign a risk value on a particular event to help decide what the risk level is. Looking at [Figure 17-1](#ch17.xhtml#ch17fig1), you can see that the risk matrix has a listing on the left for the impact level of an event, and then across the top are the probability levels of the event happening. Let’s say that an event such as a DoS attack against your e-commerce site has an impact of high (3) but a probability of medium (2). That would give you a risk value of 6 (3 × 2). Also note that each of the risk values are typically colored so that visually you can determine how risky the event is. A risk value of 1 would be green (meaning minimal risk), yellow squares are used to identify risk levels of 2 and 3, risk levels of 4 and 6 might be orange, and then a risk level of 9 uses a red square to visually indicate high risk. These colors and values can be modified to fit your organization’s methodology to handling risk.

## Risk Control Assessment and Risk Control Self-Assessment

*Risk control assessment* is the term used when an assessment is performed by a third party that understands the business risk objectives and performs a review of the effectiveness of the security controls to minimize risk.

*Risk control self-assessment (RCSA)* is the term used to describe the process of internal staff identifying risk and evaluating the effectiveness of the controls that are protecting the asset from risk. The goal of the RCSA is to ensure the reliability of the controls in place, to ensure the safety of the assets, and to ensure compliance with laws, regulations, and the security policy.

## Risk Terms

The Security+ exam will test you on a number of other terms related to risk and performing risk analysis. Some of these terms have been discussed in other areas of the chapters, but a nice listing of the terms makes it easy to review them before taking the certification exam:

- **Risk awareness** Involves raising awareness of the risks to your assets and understanding the impact of the risks, how to mitigate those risks, and how to manage any residual risks.
- **Inherent risk** Refers to the natural risks associated with a specific activity or business process before a mitigation technique is applied to reduce the risk.
- **Residual risk** The amount of risk that remains after a mitigation technique has been applied to reduce the risk.
- **Control risk** Also known as *risk control,* this is the process of identifying threats against the assets and identifying controls that can be put in place to protect them.
- **Risk appetite** The level of risk the company is willing to accept based on its goals and objectives. The risk appetite will help determine if a control is needed to protect an asset.

## Regulations That Affect Risk Posture

Every cybersecurity program should contain a list of controls that should be used to protect company assets such as systems, network devices, and data. The term *security posture* refers to how secure those assets are based on the mitigation techniques implemented. There are a number of steps a company can take to improve its security posture and lower its risk posture, such as performing a risk assessment, implementing security controls to mitigate risk, educating employees on security and risk, and implementing an incident response plan.

Regulations that govern your organization have a direct impact on your risk posture. For example, if a company is processing credit cards, it must follow the compliance requirements set out by the *Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)*. Following these requirements helps create a more secure environment when handling credit card information and, as a result, lowers your risk posture.

# Risk with Cloud Computing and Third Parties

A hot topic in today’s world is the concept of cloud computing. With cloud computing, you can subscribe to a cloud service that offers data storage space on some server on the Internet, or you can have someone host services for you, such as a web server, database server, and e-mail server. An example of the benefit of subscribing to cloud services is that your company would not need to purchase the hardware to host the company e-mail and databases—you would simply subscribe to the cloud services and they would provide the functionality for you. You would then simply log on to the management interface (typically a web interface) and manage the service (such as e-mail).
