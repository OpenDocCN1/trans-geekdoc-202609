When you multiply by 2⁻¹¹, things are not improved much. The number 2⁵¹ / 52! is approximately equal to 2.8 × 10⁻⁵³. To put the matter in cosmic perspective, the earth is composed of about 10⁵⁰ atoms. If you and a friend of yours were to pick independently an atom from the earth, the probability that you would pick the same atom would be 10⁻⁵⁰, actually greater than 2⁵¹ / 52!—the probability of pathological quicksort on a deck of cards.⁴

That explains that “normally” we pick a pivot in a more equitable way, as we said above. Excepting a streak of bad luck of cosmic proportions, we do not expect to pick the worst pivot possible each time. The odds actually work better in our favor: is by picking pivots at random that we expect to get a complexity of O(nlogn). It is theoretically possible to do worse than that, but the possibility is only of academic interest. Quicksort will be as fast as we expect it to be for all practical purposes.

Quicksort was developed by the British computer scientist Tony Hoare in 1959–1960⁵ It is probably the most popular sorting algorithm today because when implemented correctly, it outruns all others.
