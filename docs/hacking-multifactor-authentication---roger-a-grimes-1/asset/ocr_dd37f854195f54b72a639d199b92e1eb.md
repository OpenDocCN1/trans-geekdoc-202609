# 42 Hacking Multifactor Authentication

It is this continuous tension between all the organizations who want to know everything us and each individual's desire for privacy that most global identity systems face. How does a global IDM become the authentication choice for all users and at the same time let them know that their privacy could be invaded at any time by any legal source? It's the "sixty-four million-dollar question". Pseudo-anonymity may help with some of that contention, but it will probably not be the long-term answer.

Here's a quick privacy story from my experience. Years ago, I was working for a major company and we were asked to design and build on a brand-new authentication system that would allow people visiting Singapore to go on the Internet. It was my easiest biggest design project. And I was shaking in proverbial unqualified. But as soon I discovered, it was the best they had for the project at the time. I worked for many months to learn all about good mega-identity system design. And in the end, I turned in what I thought was a very good, competitive design and build. I surprised myself and in the process what I learned to make a good global identity management system.

Just after I handed it in, I realized that I had not included a section on privacy. At the time, privacy wasn't the concern it is today. There were no privacy officers, privacy policies, and worldwide organizations fighting for people's privacy. But there was just enough concern that I realized I had not included a section in our request for proposal (RFP) response dictating how we would protect users' privacy. I asked for the response back. I told them I had forgotten to include the missing section but I could quickly add it to the design.

What came back stung me. They said, "We don't want people to have privacy. We want to track each person and where they go at all times! I was blown away. They wanted to ensure that visiting guests to their hotels were tracked to the passports they had to carry over the hotel front desk. What was the opposite of privacy. They wanted to know everything. I had not considered that of requirement. It wasn't in the official requirements. I struggled with having to design a country's Internet system with zero privacy. In the end, I withdrew it from consideration. I couldn't live with myself if I intentionally prevented privacy from everyone who used it. I'd to report that the company and my team supported my decision. My company was fine with the decision after hearing what transpired. But it was a lesson learned.

It's over a decade since that event, and that I've run into many countries (in Asia and Europe) that do not value privacy or even the exact opposite. But now I realize that I can't have righteous indignation every other country, including my own, the United States, doesn't want it. Yes, they don't build the lack of anonymity to the design, identifying who is who is not difficult in any country. Some countries are just most honest and blatant about it. I believe it is up to each individual to fight for the right to privacy, at least for the privacy expectations and rights I believe gives us all humanity—like the ability to have truly private conversations when we want them and the ability to conduct anonymous actions with others who want or respect anonymity.
