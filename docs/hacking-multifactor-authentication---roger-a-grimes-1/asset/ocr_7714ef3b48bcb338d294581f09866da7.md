# 510 Hacking Multifactor Authentication

My favorite study is the first one. It shows that even SMS-based MFA—which I, along with many others, am not a fan of—significantly reduces non-targeted attacks. The keyword is non-targeted. If there is a weakness to MFA, it targets attacks. MFA stops significantly complicates the most popular type of generic, widely broadcasted, attacks, where an attacker has nearly zero information about their intended targets. The attackers just use a scattershot approach against a population of potential victims and to get lucky with a few. Cyberattacks are of this broadcast, scattershot, makeup. And MFA stops (but not all) of these types of attacks. For example, if you are an attacker trying to fish someone out of their Gmail password and the user requires uses MFA to log in to their Gmail account, that is not going to.

But attackers who spend learning about specific victims and the related potential attack areas, with a little creativity, can find a way to exploit. The MFA scenario, if I know that my victim requires MFA, I have to learn how MFA works and the methods to get around it. Any MFA method can be hacked, but it takes research and effort, and any increase in effort reduces the number of potential attackers and their chances of success. In most scenarios, MFA is a good thing to have. But an attacker can easily modify their general, broadcast attack to include MFA scenarios, and by making that one change, bring MFA-using users back into play as potential victims.

# MFA Is Not Hackable

In the same vein, “harder to accomplish” hacking and phishing does not mean impossible. The danger of thinking MFA is unhackable or nearly unhackable can be extraordinarily dangerous, especially if you are a leader or a computer security practitioner. If you believe something is unhackable, you probably aren't educating everyone involved with the specific risks they need to be aware of and defend.

Anecdotally, most of the general attendees at my Hacking webinars were shocked to learn that a simple phishing email, like the one shown in Figure 6.4 in Chapter 6, “Access Token Tricks,” can bypass their favorite MFA solution. Many have been told by their IT people that using an MFA token means they can't be phished and that they don't have to be worried about phishing emails. They have been taught a regular-looking phishing email and compromise your account even if you are using MFA. That's reality.

The wrong advice increases the likelihood of future compromise because the leaders and victims aren't worried and aren't looking out for the signs of a pending attack. It's like someone saying the Titanic was unsinkable. You can avoid icebergs if you are worried about them in the first place. This is the primary lesson of this book — MFA is not unhackable.
