Unless truly random, most human-selected passwords are composed entirely of alphabetic or numeric characters encompassing words, dates and in their chosen language. When complexity is required, passwords may include other keyboard symbols. Most human-chosen passwords are not very random; they do not contain much entropy. If humans are allowed to create their own passwords with no complexity requirements, they will be passwords like password, Michael, and query. If humans are required to create supposedly “complex” passwords, they create passwords like Frog33, P0ssword, and query12.

Truly randomly generated passwords look like: "2Vy6j1##.d! and 46avaxTiWmLCQBZZA. These truly random passwords have high entropy and are harder for a password hacker to guess and crack. Unfortunately, as you can see, most humans would not like to memorize and type those sort of passwords over and over. I care about computer security and I don't want to memorize and type those sorts of passwords.

Some security-conscious users insert use passwords, looking more like sentences, as those Cowckewallroughtthegate and ErichThreeWolvesBayathtome. These are known as phrases. And although they can contain numbers and symbols, from their length, they have more entropy than the shorter, “complex” passwords. Unfortunately, it is easy to make a password with that long, and many systems will not accept them. Many websites have a maximum password size of 8 to 12 characters, and will not accept keyboard symbols at all.

Regardless of password composition, users can only remember so many passwords before they begin to forget old ones and enter mistake in new ones, especially when forced password expiration is involved. As covered in Chapter 1, "Logon Problems," the average user has six or seven passwords they use for all their websites. When forced to update their password, they will use one of the other passwords they use all the time, or change the existing password just enough that the website accepts it (like going from frog1 to frog2). Or if they actually use different passwords for every website, they use a noticeable "base word" pattern such as FrogFB for Facebook logins, FrogFW for Twitter logins, and so forth. As covered in Chapter 1, no matter what passwords are used or how complex they are, users' passwords are incredibly easy for hackers to learn—so much that the world is trying to move to any other authentication method that doesn't involve passwords only.

The huge advantage of passwords is that they have, more or less, worked for nearly everyone well since the beginning of computer authentication. They are readily understood and easy to use (when not required to be complex). They are everywhere. Failure rates for people using multifactor authentication is high. Not for passwords. Nearly anyone, including a small child, can be easily instructed on how to use passwords in a minute or two. I've seen children under the age of two type in passwords that a 60-year-old struggled with.

## Hacking Multifactor Authentication

NOTE If you use a lot of passwords and don't use a password manager (which I'll discuss in a moment), never write your complete password in any document, password-protected or not, where a hacker could view it or steal it. Instead, write passwords using simple shorthand codes that you know. For example, if your password is FrogFB and FrorogTW33 for Facebook, only write down FFB and FTW33. If you never write your complete password anywhere, no one can easily steal it. Of course, that means you have to remember what your shorthand codes stood for. Remember that it's best to avoid easy-to-recognize patterns.

## PINS

PINs are essentially KBA passwords, usually composed only of numerals (although they can contain additional types of character sets). They are most commonly used with building entry and exit control systems, smartcards, ATM/banking cards, credit cards, cell phones, and other portable computers. The average PIN is only up to six characters.

Because PINs are short and consist of fewer characters than a password, PIN authentication typically has strict 'lockout' policies, where too many incorrect PIN attempts invalidate the PIN or device to be used. Some devices accepting PINs, like a Windows PC with an enabled Trusted Platform Module (TPM) chip, will not completely lock out account after bad PIN attempts but will instead make the user wait longer and between attempts. After more than a few bad PIN attempts, each bad attempt can have the subject several minutes before being allowed to try again. This is known as login/login throttling. Other devices may 'brick' the device, meaning the device is rendered inactive until an administrator or technician resets it.

On the good side, PINs are easy to remember and use. It's good for people who are easily guess and hack. PINs are easy for people to 'shoulder surfing' when using hidden cameras and/or more easy figuring out when we 'wear patterns'.

For example, Figure 3.1 shows a real-world PIN keypad I see every day. You can see that the 1, 3, 6, 0 and keys have increased over the other keys. A reasonable person could easily assume that the PIN includes those numbers in one combination or another, and that submitting it requires a #keypress at the end of the sequence. Without repeating characters, if the PIN is only four characters, there are only 24 (4! = 24) possible solutions; if the PIN is five characters, there are only 120 (5! = 120) possible solutions.

Note: The "!" mathematical symbol stands for all numbers multiplied against each other. For example, 5! = 5 x 4 x 3 x 2 x 1 = 120.

If you repeat, it definitely increases the number of possible solutions to remember, but nothing compared to passwords with more possible characters. A four-character password consisting of 26 alphanumeric characters has 456,976 (26²) possibilities and a five-character password has 11,884,376 (26⁶) possibilities. And if you allow numbers, as most passwords do, then the numbers really get big (36⁴ and 36³, respectively). PINs are so much easier to guess and hack compared to passwords.

They usually don't expire, so they are used frequently. Most are not randomly generated. Most are related to people's birthdays or significant dates they care about. A date like 0916 (representing September 16) are far more common than 66. Even worse, people reuse PINs. Many users have only one or two they reuse across all sites and devices.

## Solving Puzzles

A growing number of authentication solutions require that the user solve some sort of graphical, spatial, or logical.

### Connect the Dots

The common types of puzzles involved in authentication include asking the user to "connect the dots" (see Figure 3.2). Dots are evenly placed in a tic-tac-toe grid, and the user selects or swipes dots in a predefined, agreed-upon pattern.

Connect-the-dot puzzles suffer from many of the same problems as PINs, although not having stationary, fixed-positioned dots may prevent easy shoulder surfing. Still, research has shown that people using these types of puzzle solutions have predictable locations they choose and gestures and motions they make depending on the patterns and shapes in the underlying photo. Even with the device turned away from view, the shoulder surfing hacker is able to tell the target's hand movements and elbows what the target just did. I've seen this enough that I do not think connect-the-dot authentication is sufficient for anything beyond the merit suggestion of security.

### Graphical Swiping

Another type of authentication puzzle asks the user to select a picture or graphic, or supply their own, and determine a pattern that will be used for authentication (an example in Figure 3.3). Puzzle solutions like this allow the user to use a combination of gestures, such as "swiping," from one point to another without raising their finger, circles, or making other predefined gestures. These types of authentication solutions tend to be allowed on form factors larger than a few inches. On smaller form factors such as cell phones, it might be difficult to get enough movement area across the smaller representation of the figure to achieve the desired result.
