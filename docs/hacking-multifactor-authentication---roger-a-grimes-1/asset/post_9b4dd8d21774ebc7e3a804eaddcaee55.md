
## Hacking Multifactor Authentication

Every security person can repeat the well-worn computer security manta, "The only secure one is locked in a closet, not connected to a network, encased in concrete" or some hyperbolic bestestation. But the truth is most people really don't want the best security that keeps them as safe as possible. This applies to nearly every security scenario, not just computer security.

For example, car crashes are the leading killer of people age 2 to 34. In the United States, there are over 36,000 deaths a year, or around 99 per day, day after day, on top of the 2 million injuries. Car crashes are one of the leading causes of death for people of all age groups. Your odds of dying in a car crash in a given year are higher than in 8,000. There are only two risks higher, and both of them are medical conditions: cancer and heart disease. The odds of dying in a car crash in your lifetime are 100 or higher! I think these statistics shocks most people. Not only do we lose thousands of people per year to crashes, but we lose tens of billions of dollars due to insurance claims, damaged vehicles, and lost productivity.

We could prevent all the deaths by getting rid of cars. That's not practical, but we could do it. And if we have to have cars, we could significantly reduce injuries and death by requiring all drivers and riders to use five-point seat belts and safety helmets. We could make all cars to have radios, cell phones, and anything else that might possibly distract a driver in a car powered up. It's ridiculous. It's draconian. It would work. We could significantly reduce car injuries and deaths. We know how to do it.

But instead, we decided as a society all cars to be capable of going over 120 mph (even though no speed limit in the US allows it), and we've essentially left it up to drivers (even though it is required in most places) to determine whether they should seat seat belts at the time. Car drivers can wear any type of headgear they want, many of which would protect against most injuries. But drivers certainly weren't wearing helmets that would mess up their hair and look silly (to others and us). Heck, only 20 US states require that motorcycle riders wear helmets, and the odds of the helmeted motorcycle rider or passenger having more deaths and injuries to occur in a crash triple. There are all sorts of security situations in which we are a society allow because we value other things, such as usability and freedom, over greater safety. And that isn't necessarily a bad thing. Who wants to live in a society utterlyumbered to prevent all possible accidents? We couldn't use ladders, bathtubs, or showers (which account for a huge amount of serious accidents). Gas-powered lawn mowers?

Instead, knowingly allow a certain percentage of injuries and deaths to occur while trying to mitigate the worst and biggest threats. Computers and authentication follow the same rules. We allow computers to be far insecure than they could because we would also value personal freedom and the ability to run and operate a PC or device almost any the user wants. If we really cared about computer security as much as we claim, every computer would be able to run only a very limited set of computer programs and go to a very limited list of websites and services approved by IT.

Authentication would be cumbersome, but very secure. We could make computers far more secure than they are today without locking them in closets and putting in concrete. But we don't want to do that. We want computers to be “kinda” secure, with the least amount of security interference in our life. We will gladly live with just okay security as long as we can do whatever we want on our computers and as long as the level of malicious hacking and badness stays to an acceptable of essentially background noise. As long as our computers and we are not hacked most of the time, we will tolerate them hacked some of the time.
