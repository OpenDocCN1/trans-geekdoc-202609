But as the number of people in an asymmetric channel increases, not every participant may know and trust every other participant. One way to get public trust in a person you don't know is to have someone you already vouch for the other person. For example, Alice wanted to communicate symmetrically with Bob but did not know ahead of time. Then Dan knew and trusted for Bob's valid key. Dan can sign Bob's public key with his own private key; Alice could then validate using Dan's public key. This is called a peer-to-peer trust (or web trust). Many asymmetric ciphers on the Internet work this.

Peer-to-peer trust systems do not work as the number of participants scale, particularly in global asymmetric systems where most of the participants don't know one another. Enter public key infrastructure (PKI) is a commonly used cryptographic framework and family of protocols used in the world to provide identity through unrelated parties. You may read or hear about many different descriptions of what PKI is and why it is needed, but at its base requirement, PKI primarily exists to authenticate subject identities and their asymmetric keys involved in cryptographic transactions. Without this requirement, you would not need PKI.

PKI issues verified digital certificates, which are cryptographically protected documents asserting to the validity of a subject's identity and their asymmetric key pair. In practice, the subject (or something on behalf) generates an asymmetric key pair for the subject to use. The subject submits their public key to the PKI (remember, we don't share private keys). The PKI's certification authority service process is then supposed to verify the identity of the subject submitting the public key.

> NOTE Many times the PKI is creating and issuing the entire key, both the public and private portions, to the user. And when doing so, even though the PKI could know the private key, it is still considered private as long as no one outside the PKI (and PKI admins) can possibly see it.

The level of indent required of the subject by the PKI determines the level of assurance (or trust) the PKI can attest to. In any case, the primary job of the PKI is to verify the identity of the subject submitting their public key. If the subject's identity is validated, the PKI adds some information (such as validity dates, subject name[s], certificate serial number, and certification authority's name and identity) and signs the subject's public key (and other information) with the PKI's private key. This creates a digital certificate. Figure 3-9 shows a partial example of a digital certificate highlighting the public key field.

Theoretically, any entity who trusts a particular digital certificate will trust any digital certificate created by the PKI and presented by the subject. The subject presenting the digital certificate is essentially saying "I am who I say I am and you trust it." A PKI can be likened to the Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) in the United States. DMV holders must substantially prove their identity to the DMV in order to get a driver's license with their legal name, address, and birth date, it. After the driver's (i.e., subject's) identity is successfully verified (assured), the DMV will take the subject's picture, add other information, and issue a DMV license, sealed along with the state emblem (somewhat like a real-world digital certificate). If the driver is stopped by law enforcement or to purchase something requiring verification, they will often be required to present their DMV license. The officer and sales clerk trust the DMV license to be accurate and thus rely on the information printed on the license during their verification process.

Much of the Internet works on PKI. For example time you connect to a website using HTTPS, that website has Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secured/Transport Layer Security (HTTPS/TLS) digital certificate and signed by a trusted PKI. You may not personally trust that PKI, but your operating system or software does. When you connect to the website with your browser using the HTTPS protocol, the website sends you (or your browser) a copy of its digital certificate. The digital certificate, signed by a PKI, attests to the website's name (often by URL), the website's public key, and other important information.

If your browser or operating system did not immediately know of and trust a particular PKI, most of the time the involved application would present the PKI owns name and/or digital certificate, and ask if you want to trust it (before trusting anything). You could then select yes or no. If you select, the new PKI is added to a list of stored trusted PKIs that you or your software trusts, so you won't be prompted again next time.

Once verified, your browser will generate a brand-new shared session symmetric key, which then securely sends to the website (using the website's public key). Finally, both the server and client can begin communicating securely using symmetric key communications. Figure 3.10 summarizes the basic HTTPS authentication scenario.

# Hashing

Another integral major cryptographic function is integrity hashing. Hash algorithms (also known as hash functions or simply hashes) are used to create unique output results for unique inputs. They use "one-way" cryptographic functions, which create and output a representative set of characters or bits (the hash, hash result, or message digest) of unique content. Hash functions create digital fingerprints of the content that they hash. Hash functions can be used to cryptographically sign and verify the integrity of content, subjects, and other cryptographic objects. When the integrity hash (often simply as a hash or message digest) is cryptographically tied to a particular cryptographic identity (e.g., user, device, or service), it is known as a digital signature. A verified digital signature allows the receiver of signed content to have confidence that the contents have not been altered since the signing of the content by the authenticated signer. Secure, trusted hash functions have four important traits:

- For every unique input, one output result must be generated. This type of protection is called collision resistance.
