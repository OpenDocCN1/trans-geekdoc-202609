It was seven-factor authentication. It required that the user authenticate to the MFA device. The MFA authenticated to the computer and the user used to authenticate it to the application with multiple factors. The user could initiate access to a website or service only from the MFA device. They could not just open their normal browser and go to a willy-nilly. Only participating websites and services implementing their API could use the MFA option. Not only did users to satisfy some of seven factors, but they had to actually solve a problem that was not possible if they printed it out and kept a solution that was generated during the installation process. I am not making this up; the user had to look at a three-variable quadratic equation and solve complex math involving the solution.

I was amazed. Not only did the creator actually developed a solution that was fairly hard to hack (I could not think of an easy way to hit it at the time, although I had thought of a few says), but I couldn't believe that he thought his solution would work for anyone. I responded, "Congratulations, you made a very secure solution that no one will use!" He, of course, did not believe me. He was lost in his MFA inventor dream where he would have the "perfect" secure MFA solution and the world would beat a path to his and make him rich. With me he disagreed. I told him, "No one wanting to go on Amazon or log into a work network is going to put in seven factors and solve quadratic equations to just on!" He disagreed.

He said that he believed normal caring about computer security would want to use this solution because it was secure, but that he would start off promoting it to very high-security organizations like the National Security Agency (NSA). Although he did not know it at the time, I had taught hundreds of NSA personnel to hack for years when I taught H courses for Foundstone. I had been in the NSA Fort Meade and knew a fair number of NSA people. I replied, "Not even the NSA is going to buy yours. They don't use seven-factor solutions and don't need to. A good MFA solution doesn't need more than three or four factors to be secure." Is it crypto; good crypto doesn't need overly key sizes, like a billion bits big. When you need or advertise large key sizes, you're essentially buying crypto that isn't great. He disagreed and said he was disappointed that I didn't see the same vision. And then, we mutually agreed to parts.

After a year went by, I emailed him to ask how things were going. Turns the NSA and Amazon did not want his product. He said two organizations were using his product. When I asked who was using it, said both companies were using it for a limited beta testing program. I pressed for names and contact information. He refused to give me names so I could interview users to get their thoughts on what it was like to use a seven-factor solution. I had this story before. I interviewed small and new computer security businesses for a living for over 20 years. Likely, his "demonstration projects" means he is using it at whatever he works full-time, in a very limited way, and one of my friends is doing the same as a personal favor.

Ultimately, he will never sell his product to anyone. I don't mean to be a negative person, crushing dreams. I'm not always right. I probably wouldn't have seen the genius of Instagram. But I have doing computer security for 32 years and 20 years' experience as a computer security columnist
