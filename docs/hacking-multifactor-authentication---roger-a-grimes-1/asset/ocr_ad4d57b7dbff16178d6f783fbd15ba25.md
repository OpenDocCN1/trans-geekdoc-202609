# Chapter 6 Access Control Tokens 153

**NOTE** The widely trusted CAs do at least some small amount of checking for malicious intent before issuing a digital certificate, although what they do to verify the legitimacy and intent of a particular domain varies greatly by CA. But the good ones try to stop the easy-to-stop rogue domain names that appear to be attempting to spoof well-known, legitimate domains.

Today, scammers use the Level 2 method in their phishing scams. With either Level 1 or Level 2 attacks, unless the user realize they are connecting to a fake website or their authentication solution doesn't mitigate them, it is easy to fool unsuspecting users. These types of MitM attacks can be defeated by the user being aware of what the legitimate URL is and ensuring that they never get tricked. But as we know, humans can be socially engineered into clicking on the wrong links. Far superior is mandatory enforcing the use of site-specific credentials, like FIDO, to prevent these types of MitM attacks.

With the Level 3 MitM proxy method, the attacker somehow redirects the victim to a lookalike website, which appears to the victim's device or software as hosted at the legitimate domain (or is actually hosted at the legitimate domain without a valid, TLS certificate enrolled). The attacker can poison DNS so that the victim's software is redirected to the fake website even when the user types in the legitimate name. the user types or clicks `www.bankofamerica.com` and it redirects to a fake Bank of America that appears to the victim's software to be hosted at `www.bankofamerica.com` even when it is hosted elsewhere. DNS poisoning attacks used to be more popular over a decade ago, but they have significantly diminished in popularity as anti-DNS poisoning techniques have been widely adopted. Still, there are various ways to accomplish DNS poisoning and these attacks still occur.

Attackers steal the legitimate site's domain for the legitimate domain owner and use it to redirect victims to their fake website hosted under the legitimate website's domain name. This happens and is covered in more detail in Chapter 18, "DNS Hijacking".

The Level 4 MitM proxy attack method involves a fake website that appears hosted at the legitimate domain and has another legitimate TLS digital certificate. The TLS digital certificate is not the same as the legitimate site's real TLS certificate, but nevertheless it is tied to the real website's domain name. Usually this is accomplished by a trusted CA compromised and then used to issue fraudulent but trusted digital certificates. This sort of attack first broke news in 2011 with the compromise of DigiNotar(`www.computerweekly.com/224105500/DigiNotar-digital-certificate-fraud-could-affect-millions-of-users-SecurEnv`).

A newer defense could certificate transparency and another method called channel binding both try to mitigate Level 4 attacks by comparing the previously stored and verified digital fingerprint (hash) of the legitimate site's known and trusted TLS digital certificate with the currently provided digital certificate that a user (or, more realistically, the software on their behalf) is interacting with a current connection.
