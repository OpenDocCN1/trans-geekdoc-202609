## Specific Endpoint Attack Examples

Here are some specific endpoint attack examples.

### Bancos Trojans

Bancos is word "banks" in Spanish. Bancos trojans refers to a class of trojan horse programs that are specifically designed to steal money from people's bank accounts. They are known as trojans because they first became popular in South America because South America and other Spanish-speaking countries had a lack of widely accessible physical banking locations and automated teller machines (ATMs). When ATMs are available, they are often out of service, unreliable, and untrustworthy. They have high fees, and so, users often have their card information stolen and reused to make fraudulent transactions. Consequently, South Americans jumped on the "e-banking" movement quicker than other nations to secure their remote customers. So, did hackers and malicious mobile code too.

Early on, banking hackers broke into PCs and stole bank login credentials. As antivirus programs got better, they started to conduct man-in-the-middle (MitM) attacks on the network connections between the customer and the bank. They would sniff the unprotected HTTP connection, steal user login credentials, or modify the user's commands to cause an unseen malicious transaction to occur. The banks responded by making MitM attacks much more difficult, and by requiring users to use SMS-based MFA. South American customers were among the first customers to use MFA as part of a large-scale, public deployment. Using MFA seemed to thwart the hackers for a little while, but hackers responded by creating bancos trojans. Bancos trojans work by exploiting end-user systems and waiting for the user to successfully log on to an intended hosted system. After the user successfully logs on, no matter how they logged on, MFA or not, the hacker can do many things, including the following actions:

- If not using MFA, the user's login credentials are [stolen] and sent to the hacker.
- Steal the access control token and send it to the hacker.
- Inject code into the bank's web page (called web injection) that prompts the user for login credentials or other additional private information (such as email address, SSNs, or their phone number) which is then sent to the hacker.
- Open a remote backdoor session, dial home to the hacker, and let the hacker remotely do anything they want to the system.
- Intercept the user's typed-in commands and mouse clicks and modify them on their way to the bank to conduct another malicious transaction.
- Start an additional hidden browser session and conduct a clandestine malicious transaction that is not known to the user.

### Hacking Multifactor Authentication

With the latter action, the bank doesn't know that a malicious trojan is conducting the transaction. Every instruction appears to be coming from the legitimate customer and user who just successfully logged in. The user thinks they are securely logging in to check their bank balance or to transfer $10,000 to a bank in Russia. The latter type of bancos trojan is part of an attack type known as a man-in-the-browser (MitB) attack. The bancos trojan can recognize how much they can transfer without setting off the financial institution's fraud checks. It may even change the user's email address, phone number, mailing address, and login method. That way, if the bank decides to call the user to confirm the transaction, the bank ends up calling a Skype number where the hackers are in the name and personae of the targeted victim.

Alexandre Cagnoni of cybersecurity company WatchGuard (www.watchguard.com), and one of his book's editors, has years of fighting bancos trojans in Brazil. He shared that a couple of years ago, Brazilian hackers started selling a self-teaching tool, with code and everything, teaching how to create your own MitB trojan. It was around $200, as far as he remembers. One of his friends who worked on fraud prevention at a bank bought a kit. That said it in a case, a professional-looking box with a DVD and manual were very well explained.

Cagnoni also shared that around April 2019, banks in Brazil started seeing an unknown-related attack. Criminals in the streets were stealing iPhones and Android phones from people in the streets of Sao Paulo. Forty minutes later, the victim's money was transferred from the customer account to another bank, using the mobile banking application, even if the phone and banking app were both protected by PIN and biometrics (face or fingerprint). His team was baffled. At that time, the only thing they discovered was that all the phones were being taken to the GPS location—a neighborhood in Sao Paulo (called Santa Íteria), where there were a lot of shady electronics stores. Their only guess was that the attackers were using some specific high-end hardware to bypass the biometrics check within the phone and mobile banking app.

Bancos trojans have been around for nearly two decades and have stolen billions of dollars. Single bancos trojan families have been implicated in a billion dollar (www.dsolutionsgroup.com/spyeye-caused-1-billion-losses). They are still very popular. Bancos tro target not only banks but any organization the hackers can transfer money or value from. This includes stock trading, cryptocurrency, and money transfer sites.

Bancos trojans are a major class of malware and can contain hundreds of different features, but in general most of their work follows the following way:

1. First, the trojans gain unauthorized access to the victim computer using any root cause exploit, usually social engineering or unpatched software.
2. It modifies the system so that it can [persist].
3. It monitors the browser the user is using (Chrome, Edge, Safari) to capture data.
4. It monitors the URL.
5. When the user visits a banking site, the trojan displays a fake page. own6. It performs malicious actions in the background.
