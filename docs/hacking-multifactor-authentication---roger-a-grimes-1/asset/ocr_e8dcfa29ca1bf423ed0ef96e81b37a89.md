It's important to mention that OAuth is about authorization, not authentication, even though it involves authentication as well. With OAuth, the user has to authenticate, at least to the identity provider or first site/service, but after that, the first sites and software the user is using exchange authenticated access control tokens. The user doesn't have to reauthenticate to additional sites or services, although they usually have to approve (click Login or Allow) the new login/access to the new site and service (see examples of approval prompts in Figures 19.3 to 19.5). The subjects involved in the accesses aren't reauthenticating. They do provide additional authentication proofs. They are past part of the authentication process and are now in the authorization part of the identity cycle. A user's authentication proofs are not being shared between sites. The original site/service is attesting to the user having successfully authenticated and helps to establish the subject accessing future sites and services.

The future sites and services may involve another logon, the user will not be asked to authenticate if they have already authenticated the selected identity somewhere else. In Figure 19.3, the user is accessing a new site, Grup Vacation Rentals, and the Grupz site is detecting that the user has already successfully authenticated to two previous OAuth providers (in this case, Facebook and Google). Many of the times when you are seeing “log on with Facebook,”log on Google,” or “log on with Apple,” what you are seeing is OAuth involved and working. Any site with OAuth needs to register with one or more OAuth identity providers. In this example, Grupz has previously registered with at least Facebook and Google. Many participating websites have only one or the other, because they figure most people are going to have either, and why the cost of offering additional OAuth providers.

The user need only click one of the shown buttons and Grupz and Google/Facebook will exchange the necessary authorization information to allow the user to log on without providing additional authentication information (although the user can log using non-OAuth credentials).

If you haven't logged into your OAuth identity provider (as Facebook or Google in Figure 19.3) in a while, you may be asked to authenticate them again before they authorize you to log on to the additional site/service. The login credentials you will be submitting are to the site/service you trust (i.e., Facebook/Google) and not to the new site (Grupz, in this case). The new site/service will get your authentication proofs. If the additional sites or services are compromised, attackers will get only a single access control token that is only good for the single, site/service and not your authentication proofs.
