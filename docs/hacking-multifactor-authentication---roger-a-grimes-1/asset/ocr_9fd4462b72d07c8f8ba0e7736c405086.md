Comparing and identifying faces requires identifying data points on a newly acquired image and comparing to existing stored images and determining the likelihood that stored data points most accurately match a candidate. It usually isn't a 100 percent match; it's more like a "preponderance of evidence" wins the day. Although the underlying physical features and structure of someone's face doesn't change often, more transient traits in people's faces vary on a day-to-day basis, and certainly more over time. People get skin blemishes, hair growths (e.g., hair, eyebrows, mustaches, be), and weight changes. We have different facial expressions for the situation, and facial traits for a frown are distinctly different than for a big smile. Hairstyles, hats, scarves, and glasses can cover a lot of our face. And few do look like our high school yearbook photos. Facial recognition algorithms make a weighted guess about the probability of any two images matching and propose a potential match for the one(s) with the highest probability comparison part can take longer than most biometric comparison analyses. It's a very complicated analysis, with lot of math involved. If you are interested in learning more about how facial recognition works and algorithms, check out the broad class of facial recognition algorithms known as eigenfaces (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenface), but there are dozens of different facial recognition algorithms used by various systems.
