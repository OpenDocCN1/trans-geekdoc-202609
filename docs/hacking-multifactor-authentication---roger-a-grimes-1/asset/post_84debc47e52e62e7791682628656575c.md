### Third-Party Reliances

One of the fastest growing segments of phishing is rogue emails from taken-over, compromised computers of trusted people and partners you have an ongoing relationship with. Most of the normal antiphishing advice, such as “Don’t open emails from people you don’t know,” defies this. A phishing email sent from someone you have an ongoing relationship is very hard. Defenses mainly come down to “Call the person at a previously communicated phone number any time they request you to do something out of the ordinary to confirm.”

Here’s a good example of this type of phishing. In the United States, most people buying a house get a mortgage loan. As part of that process, the buyer usually has to make a down payment (ranging from 1 percent to 20 percent of the loan or property’s value). The down payment must be held in “escrow” by a third party before the mortgage can be approved and the buyers take possession of the property.

Phishers will infiltrate loan officer or escrow agents and inventory the pending property closings and note ones which have large down payments. They will then send the buyers an email, which the buyers have been told to expect on the day they are expecting, telling them to make an escrow payment that they have been told to expect, for the amount they have been told to expect, for the property they want to buy. The email arrives from a person the buyer already trusts and detail in the email is legit except for the writing instructions they infiltrate. The phisher uses the legit banking writing instructions for instructions to their bank. They usually create an email on the compromised system that deletes any future outgoing emails from the legitimate agent to the victim and re-routes any incoming emails to the scam. By the time the victim finds out what has happened, the money is long gone. The victim is out their down payment and everyone else in the financial pathway that was going to get a piece of the mortgage fees (usually more than a handful of people) are out of money because the mortgage does not close. The buyers lose their property and everyone involved other than the phisher is out their money. Phishing using previously compromised third parties is dearly costly.

All of these characteristics are common traits of phishing content. It goes without saying that social engineering and phishing is often used to bypass access accounts through MFA.

> NOTE: Anyone can be tricked by social engineering and phishing. Anyone! How susceptible you or aren’t has to do with measured intelligence or “street smart.” Anyone, appropriately motivated, can be tricked into doing something that is against their own self-interest.

### Fake Bank Alert

A very common scam these days is for someone to message or call a victim, claiming to be from the victim's bank, PayPal, hotel scam, stock investment site, and so forth, and claiming to have detected a potentially malicious fraud. When they call, they will say something like, "Hello, Mr. Grimes, we are from your bank [or insert bank name here] and we have detected potentially fraudulent transactions on your account." The victim will say, "No!" Then the scammer continues, "We didn't think you. Don't worry, we have blocked these two transactions and we are going to take care of you. We have detected other fraudulent transactions on your account and we want to block them so you won't be responsible for the charges. We just need to confirm that you are really Mr. Grimes and your relevant account information. What is your account number?" The victim gives the account number. The scammer asks, "What is your PIN or password?" The victim gives them that information.

The scammer then tries to log to the victim's account using that information. If the victim has MFA enabled, the scammer will pull the account in "recovery mode" and get the bank to send a login code to the victim's cell phone using SMS. The scammer will say something similar to "We are going to send you code to your previously registered cell phone to verify that you are who you say, Mr. Grimes. I need you to read back code once you receive it." The victim receives the account recovery PIN code sent by the bank and reads it to the scammer, who then takes to take over the account. The scammer changes the user's login method, often disabling MFA and the current password. If the victim is currently logged in their account, the attacker asks the user to log out and back in again. Of course, the victim can no longer log in their account. The fake bank support team tells them they are taking care of everything and will call the bank in a few minutes. Of course, within few minutes, the attackers steal as much money as they can. The "bank" never answers the victim's call. The victim usually ends up waiting hours, frustrated, and tries to call the "bank" back. The "bank" never answers the victim's call.

### Crying Babies

## 268 Hacking Multifactor Authentication

Almost every major service uses MFA, but attackers are still finding ways to bypass it. One common method is "MFA fatigue," where the attacker repeatedly triggers MFA requests on the victim's phone until the victim approves one out of frustration or accident. Another method is SIM swapping, where the attacker tricks a mobile carrier into transferring the victim's phone number to a SIM card under their control.
