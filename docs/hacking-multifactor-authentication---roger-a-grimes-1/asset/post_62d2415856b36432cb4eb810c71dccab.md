## Migrations

Migration is a less common hacking method and is included here for completeness. Rarely does an organization get a single authentication method that they use the rest of their online life. Usually, the authentication system or its identity databases gets upgraded or newer, better methods. Organizations, through mergers and acquisitions, often change. These changes often require that older and existing authentication systems and users be migrated to a newer or another system. There are specific hacks that can occur in these migrations.

For example, there is an older known escalation of privilege (EOP) hack known as a SID History attack (attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1098/) that can occur during Active Directory network migrations. All AD principal accounts (user, computer, group, etc.) have an attribute named SID History. This field was intended to allow administrators to populate a security principal's current and/or future group memberships when an security principal is migrated to a new AD forest. The SID History was not well known, protected, or monitored until Windows Server 2003 came along. Prior to that, a malicious admin could add elevated group memberships (e.g., Schema, Admin Domains, Administrators) to a security principal's account, and when it migrated it would become a member of the elevated group, sometimes without the destination forest or domain admitting to be or even aware that it happened. It was a little but critical privilege escalation attack for many years. Starting with Windows Server 2003, AD "filtered" out SID values by default to stop the unintended security elevations. Even today, enabling or disabling SID Filtering only changing a single binary value in AD. All a malicious admin has to change a 1 to a 0 in order to allow the SID History attack to work. See docs.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/it-pro/windows-server-2008-r2/cc794577 for more details.

Here's another example of an attack which takes advantage of migrations: When everyone is migrated to a new system, the administrator assigns everyone a common, identical, or the assigned password for each user is different but based on an easily recognizable and predictable pattern (say, the user's first initial of their first name followed by the last name). Or when users are set up for the first time on a new MFA device, all MFA devices in the same system may have a common PIN of 1234 and everyone knows this fact. Any malicious user of the repeated password or password pattern could take advantage of it by using other user accounts.

Migrations are usually frequent, irregular acts. Many processes do not check for potential mitigation exploits, and a lot of administrators do not check or worry about such attacks. Because of this, uncommon as migrations are, they are still a valid risk for any organization.

## Deprovision

To complete the authentication life cycles, deprovisioning is the process of deleting, or inactivating identities. This is usually done by the enrolling CSP, but it can be done or requested by a relying party. Deprovisioning is often weakly managed and controlled. It is not uncommon for there to be more inactive and unused accounts than are active, used accounts in an authentication system. Every inactive, unused account that is not derovisioned is a potential threat to everyone in the system. This is especially true of the unused accounts which have admin access to the authentication system being used. All authentication systems should strive to strict measurement and control over which accounts are active being used and which become inactive and not used, and deprovision the latter.

## MFA Content Conclusion

Many times a successful hack or bypass of an MFA solution was accomplished via a method that had little to do with the MFA solution. It was often done via a weakness or issue in a component the MFA solution must rely on but has no control over. The MFA vendor doesn't have the ability to strengthen the component it must rely on. It is forced to trust the security of the relied-upon component and be held hostage by that dependency's own security—in good and bad times.

One of the lessons to take away from this chapter and book is that MFA solutions are rarely dependent only on themselves. More, if not always, an MFA solution is one of a part authentication system. An MFA vendor or user's job is to implement and secure the components they are in charge of and can control, secure them with the right amount of security. As for the relied-upon you cannot control, you should threat model as well as whether you can implement mitigations and controls to offset the potential risks from required reliance. An MFA vendor or consumer should not just throw their hands in the air just they don't have control over something. No, their job is to predict, access, and mitigate risk no matter how it is involved. And when they've done now the best reasonable job at mitigating the risk from others the MFA vendor or consumer now can accept the risk.

## Main Hacking Methods

There are three main methods to hack anything digital, including MFA solutions: technical, human, and physical. Many attacks against MFA are combinations of more than one.

## Technical Attacks

Technical attacks are assaults against the technical elements of the digital solution. They are attacks against the solution itself, how it is designed, and how it operates. They are a direct attack on the digital components of the solution. For instance, a technical attack can be a method to expose stored authentication secrets.

For example, as mentioned earlier Windows Active Directory authentication logon secrets are often kept in a database called NTDS.DIT on every participating domain controller, and logon secrets are stored locally in a registry or file known as the SAM (Security Accounts Manager) store. In both instances, Microsoft has gone to technical lengths to protect the stored authentication events. The all-powerful local Administrator or members of the Domain Administrators group cannot directly access the secrets stored in the Windows or Active Directory authentication databases. To view the stored authentication secrets, a subject or process must have the even higher SYSTEM (i.e., local system) permissions or elevated. Additionally, the operating system that protects those secrets try to prevent known attacks and attack tools from accessing those secrets, even if the attacker has the necessary permissions and privileges. But each year a few attacks are announced that thwart Microsoft's best attempts to stop them. It's a constant cat-and-mouse game.

Other examples of technical attacks are compromising the encryption that protects the secrets, finding predictable patterns in what should be truly random information, locating accidental text copies of what is supposed to be encrypted information, compromising involved endpoints and other participants, hijacking namespaces, eavesdropping on communication channels, and other similar attacks. A technical attack is finding a flaw in the digital technology that underpins the solution's safety and security.

## Human Element

There is a common saying in the computer security world: "The weakest part of any defense is the human." I'm not sure if that is always true, but social engineering of humans in some form accounts for 70 to 90 percent of all malicious breaches. No root hacker cause comes close! The biggest cause for malicious hacking is unpatched software, which is involved in 20 to 40 percent of all malicious data breaches (they are often paired with each other in an attack). Every other root cause you can think of—misconfigurations, eavesdropping, data malformation—combined only account for 10 percent of the risk in most environments. Social engineering is the number one or two reason for successful malicious hacking for the entropy of networked devices.

NOTE The percentages were measured from a private study I used with the largest data breach database in the world, the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (privacyrights.org). It currently tracks over 11.6 billion breached records. I spent months researching to find the root cause behind every breach. I weeded out nonacious data breaches, as people leaving records behind or accidentally sending records to the wrong person in email. I encountered on intentionally malicious breaches or those that could have likely exposed to a malicious person. Other studies back up my figures (for example, this study says phishing is responsible for 91 percent of all cyberattacks (digitalguardian.com/blog/91-percent-cyber-attacks-start-phishing-how-protect-against-phishing), and I consider my study the most accurate within the constraints of my consideration. A coworker of mine, Javad Malik, did a meta-study (info.knowbe4.com/threat-intelligence-to-build-your-data-driven-defense) of one hundred other root cause studies and found that, although they greatly differed on the actual percentage, all hundred concluded that social engineering and phishing were the number one root cause of cybersecurity incidents.
