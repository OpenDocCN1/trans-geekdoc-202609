So, most brute-force methods guess randomly at whatever their target happens to be—password, PINs, or whatever—across the entire population of people. And some, guesses implement more logic, trying out what is statistically the most logical before trying the less likely answers. It makes sense to do things the fastest way when performing bruteforcing. The latter approach decreases the speed of the solution if the solution isn't one of the most likely, earliest, answers. It is always an accuracy versus speed trade-off. If the number of guesses is relatively small and the attacker can have an unlimited number of guesses, then it makes sense to use the brute-force attack, because eventually you will find the solution. ## Brute-Force Attack Methods

There are two main ways to conduct brute-force attacks: manual and automated.

### Manual

Manual brute-force guessing is a human being taking guesses at one time. For example, perhaps you have to guess a four-digit PIN. Human can sit there and try four-digit combination after four-digit combination. Manual, human-based guessing has a benefit that any known information about the victim, such as their birthday, their children's birthdays, favorite numbers, or anyone previously used PINs can be used to try to figure out the correct PIN than brute-force guessing alone.

The biggest problem with human-based, manual guessing is that in a short amount of time a limited number of guesses most humans remember what combinations they already tried and what combinations did not. After a few dozens, the average human attacker is likely to start making duplicate guesses using previous values without realizing it. Computer-based, automated tools are great at guessing really fast and in keeping track of what was previously tried that did not work so they won't waste time trying them again.

### Automated

The hacking world is full of hundreds, not thousands, automated brute-force guessing programs. All an attacker has to do is find the right tool that is allowed to interface with a particular target, set operational parameters (such as has many guesses to try in a particular time), and launch the tool. Hacking tools, free and commercial, such as The Ripper (www.openwall.com/john/), Cain and Able, L0ptcrack (www.10ptcrack.com), Ophrack (ophcrack.sourceforge.io/), Burp Suite (portswigger.burp), and Hydra (www.hackingtools.in/free-download-hydra-v-7-4-fast-network-cracker/), are famously used by millions of white- and black-hat hackers to brute-force their way past logins. This is not to say that brute-force guessing cannot be mitigated, and I'll discuss this in the “Developer Defenses Against Brute-Force Attacks” section in this chapter.

## Example of Brute-Force Attacks

Let's look at some real-world brute-force attack examples.

### OTP Bypass Brute-Force Test

The first example comes from a white-hat hacker who successfully brute-forced a site using a one-time password (OTP) MFA solution (medium.com/bugountywriteup/how-i-bypassed-the-otp-verification-process-part-1-e5b33274ae9). He connected to the website and performed the normal logon/account registration process until the website requested the OTP code to validate his/registration. His previous knowledge and testing confirmed that the site used six-digit OTP.

He then used Burp Suite to capture the website's packet request around the OTP submission. He located the OTP submission field (see Figure 14.1) after the labeled codetextbox. The developer could have hidden the field or its value in many ways to at least obscure what purpose of.

The hacker used the “Intruder” mode in Burp to guess six-digit OTP, ranging from 000000 to 999999. He found out that Instagram allowed a maximum of 250 guesses per IP address. With a million guesses to try, that would mean the hacker would need 4,000 IP addresses (although it says 5000 in the article). He found out that Instagram was not permanently blacklisting IP addresses that guessed too times, blocking them temporarily for a given set period. After the time period passed, they were welcome to try again. He then used 4,000 different IP addresses using a commercial service worth about $15. He eventually won over 200,000 guesses without being blocked. For his trouble and publicly reporting it to the vendor so that it could be fixed, the hacker was awarded $30,000.

### Instagram Brute-Force

Instagram was acquired by Facebook in 2012 and is arguably one of the most popular services in the world. In July 2019, a white-hat hacker announced that he bypassed Instagram's 2FA (threatpost.com/researcher-bypasses-instagram-14646/).

The hacker started off trying to guess Instagram's OTP from a single location. By testing this, he quickly learned that Instagram allowed 250 guesses per originating IP address. He found out he could double the guesses per IP by using different sessions.

You may wonder why Instagram allowed so many guesses, 250, at a time before blocking a particular IP address. Possibly, it's also possible that Instagram has seen many legitimate logins or processes that guessed a lot, and then determined from their own experience that allowing 249 guesses was a good decision for them. More on this decision in the "Defenses Against Brute-Force Attacks" section later in this chapter.

### Slack MFA

In this example (hackerrone.com/reports/121696), a white-hat hacker discovered that brute-force attacks could be used to get around Slack's MFA solution. Slack (slack.com) is a very popular messaging app. It's used by millions of people around the world.

The hacker discovered that, even if MFA is enabled on Slack, anyone could go into a user's password-reset fetch, which would then send an OTP code for verification.
