# ASN.1 Tricks

ASN.1 is a standard interface description language for defining hierarchical, tree-like data structures. It is used widely in computers, telecommunications, and cryptography. Simple Network Management Protocol (SNMP); Kerberos; LDAP; wireless radio tags; and 3G, 4G, and 5G networks. You can learn about ASN.1 here: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_Syntax_Notation_One.

In 2004, hackers used an ASN.1 vulnerability to cause a denial-of-service attack against Microsoft's core network protocol NTLv2 (www.canada.com/article/hackers-take-advantage-of-microsoft-asn-flaw-15081). In 2016, an ASN-1-based attack allowed hackers to execute code across a broad range of telecommunications equipment: commrisk.com/asn-1-bug-letters-attack-mobile-carriers. These types of ASN.1 attacks could be used to allow SMS SIM hijacking. Here is an example of a videoconferencing system being hacked using its ASN.1 elements: media.blackhat.com/eu-13/briefings/Jodeit/bh-eu-13-hacking-video-jodeit-wp.pdf.

I bring up ASN.1 to show that our OS and devices rely on shared standards that can be avenues into many different, often unexpected avenues of attack. Oftentimes the protocols are full of unknown vulnerabilities or obscure technical use. They aren't in everyone's face every day like DNS is. When hackers hack at Punycode, ASN.1, and other supporting protocols and standards, they often find exploitable vulnerabilities.

When you are on the Internet, you're reading content from a website, watching a video, or sending an email, your keystrokes and received content go through a lot of devices, usually routers, between your computer and the other side and back. The average Internet packet goes 15 to 22 different routers between source and destination. A lot of routers and routing. Anyone sniffing on those routers or routers could potentially eavesdrop on your unencrypted data (or metadata). But in order to focus on someone or an organization in particular, an attacker would need to know exactly which routers or routes your traffic will take. The Internet has millions of routers, and it's hard to predict where one network packet will go another (although there are well-known router aggregation points in every country). That's the beauty of it.

# Developer Defenses

First we'll look at some developer defenses against namespace hijacking attacks.

# Education

Education is always the primary key to fighting cybersecurity threats. Developers should understand the importance of namespaces to their MFA solution and understand the dependencies well enough to threat-model possible attacks.

# Threat Model

We rarely think of namespaces when doing cybersecurity threat modeling, but if your authentication solution depends on them—and probably does—any dependencies have to be threat-modeled, analyzed for threats, and mitigated.

# Default Packet Encryption

Encrypt all traffic headed to and your MFA solution so that if someone tries to eavesdrop on network traffic or manipulate a namespace, what little they can read is of limited value. The encryption should always use industry-accepted ciphers and key sizes.

# DNS Registrar Record Lockdowns

If your MFA solution uses DNS as part of its solution and DNS points back to your records, make sure that no unauthorized person can hijack your DNS records. Discuss with your DNS registrar how they and you can prevent DNS hijacks.

# Enable SPF, DKIM, DMARC

Sender Policy Framework (SPF), DomainKeys Identified (DKIM), and Domain-Based Message Authentication and Reporting (DMARC) are protocols used to prevent email spoofing and phishing.

- SPF allows domain owners to specify which mail servers are authorized to send email on their domain.
- DKIM adds a digital signature to emails, verifying that the content was not tampered with.
- DMARC provides instructions to receiving servers on what to do if SPF or DKIM checks fail.

# Hard-Code Values Where It Makes Sense

If your MFA solution uses DNS as part of its solution and DNS points back to your records, make sure that no unauthorized person can hijack your DNS records. Discuss with your DNS registrar how they and you can prevent DNS hijacks.

# Decrease of Namespace Manipulation

Instead of hard-coding values, a better solution is to program your solution in such a way that it would be difficult to manipulate without detection. For example, if your MFA solution connects back to a common website, the website and MFA do a known-response handshake as part of the initial authentication. For example, the website might already know a hard-coded value ahead of time that the MFA device uses to start the process, like a TOTP device. Anyone eavesdropping on the initial connection might see data being passed but not know how the data is composed or constructed. Any site without the correct handshake information would not be seen from participating clients, and vice versa.

# Site and Device Preregistration

Requiring that all sites and MFA devices be identified by a GUID and preregistered with the sites and services they will be involved with helps prevent man-in-the-middle attacks.

# Don't Support IDN or Punycode

I understand the appeal of IDN and Punycode for international language support. However, I read about malicious bypasses and social engineering of users using these standards for anything else. The best of both worlds would be filtering that prevents the malicious misuse of these character translation mechanisms while still allowing them to be used. Today, the most popular browsers attempt to recognize common IDN/Punycode redirections. But in the absence of those sorts of defenses, just ban code sets inserted. They are not worth the risk.
