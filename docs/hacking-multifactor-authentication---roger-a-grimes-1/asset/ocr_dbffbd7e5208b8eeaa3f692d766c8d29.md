# Use Industry-Accepted Cryptography and Key Sizes

If cryptography is involved, use industry-accepted cryptography and key sizes. Don't try to make your own. Many developers have taken a cryptography course or they think they can create a new cryptography solution that is uncrackable. Few of them can. Strong cryptography is much more difficult than it seems, and only a few people in the world at any time are good at it. Unfortunately, thousands of people think they can design a stronger cipher. Even the very best cryptographers get it wrong.

As an example, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) received 82 proposed quantum-resistant cryptography candidates during round 1 of its post-quantum cipher contest (csrc.nist.gov/SRC/media/Projects/Post-Quantum-Cryptography/documents/call-of-proposals-final-dec-2016.pdf). Most of these submissions were highly respected, experienced cryptographers, with PhDs in cryptography. Of those original 82 submissions, only 26 were selected to compete in the next round. Although there were other disqualifications and consolidations, most of the rejections because of obvious cryptographic flaws that others easily discovered. Of the 26 remaining candidates, observers are still finding errors. Some promising candidates turned out to have huge cryptographic flaws that, had been prematurely implemented, would have caused security issues.

And this is the result from experienced cryptography professors and practitioners. Good cryptography takes many years and a lot of testing to prove. If cryptography is your day job, don't even think about trying around to invent your own cryptography when many other great candidates already exist and are trusted as secure around the world. Don't try to prove how smart you are. Just use industry-accepted cryptography and key sizes.

For bonus points, code your application in such a way that the cryptography used is easily changed (crypto-agile) as new cryptography and schemes become available as recommended replacements. It makes so easy that your customers can easily upgrade and replace them without having to do a complete installation or replacement. Don't hard-code the cryptography in a way that is not easily replaceable or upgradable.

> NOTE Quantum computers are expected to see break traditional forms of public key cryptography and weaken by half the symmetric ciphers, hashes, and random number generators. It is likely that, by 2022 or soon thereafter, we will be upgrading our asymmetric ciphers and digital signatures to newer, quantum-resistant cryptography. If you are a developer, help the world's move to quantum-resistant ciphers and schemes easier for everyone. For more information, consider reading my book on the coming quantum break, Cryptography Apocalypse (Wiley, 2019).
