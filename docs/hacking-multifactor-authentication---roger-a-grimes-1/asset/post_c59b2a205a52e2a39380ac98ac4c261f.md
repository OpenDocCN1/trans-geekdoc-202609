## Enrollment

Enrollment (also known as *provisioning*) is the process of a subject being registered and verified to get an authenticated identity (label). An unverified and unregistered subject is known as an *applicant*. They are applying for an authenticated identity. The *credential service provider* (CSP) asks for one or more (unique) subject attributes, which can be used to clearly identify the subject. This process is known as *identity proofing*. The subject can enroll themselves or have another *enrollment agent* trusted by both the applicant and the CSP do it on their behalf.

A critical part of the process is that the CSP verifies the applicant's submitted attributes. Without the CSP strictly verifying the applicant's submitted attributes and ensuring that the applicant is who they say they are and the submitted applicant attributes truly belong to the applicant, authentication is nearly worthless. How strongly the CSP verifies the identity and attributes of the applicant determines the level of *assurance*.

Think of the enrollment process like a person applying for a driver's license. The person must pass a physical driving test to show that they have basic driving skills, answer a set of knowledge-based questions to prove they know the “rules of the road,” and prove they are who they say are. The latter involves providing sufficient proof to the driver license issuer (the CSP in this example in the United States is the Department of Motor Vehicles [DMV]) to get the official driver’s license. The applicant does this by providing multiple forms of acceptable identity attributes, such as a birth certificate, Social Security card, and a bill sent to their home residence to verify the identity. Years ago, the DMV did not require much assurance as they do now, but weak validation allowed serious identity crimes to occur, and so now the DMV requires stronger identity assurance.

But if you get a driver’s license issued to you, anyhow (e.g., law enforcement, liquor store) that requests proof of your identity will accept the DMV-issued driver’s license as proof of identity. The same thing with CSPs. Any relying party trusts the CSP will trust a CSP-verified identity by a subject to be accurate. But if the CSP doesn’t do their job and doesn't accurately verify the subject’s identity and attributes before verifying it, the whole process breaks down.

A good, everyday digital example of this is the use of Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) asymmetric encryption keys. Created in 1981 by Phil Zimmermann, PGP is a popular encryption program that comes in both open and commercial versions. Everyone gets a PGP public key from someone else is supposed to validate it before using it. But almost no one does in practice. In the past, fraudulent users noticed that the supplied PGP key was invalid (e.g., an invalid fingerprint). So, everyone who uses FGP keys and the ones received is participating in this invalid level of privacy and authentication when it is no better than regular, unencrypted email (or perhaps worse, because people think they can rely on it). A verified identity (and their attributes) is known as verified subscriber. The CSP usually maintains ownership of the identity and can renew, cancel, or update it, solely at its discretion. The authentication factors for the identity and by CSP are also known as authenticators. When the subscriber authenticates to a relying party (e.g., device, system, service), they are known as claimant. The claimant can also provide, along with their authenticators, one or more claims, which have also been verified by the CSP. This is a good overall description of the enrollment process and although individual enrollment varies greatly. The generalized enrollment process is summarized in Figure 5.2. It is a similar view of authentication life cycle presented in Chapter 2, but summarizes the CSP part (without the other complexity). The biggest risk to the enrollment process is a malicious subject being enrolled to become a verified subscriber backed by the CSP and trusted by the relying party. Malicious actors do this to obtain access to authenticated identity gets seen as more legitimate than if they posed as anonymous user. Of course the trust given identity depends the steps the CSP takes to verify the subscriber’s identity before issuing them a “verified” account. Every day the most popular CSPs (e.g., Facebook, Google, Microsoft, Twitter) suffer from millions of fraudulent accounts.

## Devices/Hardware

Any of the devices involved in authentication can be maliciously compromised. Devices include computers, phones, storage, memory, network interfaces, and any other physical device involved in the authentication process. They can be compromised to steal authentication secrets, access tokens, eavesdrop, steal data, and redirect the user or authentication process to a fraudulent site or service. If a hardware device is compromised, it is difficult (although not impossible) for the authentication process to be trusted. This is not to say that some hardware isn't more hacking-resistant than others.

Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS)-certified hardware is not easy to compromise. Other groups, like the Trusted Computing Group (trustedcomputinggroup.org) are dedicated to proving trustworthy devices that prevent unauthorized modification or detect and alert the relying parties. The programs that guarantee trustworthy devices have various names, such as Trusted Hardware and Trusted Computing. The idea is that if the user and all the software is not trust the hardware (and any other related components) to be secure, the rest of the process, including authentication, cannot be trusted.

Trusted hardware schemes usually involve a "root of trust," which verifies that the device has not been maliciously modified. The different hardware components of one device will verify themselves and/or be verified by a higher-level component in a series of trust chains. For example, in a Windows computer, the computer's hardware starts with a previously trusted basic hardware called the Extensible Firmware Interface (UEFI). It replaced the less trustworthy Basic Input/Output System (BIOS) chips upon older systems. The UEFI firmware boot chip contains coding that is digitally signed by the author. Each higher-layer relying hardware component relies on the previous level to verify its signature. Signature of such trust chains continues until the entire Windows system software boot sequence is accomplished, using processes Secure Boot, Trusted Boot, and Measured Boot. You can read more about how it works: docs.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/information-protection/secure-the-windows-10-boot-process.

Every authentication device should have a similar trusted measurement performed and verified. Although it's rare in today's world, every device in the authentication process (i.e., every computer, router, server, service) should have similar hardware and software trust verification processes. The best authentication devices promise to deliver a highly trustworthy process even if all the other components are compromised.

But essentially, if any of the hardware devices are compromised, it's "game over." You cannot trust the authentication processes if the devices inside have been maliciously modified. A vendor and designer can do their best to prevent and minimize the impact of a compromise in the chain of components.

## Software

Just as all the hardware must be trustworthy and uncompromised, so too must all the software. Software includes firmware code, boot code, operating system, and applications. The most-worthy software not only checks itself for unauthorized changes, but also has other higher-layer processes and/or trust-verified software check. It like the hardware, any malicious change to the software involved anywhere in the authentication process threatens the trustworthiness. The best authentication takes trust threats into account and tries to remain trustworthy as possible.
