My favorite study is the first one. It shows that even SMS-based MFA—which I, along with many others, am not a fan of—significantly reduces non-targeted attacks. The keyword is non-targeted. If there is a weakness to MFA, it targets attacks. MFA stops significantly complicates the most popular type of generic, widely broadcasted attacks, where an attacker has nearly zero information about their intended targets. The attackers just use a scattershot approach against a population of potential victims and to get lucky with a few. Cyberattacks are of this broadcast, scattershot, makeup. And MFA stops (but not all) of these types of attacks. For example, if you are an attacker trying to fish someone out of their Gmail password and the user uses MFA to log in to their Gmail account, that is not going to.

But attackers who spend time learning about specific victims and the related potential attack areas, with a little creativity, can find a way to exploit. The MFA scenario, if I know that my victim requires MFA, I have to learn how MFA works and the methods to get around it. Any MFA method can be hacked, but it takes research and effort, and any increase in effort reduces the number of potential attackers and their chances of success. In most scenarios, MFA is a good thing to have. But an attacker can easily modify their general, broadcast attack to include MFA scenarios, and by making that one change, bring MFA-using users back into play as potential victims.

## MFA Is Not Hackable

In the same vein, “harder to accomplish” hacking and phishing does not mean impossible. The danger of thinking MFA is unhackable or nearly unhackable can be extraordinarily dangerous, especially if you are a leader or a computer security practitioner. If you believe something is unhackable, you probably aren't educating everyone involved with the specific risks they need to be aware of and defend.

Anecdotally, most of the general attendees at my Hacking webinars were shocked to learn that a simple phishing email, like the one shown in Figure 6.4 in Chapter 6, “Access Token Tricks,” can bypass their favorite MFA solution. Many have been told by their IT people that using an MFA token means they can't be phished and that they don't have to be worried about phishing emails. They have been taught that a regular-looking phishing email can compromise your account even if you are using MFA. That's reality.

The wrong advice increases the likelihood of future compromise because the leaders and victims aren't worried and aren't looking out for the signs of a pending attack. It's like someone saying the Titanic was unsinkable. You can avoid icebergs if you are worried about them in the first place. This is the primary lesson of this book — MFA is not unhackable.

## Education Is Key

Of the critical recommendations, repeated again and again in this book, in nearly every chapter, is education. Educate your developers about potential attack scenarios so that they can implement mitigations to offset these risks. Educate your MFA users so that they are aware of the risks their MFA solution does and doesn't mitigate. Education and expectations are key to reducing security risk. Hackers are hoping users think they can't be hacked if they are using MFA. Ignorance is bliss and attackers have undetected users.

In computer security, the field of computer security education is known as security awareness training. I used the term the first time I saw it in a Gartner report. Now, after thinking about it, I love the term. It is exactly right. The single best thing you can do for anyone in computer security after implementing the best controls possible is to make people aware of the appropriate risks and scans.

For example, someone who doesn't know that Microsoft does proactively call people to warn them that their computer is compromised by malware are far more likely to think the call is legitimate. Craigslist using it for the first time often aren't prepared for all the scammers who are not willing to pay them full price, but for delivery and then, "This Craigslist is awesome! I didn't use it before!" If they don't know these scams, it makes them more likely to become a victim. And to prevent a potential victim from becoming a real victim it takes a little awareness about the possible scams — security awareness training.

## Security Isn't Everything

As covered in Chapter 4, "Usability as Security," security isn't everything. As in the real world, the 100 percent protection of the digital world isn't the primary objective of most users. Users want the amount of security that allows them to securely carry out their objectives most of the time. What the intersection of security and usability depends on the case scenario and needs and wants of the user. For example, in the real world I never want my car's brakes or accelerator to fail. Failure is unacceptable. But I might accept that my radio always works, and I probably sometimes drive with windshield wipers in a degraded state longer than I should. Sometimes I want absolute security or close to it. But most of the time I'm OK with security. I don't need 10-factor authentication to allow them to protect my Twitter posts.

This lesson is primarily intended for developers so that they will understand usability and friction are the most important factors in any MFA solution. Make a solution too hard to use and people will use something else. Delivering a very secure solution isn't the only factor buyers consider when selecting an MFA solution.

## Every MFA Solution Has Trade-Offs

Every MFA solution has strengths and weaknesses. Different types of solutions have different types of strengths and weaknesses. No solution is perfect. Every solution can be hacked in one or more ways. MFA solution buyers need to find out what those strengths are and then decide which ones they need and which they can live without.

## 3×3 Security Pillars

Using MFA as securely as possible goes beyond the MFA device itself. Every defense-in-depth solution is a combination of preventive, detective, and reactive controls as carried out by policy, technical components, and education. I call these controls the 3x3 security pillars, as graphically represented in Figure 25.1. The 3x3 security pillars apply to more than just MFA solutions, but they're useful to consider when deploying your MFA defenses.

When I think about computer security defenses, I have three control objectives in my head:

- Prevent
- Detect
- Recover

> **NOTE** The most popular security control framework, the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (www.nist.gov/cyberframework), has five tiers, but I consolidate them into three components.

I want to prevent bad things from happening to an environment I manage. If bad things get past my preventive controls, I want early warning and detection of those things in order to mitigate damage. And I have to recover from the attack and figure out how to prevent it next time. Everything in my planning, including MFA implementations, looks at computer security using these three pillars of security defenses.

I'll give you a real example. The Meltdown and Spectre vulnerabilities (meltdownattack.com) are likely the most threatening, high-risk, vulnerabilities to be announced. The world's most popular CPU chips released since 1990's (Intel x86, ARM, and IBM Power) had the flaws. Hundreds of millions of computers and devices were susceptible and the vulnerabilities were exploitable no matter OS you ran and no matter what you implemented.
