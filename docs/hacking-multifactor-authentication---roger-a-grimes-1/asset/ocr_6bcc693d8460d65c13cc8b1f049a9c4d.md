# Electron Microscope Attack

A typical microscope works using light, which is made of photon particles. When you want to measure very tiny things, microscope down to the nanometer scale and smaller, light doesn't work because the photons are bigger than the objects being measured. It would be like to use a yardstick to measure a pea.

For very small things, scientists and researchers use an electron microscope. Electron microscopes, not surprisingly, we use electrons. Electrons are up to 100,000 times smaller than photons and can be used to measure microscopic, subatomic objects of all types.

A new electron microscope (there are several types) can easily set back a research $3 million to $5 million, which you can buy in tens of thousands of dollars. Here's one marketplace for electron microscopes: www.lab.com/electron-microscope. Of course, the purchase cost is only part of the cost equation. Electron microscopes consume power to create electron beams and are usually housed in environmentally controlled labs, with teams of professional people using and maintaining them. Still, in the United States, many small communities and even high schools (www.hssemgroup.com/high-school-sems/url-s) have access to them. They aren't the rare commodities that they use. Nation-states have and use them.

An electron microscope can be used to search for and identify secrets at the molecular level. The first time this was publicly discussed in 2010 (gpn.com/Articles/2010/02/02/Black-Hat-chip-crack-02010.aspx) by Christopher Tarnovsky. After nine months, using a focused beam electron microscope, Tarnovsky was able to find and retrieve the authentication secrets stored on an Infine SLE 66PE, a microcontroller chip, which carried the secret of Trusted Platform Module (TPM).

TPM chips are designed to prevent the unauthorized retrieval of authentication secrets. Microscope is having a TPM chip to protect critical secrets for all of enterprise levels of Windows hardware. Tarnovsky revealed that anyone with access to an electron microscope could get at those secrets. Tarnovsky also found that the TPM chip had anti-tampering technologies built-in and that triggering any one of them would destroy the chip. He ended up destroying more four dozen chips in the process, but he learned how to get at the secrets without destroying the chip in the process. Tarnovsky estimates he has reverse-engineered a thousand chips in his career, and he continues to attack chip protections to his day. He often shares how what he does, including this 2019 talk: media.hardwear.io/exposed-the-deep-secure-elements-of-smartcards-christopher-tarnovsky.
