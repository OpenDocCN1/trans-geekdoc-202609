# Compromised MFA Keys

It is possible for an attacker to “zero out” (i.e., erase) the encryption keys of an MFA solution if they can get access to the involved encryption keys. But erasing the encryption keys might make the encryption functionality error out or be noticed by the user. Instead, a smart attacker would just view and record the encryption keys or supply their own known keys that the target would unknowingly rely on.

# Hacking Multifactor Authentication

I've only heard about this sort of MFA attack involving the intelligence agencies of nation-states. In one story I read a long time ago, when the United States was first looking for Osama Laden, they learned suspected terrorists were using cell phone subscriber identity module (SIM) cards, which allowed symmetric encryption keys to encrypt the messages and voice communications between all involved co-conspirators. The terrorists would secure the SIMs when needing communications and frequently buy and update their SIMs so they would be using SIM information and new encryption keys. It was a pretty good plan. The U.S. intelligence agencies could not break into the encryption, but they contacted the vendor of the SIM manufacturer and convinced them to use symmetric keys known to the intelligence agency on all SIM encryption cards. Everyone, terrorist or not, who bought the same brand of SIMs received encryption keys known by someone else. But indeed the terrorists got the compromised keys and the U.S. intelligence agency was able to listen in terrorist communications.

In a more public case, which came to be known as the Great SIM Heist, the U.S. and UK intelligence agencies broke into the world's most prolific supplier of SIM chips, Gemalto, and compromised over 2 billion SIM encryption keys (theintercept.com/2019/great-sim-heist). An April 2010 intelligence agency document details the automated collection of SIM encryption keys in fairly detail: theintercept.com/document/2015/02/19/pcs-harvesting-scale. There is no reason to believe that similar intelligence agency actions are not still occurring around the world.

# Endpoint Attack Defenses

Endpoint attack breaks down into two different categories: developer and end-user defenses.

# MFA Developer Defenses

MFA developers can do many things to limit or slow down physical and logical endpoint attacks.

### Threat Model and Protect All Dependencies

Few MFA solutions work only alone. Most rely on a host of other services out of their control, including host computers and operating systems, networks, supporting infrastructure, namespaces, the humans involved, and all the other entities, as summarized in Figure 5.1. An MFA developer should consider all relied-on services as untrusted and potentially compromised. The developer should decide which ones are critical to the security of their solution and create code that detect whether the underlying infrastructure is compromised to the best of their ability to detect an anomaly. MFA solutions should be designed to detect unauthorized hardware and/or firmware. All critical functions should be securely stored and run in secure memory areas. There should be secure boot checks and the runtime operational code should check itself for integrity. Only secure, authorized updates should be allowed.

The MFA device should stop operations reuse (i.e., “brick”) on any critical error. Any detected anomaly should be displayed descriptively to the end user if possible and the MFA solution should error out or refuse to operate. Most developers work with an attitude of trusting the other components to be uncompromising and reliable. A good MFA developer takes the opposite attitude, threat-models, plans, and codes accordingly.

### Tie Access Tokens to Registered Devices

If possible, tie access tokens to registered devices.

### Prevent or Notify the User of Instances

Your MFA solution should ask for additional authentication, perhaps something as simple as asking the user to recreate a PIN for high-risk transactions.
