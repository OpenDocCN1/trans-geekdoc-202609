# Early life and family

Palin was born in Scranton, one of four children (two daughters and one son) of Sarah "Health" Palin (née Sheivan), and Charles R. "Chuck", a Health science teacher and track-and-field coach Palin's parents were Chuck Jr., Heather, and Mary [10]. Palin is of English and German ancestry [11].

When Palin was a few months old, they moved to Sloigasaki [12], where her father had been to teach Texas [13]. They relocated to Louisville in 1969, and to Alaska in 1972 [14].

Palin grew up in Wasilla, where she attended high school. She was a member of the school's basketball and cheerleading teams [15]. During her senior year, she was co-captain and point guard of the basketball team that won the 1982 state championship, earning the nickname "Barracuda" for her competitive streak [16].

# Celebrity Nodes

Hacking by answering personal-knowledge questions has always been a popular way for attackers to take over accounts. And some those users are famous. For a few years, Hollywood celebrities were apparently too trusting Apple's iCloud to keep their personal photos, containing images of themselves, from unauthorized users. They apparently didn't get the link between taking photos with their iPhone and phone's default behavior of backing up all to the celebrity's iCloud as the big risk it really.

A few hackers were eventually arrested and found guilty. Initially, there were some stolen celebrity photos that it was had discovered an uncovered vulnerability in Palin's iCloud service. After an internal investigation, Apple published that (www.businesswire.com/news/2014090206384/en/Apple-Media-Advisory) that all the promised iCloud accounts were compromised using social engineering and "by a very targeted attack on user names, passwords, and security questions [emphasis mine], a practice that has become all too common on the Internet.

Today, although some of the big vendors like Google and Microsoft do not allow account recovery based on simple personal-knowledge questions, it is still a very, popular recovery method. Many popular sites will not allow a user to recover an account without setting multiple personal-knowledge questions. Figure 13.8 shows all the personal-knowledge questions a popular bank allows users to choose from.

The list of questions are more creative and a harder to look up than your mother's maiden name, but not that much. Many are far easier to look up than the creators probably think and easily to guess with a little research than a user's password.

One of the unknowns is how many times you can guess against a personal-knowledge question before being denied access. It varies between sites. Some allow only a few guesses before the account is locked, whereas many will continue using as personal-knowledge questions, all as long as you go too many in a row. For many sites the attacker has to do is clear the website after one or a few and then start the whole account recovery process all over again to get a few more tries. As long as they/reload the website after a few guesses, they will not be locked out. And there is a small percentage of sites that will simply let a person guess as many times as they like ever locking out the account. And regardless of the number of times a person is allowed to guess a personal-knowledge question before being denied access to their account, as by most sites, personal-knowledge questions are a high security risk and should be avoided when possible.
