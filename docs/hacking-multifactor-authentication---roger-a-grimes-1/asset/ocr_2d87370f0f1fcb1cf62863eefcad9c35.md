Those facts should be astounding to you. I think we've all been there, having trouble answering our own questions months or years later. "Was my teacher Mrs. McKee or Mr. Mutachi?" You try both and fails. Turns out, the failure rate of the legitimate user to successfully answer ours starts to go up quickly for questions that are not harder than mother's name or father's middle name.

In fact, if I look at the rate at which people successfully answered all their questions (60 percent) versus the attacker's rate at guessing some of the questions with the first try (20 percent), it appears that legitimate users are only three times more likely to answer their own questions. And I bet in more cases than we would admit to, hackers would be able to recover or guess the correct answers faster than the legitimate users.

# Sarah Palin Email Account Hack

Attackers have long putting accounts into recovery mode and then successfully answering personal-knowledge questions for decades. One of the most popular and well-known attacks (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Palin_email_hack) involved Sarah Palin, who was running as a U.S. vice presidential candidate on the Republican ticket after Barack Obama and Joe Biden in the 2008 presidential election.

The hacker, the son of a democratic state representative, answered three of Sarah Palin's personal-knowledge questions to take over her personal Yahoo! email account. The three personal-knowledge questions were:

+   - Palin's birthday
- Where she met her husband
- Current zip code

According to this article (nypost.com/2008/09/19/dem-polls-son-was-hacker/), the 20-year-old hacker was able to find her birthday and where she met her husband, Todd Palin, on Wikipedia. knew from a little research that Sarah had married her high school sweetheart and figured the most likely place they had met was in high school. There only one high school in Wasilla, Alaska, but it is also located in Sarah Palin's Wikipedia entry (as shown in Figure 13.7). The hacker found out that Wasilla had only two possible zip codes, which he looked up on the U.S. Postal Service's website. It's a misnomer to call him a hacker; the FBI, arrested, tried, and sent to a year plus one day in jail, three years of probation.
