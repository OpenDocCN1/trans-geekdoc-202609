To say that it astonished the world is an understatement. So far, later not one of the world's major computer security compliance bodies (PCI-DSS, HIPAA, NERC, CIS, etc.) is recommending the new advice. All are still following the old advice. Microsoft, a pusher of the old password policy recommendations, stopped recommending any particular password, which was essentially a passive nod to the new NIST recommendations.

For a year or two after NIST's recommendation, I strongly advocated for the new password policy position. But after debating it with other security friends and practitioners and looking at subsequent password incidents, I cannot agree with all of NIST recommends. In particular, the part about not changing your passwords you think are seems dubious to me. You can't be assured of knowing when your password was compromised. Most people and companies aren't aware that their password (or passwords) have been compromised. The fact that we have billions of accounts out there on the Internet, is a strong rebuttal to NIST recommending, especially the “no to change your password” part. Additionally, if users use a password manager to create and use separate passwords on all different sites, the password reuse would diminish.

# My Password Policy Advice

My new “official” password advice is this:

+   - Use multifactor authentication (MFA) when possible and when obviously needed for security (i.e., you don't need it for every site and logon).
- Where MFA is not an option, use password managers where you can, creating unique, long-possible, random passwords for each website or security domain.
- Where password managers aren't possible, use simple phrases.
- Change all passwords at least once a year, and change business passwords every 90 to 180 days.
- In all cases, don't use common passwords (e.g., “password” or “qwerty”) and never reuse any password between different sites.

If you don't want to use long passwords, it's okay to use simple, nonconvex passwords. Just don't reuse them between sites.

# Password Managers

The average person has six to seven passwords that they reuse between all sites and services they use. This is just asking for trouble. Out of everything I've listed, the most important recommendation is not to reuse the same password (or password pattern) between multiple unrelated sites or services.
