## Unhackable Quantum Cryptography

My previous book, Cryptography Apocalypse (Wiley, 2019), is on quantum computers and how quantum computers will one day break traditional public key cryptography. It also covers how quantum computers and devices can be used to make hard-to-break quantum cryptography. According to quantum physicists, the very laws of physics prove that quantum cryptography can't be broken—that break quantum cryptography you have to break the laws of physics, and you are not doing. So, you will hear all these theoretical physicists state quantum cryptography is unbreakable. Of course, they are wrong.

Although quantum cryptography may be theoretically unhackable, practical application is far different. One, it really isn't unhackable in the first place. What they mean by "unhackable" is quantum information cannot be eavesdropped on without the sender and receiver knowing. That's not the same as "can't be eavesdropped on." It means that if it gets eavesdropped on, the legitimate parties will know it. If critical information was eavesdropped on, it will still be stolen—it's just that the legitimate parties know it happened.

Second, and more important, referring to the previous section, "Unhackable Fallacy," humans have never made anything that is unhackable. We've tried. We've thought we could. And in every case, we've been wrong. To be human is to be fallible. We make mistakes. And those lead to hacks.

Even if we were somehow able to make unhackable quantum cryptography, we would mess up implementing it in the real world. It's what happens. We take something that is perfect in theory and run it in practice. It's the way of the world. And I don't see quantum properties and quantum physics, which are inherently as complex as humans have messed with, suddenly being the thing we got right. Odds, are that quantum cryptography will be hacked just like everything else in the world we created.

## Security Theater

There are also instances of security that are more theater and spectacle than true security (known as security theater by critics). The world is full of security theater. One of the best examples is signing checks or credit/ATM receipts for purchases in the United States. It has nearly zero security value in stopping fraud from being committed. With a check, you might possibly have a sales clerk compare the signature with your ID, but probably not. And if that clerk notes a difference, probably won't say anything to you and certainly won't stop your purchase. There is a chance a fraudulent signature can help your case in the event that fraud is noticed on the back later time, but it rarely stops the fraud from being committed in the first place.

For decades, I have signed all checks and credit cards with a known bad. I have signed thousands of my credit card receipts and checks with “Yogi Bear” and “ThisIsNotMyRealSignature” and I have never been stopped from purchasing something. I had a few people laugh, but none have stopped me from my purchase. I think, originally, most of us thought it important to make sure our signature was our legal, valid signature. We must have thought that there was a chance that someone was sitting in a background comparing signatures and that person would notice the discrepancy and stop the transaction. It has never happened. We are legally required to provide that by law, supposedly for security purpose. But that review does not happen. Most of the time, we are wasting our time. It is not sure why we are a society even tolerates having signing signatures that do almost nothing. If signing took 20 seconds on average, I the requirement to use signatures would be changed.

## Security by Obscurity

Most computer security practitioners are taught early that "by obscurity is no security." The idea is that attackers might be able to discover any fact surrounding your security system, so you can assume they have all the necessary facts and you should design your security system to be secure even if they have perfect knowledge of it (for everything the ultimate secrets). This is believed and repeated so much that it borders on religion. However, it isn't any other than the old password policy beliefs.

The truth is that obscurity is a great defense and one of the best ways to get the biggest bang for your security dollar. It shouldn't be the only or primary way your system is secured. You should still design a system as if the axiom is true. It can help you. But there's a fundamental difference between not relying solely on security by obscurity and refusing to benefit from it all. If you look at the security scenarios that use, you should absolutely include obscurity as part of your overall security defense. Any variable that a hacker has to guess or look for slows them down and makes their job harder. If obscurity is no security, why don't the world's armies tell each where their nuclear subs are traveling and where all the nuclear missile silos are located? I can tell you why. Obscurity has good value.
