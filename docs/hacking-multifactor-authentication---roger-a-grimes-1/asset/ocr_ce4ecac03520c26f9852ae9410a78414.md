# Hacking Multifactor Authentication

Another method is to steal the access control token during its use or transmission over a network medium. In most of instances, the attacker must inject themselves in the communication's stream between client and server, in what is known as a man-in-the-middle (MitM) attack. The MitM attacker can eavesdrop and/or manipulate the access control token. Network theft of access control tokens has been done for decades and is among the most common ways to hack any authentication solution, including multifactor authentication solutions.

# Reproducing Token Hack Examples

As many website penetration testers can attest, at any minute on the Internet there are probably thousands, if not of thousands, of websites that could easily guessable session IDs. You can find them all over the place, when I found Foundation in the 2000s, Moststone hacking instructors used the same website to demonstrate access token attacks. It was even written into some of our instructional guides. We found a real website that contained multiple token vulnerabilities. It was a perfect "demo" site because its control token session IDs were secured. That fact made vulnerabilities easy to see and exploit. We took much advantage of the exploit, and told our students not to attack it. But showing how easy it is to hack a real-world website gave our classes some increased credit.

We used the same website so much that I think most instructors must come to believe it was a fake site built to help us demonstrate the exploit. (I know initially thought it was a fake site.) But one day of my students told me that they had successfully purchased expensive pieces of equipment for free using the exploit we had taught them. Then, I too thought it was a fake site and that he was "exploiting it", but then the items arrived on his doorstep charged to someone else's credit card. Needless to say, we had to correct the "mistake" and the instructors stopped using the website as a demo.

But know that thousands of web penetration testers, including old, inactive pen testers (like me), are looking for repeatable patterns when they are visiting any website. It is not anything you think about—it becomes part of your DNA. If you find yourself at URLs (where the session IDs are displayed) and in cookies to see if you can find guessable, repeatable patterns. Once you understand that the vulnerability is how prevalent it is, it makes a lifetime game of virtual bug hunting.
