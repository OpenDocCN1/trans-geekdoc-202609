## Frequency Hopping

One common defense for all wireless communications is frequency hopping. All wireless communications using modulation waves of energy. These waves modulate, up and down in a sine wave, at a particular frequency across what is called the electromagnetic wave spectrum. The number of waves per second. Radio and wireless signals function at 30 Hz to 300 GHz (billions of Hz). One Hz is one complete wave modulation per second.

Attackers have length to transmit erroneous signals and noise at the same frequency as the legitimate sender. Doing so "jams" the signal the sender and receiver often cannot distinguish between the legitimate signal and the illegitimate noise. In World War II, Hollywood actress Hedy Lamarr (and her composer, George Antheil) created and patented the frequency-hopping spectrum wireless technology to fight frequency jamming and eavesdropping, although it was not widely adopted until many decades later.

Frequency hopping works as a defense because the legitimate signal is sent over frequencies (very quickly) that only the sender and receiver have agreed on (or computed) ahead of time. Anyone wishing to disrupt the signal would need a wide set of the frequencies they were hoping to when, or would have to jam the spectrum for a long time. Even then it would be fairly easy for the sender and receiver to figure out what is a legitimate signal and what is noise.

Frequency hopping prevents eavesdropping and jamming and most of today's wireless technologies possible. Not all wireless technologies use it. For example, NFC, Bluetooth, and RFID do not, because it is believed their limited range is enough of a defense. But more wider range technologies, like cell phones and Wi-Fi, do.

## Phone-Based

A very popular and growing authentication method is to use cell phones either as part of the authentication solution or by letting it receive the authentication check.

## Voice Authentication

Phones can be used for authentication in their base mode of a phone—taking a voice call. The caller or receiver involved in the authentication transaction can verify the other's identity and account information before performing a critical action. A common example is from a bank or credit card company to verify a particular suspicious transaction. Voice phone authentication can be performed by a human or automated system. An example of the latter could be a credit card using an automated system to call the user's predefined cell phone number and asking the user to “Say or press 1 to confirm the transaction. Say or press 2 to decline the transaction and report it as fraudulent.

Your voice can be recognized by voice-recognition systems to verify your identity (Something You Are). Financial companies will often recognize a caller's phone number and automatically pull up the correct account, and then ask the user to speak to authenticate their identity. Voice-recognition authentications are not super accurate and usually not trusted for the most critical, highest-risk transactions. A lot of voice recognition accuracy depends on familiarity and closeness of the relationship. Your family, friends, and coworkers may hear your voice and immediately trust that it is you. Your bank may require that you provide additional account information (Something You Know authentication step up) before moving money.
