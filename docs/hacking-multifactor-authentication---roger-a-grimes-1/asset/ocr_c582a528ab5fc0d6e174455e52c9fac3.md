An attacker can simply ask for someone's password and you would be surprised how often you can get it from a complete stranger. Years ago when I was doing full-time penetration testing, I used to go to the CEO's executive assistants and say, "Hi, my name is Roger Rimes. I've been hired to test the password security of the company, including the CEO. What is the CEO's password?" Now, remember, they aren even supposed to have the CEO's passwords—passwords are not to be shared. But want to know how many executive assistants gave me their CEO's passwords? All of them! In my 20 years of asking, not one assistant refused to give it to me. I had some haste a few seconds before giving it to me, but no one didn't conceal and reveal the top executive's.

I've participated in over the studies and tests where we would offer people in a public place (e.g., street, subway system) small prizes like candy bars or pens if they would reveal their passwords to us. Our success rate was 30 to 60 percent, depending on the country and test. Don't believe me? There are videos all over the Internet showing the same thing. It's not like I have some social engineering charm. You just have to ask.

Late-night show Jim Kimmel occasionally reveals people walking down the street in Hollywood, California, and asks for their computer passwords. Here's an example: www.youtube.com/watch?v=RJJefGK5SJA. It's hard to watch without both laughing and laughing at the same time how easy it is to get people to reveal their real passwords.

So, in summary, passwords are easy to use and hack. Using a variety of methods, including guessing, cracking, stealing, eavesdropping and asking, many billions of passwords have been stolen. The problem is not getting better after two decades—it's only getting worse. We didn't use to have billions of stolen passwords sitting on the Internet. It started to make the news when a hundred thousand of them ended up in a single password dump. Now I'm not even a billion stolen passwords will raise an eyebrow—hence, Multifactor authentication is often touted as the panacea.

# Password Hacking Defenses

I don't want to leave readers with the impression that password authentication can be a safe authentication. It can be, risks and all. And since passwords aren't going away soon, here are the ways to make password authentication safer:

+   - Don't get phished into giving out your password to a malicious entity.
- Don't get phished into running a trojan horse program.
- Make sure your system are fully patched so the attacker can't use unpatched software into your computer and steal your passwords.
- Use a unique password for website or service.
