# 98 Hacking Multifactor Authentication

During military operations, many participants will communicate with each other using radio (or other types of wireless waves). They communicate to coordinate actions, update each other on accomplishments, discuss new plans, call in airstrikes, and so forth. Today, the radio equipment they use has authentication and encryption built-in so that everything communicated is automatically protected. But back in the days of World War II, the Korean War, and the Vietnam War, did not. During those times, the enemy would intentionally seek out the main communications specialist, kill or kidnap them, and then use their radio to listen to current communications or call in fraud, harmful, battle choices. There are many stories of adversaries facing strong American accents, such as a Texas drawl, to mimic the accent of the communications soldier they captured and call in fake strikes against the wrong locations.

To fight this before the days of cryptographic radios, the soldiers would each call names to identify themselves, as well as codes (i.e., knowledge-based authentication) to confirm their participation or call to battlefield actions. These verbal codes helped the keep communication lines free of adversarial fake instructions.

# Paper-Based

Before the days of computers and pervasive digital cryptography, most cryptography was done by hand. The encryption and decryption codes were written down and copied between participating subjects. This method persisted for over a thousand years. History is replete with failed murderous acts and royal assassinations because the written-secret code and related messages were discovered and deciphered. Starting around World War II, paper-based codes and cryptography diminished machines, and later on, computers were the preferred way to do cryptography.

Still, paper-based cryptography persists. Paper-based codes. There are even digital authentication and encryption solutions that rely on printed, paper times the alternative backup option for the digital option in case the digital option gets lost or damaged. For example, many password managers generate printed codes that users may need in order to recover their implementation of the password manager program if it gets lost or corrupted. Those printed codes risk losing all your stored passwords at once. It can be a real pain. It's also a pain because you have to securely store that piece of paper containing the code. The theory is that you should store it in a fireproof safe, a bank deposit box, or something like that, and it should be stored in two separate physical locations that cannot be compromised by a single disaster event (fire, flood, hurricane, tornado, earthquake, etc.). Many people keep a single copy in their house in a paper file or desk drawer. For solutions that use paper-based components, the thinking is that since the MFA solution uses a paper component it is far harder to hack. Unfortunately, it also makes the solution harder to use.
