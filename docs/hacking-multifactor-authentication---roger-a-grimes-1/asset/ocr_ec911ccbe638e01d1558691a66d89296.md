# Chapter 15 Buggy Software 317

I've implemented attacks (e.g., Smurf attack) where a single malformed network packet locked an entire server or device. I have twice locked up smart devices by sending single malformed packets.

Many security vulnerabilities result in a device or service becoming "locked" All that needs to happen for the impacted device or software to be restarted or rebooted, and sometimes the lockout is more permanent. Attacks against firmware can result in "hard lockups," which made the impacted device unusable until the firmware gets rewritten back to a good state.

# Examples of Vulnerability Attacks

When I first started in computer security three decades ago, I thought that in computer security you just magically know how to stop all potential security issues. I felt I did, at least in my area of expertise. But I soon found that most programmers, even programmers of security software, had very little, innate understanding of computer security issues, including how to avoid software vulnerabilities. This isn't surprising, yet few programming schools and universities devote much time to teaching programming. Currently, I know of two universities that offer a complete course in how to program securely. So, it should be no surprise that most of our programmers, even security programmers, aren't so strong in avoiding vulnerabilities in their code.

I remember being shocked when I worked for Foundstone when they were purchased by McAfee in 2004. One of the first tasks McAfee asked us to do was a vulnerability analysis of all their software. McAfee was one of the largest sellers of computer software in the world. We found hundreds of vulnerabilities, often in a single program. At the time, I couldn't believe how many errors we found. I worried the news would leak out. Over the years, I've learned that McAfee's buggy software is not an outlier. It's normal. The reality is unusual to find computer security without a lot of bugs. Still, to this day, most security programmers do not get training in how to avoid common vulnerabilities. This includes the programs of MFA solution software.

Let's look at examples of vulnerabilities found in MFA solutions.

# Uber MFA Vulnerability

The popular ridesharing vendor, Uber, offered MFA since 2015. A white-hat hacker noted a two-factor bypass in 2018 and reported it to the trusted Hacker (www.hackerone.com) bug bounty program. Uber responded by saying that his finding was not substantial and that at best all we needed was a documentation update. Frustrated, the hacker contacted the media and after being contacted by a popular tech site (ZDNet), Uber finally fixed the bug and paid the hacker for the report. For over two months after it was reported, Uber did not fix the bug. For more details, see www.zdnet.com/article/uber-security-flaw-two-factor-login-bypass.
