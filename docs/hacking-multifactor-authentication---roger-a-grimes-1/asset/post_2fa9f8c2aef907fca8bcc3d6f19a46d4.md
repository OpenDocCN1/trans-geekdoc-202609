## Usually Involves Well-Known Brands

Most social engineering and phishing scams involve the names of well-known companies when the victim is familiar. This is because well-known companies are often known to have many proactive marketing campaigns and, as would have a valid reason for reaching out to a person "out of the blue." Well-known brands are also well-thought of by the potential victim so the scam gets the goodwill for the company being used. Scammers pretending to be from below, well-known companies are more likely to be successful than if using company no one has heard.

> NOTE: Well-known companies are not always beloved. For example, scammers pretending to be the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) or law enforcement. The widespread fear of such organizations can play a major role in getting the victim to drop their normal skepticism.

Scammers will often use look-alike or sound-alike email addresses and websites of well-known brands and include content taken from or linked to the defrauded organization. Many times, the only truly malicious content beyond the fraudulent text is the one URL the scammer is hoping the victim will click on. ## Based on Notable Current Events and Interests

Phishing increases and focuses during popular notable events, such as natural disasters, celebrity deaths, and big events. Phishers know that people are interested in notable events and will prey on their interest in the topic. As I write this, the global coronavirus COVID-19 pandemic is accelerating—and predictably, the amount of phishing using COVID-19 is increasing. Here are a couple of blog news stories: blog.knowbe4.com/hackers-use-interactive-malicious-covid-19-map-to-spread-malware and blog.knowbe4.com/extreme-measures-the-epidemic-of-covid-19-phishing-emails-org. Phishers often post contrarian, counter-factual news stories in forums dedicated to specific topics, knowing that a certain percentage of the readers will be emotionally incentivized to click on the embedded phishing link.

## Uses Stressors

Phishers like to use stressors in their initial contacts. Stressors are descriptive stories that are intended to push the receiver's thoughts into a "fight or flight" mode. Stressors include things such as someone pretending to be the victim's boss saying that such and things need to be done ASAP or some big business deal will fail, or someone's grandchild has been arrested and bail needs to be paid to the police, or the IRS has found a tax discrepancy and will be arresting them unless they pay money immediately. Stressor events demand immediate action. They don't want the victim time to research the more to determine the validity. In the first case, they prey on people's personal shame and embarrassment, such as when phish claim they recorded the victim privately masturbating using their webcam.

## Advanced: Pretexting

Pretexting is used in advanced phishing attacks. Pretexting is when the phisher initiates multiple contacts with the victim, with the first communication being something that is not asking the victim for anything suspicious. The phisher is “laying the groundwork” and establishing an ongoing relationship so that when they have a requested action in the future, the victim already feels comfortable with the phisher.

An example pretexting call might be a hacker calling a company's human resources number and talking to the accounts payable clerk and acting as they are the new primary contact for an existing company that regularly invoices. In the first case, the caller just says hello and communicates that they are the new contact, gives them new contact information, and tells the accounts payable clerk that all questions and requests related to the should now go to them. They may even indicate that the change is happening as part of a “big update” and that they will be changing banks to a new bank that has more favorable terms, or something like that. The phisher waits a few days or weeks and asks the accounts payable clerk to update the payment information supposedly due to a new banking change. When the regular invoice from the real company comes into the accounts receivable, the clerk not knowing anything different, sends the regularly scheduled payment to the new bank and phisher. This are often not discovered for months.
