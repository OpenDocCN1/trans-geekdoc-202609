One of the side-channel attack possibilities arises when a particular encryption algorithm takes a different amount of CPU time when working with 1 versus Os. On an attacker with the right sensitive equipment could possibly determine an encryption key by looking at the jumps in CPU processing time as it reads and processes the different bits of a particular key. Today, for an encryption algorithm to be successfully selected as a “standard” it must undergo side-channel analysis and be shown to be easy to resist, known, side-channel attacks. Otherwise like encryption algorithms are dismissed because they are overly susceptible to side-channel attacks. Other times, modifications are made to the algorithm to fix the potential side-channel issues.

NOTE I have heard many times from people I concern the ability for the U.S. government to use remote listening to detect secret keys stored on hardware modules (HSMs) located within a building from that building. I have talked to two people who witnessed a test like this working at a company I was involved in. In their recounting, a vendor told a secure executive at a Fortune 10 that they could read their private keys stored on an HSM in the middle of a building inside a locked computer room from a car in the parking lot. The purported beat was if the vendor could successfully perform the eavesdropping test, then the company would buy the vendor's protection product. The vendor proved they could remotely eavesdrop from outside the building and sold their product. I am not sure if the bit and test were real, but I do know the rest of us were told to open up our existing HSMs and insert new shielding products from that vendor, and then on every new HSM had to come with the vendor's shielding product already installed.

Side-channel attacks have been well understood and defended for decades. Many governments name the technologies that both attack and defend against side-channel attacks TEMPEST (Telecommunications Electronics Materials Protected from Emanating Spurious Transmissions). You’ll find a good summary of TEMPEST attacks and defenses for: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempest_(codename). Defense against side-channel attacks include design, shielding, jamming, filtering, distance, and isolation.

# Physical Threat/Destruction

Of course, an attacker can just physically destroy or take your MFA solution—which is an availability issue. You’ve got a time-based-one-password (TOTP) device? A thief can just steal it. Many people keep their MFA device stored in their satchel or backpack along with the device that is used with. They may place their TOTP device on their keyring or put their smartcard in their wallet or purse that is stolen, so too, is their MFA solution. Or as covered in the last chapter, most biometric traits are easy to steal.
