As systems begin to scale and technical support people no longer personally knew most of the users they were helping, hackers began taking advantage of human-based account recovery by using social engineering of the technical support people (as discussed in the previous chapter). It's also very expensive to staff a help desk to respond to all the calls related to forgotten passwords. In most organizations, login issues and forgotten passwords are among the most popular calls.

In response, authentication providers started to create processes and automated services, which often involved questions that (supposedly) only the legitimate user should easily know and be able to answer, as a way to verify a user's identity before unlocking their account and handing over the new password.

Someone—I don't know who was first—came up with the "brilliant" idea of asking users to answer a series of pre-questions, and those questions could be used with the systems to verify the person's identity when they needed their password reset. "What is your mother's maiden name? became the first widely used personal-knowledge question. How anyone ever thought that question answer could not be easily looked up by nearly is a mystery to me. A woman's maiden name has been widely available in public records for decades, long before the Internet made it child's play to look.

At first, personal-knowledge questions were few and hard-coded. A user didn't have a choice as to ones which would be involved. In many systems the number of hard-coded questions increased, but you still could not make up your own questions. In a minority of systems, users were/allowed to create their own custom questions. Some personal-knowledge question systems ask only one question and successfully answering that question is enough for authentication. On other systems, the user must successfully answer two or more. In instances, if the user wrongly answered a question, they would get opportunities with other questions. In more secure systems, a single wrong answer would unlock the account another way.

The huge problem from the beginning is that the questions and answers are public knowledge or can be learned by socially engineering the user. Even the ones that aren't common knowledge or that are a bit harder to socially engineer someone out of (such as "Model of your first car?" or "Your favorite elementary school?") are far easier to guess than guessing a password. A typical password (e.g., 6–8 characters long and multiple character types) can have billions of combinations. A password guesser has to come across an extremely poor password choice (such as password or qwerty) to actually guess someone's password.

But the number of models of cars that someone possibly have to guess is maybe a few thousand. I counted the current number of car models sold in the United States today (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_automobiles_manufactured_in_the_United_States). It was just over 100. If an attacker was faced with the model of car that someone used or liked as their first car, then they would have to guess 100 or more models that were available around that person was of legal driving age.
