In the early days of telephones, human operators handled all the background setup work. Eventually, everything the human operator could replaced by control signals sent with the voice portion of a phone call. It was not unusual for a caller or receiver to hear the control instructions (possibly modulating tones).

SS7 gained popularity because it allowed the control portion to be "out-of-band" from the data or voice portion. They can even take different paths from the originating caller to the destination as long as they end up in the same location. Earlier versions had both the control and voice components in the same channel together (called in-band), which made phone hacking (known as pheaking) easy. Pheakers made whistles that mimic important tones to the phone system. You can read about one of the early and most interesting hackers, John Draper (as Captain Crunch), here: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Draper.

NOTE If you are wondering, there were previous versions of SS7 as Signaling System No. 6 (SS6), Signaling System No. 5 (SS5), and so. SS7 partially came about because hackers were too good at hacking previous versions. First implemented in 1975, SS7 is regularly attacked and abused today. If you want to learn the details and ins of SS7, protocols, and formats for today’s digital phone networks, you can read this document: www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_ts/151000/151099/151001v120500_60/ts_151001vl120500p.pdf. But be aware it is over 6 pages long.

Since SS7 was invented, hackers have centered on finding vulnerabilities in it and abusing it. Many hacks directly attacked cell phones and phone networks can happen only because of cell phones’ and phone networks’ reliance on SS7. For example, when you read about “stingray” devices that fraudulently act as a cell tower so that the user can eavesdrop on cell phones and locations, it is due to vulnerabilities in SS7. SS7 white-hat researchers and attackers have intercepted people’s phone calls and read their SMS messages for decades. They have performed nearly identical MFA-bypass attacks, as well as direct, by using methods SS7 directly (thehackerners.com/2017/05/ss7-vulnerability-bank-hacking.html). SS7 vulnerabilities are so well and known to abuse that in 2016 a U.S. congressman called for an official investigation (www.guardian.com/technology/2016/apr/19/ss7-hack-congressman-calls-texts-snooping), nothing coming out.

Most-states and governments do not want to fix SS7 vulnerabilities because they use them when they want to spy on their targets. They like that their populace is not paying for much attention and doesn’t seem to mind that their communications are regularly intercepted and redirected at will. Regardless, the inherent issues in SS7 underlie many of the hacks of the upper-layer applications, like SMS, which relies on SS7 for the basic connection handling between phones. Of course, SMS has its own problems that go beyond and what SS7 has, but it’s a problem that any MFA relying on them is weakened. So knowing that, we can fully trust.
