# Standards

The Internet has made it easy that any authentication credentials we between systems use generally accepted protocol standards. A protocol is a predefined way of doing and communicating information between multiple, unrelated entities. Most of the time these standards need to be royalty free, not patented or copyrighted—that is, open. The digital world is full of open authentication standards (e.g., Security Assertion Markup Language [SAML], Open Authorization [OAuth], FIDO, and programming interfaces [APIs].

Open authentication standards come and do fail. Like the ones mentioned here, are sticking around. And that's a good thing. Without open standards we would be forced to work in a Tower of Babel mixing competing authentication standards and protocols. It would make a big mess of what is already a complex task. It might make it impossible. When, you hear of another authentication standard being adopted, celebrate. Your life just got much easier.

# Laws of Identity

All of us in the computer security world stand on the backs of previous giants. A book on authentication would be without mentioning Kim Cameron's Laws of Identity [docs.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/dotnet/articles/ms99646(v-msdn.10)]. Cameron was an early brainiac in the field of meta-data systems. He thought of defining and the use of identity in huge global systems better than most. And he shared ideas, many of which have taken over the world. He is probably best known, if known at all, for his 2005 Seven Laws of Identity. I share his one-sentence descriptions of each law here so you'll know what those universal laws are or should be: Cameron's Laws of Identity:

+   - Technical identity systems must only reveal information identifying a user with the user's consent.
- The solution that discloses the least amount of identifying information and limits its use is the most stable long-term solution.
- Digital identity systems must be designed so the disclosing of information is limited to parties having a necessary and justifiable place in a given identity relationship.
- A universal identity system must support both "omni-directional" identifiers for use by public entities and "unidirectional" for use by private entities, thus facilitating discovery while preventing unnecessary release of correlation handlers.
