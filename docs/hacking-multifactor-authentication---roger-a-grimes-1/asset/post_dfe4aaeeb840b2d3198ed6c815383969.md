You may be wondering, “What should we do for automated password recovery then?” Although relying on the standard types of simple personal-knowledge questions is a poor security practice, advanced recovery systems (covered in a moment) are still based on personal-knowledge questions implemented in a way that does not allow easy social engineering or guessing.

No matter how it was stolen, an attacker has successfully taken over the victim's account, they usually de-prioritize the MFA solution, "downgrading" the victim's account to use something less secure. They always change the password, often change the phone number (to phone-based account recovery processes), and change other information as well. They then utilize the stolen account to steal money or use it to pose as the victim to other potential victims. It can take the real victim hours or days to recover the account. I've read of people who never regained use of their stolen account and some permanently lost the content they had saving in the account for decades.

## Defenses to Downgrade/Recovery Attacks

Here are some developer and user defenses for downgrade/recovery attacks.

## Developer Defenses to Downgrade/Recovery Attacks

First we'll look at developer defenses for downgrade/recovery attacks.

### Educate Users

Users need to know that master codes must be stored securely and not stored along with the identity of the accounts they are related to. Users need to understand that personal-knowledge questions and their answers can be socially engineered and guessed, so they should not choose questions with answers that are super easy to research or guess.

### Threat Model Recovery Processes and Tools

Threat model recovery processes and tools for legitimate and illegitimate recovery attempts. Design your processes and tools and educate technical support to handle possible cybersecurity risk. Educate tech staff in how to detect and handle possible social engineering. Model ahead of time how to distinguish between legitimate and unauthorized users who are in a real-life struggle to obtain and maintain control over the account. You need to be able to return control of the account to the legitimate owner and not make them too hard in regaining control after a malicious takeover. You need to decide ahead of time what information posing as a legitimate user can prove that legitimate ownership of the account. Make it too easy, and an unauthorized user can more easily fake being the legitimate owner. Make it too hard, and the legitimate owner has trouble regaining control of their account.

### Expire and Anonymize Master Codes

Any master codes should have reasonable expiration dates, and the associated account identity should never be fully printed and attached to the list of codes. Instead, the full identity (i.e., roageragrimes3@gmail.com), if printed or saved next to the codes, should be instead partially represented (e.g., ro*s3@gmail.com). You don't want the compromise of the master codes to be immediately available to use for login by an attacker.

### Require an Acquaintance to Approve Recovery

Personal approval by a prereistered acquaintance, boss, coworker, or friend is one way to significantly cut down on rogue recoveries (although even acquaintances can be socially engineered). Early on, support technicians manually verified account recovery by personally recognizing the person's voice (or in-person request). On large systems, not each tech support person can recognize every legitimate user. Instead, an authentication recovery system can ask any user to designate two or more acquaintances who can be used to verify the requester's account recovery. Any approval must come from at least one of the designated verifiers, who are instructed to confirm, by voice or in person, the legitimate party needs the account recovery. This type of control was used in Microsoft when I was there and worked well, although there were times when the designated acquaintances were unreachable and the recovery operation was delayed until they responded. Implementing a defense like this is easy to do in an online, automated way, but it has been done, as in the case of Facebook's Trusted Contacts feature www.facebook.com/help/1198975144086.
