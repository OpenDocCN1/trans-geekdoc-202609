Much of the Internet works on PKI. For example time you connect to a website using HTTPS, that website has Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secured/Transport Layer Security (HTTPS/TLS) digital certificate and signed by a trusted PKI. You may not personally trust that PKI, but your operating system or software does. When you connect to the website with your browser using the HTTPS protocol, the website sends you (or your browser) a copy of its digital certificate. The digital certificate, signed by a PKI, attests to the website's name (often by URL), the website's public key, and other important information.

If your browser or operating system did not immediately know of and trust a particular PKI, most of the time the involved application would present the PKI owns name and/or digital certificate, and ask if you want to trust it (before trusting anything). You could then select yes or no. If you select, the new PKI is added to a list of stored trusted PKIs that you or your software trusts, so you won't be prompted again next time.

Once verified, your browser will generate a brand-new shared session symmetric key, which then securely sends to the website (using the website's public key).
