# Instagram MFA Brute-Force

Instagram was acquired by Facebook in 2012 and is arguably one of the most popular Internet services in the world. In July 2019, a white-hat hacker announced that he successfully brute-forced Instagram's MFA six-digit OTP solution (threatpost.com/researcher-bypasses-instagram-2fa/146466/).

The hacker started off trying to guess Instagram's OTP solutions from a single location. By testing this, he quickly learned that Instagram allowed a maximum of 250 gues per originating IP address. With potentially a million guesses to try, that would mean the hacker would need 4,000 IP addresses (although it says 5000 in the article). He found out he could double the guesses per IP by using different IP addresses for each sequential session and learned how many guesses he did and within a time period. He found out that Instagram was not permanently blacklisting IP addresses that guessed too many times, blocking them temporarily for a given set period time. After the time period passed, they were welcome to try again. He then calculated using 4,000 different IP addresses using a commercial service worth about $150. The hacker eventually won over 200,000 guesses without being blocked. For his trouble and publicly reporting it to the vendor so that it could be fixed, the hacker was awarded $30,000 from Facebook's bounty program.

So, to be clear, as recently as 2019, one of the largest and most sophisticated and well-funded websites in the world has a brute-force MFA vulnerability. Brute-force bugs don't happen to naive websites and services to clueless people.

> NOTE You may wonder why Instagram allowed so many guesses, 250, at a time before blocking a particular IP address. Possibly, it's also possible that Instagram has seen many legitimate logins or processes that guessed a lot, and then determined from their own experience that allowing 249 guesses was a good decision for them. More on this decision in the "Defenses Against Brute-Force Attacks" section later in this chapter.
