The crossover error rate (CER), where false acceptance errors and false rejection errors equal each other, gives you a bit of insight into how a particular biometric solution is configured as far as sensitivity is concerned. When comparing biometric systems, look at all the error rates, but for quick comparisons you use the CER alone. It applies to-or-anges comparisons about where the sensitivity settings begin to equal out error. Beware that many biometric vendors, like manufacturers, trying to drive overly optimistic ratings that aren't nearly as small when tested in the average environment.

Before you buy sure, so to speak, there are biometric systems with a low number of errors. Most are more expensive, slower and only able to be used in a limited number of high-security scenarios where the cost is not worth it. The majority of biometric systems are cheap and high in errors, and it's not known to the customers and users who evaluate biometric systems. For example, when fingerprint and facial recognition scanners for cell phones have very high rates of false acceptance.

Most customers would not put up with very accurate, low-error biometric solutions because of the slower processing time. When using fingerprint or iris readers, users have to stop and let the reader spend 3 to 15 seconds to complete a decision. This might not sound like a lot of time, but it is too much for many scenarios. If you had to wait 10 seconds to enter your building or unlock your phone, as described in Chapter 4, most people are not okay.

### Privacy Issues

Privacy issues surrounding biometrics are a big deal. People are worried about the increasing number of databases that have their biometric traits stored and used. A large percentage of the population would probably not agree to having their pictures scraped from social media accounts and used by biometric vendors for identification purposes without explicit permission. Most do not want government-state surveillance. Many people are worried that their biometrics may be used by insurance companies to charge higher rates due to preexisting conditions or anomalies that may make them at higher risk. The overall problem is that biometric data is being used by many companies for reasons for which we did not give explicit permission, and that data can be stolen from any of those companies and used in harmful ways.

NOTE: As an example, there is strong blowback on law enforcement, which has been using large databases to solve hundreds of previously unsolved murders. Some services have begun to deny the ability to use biometrics because of privacy concerns.

### Disease Transmission

Lastly, shared biometric readers that require physical touching using a common device (e.g., fingerprint readers, palm scanners, iris readers) can easily spread disease. If a company offers disinfectant solutions or wipes for users to avail themselves of, there is absolutely no assurance those quick remedies actually work. From discussion with health officials on the transmission of diseases, recommend copious amounts of disinfectants and a longer period of time—certainly more than just a quick application of gel or wipes. Not to mention airborne diseases that will collect in common areas where shared biometric devices are forced to be used. Taken as a whole, biometrics aren't the perfect or accurate authentication that many people make them out to be. This is not to say they aren't a good solution in applications. If you use biometrics, you should do so with a clear idea of their strengths and weaknesses.

### Stolen Biometrics

If an attacker can steal a whole bunch of biometrics at once, what can a victim impacted by the incident do? Their biometric traits are now out of control by an unauthorized party and available for reuse and duplication at any time.

### OPM Fingerprint Theft

The biggest known biometric trait theft was the 2014–2015 of 5.6 million people's fingerprints (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_Personnel_Management_data_breach). Everyone who had applied for a U.S. government security clearance and submitted fingerprints (all 10 digits in most cases) were victims of a nation-state attack against the Office of Personnel Management (OPM). Even the fingerprints of secret spies, now living under assumed names, were taken. My fingerprints from an application for U.S. government security clearance 15 years ago were involved. My wife's fingerprints from her work at a local shipyard in the 1980s as she was a teenager. The fingerprints were among a total of 22 million records stolen, including detailed personal background of every government applicant (e.g., previous employers, family relations, addresses, travel, drug use, psychological issues)—all the information any good social engineer would love to have.

The theft was traced to China. Eventually, some records were made and some of the key perpetrators were identified. Years later, the records were returned at the request of President Obama by Chinese Xi Jinping, but there are assurances that no copies were made. If I was a nation-state, I'd make a copy before "hacking" back.

### Suprema BioStar 2

BioStar 2 is a fingerprint and facial recognition biometric security platform made by a company called Suprema. It allows to control access to facility areas and user permissions. In August 2019, security researchers announced (www.vpnmonitor.com/blog/report-biostar2-leak) that BioStar's biometric database could be breached, potentially exposing over 1 million users' fingerprints, facial recognition information, and passwords. The security researchers found Suprema generally unresponsive and unhelpful to their report; the reported breach hole was closed a week later. The question is, did anyone else discover the breach before it was closed by the researchers and console?
