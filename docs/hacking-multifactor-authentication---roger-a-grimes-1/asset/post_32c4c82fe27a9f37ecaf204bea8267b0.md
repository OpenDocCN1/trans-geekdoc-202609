## Dangling CDN Hijack

Content delivery networks (CDNs) are hosted by servers all over the Internet and lots of fast and widespread Internet bandwidth. If you are a company that wants the fastest response to all your potential customers on the Internet, you can buy the services of a CDN. The CDN will host your services, or objects on their servers, and when one of your customers goes to access your domain names, they get content delivered by the closest or fastest CDN. For example, when Microsoft pushes out a new patch, they will often use a CDN to make that patch available to everyone quickly. To use a CDN, the vendor must have DNS records that point to the CDN instead of their own DNS domains for control. Read more about CDNs: https://cloudvdps.com/helpcenter/content-delivery-network-cdn/how-does-cdn-work.

In 2016, a white-hat hacker noted (hackrone.com/reports/168476) that Snapchat had "dangling" MX records. Dangling DNS records occur because of a few reasons (e.g., abandonment, unintentional orphaning), but the result is that the domain can be claimed by an unintended party and maliciously redirected elsewhere. It's the digital equivalent of moving out of your house and putting in any male forwarding request or an unauthorized intruder in your home to open up your mail. You can read more on dangling DNS here: omit.com/dangling-dns-is-no-laughing-matter.

In this case, the hacker noticed that Snapchat had all of their MX records pointed to a CDN domain that was no longer registered with their DNS registrar, GoDaddy, which controlled those records. He moved the dangling DNS names to his account. He was a good hacker, so he contacted Snapchat on the HackerOne bounty program and got a $250 reward for reporting the dangling DNS.

## Registrar Takeover

Why just take a few DNS records when you can take the whole DNS registrar? If you don't already know it, the companies that allow you to buy/register your DNS hostnames and IP addresses are known as DNS registrars. In the early days of the Internet, they used to be a very controlled and limited group. But today, there are tens of thousands of them, with varying levels of security and ethics.

Brian Krebs (krebsonsecurity.com/2015/02/webnic-registrar-blamed-for-hijack-of-lenovo-google-domains) that Google's Vietnam subdomain (google.com.vn) was hijacked by a group Lizard Squad. The hackers have taken over the Malaysian DNS registrar Web.cc, with over 600,000 domains controlled using web server injection. The hackers also took over Lenovo.com, which is a laptop vendor and all other sorts of computer equipment. For Lenovo, the hackers switched their MX record and began directly responding to emails the media had sent to Lenovo (you can see the screenshot of the email here: www.tripwire.com/state-of-security/security-data-protection/hackers-can-hijack-your-website-and-read-your-email-without-hacking-your-company/).

It is notable that in this attack, the hackers also gained control of "transfer codes", which are secret registrars use to authenticate themselves to another for quick domains before the scenes. This particular hack was done by a hacking group more interested in pranks than in espionage, but the damage to Webcam's customers and the broader Internet could have been way worse if a serious group had controlled.

## DNS Character Set Tricks

Most of us type in our DNS URLs using English or our local language, whatever that may be. By many apps, including browsers, use different character sets, and those character sets can be used to hack users and bypass defenses.

Computers display characters on a screen or printer by choosing a particular number of an agreed-upon set that displays a particular character. For example, if I wanted to display a lowercase "f" as ASCII, I would use ASCII decimal 102. To display an uppercase "F" as ASCII, I would use decimal 70. And on any computer supporting ASCII, my program could use an ASCII table to look up and translate the numbers to letters. Here's a full ASCII table: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:ASCII-Table.svg.

A character set is also a way for one language to be converted into another. For example, suppose I wanted to use my U.S.-centric application to type the word frog and wanted users in Russia to type it in Cyrillic (i.e., лягушка) and have my program understand it either way. I could code my application to have two or more languages or use a character set that automatically translate and display between different languages. Character sets are usually defined at a device, OS, or application level.

The first computers used the ASCII character set. It could only support 128 English characters. The first 32 ASCII characters are "control characters" like line feed and end-of-text delimiters. The rest cover uppercase and lowercase English letters, the 10 numbers, and common symbols you find on your keyboard like !@#$%^&*. But a limit of 128 characters is a bit restrictive for non-English speakers.

Early on, Windows used what is known as American Standards Institute (ANSI) character set. It supported only 218 characters, which was better than 128-character ASCII, but it wasn't built to handle complex languages like Cyrillic. With Windows 2000, Microsoft began to use Unicode. Unicode represents every known language, and it can represent millions of characters.

Since 2009, the World Wide Web (essentially the Internet) uses a character set as UTF-8 (Unicode Transformation 8-bit). It's a subset of over 1 million characters. Consequently, most of the world, including DNS (which officially only uses ASCII), actually uses UTF-8. When you type a character into your browser, behind the scenes the computer is dealing with the typed in character as its Unicode number. It's the way the web work the scenes.

Many websites and applications support of UTF-8 with what is known as Punycode (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punycode). Punycode is a Unicode version for hostnames.

You may also see IDN (internationalized domain names; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationalized_domain_), which is a method for converting and displaying domain names between languages using Punycode and IDN characters

Hackers use Punycode and IDN characters to look like other characters to create-alike domain names. These types of attacks are known as homograph attacks (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDN_homograph_attack). They not only fool users but get beyond by expensive defenses. For example, the Unicode Latin "a" (U+0061) and Cyrillic "а" (U+0430) may look the same in a browser but all different characters are represented in different. Any DNS address containing one versus the other can point to different IP addresses, even though they look the same when sent in an email or displayed in a browser.
