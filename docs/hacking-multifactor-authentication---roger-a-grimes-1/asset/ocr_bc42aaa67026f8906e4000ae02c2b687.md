In the early days of cryptography and authentication codes, the legitimate parties could just generate and share a big list of random numbers as codes and agree to use them in the future. But what type of scheme has at least two programs? First, how do we make the codes truly random? How do you create the codes randomly and verify that they are truly random? Humans could “guess” at code characters—say, each of the legitimate parties “randomly” guess a number between 0 and 9 over and over until all the codes are created and shared. For instance, suppose the first might say 3, the second might say 5, the first person says 9, and the second person says 2. This would give a shared four-digit code of 3592. You could repeat the guessing until they have all the codes they need to share for all the possible authentication transactions.

To the average person, this seems random and fair. But it is not. Humans cannot simply do anything random. Humans tend to be “un-random” and have a hard time doing anything random, especially in picking numbers and codes. If humans were asked to participate in a task like above, they would invariably pick numbers considerably more than others without to avoid repeating patterns. For example, humans are far more likely to pick lower numbers than higher numbers and to avoid repeating patterns. In a truly selected set of numbers, over time each (0–9) would have a 10 percent chance of being selected. But if you humans pick the numbers you would see numbers selected below 10 percent of the time and numbers picked over 10 percent of the time. In studies of this human behavior, some of the numbers were picked nearly 20 percent of the time and others less than 5 percent. No matter what your loved says to the contrary, humans aren’t random.
