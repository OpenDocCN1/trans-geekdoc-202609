
## Chapter 13 Downgrade/Recovery Attacks

Attackers abuse alternate email address recovery in at least two ways: takeover of alternate email addresses and rerouting recovery.

## Takeover of Alternate Email Addresses

In this method, attackers will confirm that alternate email address a particular victim is using and then compromise that alternate email address (usually social engineering or phishing, or by learning the password from a previous password dump), and then send the MFA-protected account into recovery mode. I've also heard of password guessing being used to compromise the alternate email account, although it doesn't seem to be popular these days since most major email accounts have password lockout settings enabled.

Compromised email accounts happen every day. Many users have Gmail, Microsoft, or Yahoo email addresses as their personal accounts, which are often used for MFA alternate account recovery. A small percentage of these free email accounts are compromised every day. Because these services have tens to hundreds of millions of accounts, a "small percentage" of accounts translates to millions of compromised accounts every day.

The large free email providers afford not to provide human tech support technicians. It's too expensive. They don't bankrupt trying to stay up for email account recovery. Instead, they the account recovery process. The hardest part is finding the "account recovery" link to start the process. But once done, the automated process goes through a series of questions and steps to help the involved party self-recover their account. ## Hacking Multifactor Authentication

Unfortunately, the first recovery option offered by Microsoft is a previously registered alternate email recovery account (see Figure 13.3). Oftimes the recovery process will provide a partial email address of a previously registered email account, which could give the attacker a clue as to what the alternative recovery account they need to compromise be. Some account recovery processes will even offer to let the recovery person use new email accounts that have not been previously registered, but most services will require additional details before using any newly registered account. ## Routing Recovery

The second popular email address recovery attack method is the hacker somehow convincing the MFA solution provider to switch the alternate email address to an attacker hacker controls. This usually requires that the attacker gets enough of the victim's details to successfully answer the MFA account update features or utilize a bug in the account update process.

Here's a real-world, sophisticated, multi-step, multi-victim example: blog.cloudflare.com/post-mortem-todays-attack-apparent-google. In this example, the attackers successfully convinced AT&T, the victim's cell phone, to redirect the victim's voicemail inbox to a new, temporary phone number. When doing this, the attackers did not know any of the normal recovery questions that the AT&T technical support person asked but knew the last digits of the victim's social security number, which the tech support person accepted as valid authentication.

NOTE The victim was at a loss as how AT&T had his Social number.

The attackers then put the victim's Google account into recovery mode and chose, out of the multiple options offered by the automated recovery, have Google the recovery account PIN to the victim's previously registered phone number. The victim did not recognize the incoming call from Google and let it go to voicemail. The AT&T had rerouted the victim's voicemail to a number under the hackers' control, and the hackers listened to the voice-mail from Google tech support and heard the Gmail recovery code.

The hackers used the Gmail recovery code to take over the victim's Gmail account. They then put the victim's CloudFlare account (their ultimate target) into recovery mode and had CloudFlare send the recovery code to the victim's Gmail account. The attackers then used CloudFlare recovery code to take over the victim's CloudFlare account.

The attackers then used the victim's CloudFlare account to make changes to victim's DNS records, which allowed the attacker to control the DNS address of the hacker's ultimate attack target. Whew! these admin abilities to the intermediate victim, the chief executive of CloudFlare, was actively involved during the incident and fighting unsuccessfully against the hacker while his authentication was stolen. The attackers turned out to be hackivists who wanted to punish their ultimate target, 4Chan, for not self-policing well enough.

You can read more at krebsonsecurity.com/2012/06/attackers-target-weak-spots-in-2-factor-authentication/www.darkreading.com/vulnerabilities-and-threats/google-attack-by-cloudflare/hackers/d-id/1041666 and www.csonline.com/article/2222511/hackivists-ugmani-attack-4chan--cloudflare-and-wounded-reporter-project.html. CloudFlare said the attackers were eventually arrested by the FBI, but the link to that statement is no longer and I cannot confirm the arrests.

There is a fourth way to take over or change the alternate email account, but they are not common. The third way is if the attacker knows the password. The fourth way is when the attacker compromises the device.

## Abusing Master Codes

The problem with master codes is that they often expire and securely storing them is difficult. Many people are told to back up their backup codes. That sounds like a reasonable idea until you realize that backup codes in both downloaded and print contain the Gmail address as well. If an attacker comes across the backup codes, they can use them anywhere in the world. If the codes do not expire, you can get new codes and cancel the old codes at any time.

## Guessing Personal-Knowledge Questions

Of all the ways someone can recover their MFA-protected account, answering perhaps simple, prereistered personal-knowledge questions is the weakest and easiest of hack—so easy, that many people, including this author, believe the traditional way of using them as implemented by most websites had forbidden by law. Several large authentication providers, including Google and Microsoft, have forbidden their simplistic questions on their sites and services.

Sadly, simple personal-knowledge questions have been one of the most popular account recovery methods and have been for decades. They came out of a need for authentication providers to allow users to recover their forgotten passwords without needing human intervention. Before automated self-service account recovery, a user locked out of their account or forgetting their password had to call a human technical support person, explain the problem, and have the tech support person unlock their account and give them a temporary password.
