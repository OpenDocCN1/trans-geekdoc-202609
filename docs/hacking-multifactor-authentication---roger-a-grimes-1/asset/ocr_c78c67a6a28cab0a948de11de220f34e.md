# Password Guessing

One of the simplest and easiest ways for an attacker to compromise someone's password is to simply guess it. Although there are literally billions to trillions of possible password combinations that anyone could select if they used all possible allowed passwords, most people select among the same 30 characters, and the order they are in isn't much of a mystery.

Humans, making up their own passwords, like to use their default language and like to use words that are comfortable with. When I was a full-time penetration tester and I was on-site physically at a location trying to guess someone's password, I would look around their office. I would often notice what pictures were closest to them. If they had a picture of a child, I would guess password combinations that included the names of children. If they had a boyfriend/girlfriend or spouse's picture nearest the computer, I would use that. If they had a picture of a hobby or favorite sports team, I tried that. You'd be amazed how many CEOs have a golf-related word which.

Even if the password was supposed to be "complex," most passwords rarely't complex. If an organization requires a "complex" password, that tells me that most of the passwords are going to begin with an uppercase consonant followed immediately by a lowercase (usually an a, or o); if a number was required, it would be a 1 or a 2 at the end; and if a symbol was required, it would be a !, @, #, $, or & and be located in place of a look-alike letter. If I've described your password, don't worry—80 percent of your coworkers have a similarly formatted password.

Most passwords are fairly easy to guess. They aren't as easy to guess as Hollywood shows. You can't hold a gun to a hacker's head and get them to guess someone's password in a minute. It doesn't work that way, although I did once—once in my 30-year career—do such thing. I was trying to log on an old accounting system that I had to replace with the company's new accounting system. The upgrade was scheduled for the weekend so that it would not disrupt the business. I was let into the building by a security guard and left alone to complete the upgrade. I didn't know before, while trying to perform the action, it asked me for a "master password." I tried a few things, like password, Password, password123, and quince are all the most used passwords in any given year), they didn't work. I was frustrated, and in frustration I "rosebud" because I had just watched one of the world's most treasured old films, Citizen Kane. In the movie, Orson Welles' character whispers that as he is, and the whole film is about the origin of that word. I won't be a plot spoiler; you'll have to watch the film. But apparently the creators of the old accounting software were fans of the movie, because when I typed in it, it worked! I was very proud of myself. And 30 years later, I've never been so lucky.
