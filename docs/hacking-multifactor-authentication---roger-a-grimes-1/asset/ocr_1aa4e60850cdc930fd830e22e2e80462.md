# Snooping on RFID-Enabled Credit Cards

Radio-frequency RFID is a near-field communication (NFC) wireless technology that many products use to communicate wirelessly. It uses radio waves (of the electromagnetic spectrum) from 120 kHz to 10 GHz. Most wireless credit cards (smartcards and other contactless products we use for wireless payments) operate at the 13.56 MHz frequency (known as the ISM band).

The RFID components in credit cards are known as "passive" (versus "active") and energized by external devices that wirelessly supply a DC power current (a process known as induction). When an RFID device comes within range of the RFID power source and signal, it turns on and does what it is preprogrammed to do—just if it been inserted into a physical reader. Passive RFID cards must be within a few centimeters for a standard passive card reader to supply the power and energize the device, although active devices (with their own power supplies and operating at different frequencies) can communicate with each other up to several feet away.

As an end-convenience, the RFID interface is now commonly installed on credit cards (and other devices) to make payments during a transaction. The user usually hovers their card within a few centimeters over the wireless reader and the transaction is confirmed in one to three seconds. Wireless credit cards usually fall under the ISO/IEC 14443 standard and are also known as proximity cards. RFID-based credit cards are the most common type of contactless used for electronic payment. There are a handful of different RFID-symbols placed on RFID-enabled credit cards, but this is the most common one.

Anyone can install an RFID reader app on their phone, hover over an RFID-enabled credit card, and read a limited amount of unprotected information, including the credit card account number and expiration date (an example is shown in Figure 17.4).
