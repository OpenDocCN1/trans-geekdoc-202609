# The Problem with Passwords

The move to MFA and from passwords didn't happen without a significant reason. Hacked passwords play an oversized role in malicious hacking and are the internationally accepted currency of the hacker world. Many passwords have been hacked and are unavailable for use that hackers have a hard time selling them for decent. Just a decade or so ago, a simple compromised password could earn a hacker many tens of dollars, and collection of compromised passwords were guarded like gold at the end of a digital rainbow. But today hackers have so many of them sitting around they are willing to pay much, if anything. It's not that passwords aren't being used to hack people and organizations—they are still in very large numbers. There are just so many minutes in a day. It's an embarrassment of riches.

Instead, compromised logons ended up being placed on the Internet where anyone can see and use them. Simple password dumps contain billions of previously compromised logon names and passwords. In 2019, a single password archived dump, named Collection1, contained more than 770 million logon names and made all the news channels—one of cybersecurity events that goes far beyond the IT-centric world. It was to be surpassed a few days later by related Collections#2-5 with 2.2 billion passwords. Altogether they contained nearly 3 billion logon names and passwords that, including you, could download and use. Read about it here www.forbes.com/sites/dayeswriter/2019/02/01/2-billion-accounts-found-in-big-data-takeover-data-dump-to-check-if-your-a-victim. Another hacker revealed another 1 billion newly obtained accounts in 2019 (www.zdnet.com/article/a-hacker-has-dumped-nearly-one-billion-user-records-over-the-past-two-months). So that makes more than 4 billion logon accounts within a few months of each other available on the web. There is a good chance that at least one of your logons, with your password, is available on the web for you to use.

Multiple websites are telling you that your passwords ended up in one of these many password dumps. The most used and famous one is Troy Hunt's HaveBeenPwed (havebeenpwed.com). Started as a hobby, it now contains more than 8.5 billion logon names and passwords from copied password dumps from the Internet and web. You type in your email address, and the website will tell you how many times your logon and password appear in its database from all the collected passwords it knows about.
