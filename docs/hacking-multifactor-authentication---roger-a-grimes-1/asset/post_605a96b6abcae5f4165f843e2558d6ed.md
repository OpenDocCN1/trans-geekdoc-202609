## SIM Swap Attacks

Before I discuss SIM swap attacks, I need to make sure everyone understands what a SIM is.

### Subscriber Identity Module (SIM)

Most mobile devices that accept cell phone calls and messages have several globally unique numbers associated and stored on them. Each cellular device and network can have different requirements and often store many pieces of various information, but most have at least a few minimum types of unique numbers.

One is an International Mobile Subscriber Identity (IMSI) number. An IMSI contains three parts:

- Three-digit Mobile Country Code (MCC), which identifies the mobile device it registered in
- Three-digit Mobile Network Code (MNC), which identifies the device's registered provider (visit the following for example codes: www.roamingzone.com/mnc)
- Nine-digit Mobile Station Identification Number (MSIN), which identifies a specific subscriber no matter device they use

Most cell phones also have an International Mobile Equipment Identity (IMEI), another 15-digit number that identifies the specific mobile device and stays with the device. Another common number is a Mobile Station Integrated Services Digital Network (MSISDN) number, which is the full phone number of a mobile device subscriber, beginning with including the country code (e.g., 1 for the United States and Canada, 44 for the UK, 61 for Australia).

These numbers are used to identify you and your device on a cellular network and to route and manage voice and message conversations to you and your device. In essence, these is numbers that allow your phone to be your phone. When some of these codes are moved from your current phone to yours, it's what makes the new phone your phone.

Most cell phone and cellular providers stored this information, and anything else they liked, in a physical small memory card (in Figure 8.5) known as the subscriber identity module (SIM) card or chip. Each SIM chip or storage area has a 19-digit or larger unique Integrated Circuit Identifier (ICCID) number.

If you ever had to move from one cell phone to another back then, your cellular provider would make you use the existing SIM card from the old phone to the new phone. Most cell phone users would spend time trying to find exactly where the SIM card was (often in a tiny slot on the end of the phone or underneath the removable battery, when batteries were removable), and get a paper clip to eject or pop the card out. It was an exercise in patience.

If you lost or replaced your old phone, SIM chip and all, the cell phone provider would provide you a new SIM chip and you place it in the new cell phone, and then you would dial a special short phone number (say #69# and that service would push down your SIM information to the new card and phone. Increasing, future cell phones will not come with SIM slots or cards anymore. Instead, the information that was normally stored on a SIM card will be stored on the cell phone's permanent memory in a protected location. This technology is known as virtual SIM. When the physical SIM is no longer present, people will still refer to the stored information as the SIM card or SIM information. For more information on virtual SIM, visit www.allspace.com/virtual-sim-card-es-the-full-guide and www.kaspersky.com/blog/virtual-sim-15572.

The SIM storage area can also act as storage for the phone, holding application data such as the user's pictures and contact information, although using a SIM for customer data storage is being deprecated. As I said earlier, it is the SIM information that makes your current phone your phone.

## SIM Swap Methods

For over a decade, hackers have been obtaining a legitimate subscriber's SIM information and transferring it to a phone in their possession. This can be done in many ways:

- Social engineering of the user
- Social engineering of the network provider
- Insider attack at the network
- SIM information-stealing malware
- SMS infrastructure exploitation

It is very common for SIM information to socially engineer a user, via email or phone, to get enough information so that the hacker can socially engineer the user's phone provider into switching the user's SIM information to the hacker's phone. Hackers have also paid cell phone provider employees to perform SIM transfers. No matter how it is accomplished, the SIM information is installed and active on the new phone, the old (original, legitimate) cell phone stops receiving calls and messages. Other than an "out of network" indicator, most victims realize for hours that their SIM information has been transferred. Usually the victim notices after a few hours then they realize they haven't received any calls or messages. Usually only then they realize they do not have cellular service. The victim may think they are involved in a normal, temporary disruption. By the time they realize it's a temporary service disruption, they have to find out a way to call their cell phone provider's technical support. Usually at this point the victim is still unaware that they are victims of a crime and that their SIM information has been transferred to an attacker's phone.

Normally they call their cell phone provider's technical support and are told something like "You activated your new phone" or something like that by the technical support person. When the victim says they did not do that, the victim and the tech support proceed to get the SIM swap back to the original. It can take as long as days for the victim to get their cell phone service back. During all those hours the attacker is usually using their ill-gotten SMS access to their advantage and to the victim's disadvantage.

When a hacker does a malicious SIM swap, SMS-based MFA messages are sent to the hacker s cell phone instead of the victim. The hacker can then compromise your online account using the misrouted SMS-based MFA message. Usually whatever the hacker goes into the user's MFA-protected services (Gmail, Microsoft 365, etc.) and act like they cannot access the account without getting an SMS recovery message.
