
## Chapter 17 Physical Attacks

## Cold-Boot Attacks

But suppose you don't have the money or time to run out your electron microscope. If you have $5, you might be able to reveal protected secrets with a can of compressed air. Let me explain.

On most devices, in order for encryption and decryption to work, the encryption/decryption key(s) must be in plaintext in memory (or at least derivable to plaintext from what is located in memory). It has to be that way. You cannot encrypt the encryption/decryption key when it is actually used to encrypt or decrypt data. If you use a memory inspection tool, you can look for and locate the encryption key. And some types of memory chips, especially nonvolatile, will keep the encryption key in memory even when power is not supplied. For decades, it was popularly believed that if you powered off a computer, the information located in its memory chips would be gone. Turns out that this isn't always true.

If you lower the temperature or freeze the memory chips, it's more likely that memory contents will last longer after the power goes away. This new understanding led to what is now known as cold-boot attacks. Essentially, you lower the temperature of the memory chips as much as possible while they are still energized, and then pull off the power of the device which is currently located in and move the memory to another device, which can dump and analyze the contents of the memory in a static state. For a few years the first publications became all rage. Stopping them wasn't so easy because of how memory is inherently volatile. See www.windowsclub.com/cold-boot-attack and en.wikipedia.org/wiki/cold_boot_attack for more details.

The first time I saw a cold-boot attack in person, I had just learned about these types of attacks in 2008. A Princeton University team, including well-known Java and voting machine hacker Edward Felten (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Felten), announced (citp.princeton.edu/our-work/memory/) that they could have cracked a well-known disk encryption program using a simple and inexpensive cold-boot attack using canned air, like the anyone can do to shut off their computer and keyboard. The programs they could unlock Microsoft's BitLocker Encryption.

Even though BitLocker is named "Drive Encryption," it doesn't encrypt a whole disk as the name might. BitLocker, like many other disk encryption programs, just encrypts smaller, logical disk volumes and not the entire disk. It is a small but important distinction. True encryption usually requires equipment or software in the disk microcontroller or some external hardware encryption component sitting higher/prior in the execution stack than the disk itself.

This was startling and noteworthy because Microsoft had just released Windows Vista with BitLocker to capture the trust of more enterprise customers with the promise of stronger, built-in, reliable disk encryption. And here were kids saying they could break the encryption from the memory modules of a multibillion dollar company with a $5 of compressed air. And it truly worked! Felten's team supplied (citp.princeton.edu/our-work/memory/) a white paper, FAQ, and even source code so that anyone could replicate what they had done. Their technique worked not only against Microsoft's BitLocker, but also against Apple's FileVault as well as open source dmcrypt and TrueCrypt. The Princeton team were opportunity hackers.

NOTE TrueCrypt was a very popular open source encryption program for a decade. It was eventually discontinued after subsequent analysis showed it was overly vulnerable to other forms of hacking (or that is the well-known rumor). Either way, it is not used anymore.

At a security conference held by Microsoft (where I worked at the time), a hacker demonstration of the cold-boot technique was going on. The hacker asked for volunteers. I raised my hand and gave him my laptop. He removed the panel that was covering the laptop's memory modules, and sprayed a can of air on the memory until an icy frost appeared on the chips. He then pulled the power, killing power to the laptop, and continued to spray the chips, putting more ice frost on them until the water was drained. He popped out the memory chips, put them into his external memory to try to access my computer, ran a search program, and found my BitLocker encryption keys.

With BitLocker, the master keys that protect the BitLocker encryption keys are located on the TPM hardware chip on the computer's motherboard. You can't simply take someone's BitLocker-encrypted hard drive to another computer and boot it up because the keys to unlock the BitLocker encryption keys are located only on the original computer. A BitLocker-protected disk will not boot without its encryption keys, which are protected and unlocked by the TPM chip. The hacker moved my drive to his computer, my disk should not have booted on his computer. But the hacker booted my hard drive and supplied the spied-on-BitLocker encryption/decryption key, and my hard booted normally to my regular Windows login prompt. He then began doing searches on my BitLocker-protected hard drive.

I was astonished. But the world was not, not in computer security, even in the mainstream markets. Today, you can do an Internet search for cold boot attacks and find dozens of videos showing how it works. The key is that the decryption keys (or whatever information you are seeking) have to be stored in plaintext in a memory area where it stays at semi-permanently when power to the original device's memory is no longer continuously supplied. Most of the time, the memory copying is done on the original device, if possible, with the least amount of power interruption to maximize the secrets remain in memory. But I've seen the memory moved to other devices plenty of times and seen the attack work. Basic cold boot memory sprays are as follows:

- 1. Confirm the target device will booting to an external device such as a USB key, and modify it to boot from this external device (i.e., hacker computer or device). This may require modifying the CMOS BIOS or UEFI settings.
- 2. Ensure the targeted device has protection in memory.
- 3. Lower the temperature of the memory chips without freezing, if possible.
- 4. Connect the hacker device to the target device using the connection method indicated as the primary boot sequence connection.
- 5. Power down the targeted device (do not do a normal shutdown, which might clear memory).
- 6. Boot up the externally connected hacker device.
- 7. Run a program to copy the memory of the targeted device. There are dozens of forensic tools that can copy memory contents to an external storage device.
- 8. Search for and identify the protected information from the copied memory.

Many cold-booting hacking guides are available that will help you identify the areas in memory encryption keys are likely to be stored and show you how to identify and copy the correct encryption keys. For example, here is the Princeton's group code for finding RSA and AES keys: citp.princeton.edu/our-work/memory/.

## Snooping on RFID-Enabled Credit Cards

Radio-frequency RFID is a near-field communication (NFC) wireless technology that many products use to communicate wirelessly. It uses radio waves (of the electromagnetic spectrum) from 120 kHz to 10 GHz. Most wireless credit cards (smartcards and other contactless products we use for payments) operate at the 13.56 MHz frequency (known as the ISM band).

The RFID components in credit cards are known as "passive" (versus "active") and energized by external devices that wirelessly supply a DC power current (a process known as induction). When an RFID device comes within range of the RFID power source and signal, it turns on and does what it is preprogrammed to do—just if been into a physical reader. Passive RFID cards must be within a few centimeters for a standard passive card reader to supply the power and energize the device, although active devices (with their own power supplies and operating at different frequencies) can communicate with each other up to several feet away.

As an end-convenience, the RFID interface is now commonly installed on credit cards (and other devices) to make payments during a transaction. The user usually hovers their card within a few centimeters over the wireless reader and the transaction is confirmed in one to three seconds. Wireless credit cards usually fall under the ISO/IEC 1443 standard and are also known as proximity cards. RFID-based credit cards are the most common type of contactless used for electronic payment. To see if your current card is RFID enabled, look for a symbol similar to the one shown in Figure 17.3. There are a handful of different RFID-symbols placed on RFID-enabled credit cards, but this is the most common one.

Anyone can install an RFID reader app on their phone, hover over an RFID-enabled credit card, and read a limited amount of unprotected information, including the credit card account number and expiration date (an example is shown in Figure 17.4).
