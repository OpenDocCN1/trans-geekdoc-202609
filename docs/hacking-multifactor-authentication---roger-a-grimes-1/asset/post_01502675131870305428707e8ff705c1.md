## Common Authentication Standards and Protocols Involving APIs

The use of APIs with authentication standards and protocols is no different. For the last two decades, the digital world has been trying to come to an agreement on some common open standards, which often include APIs. There have been many attempts and nearly many failures in trying to establish standards and APIs that everyone trusts. For the last few years, there have been a few authentication-related standards and their APIs that seem to have popular staying power. They are becoming facto standards, and vendors are building their products to interface with them.

This section explores some of the most popular standards and APIs. Some of them have been covered well in previous chapters and are included here again for completeness.

## OAuth

As covered in Chapter 3, Open Authorization (OAuth) is one of the most popular open federated authentication standards used on the web today. Because of its widespread adoption and use, I'll spend more space on it than one of the other standards.

Most of the major, huge websites and services (e.g., Facebook, Twitter) support it. OAuth is a framework that describes how unrelated services and services can safely allow access to their assets without actually sharing the initial login credential. An identity provider, which may or not be a website or service the user has authenticated to, attests to the already existing successful authentication and helps the user to log on/access additional sites or services.

It's important to mention that OAuth is about authorization, not authentication, even though it involves authentication as well. With OAuth, the user has to authenticate, at least to the identity provider or first site/service, but after that, the first sites and software the user is using exchange authenticated access control tokens. The user doesn't have to reauthenticate to additional sites or services, although they usually have to approve (click Login or Allow) the new login/access to the new site and service (see examples of approval prompts in Figures 19.3 to 19.5). The subjects involved in the accesses aren't reauthenticating. They do provide additional authentication proofs. They are past part of the authentication process and are now in the authorization part of the identity cycle. A user's authentication proofs are not being shared between sites. The original site/service is attesting to the user having successfully authenticated and helps to establish the subject accessing future sites and services.

The future sites and services may involve another logon, the user will not be asked to authenticate if they have already authenticated the selected identity somewhere else. In Figure 19.3, the user is accessing a new site, Gru Vacation Rentals, and the Grupz site is detecting that the user has already successfully authenticated to two previous OAuth providers (in this case, Facebook and Google). Many of the times when you are seeing “log on with Facebook,”log on Google,” or “log on with Apple,” what you are seeing is OAuth involved and working. Any site with OAuth needs to register with one or more OAuth identity providers. In this example, Grupz has previously registered with at least Facebook and Google. Many participating websites have only one or the other, because they figure most people are going to have either, and why the cost of offering additional OAuth providers.

The user need only click one of the shown buttons and Grupz and Google/Facebook will exchange the necessary authorization information to allow the user to log on without providing additional authentication information (although the user can log using non-OAuth credentials).

If you haven't logged into your OAuth identity provider (as Facebook or Google in Figure 19.3) in a while, you may be asked to authenticate them again before they authorize you to log on to the additional site/service. The login credentials you will be submitting are to the site/service you trust (i.e., Facebook/Google) and not to the new site (Grupz, in this case). The new site/service will get your authentication proofs. If the additional sites or services are compromised, attackers will get only a single access control token that is only good for the single, site/service and not your authentication proofs.

Figure 19.4 shows OAuth recognizing that the subject has one identity account registered with the same OAuth identity provider (Google in this example) and is asking the user to choose which identity they want the future authorization to occur under.

A site/service can also request access to your personal information, files, and data. Figure 19.5 shows Dropbox requesting access to see subject's contact list. You should only allow the access if you trust the requesting site/service plans to do with. Not-allowing the permission request cancels the requesting site/service's ability to use OAuth with that.

Be aware that if site/service gets compromised, any attackers would have the same access to your personal information, give out any personal access to quickly and sparsely.

OAuth identity providers allow you to see a list of every allowed site/service (see Figure 19.6 for an example), and you can remove that site/service from your list of OAuth sites/services you allow to interact with that particular provider (see Figure 19.7 for an example).
