And there are tools, like Bloodhound (latesethackingnews.com/2018/09/25/bloodhound-a-tool-for-exploring-active-directory-domain-security), that help hackers map networks and vulnerabilities to move from point A to point Z using collected information. I have seen demonstrations that married a vulnerability scanner with Bloodhound and Metasploit, allowed a hacker to click on a desired target on a screen and the automation did everything else. In the demo, after clicking the computer on a screen, the system reported back that remote access had been gained to, and it opened a remote admin console to the victim for the “hacker.” If this is being shown to me and others, it's certainly being used by nation-states and well-heeled adversaries.

All the malware or bots do need to detect what offensive capability is needed when it's needed. Detecting what it is (i.e., fingerprinting) is easier than to actually defeating them. The bots that gained initial access will detect what defenses are standing in the way of achieving ultimate success, however that is defined, and then download the method or tool required to achieve its goal and use.

Defenders will be forced to meet the growing threat of malicious automation, and so computer will evolve in the same way. Proactive defenses will automate threat hunting and load various defenses on the fly for the different threats that are detected. Vulnerabilities will be patched, firewall rules will be updated, malware will be detected and removed, and indicators of compromise (IoCs) will be detected in the central database. Bots will use rules-based engines, machine learning (ML), and artificial intelligence (AI). It will eventually be better than versus bot in a never-ending battle of bot and automation is better. I first wrote about this future scenario in 2017: www.infoworld.com/article/316839/security/prepare-for-the-smart-bot-invasion. Humans will only be involved with the computers they think they should.
