A cipher key that is known to and used by a single subject that is not intentionally shared with anyone else is a private or secret key. A cipher key that is intentionally shared between multiple subjects is a public key. A key that can be known and used by anyone is a public key. A key to be used only temporarily is a session key.

If the same key is used to encrypt and decrypt the message, the cipher is symmetric. If one key is used to encrypt messages and another is used to decrypt messages, the cipher is asymmetric. Authentications often use multiple keys to accomplish their services, although asymmetric cryptography is more directly responsible for the authentication.

# Symmetric Encryption

Symmetric keys are usually used for encrypting, and the globally accepted symmetric ciphers are very good at it. Symmetric ciphers are stronger, faster, and easier to validate than asymmetric ciphers, and they require smaller key sizes. From a cryptographic viewpoint, symmetric ciphers are easier to prove as strong and reliable. They have less complicated math. They require fewer assumptions and less guesswork. They are harder to attack. Accordingly, symmetric ciphers do most of the world's encryption.

The world has used many different cipher standards since the 1970s, including Data Encryption Standard (DES), Triple DES (3DES), International Data Encryption Algorithm (IDEA), and Rivest Cipher 5 (RC). All of the older symmetric ciphers are weakened and broken today. Since 2001, the most popular symmetric cipher is Advanced Encryption Standard (AES). Currently, AES uses key sizes of 128-, 192- and 256-bit lengths, and its strength has held up well under years of cryptographic scrutiny and attacks.
