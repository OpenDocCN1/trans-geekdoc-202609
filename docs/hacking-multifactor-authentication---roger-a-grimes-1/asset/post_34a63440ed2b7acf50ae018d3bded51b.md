In many years, a single unpatched program was accountable for most hacks. For example, according to the Cisco 2014 Annual Security Report (www.cisco.com/assets/global/UK/pdfs/executive_security/sc-01_casr2014_cte_liq_en.pdf), unpatched Java was responsible for 91 percent of successful web attacks. Imagine: patch one program successfully and 91 percent of your cyber-security risk goes away!

Eventually, over time, browser and operating system vendors made unpatched Java less of a threat. They made it less likely that an unauthorized Java program would be able to run without approval by an end user or administrator. Around 2015, social engineering became the predominant way computers were programmed, and that hasn't changed. Attackers are so successful at social engineering to get what they want, and works cross-platform—attackers don't separate efforts for operating systems and programs—that it is likely to stay near one vector until social engineering can be defeated overall, which is not any time soon.

And it comes no surprise that social engineering is used to defeat and get MFA solutions. Many of the chapters of this book are full of different types of MFA attacks that involve social engineering. Social engineering gears the skids of attacks and makes them easier. Want to get someone to run a trojan program that gets MFA? Socially engineer them into. Want to get a user to click on a malicious link? Then socially engineer them. There is little maliciousness that social engineering can't make easier to accomplish.

Part of the problem is that most humans inherently trust other humans. We do this as part of our daily lives. When someone says something, most of us believe it. Most of us don't see other human beings as inherently evil or untrustworthy. We all make incredible risk decisions as a part of our daily lives. Hey, any woman who goes on a date with a guy, who is usually bigger and stronger, is making a big risk decision in life. We order pizza or delivery food that gets delivered by someone. We've read the many stories of others driving home to previous customers, and those drivers continue to welcome new customers into their personal cars even though there are tens of thousands of reports of other customers harming drivers. We are a world of people who inherently trust each other. Phishing and social engineering take advantage of inherent human trust.

# Social Engineering Commonalities

Anyone can lie to another. Professional social engineering is a special kind of thoughtful lying. Here are some of the common traits of social engineering scams.

# Unauthenticated Communication

Criminals don’t like to get caught. So, the first rule of business for a scammer is to use a communication channel that is largely unauthenticated, weakly authenticated, or if authenticated, easy to break into so that the scammer can easily pretend to be someone else. As examples, anyone can send a physical document by the postal service claiming to be anyone. You could claim to be the president of a country or the Pope and as long as you got the rights on the document, there’s no one to say that it isn’t. The post office doesn’t take the time to authenticate that who is sending the mail is the same as the person it is claiming to be (with exceptions).

Email is only authenticated to the receiver by the sending email address (maybe). Any email can claim to be from anyone, and many times include fake sending addresses. Unless the receiver’s receiving email services check for fake sender addresses, it will not be discovered. There are common email standards to check whether or not the sending email is accurate (e.g., Sender Policy Framework (SPF), Domain Keys Identified Messaging (DKIM), and Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance (DMARC, for three), nearly all of these can be gotten around in multiple ways.

More often, phishers use email addresses from valid domains that look like other valid email address. For example, phishingers send emails with sender addresses formatted like bill-gates-microsoft.com@bbiz-microsoftemail.com, where an unsuspecting user might not understand that the email is not from Microsoft. Or as seen in Chapters 6, "Access Control Tokens" and Chapter 8, "SMS Attacks," covered how phone calls and SMS messages are not authenticated beyond the phone number, which may or not be accurate. I can call from any phone claiming to be the president of the United States, using a second person acting as my "call setup" assistant, and with enough mimicry it might be tough for any receiver to prove I'm not the president. Comic radio DJs do it all the time. When someone calls us claiming to be our bank, or phone company, rarely do we tell the caller to prove it in trust, irrefutable, and maybe.

Most phishers avoid real-life phone calls for a number of reasons, including many times the scammer is of a different nationality and may even speak the victim's native tongue well enough to pull off the scam or answer basic questions. Most phone callers receive any vendor's call, even if coming from someone foreign, to be able to easily communicate in the customer's expected language and know basic details about the customer's referenced matter. Using a real phone number (versus the Internet) slightly increases the risk of being caught, but only slightly versa.

# Nonphysical

Direct person-to-person social engineering happens all the time, but criminals like the least of all possible methods. First, and foremost, the scammer can be more easily detected and arrested. Second, it involves appearing in person and answering any skeptical person's questions. Third, it is a very inefficient way of scam a large group of people. Most people do not fall for social engineering scams. This means scammers must try their phishing scam on a bunch of people for each unlucky victim who takes the bait.
