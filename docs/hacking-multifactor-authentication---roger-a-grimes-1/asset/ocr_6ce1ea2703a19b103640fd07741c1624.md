# Chapter 12 Social Engineering Attacks

I often respond with the facts: that your chances of being hit by a shark are about 1:370,000 and that yours of dying in your car during your lifetime are about 1:100 to 1:50. Your odds of dying of cancer (1:7), heart issues (1:6), or falling in home (1:111), are much higher than even. This is a great site to see your odds of dying from a particular event: injuryfacts.nsc.org/all-injuries/preventable-death-overview/odds-of-dying Various sites give you wisely different statistics, but the relative comparisons are roughly the same (i.e., you should be worried about your car ride to the ocean than a shark attack in the ocean). One of my favorite related statistics is from a 2018 report from the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation (www.gatesnotes.com/Health/Mosquito-Week-2018). It revealed that more people die every day from the impact of mosquito bites than the last hundred years of shark attacks, yet more people are afraid of shark bites than mosquito bites. There is no Mosquito on Discovery channel. We are often worried about the wrong things and can’t help ourselves. There is something more visceral and emotional about getting eaten by a shark than killed slowly by a blood disease transmitted by a mosquito. And attackers understand emotions and fear as motivators. As I discuss in a moment, phishers often use “stressor events” as an emotional motivator to compromise their victims.

# Social Engineering Commonalities

Anyone can lie to another. Professional social engineering is a special kind of thoughtful lying. Here are some of the common traits of social engineering scams.

# Unauthenticated Communication

Criminals don’t like to get caught. So, the first rule of business for a scammer is to use a communication channel that is largely unauthenticated, weakly authenticated, or if authenticated, easy to break into so that the scammer can easily pretend to be someone else. As examples, anyone can send a physical document by the postal service claiming to be anyone. You could claim to be the president of a country or the Pope and as long as you got the rights on the document, there’s no one to say that it isn’t. The post office doesn’t take the time to authenticate that who is sending the mail is the same as the person it is claiming to be (with exceptions).

Email is only authenticated to the receiver by the sending email address (maybe). Any email can claim to be from anyone, and many times include fake sending addresses. Unless the receiver’s receiving email services check for fake sender addresses, it will not be discovered. There are common email standards to check whether or not the sending email is accurate (e.g., Sender Policy Framework (SPF), Domain Keys Identified Messaging (DKIM), and Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance (DMARC, for three), nearly all of these can be gotten around in multiple ways.
