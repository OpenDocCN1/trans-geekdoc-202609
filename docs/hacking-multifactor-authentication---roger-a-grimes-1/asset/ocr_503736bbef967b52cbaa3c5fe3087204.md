# Password Stealing

One of the most common ways an attacker gets a password is to steal it. They steal it from the user's computer or device or from one of the many sites or services that store their password/hash. As explained earlier, if an attacker can gain admin access to a user's computer or device, there are hundreds of hacking tools (such as Mimikatz) that will reveal a user's (and the system's) passwords, either in plaintext or in their hashed forms. Any worth they might use one or more of these tools as soon as they gain admin access to a system.

Many automated malware programs, if they successfully exploit a computer or device, will go looking for passwords, as either their main objective or one of their primary objectives. They will look for user system passwords stored on disk and in memory and steal passwords from browser databases (when the user allows their browser to store their passwords for auto-logons to websites and services requiring authentication).

Any hacker who gains access to a website or service that requires authentication for all users will immediately looking for and steal any authentication databases that contain user logon information. This is how password dumps end up on the Internet. Why steal passwords one at a time when you can steal thousands to millions of them for nearly the same effort?

# Password Reuse Risk Is Huge

The biggest risk from any password theft is that even though a password may be stolen from one website or service, it is often reused by the same user at more than one website (and sometimes every they have). Again every website and service they use. As I mentioned, the average user has six or seven passwords that they use. If I was an attacker and I compromise some rinky-dink, no-one-cares-about-website, and find that a logon name roger@banneretc.com has a password of frog11, I'm going to start checking whether that same logon information works on a bunch of other popular websites, like Amazon, Banks, and stock websites as well. And any good hacker will automate that checking process.

Password reuse is a huge risk. One compromise can lead to a bunch of other compromises. Not only can they reveal currently, actively used logons, but they can also reveal patterns between passwords. Most people, if they don't use random passwords, and even if they use different passwords for every website and service, use passwords with a pattern (such as Frog32, Frog33, Frog34...). An attacker seeking that pattern may figure out what the user likes and can break into additional details.

Similarly, sharing passwords with other people is a hazard, risky practice for several reasons. Many a person has shared their personal passwords with their one true love to learn that true love often does not last, and that previous love can sometimes access their accounts in an unauthorized way to make their life much harder than it needed to be.
