# Introduction

This book came about through an interesting happenstance. Arguably, the world's most infamous hacker, Kevin Mitnick, co-owner and Chief Hacking Officer at KnowBe4, Inc., did a presentation that included showing how he could easily “hack around” two-factor authentication using a simple phishing email. Kevin is a lot more famous than I am, and his demonstration was viewed by thousands of people. And that many people wrote to him to get more details.

Many people wrote and interviewed the KnowBe4 (who I also work for) public relations team asked if I could answer queries Kevin couldn't get. I was glad to. I've got decades of experience in hacking covered security topics frequently if we asked the exploit Kevin used in the MITRE list of Vulnerabilities and Exposures (cve.mitre.org). The CVE is where most cybersecurity vulnerabilities, new or old, are listed and tracked. When a report it to the CVE, with relevant details. Most of us in the cybersecurity world it to check out new exploits have been found and to see if really needed to be worried about them.

I laughed. The Kevin demonstrated (which is called session cookie hijacking and is covered in Chapter 6, “Access Control Token Tricks” of this book) has been around for decades. It's not new at all. In fact, it is one of the most common forms of network hacking. Dozens of free hacking tools are available that hackers do to, it's likely used to take over millions of user accounts over three decades. It's been used to take over accounts protected by two-factor authentication, at least since the late 1990s. It's the opposite of new.

I was surprised when I talked to the beat reporters and computer security people I knew, most thought it was a new attack. So, not everyone was and and regular people not know that it wasn't, but knowledgeable, experienced computer security people—who expected to know—didn't know that. It was surprising to me.

I was also surprised that many of the people I thought the attack was due to a vulnerability in LinkedIn, the website Kevin used in his demonstration. It wasn't. What Kevin showed could be used against hundreds of popular sites, and LinkedIn, in particular, didn't have a flaw that they were going to have to. It was an attack against a very common form of multifactor authentication and how it worked in general. No patch was coming to fix some flaw. And you could update the multifactor authentication solution that was used to prevent the particular type of attack Kevin demonstrated, but it could be attacked at least another different ways, and as any multifactor authentication method.
