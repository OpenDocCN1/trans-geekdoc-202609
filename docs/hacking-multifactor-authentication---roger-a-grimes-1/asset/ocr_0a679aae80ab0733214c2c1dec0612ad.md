The latter involves providing sufficient proof to the driver license issuer (the CSP in this example in the United States is the Department of Motor Vehicles [DMV]) to get the official driver’s license. The applicant does this by providing multiple forms of acceptable identity attributes, such as a birth certificate, Social Security card, and a bill sent to their home residence to verify the identity. Years, the DMV did not require much assurance as they do now, but weak validation allowed serious identity crimes to occur, and so now the DMV requires stronger identity assurance.

But if you get a driver’s license issued to you, anyhow (e.g., law enforcement, liquor store) that requests proof of your identity will accept the DMV-issued driver’s license as proof of identity. The same thing with CSPs. Any relying party trusts the CSP will trust a CSP-verified identity by a subject to be accurate. But if the CSP doesn’t do their job and doesn't accurately verify the subject’s identity and attributes before verifying it, the whole process breaks down.

A good, everyday digital example of this is the use of Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) asymmetric encryption keys. Created in 1981 by Phil Zimmermann, PGP is a popular encryption program that comes in both open and commercial versions. Everyone gets a PGP public key from someone else is supposed to validate it before using it. But almost no one does in practice. In the past, fraudulent users noticed that the supplied PGP key was invalid (e.g., an invalid fingerprint). So, everyone who uses FGP keys and the ones received is participating in this invalid level of privacy and authentication when it is no better than regular, nonencrypted unauthenticated email (or perhaps worse, because people think they can rely on it).

A verified identity (and their attributes) is known as a verified subscriber. The CSP usually maintains ownership of the identity and can renew, cancel, or update it, solely at its discretion. The authentication factors for the identity and by the CSP are also known as authenticators. When the subscriber authenticates to a relying party (e.g., device, system, site, service), they are known as the claimant. The claimant can also provide, along with their authenticators, one or more claims/attributes, which have also been verified by the CSP.

The biggest risk to the enrollment process is a malicious subject being fraudulently enrolled to become a verified subscriber backed by the CSP and trusted by the relying party. Malicious actors do this to obtain access to an authenticated population, where they can introduce maliciousness. An authenticated identity gets seen as more legitimate than if they posed as an anonymous user.

Of course, the trust given identity depends on the steps the CSP takes to verify the subscriber’s identity before issuing them a “verified” account. For some of the most popular CSPs the verification party is limited to no verification before issued an account.
