#### OpenID and OpenID Connect
OpenID Connect (openid.net) is an authentication layer that goes on top of and is frequently used with OAuth. There was a preceding simple open and decentralized version known as OpenID (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenID). It was an interesting authentication standard and was supported by many members, including Microsoft, which deployed it as Windows CardSpace. The part that excited most identity experts was the ability to have a shareable identity and authentication that was completely decentralized. The identity and authentication information was stored by the user on their own system. There was no one entity or set of entities that controlled it or stored the authentication information (such as Microsoft Passport in the past). No one entity controlled the standard, which was good, and there was no one location that could be compromised, other than the user's device, that would lead to access to all the user's identities.

A severity of security concerns have been announced over the years against OpenID (see en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenID#Security), and in general, the idea of a completely decentralized standard, no matter how promising, evaporated. At least part of the reason was that if a user stored all of their identity and authentication information on a single device, it could not be easily shared among the user's other devices without re-creating the identities or physically copying the OpenIDs to the new devices so that they could be used there.

In the third version of OpenID, it morphed into OpenID Connect (OIDC) in 2014 and is not aligned with the official authentication layer over OAuth. When you use OAuth to log on to another website, most of the time you are using both OAuth and OpenID Connect. OpenID Connect is the protocol that helps you create identities and authenticate to the OAuth site/service provider.

The OAuth provider allows you to share OpenID Connect between systems while joining on a new site/service (as shown in Figure 19.4), that's available OpenID Connect identities you can register with OAuth provider. When a site/service asks for permissions (as shown in Figure 19.5), that's OAuth and its authorization features, those permissions and relationships are tied to a particular OpenID Connect identity with a particular OAuth provider. The OpenID Connect identities, allowed sites/services, and approved permissions are tied to a particular OAuth provider. You won't see the same IDs, entities and relationships with another OAuth provider.

So, the original dream of a completely decentralized identity with the former versions of OpenID, but it is still decentralized in that you can have many OAuth identity providers, each with different OpenID Connect identities and relationships. You are not necessarily tied to one provider or identity. You have flexibility in deciding who to trust for. For example, I may allow Dropbox to access my Gmail.

You can see a slightly more technical discussion on how OAuth and OpenID Connect work with each other: stackflow.com/questions/1087031/whats-the-difference-between-openid-and-oauth. For more information on OpenID Connect specifically, see openid.net/developers/specs and en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenID_Connect.

#### SAML
Security Assertion Markup Language (SAML) is an identity federation that allows the secure exchange of authentication and authorization information of subjects, identity providers and service providers in or better. It is frequently used to transmit security credentials of a user to log into something and is a favorite format of single sign-on solutions. SAML competes against OAuth + OpenID Connect in many areas. SAML gets used in the enterprise space and OAuth + OpenID Connect in the consumer Internet space. One big benefit to OAuth is that SAML can be used for both authentication and authorization, whereas OAuth, itself, is used for authorization. For more information on SAML, see developers.onlogin.com/saml and www.csoonline.com/article/3232555/what-is-saml-how-it-works-and-how-it-enables-single-sign-on.html.

#### OATH
The Initiative for Open Authentication (OATH; openauthentication.org) is an industry consortium focusing on strong authentication (i.e., token devices) and creating and using open standards. They focus on three major types of MFA solutions:
- Subscriber identity (SIM)
- Public key infrastructure (PKI)
- One-time password (OTP)-based

OATH has a large list of members and has created widely adopted token standards (most covered in previous chapters), including the following:
- HOTP: HMAC-Based OTP (RFC 4226)
- TOTP: Time-Based OTP (RFC 6238)
- OCRA: Challenge/Response Algorithms (RFC 6287)
- PSC: Portable Symmetric Key (RFC 6030)
- DSKP: Dynamic Key Provisioning (RFC 6063)

Even though OATH seems to have a lot of promise and there are fairly recent announcements, such as Microsoft in 2018 (techcommunity.microsoft.com/azure-directory/oath-tokens-in-mfa-in-the-cloud-are-now-available/), it appears to have dissolved. The FIDO standard seems to be capturing the lion's share of news and progress, even from the same members. OATH has come into existence, released a bunch of important RFCs, and then disappeared. Sadly, some of the methods that became more popular—such as generating keys in cleartext, like Google Authenticator—seem to have won out even though OATH is clearly technically superior.

NOTE: One very interesting OATH initiative is Sharing Transaction Data, RFC 5941 (www.ietf.org/rfc/5941). It is a RFC that recognizes the world would be better served if we could share information about incidents in a faster, previously agreed-on format and structure. Information about fraud and cybersecurity incidents could be uploaded and shared by participants to help respond to and prevent future threats. Unfortunately, doesn't appear much traction has occurred since its submission in 2010. Something like this needs to happen and is likely to be what ends up making the Internet a safe place.

You can learn more about OATH here: openauthentication.org and openauthentication.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/IndustryRoadmapforOpenAuthentication.pdf.

#### FIDO
As we covered in previous chapters, FIDO specifications are a set of popular "passwordless" open authentication standards that rely on public–private key cryptography. FIDO supports a wide range of MFA devices (computers, USB tokens, mobile phones, wired and wireless, etc.) and biometrics. FIDO2 (its current version) has two main authentication paths and four specifications. Any FIDO2-participating software (OS, browsers, services, etc.) must use the World Wide Web Consortium's (W3Cs) Web Authentication standard (WebAuthn) and APIs.

The Client to Authentication Protocol (CTAP) specification covers wireless devices. The Universal Authentication Framework (UAF) specification is a passwordless method, which is a physical device. The Universal nd Factor (U2F) specification covers MFA and requires a second factor and device of some type. The U2F standard is the one most adopted by MFA solution providers.

With U2F, users register their devices with the particular service or services to choose how to implement an authentication factor, such as a PIN or biometric ID. When connecting to the site or conducting a transaction that requires strong authentication, the device performs local authentication (verifying the PIN or biometric identity) and passes the success or failure to the remote site. With U2F, an additional device (such as a cell phone or USB dongle) is used as the second factor after the password or PIN has been provided.

Traditional TLS guarantees authentication only to the client. FIDO authentication goes further by linking "registered" devices to their users and to the eventual websites or services. One authentication device can be linked to many (or all) websites and services. The registration prevents many, but not all, types of authentication attacks.

U2F prevents man-in-the-middle (MitM) attacks, as those discussed in Chapter 6, "Access Control Token Tricks," and summarized in Figure 6.3. For example, the Kevin Mitnick MitM demo attack against LinkedIn would not have worked. This is because U2F requires authentication of the device and server client from locations not previously registered, and the U2F authentication solution would not respond (or would respond with an error). You can read about FIDO MitM protections in this section of the FIDO specification document: fidoalliance.org/specs/fido-u2f-v1.2-ps-20170411/fido-u2f-overview-v1-2-ps-20170411.html.

NOTE: Part what I and others love about FIDO is that even in their specification section they prevent MitM attacks, they will tell you what would be needed to bypass those defenses (it's a pretty set of circumstances that are unlikely to be present in the real world) and how you to prevent even those. They aren't afraid to say they can't prevent every possible attack and don't pretend to. I wish more MFA solutions were as honest and direct. Transparency builds trust.
