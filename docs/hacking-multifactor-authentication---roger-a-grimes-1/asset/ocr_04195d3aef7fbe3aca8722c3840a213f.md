The scanner doesn’t know the difference between your finger almost being placed on the scanner and your finger being solidly on the scanner. Instead, it’s scanning over and many times a second just waiting for the right fingerprint image to be placed in the right way.

All biometric scanners are intentionally “de-tuned” to be less accurate. If they were tuned to be as accurate as possible, it would result in too many false negatives due to variations in legitimate fingerprints from normal wear and tear. This de-tuning, along with a lock of rate limiting, opens up the opportunity for an attacker to try many, many biometric attributes it hits or one “sort” of matches a legitimate one. Your fingerprint or retina may be unique in the world, but how it is stored and analyzed is a lot more susceptible to brute-force guessing.

# Defenses Against Brute-Force Attacks

The risk of brute-force attacks is exacerbated by the fact that most MFA solutions require input only numbers 0–9 and have a maximum number of 4-to-6 digit positions. In the regular password authentication world, common rate-limiting and back-attempt lockout controls against brute-force attacks have been the norm for decades—but not in every login portal. For some reason these controls, which have proven their value in the password world, just aren’t seen as important in the MFA world. In fact, brute-force attacks are often ignored until some hacker tries a brute-force attack and tells the vendor. It seems uncommon for a new MFA vendor to implement anti-brute-forcing controls from the very beginning. I don’t know why. Perhaps it is because the MFA vendors and their users think that MFA has mysterious property that makes them far less likely to be hacked using the normal, everyday methods. When the flaws are reported, the vendors, big or little, often take months to close the holes.

Here are some developer and user defenses against brute-force attacks. Most of the controls are developer-focused and the user’s risk is mostly controllable only by the MFA solution vendor.

# Developer Defenses Against Brute-Force Attacks

First, let’s explore developer defenses.

# Enforce a Maximum Number of Guesses in a Particular Time Period

Developers should allow a maximum number of bad attempts in a particular time period before making the attacker wait for a set of time to try again, require an unlock from a most trustworthy service or person, or permanently lock the device. How many attempts and in time period is up to the developer. I will say I was surprised to read that Instagram allowed 250 attempts. That seems insanely high to me, but they must have their reasons. A value of six attempts within minutes as the maximum without causing a lockout seems reasonable to me.
