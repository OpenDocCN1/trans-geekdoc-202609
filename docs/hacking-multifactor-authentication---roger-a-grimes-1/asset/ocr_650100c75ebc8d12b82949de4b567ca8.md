# Chapter 1 Logon Problems 31

+   - Passwords should have strong entropy.
- Don't share your password with anyone.
- Enable account lockout to prevent unrestrained guessing.
- Change your passwords periodically, not to exceed 180 days for corporate passwords (90 days is better) and not to exceed 1 year for personal passwords.
- Consider using a password manager to create strong, unique passwords for each website and service.
- When typing passwords across networks, make sure the network channel is protected/encrypted to prevent eavesdropping.
- Only provide critical personal or financial information to websites that need it and that you trust to protect your logon account and credentials.

If you follow the first four recommendations in the previous list, your chances of being maliciously hacked because of your password are significantly diminished.

# MFA Riding to the Rescue?

Passwords aren't inherently evil, but they do, as discussed, have a lot of weaknesses and are hacked a—so much that the entire world pretty agrees they should not be relied on for serious authentication. We need to provide the world, everyone, with a significantly stronger and trustworthy authentication method that is easy to use and significantly diminishes the risk of promise. And so far, the most often tweeted answer is multifactor authentication. Will MFA be that shining authentication knight that many people are hoping for? Only time will tell. But can we say that MFA has its own security risk and issues, and what is the rest of this book is all about.

What I can tell you is that if all passwords go and all we have MFA, then authentication hacking will continue to occur. For one, it this book is about. MFA is harder to hack, but not impossible to hack, and there is a world of difference between the two statements. For decades I've seen system from using passwords for MFA, thinking that all of us suddenly didn't worry about authentication hacking anymore. And all of those systems (and their users) discovered that the hackers don't get. MFA may slow hackers down, but it doesn't stop them. It's not like the hackers are seeing MFA, and knowing there are dozens of ways to hack it, and just call it a day, go home, and quit, because it might be harder to attack. The opposite is usually true. MFA may slow down the bulk, random attacks, but are far less likely to get stopped attacks. I've seen people's MFA so easily hacked that they gave up and went back to simple logon names and passwords (I'll cover this in future chapters).
