The user thinks they are securely logging in to check their bank balance or to transfer $10,000 to a bank in Russia. The latter type of bancos trojan is part of an attack type known as a man-in-the-browser (MitB) attack. The bancos trojan can recognize how much they can transfer without setting off the financial institution's fraud checks. It may even change the user's email address, phone number, mailing address, and login method. That way, if the bank decides to call the user to confirm the transaction, the bank ends up calling a Skype number where the hackers are in the name and personae of the targeted victim.

Alexandre Cagnoni of cybersecurity company WatchGuard (www.watchguard.com), and one of his book's editors, has years of fighting bancos trojans in Brazil. He shared that a couple of years ago, Brazilian hackers started selling a self-teaching tool, with code and everything, teaching how to create your own MitB trojan. It was around $200, as far as he remembers. One of his friends who worked on fraud prevention at a bank bought a kit. That said it in a case, a professional-looking box with a D and were very well explained.

Cagnoni also shared that around April 2019, banks in Brazil started stealing an unknown-related attack. Criminals in the streets were stealing iPhones and Android phones from people in the streets of Sao Paulo. Forty minutes later, the victim's money was transferred from the customer account to another bank, using the mobile banking application, even if the phone and banking app were both protected by PIN and biometrics (face or fingerprint). His team was baffled. At that time, the only thing they discovered was that all the phones were being taken to the GPS location—a neighborhood in Sao Paulo (called Santa Íteria), where there were a lot of shady electronics stores. Their only guess was that the attackers were using some specific high-end hardware to bypass the biometrics check within the phone and mobile banking app.

Bancos trojans have been around for nearly two decades and have stolen billions of dollars. Single bancos trojan families have been implicated in a billion dollar (www.dsolutionsgroup.com/spyeye-caused-1-billion-losses). They are still very popular. Bancos tro target not only banks but any organization the hackers can transfer money or value from. This includes stock trading, cryptocurrency, and money transfer sites.
