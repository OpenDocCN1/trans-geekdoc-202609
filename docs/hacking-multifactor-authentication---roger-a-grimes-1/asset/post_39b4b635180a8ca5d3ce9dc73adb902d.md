# Just Ask For It

The easiest way for a hacker to get someone's password is to just ask for it. Phishing is the process of maliciously masquerading as a trusted entity to acquire unauthorized information or to create a desired action that is contrary to the victim or their company's self-interests. It can be accomplished using email, websites, Short Message Service (SMS) messaging, chat channels, written documents, and voice phone calls. Basically, any method used to communicate can be used to phage someone. Right now, phishing is the one way that most organizations are successfully hacked the most. Either the user is tricked into revealing their logon information or they get tricked into running a trojan horse program, which leads to the same result. Many of the password dumps contain of passwords that were obtained solely by phishing.

An attacker can simply ask for someone's password and you would be surprised how often you can get it from a complete stranger. Years ago when I was doing full-time penetration testing, I used to go to the CEO's executive assistants and say, "Hi, my name is Roger Rimes. I've been hired to test the password security of the company, including the CEO. What is the CEO's password?" Now, remember, they aren not even supposed to have the CEO's passwords—passwords are not to be shared. But want to know how many executive assistants gave me their CEO's passwords? All of them! In my 20 years of asking, not one assistant refused to give it to me. I had some haste a few seconds before giving it to me, but no one didn't conceal and reveal the top executive's.

I've participated in over the studies and tests where we would offer people in a public place (e.g., street, subway system) small prizes like candy bars or pens if they would reveal their passwords to us. Our success rate was 30 to 60 percent, depending on the country and test. Don't believe me? There are videos all over the Internet showing the same thing. It's not like I have some social engineering charm. You just have to ask.

Late-night show Jim Kimmel occasionally reveals people walking down the street in Hollywood, California, and asks for their computer passwords. Here's an example: www.youtube.com/watch?v=RJJefGK5SJA. It's hard to watch without both laughing and laughing at the same time how easy it is to get people to reveal their real passwords.

So, in summary, passwords are easy to use and hack. Using a variety of methods, including guessing, cracking, stealing, eavesdropping and asking, many billions of passwords have been stolen. The problem is not getting better after two decades—it's only getting worse. We didn't use to have billions of stolen passwords sitting on the Internet. It started to make the news when a hundred thousand of them ended up in a single password dump. Now I'm not even a billion stolen passwords will raise an eyebrow—hence, Multifactor authentication is often touted as the panacea.

# Password Hacking Defenses

I don't want to leave readers with the impression that password authentication can be a safe authentication. It can be, risks and all. And since passwords aren't going away soon, here are the ways to make password authentication safer:

- Don't get phished into giving out your password to a malicious entity.
- Don't get phished into running a trojan horse program.
- Make sure your system are fully patched so the attacker can't use unpatched software into your computer and steal your passwords.
- Use a unique password for website or service.
- Passwords should have strong entropy.
- Don't share your password with anyone.
- Enable account lockout to prevent unrestrained guessing.
- Change your passwords periodically, not to exceed 180 days for corporate passwords (90 days is better) and not to exceed 1 year for personal passwords.
- Consider using a password manager to create strong, unique passwords for each website and service.
- When typing passwords across networks, make sure the network channel is protected/encrypted to prevent eavesdropping.
- Only provide critical personal or financial information to websites that need it and that you trust to protect your logon account and credentials.

If you follow the first four recommendations in the previous list, your chances of being maliciously hacked because of your password are significantly diminished.

# MFA Riding to the Rescue?

Passwords aren't inherently evil, but they do, as discussed, have a lot of weaknesses and are hacked a— so much that the entire world pretty agrees they should not be relied on for serious authentication. We need to provide the world, everyone, with a significantly stronger and trustworthy authentication method that is easy to use and significantly diminishes the risk of promise. And so far, the most often tweeted answer is multifactor authentication. Will MFA be that shining authentication knight that many people are hoping for? Only time will tell. But can we say that MFA has its own security risk and issues, and what is the rest of this book is all about.

What I can tell you is that if all passwords go and all we have MFA, then authentication hacking will continue to occur. For one, it this book is about. MFA is harder to hack, but not impossible to hack, and there is a world of difference between the two statements. For decades I've seen system from using passwords for MFA, thinking that all of us suddenly didn't worry about authentication hacking anymore. And all of those systems (and their users) discovered that the hackers don't get. MFA may slow hackers down, but it doesn't stop them. It's not like the hackers are seeing MFA, and knowing there are dozens of ways to hack it, and just call it a day, go home, and quit, because it might be harder to attack. The opposite is usually true. MFA may slow down the bulk, random attacks, but are far less likely to get stopped attacks. I've seen people's MFA so easily hacked that they gave up and went back to simple logon names and passwords (I'll cover this in future chapters).

# Authentication Basics

Chapter 1, "Logon Problems" covered logon problems and, in particular, the problems with password authentication. Chapter 2 will cover the basics of authentication in general. With that said, it's a pretty in-depth look at authentication from top to bottom, from beginning to end, simple logics to complex mega-systems, and everything in between. If you ever wanted to know about authentication and what it entails, this is the chapter to read.

You will hear about the CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability) triad used to summarize the major security categories. Certainly, authentication includes all those components, but it also includes more. Here are the components and considerations of any authentication system, all of which will be covered in this book:

- Confidentiality
- Integrity
- Availability
- Identity management life cycle
- Scope of control/security domain
- Usability
- User control
- Privacy
- Protocols/standards/APIs
- Auditing/accounting/event logging

The primary reason for authentication is to confirm a subject's ability to access protected resources (e.g., security domains, files, folders, sites, services). The process determines whether the subject who they say they are and whether they can prove it.

Not all things require authentication. For example, most of the web surfing requires no authentication. We don't want to authenticate to do an Internet search, download a public document, or read the news. But much of our online world requires that we authenticate to access private or stored personal information.
