# Hacking Multifactor Authentication

Social engineering can be defined as the process of maliciously masquerading as a trusted entity to acquire unauthorized information or create a desired action that is contrary to the victim's or their organization's self-interests. Simply put, social engineering is a "con", with criminal or unethical intent, focuses on manipulating human behavior. It can be done by many ways, in-person or by using instant messaging. Short Message Service (SMS), social media or phone. Depending on the social engineering form and intent, it can also be called phishing, spear phishing (i.e., targeted), spamming, or vishing (i.e., done via voicemail). Social engineering schemes involve authoritative subjects claiming to be enforcement, government officials, friends, coworkers, social websites, banks, auction sites, or IT administrators. Any relationship that might be allow motivate someone to follow suggested instructions is commonly used to lure the unsuspecting victim.

It can be argued that any authentication failure resulting from a human mistake or bad risk decision more likely be blamed on a technical failure. You might think that if the solution were better designed, the human would not be asked to make a risk decision that could possibly result in self-harm. And if it were possible to design a system that did not involve a human being, this would be true. But at minimum, a human is usually involved (although not always) in invoking the authentication system to gain access to a protected resource they are trying to access.

Most systems of any type invoking trust also require that the involved being makes critical decisions, often because the technical systems cannot be trusted to make the correct decisions in all cases. For example, one day we will have self-driving cars, which never (or very rarely) make a mistake. We badly need self-driving cars to save millions of other innocent lives. But right now, the technical sophistication of self-driving cars cannot be fully trusted. Cars come with numerous self-driving features, which monitor and warn of all other vehicles driving in our blind spots, as sensors that will anticipate what the car is going to do otherwise get in a front-end collision. These systems are improving every day. They are even more accurate and safe than the humans they trust. But still, we have self-driving cars running over pedestrians, running into the sides of fire trucks, and causing accidents that a human could easily avoid. Recently, I saw a relative of mine ignore an overly loud warning that there was a car right beside her as she diverted into the other vehicle's lane and almost caused a severe accident. One day they will be perfect and able to out humans in every case, but that day has not arrived.

It's same with authentication. Perhaps one day we will have authentication so perfected that a human being will not be asked to make a trust decision that could possibly result in self-harm, but that day is not here yet.
