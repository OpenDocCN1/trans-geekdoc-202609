
## Hacking Multifactor Authentication

### Google Authenticator Vulnerability

The Google Authenticator time-based one-password (TOTP) app was extensively covered in Chapter 9, “One-Time Password Attacks.” In that chapter, it was stated that the secret seed values of any OTP need to be heavily protected. If an adversary can gain access to these values, they can generate additional unauthorized instances and use them to respond as if they were the legitimate users.

In order to implement and use Google Authenticator with your device, an admin has to install and configure the Google Authenticator software on a server that is configured as the authentication provider to the sites it wants. Google Authenticator is implemented by installing the pluggable authentication module (PAM) pam_google_authenticator.so on a Linux host computer. The installation process creates a secret file setting the generation codes that must be kept from unauthorized users.

In 2012, it was discovered that the secret file had overly permissive permissions, in this case, readable by all users. The Google Authenticator PAM's operations were changed to work with stricter permissions and elevate user access (i.e., sudo). To access more, see www.openwall.com/lists/oss-security/2013/04/18/10 and .cvedetails.com/cve/CVE-2012-6140.

A second Google MFA bypass (www.cvedetails.com/cve/CVE-2013-0258) was discovered in 2013. If a user's account was enabled for Google Authenticator but their token was not associated with the account, anyone could log on to the user's account using the login name alone (no password required). Details are here: www.drupal.org/node/1903282.

### YubiKey Vulnerability

YubiKey makes some of the most beloved MFA devices and tokens on the Internet. They are used by millions of users and might be the second-most popular MFA solution (after RSA) of all time. In 2018, a researcher discovered multiple vulnerabilities in various versions of Yubico software that would allow anyone with knowledge of how to craft a custom MFA device that would successfully bypass YubiKey authentication checks on devices where the software was installed. The attacker could insert their custom device and they would immediately be logged in as if they successfully presented a legitimate YubiKey device. This was due to buffer overflow vulnerability. Details are here: www.yubico.com/support/security-advisories/ysa-2018-3/. A more widespread exploit, known as ROCA, that worked against Yubico devices was found in 2017 and was discussed in a moment.

### Multiple RSA Vulnerabilities

Founded in 1982, RSA Security, Inc. has been around longer than most other security vendors. They were the earliest implementers of public key cryptography and MFA authentication. RSA was popular that for decades if you saw an MFA solution, it was most often an RSA token (RSA SecurIDs) or RSA piece of software.

## Chapter 15 Buggy Software (p. 319)

So it should be no surprise that RSA has a higher number of vulnerabilities than most of its competitors. CVE shows 79 different RSA vulnerabilities (www.cvedetails.com/vendor/334/RSA.html) since 1999. There are at least nine security bypasses (www.cvedetails.com/vulnerability-list/vendor_id-334/obyp-1/RSA.html). Many MFA solutions, including this one: www.cvedetails.com/CVE-2010-3321. With this particular vulnerability, someone with authorization to access an insecure SecID token could export the device's private key off. Private keys on smartcards and SecID tokens are supposed to be protected from being exported off the devices. If someone can export it, they can essentially duplicate the device and how it authenticates. RSA Security was also purportedly involved in one of the largest seed value thefts, as discussed in Chapter 9.

### SafeNet Vulnerability

I've been a big PKI guy for decades. In that world, the three most trusted companies for securely storing PKI keys for certificate authorities (CA) and other critical PKI equipment were, SafeNet and Gemalto (which was a target of a massive SIM card hack in 2010, covered in Chapter 8, "SMS Attacks"). Now all brands are owned by the single company, Thales (www.thalesgroup.com).

Thales is pronounced "Talice", similar to malice or Alice but with a t.

In 2016, SafeNet announced (labs.nettitude.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/160125-1-Gemalto-IDSS-Security-SAS-Privilege-Escalation.pdf) 10 separate CVEs dealing with overly permissive permissions. In particular, the Microsoft Windows Authenticated Users had Full Control permissions to SafeNet critical files. This would allow any authenticated user over those impacted systems to see confidential authentication information and be able to maliciously modify SafeNet files.

### Login.gov

Login.gov (login.gov) is a website that allows "secure and private online access to participating government programs." It's the tens of millions of Americans access their public government resources. In 2017, a hacker reported an MFA bypass using (hackrone.com/reports/264090). He was able to start the registration process, then they sent him a confirmation email. The website expected the user to click the link to confirm their registration and told him he had only 24 hours to do so. Then he noticed it did not require it.

### ROCA Vulnerability

A company called Infineon created a cryptographic library known as RSAlib. It was implemented in the software and hardware of hundreds of devices. In 2017, RSA-generated keys (from 512 to 2048 bits) had a severe vulnerability—if you could access the public key (which theoretically anyone in the world could), you could immediately determine the private key. This was such a big deal even that although discovered by researchers in February 2017, it was not publicly disclosed until October 2017. The intervening time was used to invoke vulnerability and make patches. I had heard inklings of something related few weeks before it was publicly announced, but no one I personally knew any idea of the severity. It was a well-kept, but somewhat selectively known, secret about the vendor's employees. In an instance, as soon as the flaw was made public, millions of devices (e.g., Windows computers with TPM chips) were announced as being exploitable. We had emergency meetings at Microsoft, where I worked at the time, about what we needed to do. It was determined that impacted Microsoft smartcards needed to be replaced. It was followed by device and software updates across hundreds of thousands, just at Microsoft.

Tens of millions of devices had to be upgraded. Impacted smartcards to be replaced. What makes me do this day is that I frequently run into those who sold smartcards and MFA administrators who more than lately had impacted devices, who have never heard of ROCA. Early on we estimated that if not tens of millions of vulnerable devices and asymmetric keys were never fixed. Two years out, most of the vulnerable keys have now likely expired (the average asymmetric key is only good for 1 to 2 years). But there is no doubt that some keys with longer lives are still being used around the world. For more information, check out the following sites:
