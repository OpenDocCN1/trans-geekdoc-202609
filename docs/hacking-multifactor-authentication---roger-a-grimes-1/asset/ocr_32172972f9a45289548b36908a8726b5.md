The most famous example was a compromise of several RSA customers in 2011 (www.wired.com/2011/06/rsa-replaces-securid-tokens/). An APT actor phished RSA employees with emails containing attachments (i.e., Microsoft Excel spreadsheets). Some of the RSA employees who fell for the has long unpatched versions of Microsoft Office, and when they opened the malicious files included malware program compromised their workstations and eventually much by RSA's network. With the unpatched software, the employees had to ignore or bypass multiple warnings from Microsoft Office products. During other things, it was rumored that several RSA customer databases, including Lockheed Martin's, containing their RSA SecID seeds and device guides were compromised.

Over the years I have heard stories from press and RSA employees as to whether the APT attackers truly obtained RSA SecID secret information and use to compromise customer accounts. RSA denied that such events have never happened. This is made difficult to believe because of a few factors. One, RSA confirmed the breach and ultimately told customers to replace all customer's RSA SecID devices. This course could have been out of an abundance of caution and to head continuing negative goodwill headwinds. Second, at least two RSA customers said they were compromised directly because of the RSA compromise, including Lockheed Martin (www.theregister.co.uk/2011/05/27/lockheed_seurid_hack_flap/) and I-3 (www.wired.com/2011/05/1-3).

Lastly, I have re-created SecID myself. I'm not alone. Likely hundreds of interested computer people did. For over a decade a hacker called Cain & Abel proved it easy to make any RSA SecID if you knew the serial number and value. Cain & Abel contained dozens of features of hacking tools all wrapped up in one package. It excelled at sniffing passwords over the wire and doing man-in-the-middle attacks, ARP poisoning and password cracking. It also included a feature that would allow anyone to create virtual instances of RSA SecID tokens and even a way to import many SecID key files. For over a decade I used to gladly direct to and use Cain & Abel as a versatile hacking tool. But it hasn't been greatly much since the days of Microsoft Windows XP (2004) and is no longer hosted on creators' original website (oxid.it). If I'm not sure if you can use Cain & Abel today without some malicious modification to abuse people who use it. So, it's best to avoid it unless you can be assured you can get and use a clean version. I assume since the big hack RSA has updated the SecID token algorithm even if you had a clean copy of Cain & Abel would not work against today's SecID tokens. RSA is likely using updating hashing algorithms and more secure changes.
