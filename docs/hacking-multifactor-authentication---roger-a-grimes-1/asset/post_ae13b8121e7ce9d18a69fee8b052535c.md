Every SMS message includes all of the following information, even if much of it is not displayed directly to the user:

- Originating address (originating mobile number or generated code)
- Header – Message Type, Service Center Time Stamp, Protocol Identifier, Data Coding Scheme, Length Of User Data
- User Data (the message)

The originating address is usually the sender's phone number, but it can be a variety of codes, usually all numeric. It can be a simulated phone number or any code that part of the network involved in sending and directing the SMS message wants to inject. It doesn't have to be a valid phone number or even originate from the phone number it says it is from. Phone numbers can be spoofed using several different methods and tools. Here's a story about spoofed phone numbers which were used to compromise user's credit card accounts: krebsonsecurity.com/2020/04/would-you-have-fallen-for-this-phone-scam.

One of the key factors to note is if an SMS message comes from a phone number or code we have already stored in our contact list, as my phone number in this example, SMS-based applications typically will convert the attached phone number to the name as listed in the contact list. If the phone number is not found in the contact list, the user is just shown the phone number the SMS message was sent from or what is known as the short message code. ## SS7

The original limitation of SMS was due to its reliance on an underlying protocol as Signaling System No. 7 (SS7). SS7 is the international standard protocol that made regular voice calling, known as the public switched telephone network, possible. SS7 splits the "voice part" of the channel apart from the "control" or "signal" channel, which helps to manage and disconnect channels. It takes both channels, the control and data, to make a conversation happen. In the early days of telephones, human operators handled all the setup work. Eventually, everything the human operator replaced by control signals sent with the voice portion it was not unusual for a caller or receiver to hear control instructions (possibly sniffing).

SS7 gained popularity because it allowed the control portion to be "out-of-band" from the data or voice portion. They can even take different paths from the originating caller to the destination as long as they end up in the same location. Earlier versions had both the control and voice components in the same channel together (called in-band), which made phone hacking (known as phishing) easy.

NOTE If you are wondering, there were previous versions of SS7 as Signaling System No. 6 (SS6), Signaling System No. 5 (SS5), and so. SS7 partially came about because hackers were too good at hacking. First implemented in 1975, SS7 is regularly attacked and abused today. If you want to learn the details of SS7 protocols and formats for today’s digital phone networks, you can read this document: etsi.org/deliver/etsi_ts/102 000 00 to 099/ts 102 001/ts 102 001.pdf. But be aware it is over decades long. SS7 vulnerabilities are so well known to abuse that in U.S. congressman called for an official investigation (www.guardian.com/technology/2016/apr/ss7-congressman-calls-snooping), nothing coming out. Most states and governments do not want to fix SS7 vulnerabilities because they use them when they want to spy on their targets. They like that their populace is not paying much attention and doesn’t seem to mind that their communications are regularly intercepted and redirected at will. Regardless, the inherent issues in SS7 underlie many of the hacks of the upper-layer applications, like SMS, which relies on SS7 for basic connection handling between phones. Of course, SMS has its own problems that go beyond what SS7 has, but it’s a problem that any MFA relying on them is weakened. So knowing that, we cannot fully trust.

## Biggest SMS Weaknesses

Ignoring the inherent SS7 issues, the biggest problem with SMS-based MFA solutions from a security perspective is that an SMS sender or receiver is often not authenticated beyond a phone number. Phone numbers are amazingly to steal. Anyone sending or receiving an SMS message can only, at best, be assured that the phone number the message comes from is accurate, and even that is not guaranteed. Rogue applications allow us to send SMS messages from spoofed or borrowed/shared phone numbers. If the receiver doesn't have the phone number previously registered as a contact in their phone, the SMS shows up on their phone as originating from a particular number and that's.

SMS is unauthenticated, meaning anyone can send another person an SMS by simply knowing the recipient's phone number. And as long as that person hasn't previously noted the number as a particular sender's ID and stored it in their contact list, it will show up like any other SMS without attachment. A receiver might not believe the sender is the president of the United States (unless they already have a formal relationship with the president), but otherwise most people are susceptible to simply accept that the SMS sender is who they claim to.

Even if the SMS message appears to come from a phone number the receiver trusts or is in the contact list, it may not be from that number. For attackers, using a variety of methods, to specify whatever phone number (or code) they want. Many web-based interfaces and APIs allow the sender to specify any phone number or code they like.
