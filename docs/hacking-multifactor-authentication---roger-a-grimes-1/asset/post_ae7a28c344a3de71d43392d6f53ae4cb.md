## Cyber Crime Is Here to Stay

Despite the increasing use of MFA and other sophisticated computer defenses, cybersecurity incidents are here to stay and likely to get worse. I've been a security journalist for nearly 30 years and for as many years I've been asked, "Do you think we will have fewer attacks next year than this?" or something similar. People want to know if the increasing sophistication and wider deployment of computer security is starting to make digital crime harder to commit. And each year, based on the past 30 years of experience, my reply is "No".

Every year I say to myself, "Cybercrime is so bad that I can't imagine how it could get worse," and then each year it gets worse. For last year, ransomware was pretty bad. Ransomware broke into, encrypted data, and brought down businesses (large and small), hospitals, cities, and every law enforcement, all I've ever seen. Ransomware started deleting backups, updating itself ("ransomware-as-a-service"), and asking for a lot more money. I thought to myself, "How could it get worse?"

And ransomware started stealing data and threatening to release it publicly or to other hackers if the ransom wasn't paid. So much for your backup saving you. They stole employees' personal credentials and threatened to reveal them publicly or give them to hackers so that the employees would know if the business cared enough about them to pay the ransom. Ransomware started attacking customer accounts of the businesses they invaded, letting those customers know that the only reason they are extorting them individually is that the business they originally invaded didn't want to pay. And just like that, ransomware got much worse.

## Hacking Multifactor Authentication

Every cybercrime gets worse. I don't necessarily mean with the number of attacks, but in overall damage and whether or not we can as a society feel safer computing this year last. The answer each year for three decades has been no. Each year I hope my usual, pessimistic expectations get surprised and that one day, journalists report that the damage from cybercrime has gone down. So far, it has been false hope.

This is unfortunate, because the Internet could be a far safer place for everyone. It doesn't have to be a place where cybercrime is rampant. There are ways to make it a significantly safer place. But it would take international cooperation and a rebuilding of the Internet from a place of pervasive anonymity to a place of default identity and authentication. So far, the appetite for even just good security doesn't seem strong enough for the majority of Internet users to require it. Somehow, no matter how bad the level of cybercrime is, many people treat it as acceptable and don't want more security by default.

Perhaps a digital 9/11 event will happen one day, like the Internet or stock market being down for a day or a week, but then then, business as usual. And business as usual means increasing crime and damage due to malicious hackers and malware. Even the creation of the best MFA solutions will not change because many people won't use it.

With all of that, you said, your organization can make a safer place to compute—we'll never do that anymore than we will get rid of all crime or all. Our goal is to make the environment we are tasked with securing a significantly harder place to hack. And using good MFA is less hackable is a part of how to make a more secure computing environment.

MFA is now in what I would call it 2.0 form and quickly moving into one more mature 3.0 version. This chapter will cover the traits that the future of authentication, in the coming years and beyond, might look like.

## Increasing Sophisticated Automation

Hackers and malware writers have been using automation for decades to perform their attacks. But the automation actions and the sophistication have been increasing significantly the last years. For example, ransomware used to exploit a computer, immediately lock it up, and ask for money. Now when ransomware program breaks into a computer, it "dials home" to let its creator know it's in a new victim's environment so that ransomware can investigate further. It usually downloads updated versions of itself or new malware. As traditional ransomware gains sophistication, it would look for admin passwords on the first exploited machine and use what it found to break into additional computers within the network. It used to be that hackers would remotely access computers and run scripts, disable antivirus defense, delete backups, and copy data off the network. Now the entire process became part of ransomware's coding; no human attackers needed to be involved. What manually you can automate?

Malware is quickly morphing into "malware-as-a-service", which malicious programs are constantly updating, on the fly. Plenty so malware program will likely just download the components when it needs them. It will detect that it must bypass a particular malware defense and download the component it needs to do so. For example, it will detect that it needs to disable User Account Control to get full control of a Microsoft Windows system, and it will download the component to accomplish that. If malware detects it needs to access data encrypted by Apple's FileVault protection, it will download a module to do that, and so on.
