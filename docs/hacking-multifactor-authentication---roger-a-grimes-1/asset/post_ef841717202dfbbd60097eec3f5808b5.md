When assigning potential likelihoods of risks and different attack components, sometimes you'll be taking with what technical folks call a "guess." That's okay. You just need to take your best guess, whatever it is, and put it on the attack tree so that risk calculations can be made. But even when you guess, think about the methods and what have been most successfully used against your organization or similar solutions in the past and are most likely to occur in the near future. Real attacks by malicious hackers mean that. Theoretical attacks that have never been accomplished against your solution or a solution like yours have a far lower likelihood of occurring.

For example, in today's world, social engineering and attacks against unpatched software are common, making up the vast majority of risk. We've also seen, as covered in this book, attacks against names, but those types of attacks are a minority in the MFA world. Most are theoretical, but not all. More popular are credential theft attacks and exploits due to incorrect permissions—those that are moderate, risks for most solutions. Take your best guess at the likelihood of a particular type of attack component handling and put your best guess percentage to it occurring in a particular time period.

Then add or multiply the different likelihoods on each possible attack chain (e.g., 1, 5, 8, 12, 14 versus 3, 7, 11, 16 as from Figure 21.2) to calculate the highest risk, most likely attack trees. If I did my math right, the first tree (1, 5, 8, 12) adds up to 155 percent. The second tree (3, 7, 11, 16) adds up to 110 percent. You need to perform this type of analysis across all the attack trees to find out pathways have the highest risk of occurring. Concentrate your analysis and mitigations on the most likely (and potentially most damaging) trees and individuals first and best. In my hypothetical scenario involving Figure 21.2, we would concentrate on mitigating the first attack.

Some threat modelers assign damage to the different hacker objectives and use those damage (i.e., cost) as the starting point value which is then multiplied by various attack trees. For example, using Figure 21.2, let's assume hacker objective 14 might cause the MFA solution vendor $2 million in damage might cause $3 million in damage. If I take the first tree (1, 5, 8, 12, 14) that equates to $2,000,000 x 20% x 30% x 25% x 50% = $4,500 of estimated risk loss. In the considered risk tree (3, 7, 16) that equates to $3,000,000 x 35% x 40% x 25% x 10% = $10,500. Just based on analysis, mitigating the risk tree first and best makes more sense.

After over 30 years of doing this, I've come to both hate and love the process of threat modeling and risk mitigation. If done right, what it thinks it does is to force you to consider your solution and scenarios can be attacked and make you think about what you need to mitigate first and best. I'm not concerned with the exact numbers or percentages. I am far more concerned with making I as much of the risk as I can, starting with the most likely stuff first. If I don't want to do is get mitigation staff spending most of their time on a threat scenario that has never happened and likely never will. If nothing else, aside the numbers, make sure your threat models are thoughtful in what they needs to be mitigated first and best.

## Create and Test Mitigations

The next step is figuring out how to mitigate the biggest risks. Parts I, Introduction and II, Hacking MFA of this book lists some of the common attacks against MFA solutions and the mitigations a developer and a user can use to minimize abuse. Use this book as a starting point for your analysis and ideas for mitigations.

What mitigations can be deployed to reduce the risk? What do the mitigations cost? Do they outweigh the potential damage? Are they difficult or easy to implement? How long will they take to implement? What residual risks are you left with all the mitigations have been applied? Remember, everything is hackable. Don't forget to use Security Development Lifecycle (SDL) tools and practices while creating and updating your project. Ensure that you include a way to automatically update your solution without end-user approval, and make it easy for people to report issues. Respond to vulnerability reports quickly and with care.

## Do Security Reviews

After everything is applied, allow your MFA solution to be penetration tested. Use an internal team and use an external, more independent, team. Do penetration testing at least once a year, and change external teams every few years to get a different team's perspective on it. I recommend opening your solution up officially to one of the popular bug bounty programs BugCrowd (www.bugcrowd.com) and HackerOne (hackerone.com). It's always better to be proactively notified of an externally found bug to read about it in the computer security news. Get a code review done in-house by trained SDL professionals and use an automated tool that looks for security vulnerabilities in code. Use a combination of automated tools and human reviewers at securing your product. One will catch what the other doesn't. Threat modeling and reviewing your MFA solution will likely decrease the number of critical bugs after the MFA solution is released.

## Bloomberg, L.P. and the Bloomberg Terminal

Bloomberg is the millionaire of New York City who ran for the 2020 presidential democratic nomination. He got billions of dollars from his Bloomberg, L.P. company and it has tweeted Bloomberg Terminal (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomberg_Terminal) device, which is used by nearly every stock trader who can afford it. It first appeared in 1982 as a "dumb terminal" device. It quickly moved to the PC platform when PCs became dominant and, today, is essentially a mobile software program that remotely interfaces with the backend components. Most financial people and traders love Bloomberg Terminals. They are fast, relatively easy to use, and they access to tremendous amounts of information. For example, you can ask "show me the end-of-day stock price for IBM from Friday it was listed on the stock market" and get an answer in one to two seconds. You want financial statements for the last 20 years? They can get them in seconds. You want the latest news? They have a ton of data and news about any public company, in all place, very quickly. It is amazing to see. The quality and speed of they deliver isn't identical. Bloomberg has committed strongly to offering information more than any other company or product. It's their driving competitive advantage. If you're a computer scientist trying to help Bloomberg accomplishing something, if your solution slows down a Bloomberg information response by even a few milliseconds, you will have the job. The Bloomberg experience doesn't come cheap. Because of that, there has always been a great incentive for crooks and cheats to get or free access to a terminal or the software that allows them to connect to remotely. Bloomberg, from the beginning, has always had cutting-edge physical security controls, dongles, and MFA, to control which one of their terminals. Early on, you had to have a physical Bloomberg Terminal to get access. Later on, you could download Terminal software and register your device and a subscriber key and run the software on your computer. Today, a Bloomberg session is remotely accessible from other computers in what is called Bloomberg Anywhere (bba.bloomberg.net).
