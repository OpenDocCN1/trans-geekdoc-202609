# Zero Trust

Zero trust is the idea that every logon is treated equally with no inherent trust no matter where it originates. Prior to zero trust concepts, connections and logins inside a network were treated as more than those that originated a network. I am not sure that model ever truly represented how risk impacted an organization, the idea was that firewalls and other access controls made it harder for an outsider to get inside a network, and that any connection and logon from the inside was highly likely to be a trusted insider and did not undergo much investigation and control.

“hard, crunchy outside with a chewy inside” shredded the moment we all realized that 99 percent of successful attacks were client-side attacks. Client-side attacks originate on user computers and devices and move out over the network to other areas. The original compromise occurred because of social engineering or unpatched software. Either way, once an attacker was inside a network, they were fairly free to roam the network, steal admin credentials, and access other computers and data across the organization. There was (and still) very little isolation.

Zero trust was the recognition that the attacker was often already in the organization (“assume breach”) and that each connection to any site or service was deemed as untrustworthy as, whether it was originating from inside or outside the network. Without announcing it, zero trust turned identity into the verification checkpoint. Identity became the new network isolation point: The reality was always this way—identity was always the ultimate isolation point; it took us a few decades to realize it.

Industry leaders and researchers created zero trust framework well-known models that they themselves followed:

+   - Gartner Continuous Adaptive Risk and Trust Assessment (CARTA)
- Forrester's Zero Trust Extended (ZTX)
- Google'sBeyondCorp

> NOTE You may also see a reference to the Gartner Trusted Identity Corroation Model (TICM), which is a subset of CARTA.

Each of these zero trust encourages continuous, adaptive, risk-based decisions. In the previous world, let’s say a logon on their network using a local computer or remotely using a virtual private network (VPN) connection. Once they authenticate, however they do, they are authenticated for a long period of time. They can do whatever their role, permissions, privileges, and group memberships allow them to do. And they have the same access control privileges across the network regardless of what they are doing (although Microsoft User Account Control and Linux sudo attempt to control it a bit little).
