### Symmetric Encryption

Symmetric keys are usually used for encrypting, and globally accepted symmetric ciphers are very good at it. Symmetric ciphers are faster and stronger than asymmetric ciphers and require smaller key sizes. From a cryptographic viewpoint, symmetric ciphers are easier to prove as reliable. They have less complicated math and are harder to attack.

The world's encryption has used many different standards since the 1970s, including Data Encryption Standard (DES), Triple DES (3DES), International Data Encryption Algorithm (IDEA), and RC5. All older symmetric ciphers are weakened and broken today. The most popular symmetric cipher is Advanced Encryption Standard (AES). Currently AES uses key sizes of 128-, 192-, and 256-bit, and its strength has held up well under cryptographic scrutiny.

### Types of Authentication

Symmetric ciphers cannot be used alone to do authentication, because anyone involved with a single symmetric cipher uses the same key. Without some additional features and functions, to prove who encrypted or decrypted something, asymmetric encryption and digital signing are required.

### Asymmetric Encryption

Asymmetric ciphers, also known as private/public key pairs, use one key to encrypt content and another cryptographically related to decrypt. Each participant is using their own private/public key pair. The private key is known only to the subject and shared with no one else. The public key can be given to anyone else. Even though both keys can be used to encrypt messages to the other, and versa, the nature of who has the private and public key is important. Remember, the private key is never shared with anyone else. Because of this, if someone wants to send a confidential message to another person, they must use the receiver's public key to encrypt. This will keep the message confidential until the receiver uses their related private key to decrypt. Since no one else has the receiver's private key, no one else can decrypt the message. With asymmetric encryption, we must use the public key of the receiver to encrypt messages.

### Key Exchange

Symmetric encryption is faster and stronger with small sizes than asymmetric encryption. Because of this, asymmetric encryption is often used to securely communicate symmetric keys which do most of the encryption over insecure network channels between two or more subjects. This process is known as key exchange. A very basic summary of the key exchange looks similar to:

1. The client and server connect to each other.
2. The server sends the public key of its asymmetric key pair.
3. The client uses the server's public key to encrypt the client's newly generated "session" symmetric key back to the server.
4. Both the server and the client now use the shared session symmetric key to send encrypted content back and forth to each other.

In the real world, using asymmetric key exchange to securely transmit a shared symmetric key between the client and server has a few steps and complexity, but this is a good summary of the basic steps of asymmetric key exchange. It happens billions of times a day to make our digital world run. When you connect to an HTTPS-enabled website, that's what is happening. When you pay using your credit card online, this is happening. And many times, when you use MFA, this is happening in the background somewhere.

Common types of asymmetric cryptography include Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA), Diffie–Hellman (DH), Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) and ElGamal. RSA is easily the most popularly used asymmetric encryption cipher, accounting for the supermajority of all asymmetric ciphers. Although all asymmetric ciphers are used to perform exchange, Diffie–Hellman (also known as Diffie–Merkle) and Elliptic Curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) are more often associated with exchange-only implementations, instead of being used as asymmetric encryption or authentication alone as RSA. RSA and DH keys typically range from 2,048–4,096 bits and ECDH keys are smaller at 256-bits. Both provide comparative protection.

### Digital Signing

Asymmetric cryptography can also use their key pairs to authenticate and digitally sign content. Digital signing is the act of providing proof that the content is signed and also at the moment of the signing. To sign content, a user uses their private key to “encrypt” the content (or a hash result, covered later in a). We do not call “encryption” since anyone who has the public key (which theoretically could be the entire world) could decrypt and read it. Instead, we call it digital signing.

Any content signed by the private key can be verified (“decrypted”) only by the related public key. If the content must have been signed by the related private key because the only thing the related public key can “decrypt” is something signed by the private key. Similar processes can be used to authenticate identities involved in cryptographic operations, some of which will be covered later in this chapter. Common digital signature ciphers include Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA) and Elliptic Curve DSA (ECDSA).

### Public Key Infrastructure

In order for asymmetric systems to work with authentication, the people communicating them must have trust that everyone’s public key is valid and belongs to who they think it belongs. In the early days of asymmetric cipher communications, it was enough for one person to send another person they already knew their public key, and the receiving person would trust that the person who sent it to them was the correct, valid person with the correct, valid private key.

Each widely used key pair can be tied to a particular subject, which allows attribution and authentication. Usually, symmetric encryption, asymmetric encryption, and signing is used by more directly involved with and responsible for the authentication components.
