Unless truly random, most human-selected passwords are composed entirely of alphabetic or numeric characters encompassing words, dates and in their chosen language. When complexity is required, passwords may include other keyboard symbols. Most human-chosen passwords are not very random; they do not contain much entropy. If humans are allowed to create their own passwords with no complexity requirements, they will be passwords like password, Michael, and query. If humans are required to create supposedly “complex” passwords, they create passwords like Frog33, P0ssword, and query12.

Truly randomly generated passwords look like: "2Vy6j1##.d! and 46avaxTiWmLCQBZZA. These truly random passwords have high entropy and are harder for a password hacker to guess and crack. Unfortunately, as you can see, most humans would not like to memorize and type those sort of passwords over and over. I care about computer security and I don't want to memorize and type those sorts of passwords.

Some security-conscious users insert use passwords, looking more like sentences, as those Cowckewallroughtthegate and ErichThreeWolvesBayathtome. These are known as phrases. And although they can contain numbers and symbols, from their length, they have more entropy than the shorter, “complex” passwords. Unfortunately, it is easy to make a password with that long, and many systems will not accept them. Many websites have a maximum password size of 8 to 12 characters, and will not accept keyboard symbols at all.

Regardless of password composition, users can only remember so many passwords before they begin to forget old ones and enter mistake in new ones, especially when forced password expiration is involved. As covered in Chapter 1, "Logon Problems," the average user has six or seven passwords they use for all their websites. When forced to update their password, they will use one of the other passwords they use all the time, or change the existing password just enough that the website accepts it (like going from frog1 to frog2). Or if they actually use different passwords for every website, they use a noticeable "base word" pattern such FrogFB for Facebook logins, FrogFW for Twitter logins, and so forth. As covered in Chapter 1, no matter what passwords are used or how complex they are, users' passwords are incredibly easy for hackers to learn—so much that the world is trying to move to any other authentication method that doesn't involve passwords only.

The huge advantage of passwords is that they have, more or less, worked for nearly everyone well since the beginning of computer authentication. They are readily understood and easy to use (when not required to be complex). They are everywhere. Failure rates for people using multifactor authentication is high. Not for passwords. Nearly anyone, including a small child, can be easily instructed on how to use passwords in a minute or two. I've seen children under the age of two type in passwords that a 60-year-old struggled with.
