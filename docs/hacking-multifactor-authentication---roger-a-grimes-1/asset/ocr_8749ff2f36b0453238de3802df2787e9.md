It turned out that stricter account lockout policies were causing a lot of unintentional, unneeded widespread lockouts in networks where the password policy by itself any account lockout would never have successful anyway. And, on a limited basis, sometimes attackers would knowingly cause an organization to have a denial-of-service (DoS) by guessing with every valid login name and a bunch of passwords against a public-facing login portal. For example, may company has an Outlook for Access OWA email login web portal employees. An attacker knowing a bunch of company email addresses could guess the portal (using passwords they know won't work, like frog, dog, or tail) just to intentionally cause DoS problems with us. In end, Microsoft could after recommending a one-size-fits-all account lockout policy (which there are built-in defaults in some products, features, and versions).

Still, deciding on the "right" account lockout policy is not an exact science and is debated even within Microsoft, who thought about it more than most. For example, their Azure Directory default settings are 10 guesses in a row leads to a one-minute lockout (docs.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/directory/authentication/howto-password-smart-lockout). Each bad guess after 10 leads to a 1-minute or longer lockout for each additional bad attempt Microsoft Active Directory Federated Services (ADFS) has different account policy recommendations depending on the feature being used and docs.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-fs/operations/configure-ad-fs-extranet-smart-lockout-protection and docs.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-fs/operations/configure-ad-fs-extranet-smart-lockout-protection). There are differences between on-premises Active Directory user accounts and those same entities in the Azure cloud. It just goes to show that any account lockout policy is not an exact science. There is no "right" policy.

With that said, I think having any account lockout policy with any reasonable setting is better than having no policy. That's because you enable account lockout policy, when someone or something makes guesses too many times, systems will generate an account lockout security event, and that event can be sent to an alerting mechanism and can be investigated. It's another tool in a defense-in-depth strategy to help defeat malicious hackers and malware. Without account lockout, most enterprises would probably be unaware that a bunch of bad password attempts were happening (either unintentionally from misconfigured scripts and tools or from malicious hacking).

In any case, when you are using MFA it's because you've decided to use stronger security than what is normally provided with passwords.
