The process of creating the new subject identity account is known as enrollment or registration. The subject or the enrollment agent or enrolling the subject on their behalf often requires additional information about the subject beyond their login identity during the enrollment process. This information may be required or optional. It may be used as part of the subject's identity attributes to determine uniqueness and may be used as part of the future authentication process. Information created or used during enrollment should be verified, although this is not always the case for all included information. As part of subject's enrollment, usually the identity is associated with one or more authentication secrets (discussed in a moment).

A reaffirmation that all the issued and submitted attributes are still actively used and accurate should be done periodically with fairly good accuracy, but the change of information over time (such as an employee moving to a new role or department) and the final deprovisioning process are not nearly as accurately monitored in most environments. Thus, over time the number of accounts in an IDM system often just grows and grows, without the accompanying real growth in active physical users. The growth is partly due to the IDM system failing to detect the no longer valid accounts and not making sure they are removed or disabled.

## Assurance Levels

How the subject's identity attributes are verified by the IDM processes determines the identity's assurance. Identities created with strong verification processes and protections are considered to have high assurance. Identities created with weak (or no) protections are considered to have low assurance. Most IDMs are somewhere in between. As example, most websites allow you to register a new logon account without verifying your identity are considered to have low assurance. Today, many website registrations require you to click on a verification link submitted to your Internet email address so that at least your control of the registered email account is verified. This is some assurance, but it is still fairly.

On the other end of the spectrum

One or more domains can use the same identity and authentication information stored and authenticated in one place, share the same authentication in two or more places (even if the information is identical). That information may be copied to or synchronized between the two or more locations, either on demand or automatically, or to some preset interval of time or specific event action.

If a single authenticated identity leads to automatically and seamlessly logging onto other domains, the logon may be known as single-sign-on (SSO). SSO systems often contain an intermediate database that stores the login information for all the interfaced domains. The user may believe that one logon is working for all their relying resources, but behind the scenes, the SSO solution is actually using separate logins for each interfacing system. SSO may also be a single solution that is accepted by all participating sites and services (like Fast Identity Online (FIDO) authentication, covered in more detail in later chapters).

On the opposite end of the identity spectrum you have truly anonymous identities (i.e., *anonymity*), where no holder of an identity account is verified. In fact, it's an intended attribute that the real holder of the identity is never known. Many times, the anonymity simply happens because the requirements of the IDM system are not assured at all. Anyone can create nearly any identity label (within the scope of the acceptable identity label format) and are not required to submit any qualifying information, and thus no submitted information is verified. The vast majority of Internet websites and services fall under this category. But the anonymity is not necessarily guaranteed or near-guaranteed and is an advertised feature of the system—it's a fact that real identity was not guaranteed or the IDM was intentionally leaky.

Other times the anonymity is intentional. The Tor browser ([www.torproject.org](http://www.torproject.org)), for example, was intentionally made to maintain users' total anonymity. There are also ways to track individual Tor users at times, depending on the scenario, but doing so is not intentional.

Total and complete anonymity projects and services are trying to give users as much anonymity as possible. They will not know the identity of the subject or any attribute about them. If they store information about the subject, they will often store it encrypted in such a way that even they, the IDM service, cannot determine the real subject's identity or location. A law enforcement agency could serve a legal warrant to obtain as much information as they could on the participating subjects and gain little to no information.

Total anonymity is seen by many people, including this author, as a human right. If people want total anonymity to conduct particular actions or scenarios, they should be allowed to have it. There are many legitimate reasons to have and allow anonymity, including political protests without fear of political reprisal, whistleblower reporting, and sensitive group meetings, such as for cancer, AIDs, and rape survivor groups.

## Privacy

I think most people in our society want sort of basic, guaranteed level of privacy. I’m surprised it isn’t in the U.S. Bill of Rights—yet. Dare to dream! Part of the reason why it is so hard to get and use anonymous identities is that nearly the world is going against us being anonymous. In fact, they want to identify us in every way. Advertisers want to track and target us. They want to know our utmost secrets so they can track us in specific ways. The websites want to make money and track us (using browser cookies and a thousand other ways). Most governments, even claiming they want to protect your privacy (not all governments make this claim), really don’t. It is in their best interests to know who you are and what you are up to all the time. Law enforcement wants to be able to see everything you are doing, although some are kind enough to note that they should have a valid, legal search warrant.
