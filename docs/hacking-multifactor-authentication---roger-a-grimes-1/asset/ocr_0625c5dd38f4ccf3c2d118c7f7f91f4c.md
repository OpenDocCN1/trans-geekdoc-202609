# Hacking Multifactor Authentication

# ROCA Vulnerability

There are vulnerabilities and then there vulnerabilities! The 2017 ROCA vulnerability is one of the most severe authentication vulnerabilities of the modern area. It involves public key asymmetric cryptography. As you learned in Chapter 3, “Types of Authentication,” asymmetric cryptography involves cryptographically related private and public keys. Whoever in the key pair can encrypt, the other can decrypt. Public keys are to be shared with anyone, and private keys are intended to be known only by one (or a few users or if it is purposely shared). If an unauthorized person is able to access the private key, then encryption pair is considered broken and useless. Private keys must be protected.

A company called Infineon Technologies (www.infineon.com) created a cryptographic subroutine program known as RSA library. It was named RSAlib. It was implemented in the software and hardware routines of hundreds of devices. In 2017, RSA-generated keys (from 512 to 2048 bits) had a severe vulnerability—if you could access the public key (which theoretically anyone in the world could), you could immediately determine the related private key. This was such a big deal even that it was discovered by researchers in February 2017, it was not publicly disclosed until October 2017. The intervening time was used to invoke vulnerability and make patches. I had heard inklings of something related to a widespread security vulnerability few weeks before it was publicly announced, but no one I personally knew any idea of the severity of the discovery. It was a well-kept, but somewhat selectively known, secret about the involved vendor's employees. In an instance, as soon as the flaw was made public, millions of devices (e.g., Windows computers with TPM chips were announced as being easily exploitable. We had emergency meetings at Microsoft, where I worked at the time, about what we needed to do. It was determined that impacted Microsoft administrator smartcards needed to be replaced. It was followed by device and software updates across hundreds of thousands, just at Microsoft.

Tens of millions of devices had to be upgraded. Impacted smartcards to be replaced. What makes me do this day is that I frequently run into those who sold smartcards and MFA administrators who more than lately had impacted devices, who have never heard of ROCA. Early on we estimated that if not tens of millions of vulnerable devices and asymmetric keys were never fixed. Two years out, the most of the vulnerable keys have now likely expired (the average asymmetric key is only good for 1 to 2 years).
