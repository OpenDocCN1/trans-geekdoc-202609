## Physical Design and Defenses

Since I do not fully trust for high-security applications, our MFA solution will be a separate physical device. Because of the highly required feature set, it will look something like a Bloomberg B-Unit (i.e., a thick credit card). Like B-Units and smartcards, it should be built to withstand use and abuse, and should operate in reasonable temperatures. This device contains its own power source (for, which likely means a lithium-ion battery), have a useful life of at least 10 years, and be resistant to physical tampering. Any attempts to open the physical package should render the device permanently, logically broken. It should be designed to prevent EMI, electron microscope, and wireless side-channel eavesdropping.

Our solution should have the most popular physical connections, such as micro-USB, and provide several popular physical connection types. It can include one or more wireless methods, such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and cellular. Any wired or wireless connection points should be hardened against attack and operate at the secure implementation of their standards. Any wireless method should require the user to physical press or move a switch that is clearly indicated for such a purpose; otherwise, the connection methods remain off.

All connection points need to be protected by physical attacks, such as "Bad BIOS" or evil USB cable attacks like the one discussed here: blog.knowbe4.com/new-evil-usb-cable-shows-how-attacks-like-physical-hardware. All communications, physical or wireless, must be encrypted and protected to prevent interception, modification, or eavesdropping. Any memory, chip, or storage areas should not be susceptible to cold-boot attacks.

The device should have an organic light-emitting diode (OLED) display area large enough to show codes and voter answers (e.g., candidates' names and Y No Votes to ballot questions) along with detail that the voter is assured their vote was registered.

## Hacking Multifactor Authentication

**NOTE** The actual voting and display of the ballot is not on the MFA device. The MFA device displays only authentication codes, validity-checking codes, and answers from the voter for verification. Most voting ballots contain far too legally required text for a small MFA device to show all the required text in a readable way on a small factor.

All hardware should have a "trusted boot" process, similar to what Universal Extensible Firmware Interface (UEFI) uses. All chips, firmware, software, processes, and components should check themselves for integrity upon boot up and continuously while operation. Updates should be accepted only from prepped sources using trusted digital signatures.

## Cryptography

Any cryptography should employ generally accepted open standard algorithms and key sizes. All cryptographic components should be easily updateable (by trusted sources using verifiable digital signatures) and should be crypto-agile. The cryptographic algorithms should not be susceptible to side-channel or timing attacks.

It is notable that Microsoft's proposed safe voting standard (blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2019/05/06/protecting-democratic-elections-through-secure-verifiable-voting) uses homomorphic encryption (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homomorphic_encryption), which we haven't covered previously.

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) systems protect privacy and anonymity while still recording each person's vote to their identity. FHE is the idea that an organization can send encrypted content to a third party (in our case, and allow the third party to purposely manipulate the encrypted content in some authorized and intended manner, without the encrypted text (i.e., voter or vote choices) being decrypted by the third party).

Most homomorphic systems require an additional evaluation algorithm that is cryptographically linked to the original cipher, which can be used by the third-party processor to do their work. Homomorphic systems would allow the necessary transactions (i.e., votes) to occur and without revealing individual votes or voter identity. This would protect the entire voting ecosystem and the voted and voters from unauthorized data leaks.

Homomorphic systems have been postulated and researched since the creation of public key crypto in the 1970s, with varying results. Most of the attempts have resulted in halfway solutions, which could not be used in all situations and are partially homomorphic. A dozen or so FHE systems have been proposed in the pre-quantum world, but they have more overhead than the theory implementation. Quantum computing, and especially the quantum property of entanglement, promises to allow more, and practically implemented. There is a subset of, and de-facto.

## Provisioning/Registration

Voters should be given a free MFA device when they register. All voter registration must be done in person. For people unable to go to registration locations (e.g., disabled, elderly), an authorized, trained registrar should go to their location. The voter's identification should be determined by reasonable doubt, however that is required by state or county regulations. Each voter should be given a global unique number tied to their identity throughout their life. Each with its own GUID, should be tied to the voter's ID, and only one MFA can be active at one time.

I considered biometrically identifying the voter to their MFA solution, like Bloomberg's B-Unit does. This can possibly be part of the solution, but I prefer nonbiometric methods, or if a biometric method is used, to include an additional authentication factor. In case, I prefer a two-factor authentication method where the user must always include a four-digit (personal knowledge) static code that they input to their MFA whenever they use it. This code is locked to the device by the user when they use it and thereafter permanently stored in the centralized voting database, similar to how credit card CVV (card verification value) codes are done, except the code is not printed on the device or anywhere. If the voter forgets their static code, they must get a new MFA device assigned and registered to them. Registration to a new device should require physical identity verification just like the one did.

> NOTE Some voters may be wondering since mail-in ballots, and the like, don't require in-person registration, who would our high-security electronic voting MFA device? First, I'll counter that even voters who can now use mail-in voting methods at some point had to register and be validated in person. Second, remote voting is so high risk and subject to impacting every voter potentially at that we should offset that risk with an in-person registration requirement until it is demonstrated that it not be part of the requirement.

Any time a voter enters the static code, there should be an account lockout and throttling mechanism involved so that an attacker can't guess at the static code until they are successful. All bad guesses should result in a waiting timeout period before the next guess can be made.
