# 76 Hacking Multifactor Authentication

Common types of asymmetric cryptography include Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA), Diffie–Hellman (DH), Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) and ElGamal. RSA is easily the most popularly used asymmetric encryption cipher, accounting for the supermajority of all asymmetric ciphers. Although all asymmetric ciphers are used to perform exchange, Diffie–Hellman (also known as Diffie–Merkle) and Elliptic Curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) are more often associated with exchange-only implementations, instead of being used as asymmetric encryption or authentication alone as RSA. RSA and DH keys typically range from 2,048–4,096 bits and ECDH keys are smaller at 256-bits. Both provide comparative protection.

# Digital Signing

Asymmetric cryptography can also use their key pairs to authenticate and digitally sign content. Digital signing is the act of providing proof that the content is signed and also at the moment of the signing. To sign content, a user uses their private key to “encrypt” the content (or a hash result, covered later in a). We do not call “encryption” since anyone who has the related public key (which theoretically could be the entire world) could decrypt and read it. It cannot be confidential or encrypted if everyone in the world can see it. Instead, we call it digital signing.

Any content signed by the private key can be verified (“decrypted”) only by the related public key. If the content must have been signed by the related private key because the only thing the related public key can “decrypt” is something signed by the private key. Similar processes can be used to authenticate identities involved in cryptographic operations, some of which will be covered later in this chapter. Common digital signature ciphers include Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA) and Elliptic Curve DSA (ECDSA). A message can be encrypted and signed if both protections are needed using the appropriate keys during the associated process (i.e., private keys of the sender to sign, public keys of the receiver to encrypt).

Each party has its own, unique key pair, and only that key can encrypt and decrypt messages between each other, asymmetric cryptography also allows signing and message authentication. Each widely used key pair can be tied to a particular subject, which allows attribution and authentication. Usually, symmetric encryption, asymmetric encryption, and signing is used by more directly involved with and responsible for the authentication components.

# Public Key Infrastructure

In order for asymmetric systems to work with authentication, the people communicating them must have trust that everyone’s public key is valid and belongs to who they think it belongs. In the early days of asymmetric cipher communications, it was enough for one person to send another person they already knew their public key, and the receiving person would trust that the person who sent it to them was the correct, valid person with the correct, valid private key.
