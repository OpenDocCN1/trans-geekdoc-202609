# EMV

A common hardware authentication standard, similar to smartcards, EMV-enabled credit cards. EMV chips look like smartchips because they have a specific application. EMV stands for Europay, Mastercard, and Visa, the three credit card companies that created the standard (although the standard is now managed by a larger group of financial companies known as EMVCo). EMV-enabled credit cards, which do in contact and contactless varies, protect credit card information (which used to be stored on the unprotected magnetic stripe) so that the card cannot easily be duplicated. Before EMV chips were included, a malicious attacker could buy a cheap credit card reader and swipe your credit card to get all the information about the card from the magnetic stripe on back of the card. Physical credit card duplication was rampant.

EMV (also known as pin and pin cards) put some of the most critical information within the secure confines of the EMV chip, where it cannot be easily read or duplicated. EMV credit cards also hold information that helps conduct each transaction it is involved. In today, a credit card magnetic stripe does not contain enough information to completely duplicate the card, although it is possible to get the credit card number and some other credit card holder information. Neither the EMV and magnetic stripe hold the credit card's CVV number (which three- or four-digit code you are often asked to provide when using a credit card remotely or on the Internet). Theoretically, anyone trying to duplicate the card will not be able to get enough information to conduct credit card fraud unless the vendor accepts the limited information found on the magnetic stripe. This goal of EMV has been realized, evidenced by the fact that credit card theft and duplication from physical (or wireless) reading has significantly diminished its since its inception.

One area of contention is that order to use EMV cards in Europe and most non-U.S. countries, users must not insert the card into the EMV chip reader, but also type in a PIN (Something You Know). This requirement has significantly reduced credit card transaction fraud overall, in both physical and remote scenarios. The United States was a latecomer to the EMV scene, adopting the security only in the last half decade or so, and merchants in mass decided not to require the PIN. Because the U.S. has far more credit card-accepting vendors and users than other countries, requiring PINs would cause issues with a non-minor percentage of those users who didn't set up their PINs or forgot them. So, American vendors decided it was fine to require less security as a trade-off for happier users. More on this sort of decision making in the next chapter.
