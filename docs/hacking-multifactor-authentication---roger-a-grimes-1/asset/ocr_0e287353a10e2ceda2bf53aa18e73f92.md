# Your Physical Safety Worth It?

You may ask yourself whether your physical safety is worth the defense you are using. There's a famous computer XKCD cartoon (xkcd.com/538/) where the answer to any fancy, expensive, super-duper computer system is to physically hurt the legitimate user until they give up and let the bad guy in. This is a real consideration for any computer system protecting valuable assets by others.

As covered in the previous chapter, a Malaysian lost his finger because his antiteth system could only be deactivated by his fingerprint. The criminals chopped off his finger and solved the problem. This sort of situation, purported in multiple Hollywood films for years, has come to life in the real world. The thieves have learned how to clone his fingerprint and cloned, resulting in no harm to the end user—but they didn't. They likely didn't have the intelligence or the time, or the additional risk from what it would take to make a 100 percent, accepted-all-the-time, cloned fingerprint. So, they took the easy way and cut off his finger. Easier for them, not so much for the victim. This isn't there any scenario where the victim would have traded his finger for keeping his car, which was likely insured.

A more common example is thieves who hack technology to steal wirelessly protected cars. Today, many cars are protected by keys that contain a wireless activation device, built into the physical keys, that control whether the car can be started and driven away. The thinking is that if the thieves are able to break into the car, it can't be started without the legitimate keys, which contain a wireless chip synced to the car. Today, many cars can be started using cell phones and remote key systems.

In the many old stories and videos on the Internet, such as www.youtube.com/watch?v=bRRRrmEI1Zg, the thieves use to extend the electronic signal from home where the car is parked and the owners sleeping, to the car, which they then start and drive away. I'm assuming they then drive to a safe spot, disable the wireless ignition component and any theft-tracking devices, and quickly disassemble the individual parts.

There is a school of thought that says if we are able to make cars impervious to stealing without the thief getting potentially dangerous criminals into your house, where you and your family members are, to get the device they need to steal the car? When I now interview car vendors who offer wireless starting security options, they tell me that the wireless relay tricks of the past will no longer work on today's newer car models. Today, they say, a thief would need to have the actual authentication token (usually stored in the owner's house) to start the car. Is that a good thing? Is really a security improvement?
