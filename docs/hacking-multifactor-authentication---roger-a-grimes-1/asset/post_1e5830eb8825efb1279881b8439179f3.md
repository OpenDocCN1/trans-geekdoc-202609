# Grab Android MFA Brute-Force

This MFA brute-forcing attack applied to Android cell phones. A white-hat hacker let the vendor, Grab, know about a successful brute-force attack against their (see hackerone.com/reports/202425 and hackerone.com/25000). Grab is a very popular app, similar to Uber, in Southeast Asia. Users can call hail, get food delivered, and so forth.

The hacker discovered Grab's four-digit SMS codes could be guessed at a rate not to exceed six attempts, equating to 8,640 attempts a day. A four-numeric code only has 1000 total possibilities, and the birthday attack makes it likely take fewer attacks. In the hacker's own test, he was successful to guess multiple codes after several hours each.

All of these were found and ethically reported to the vendor by good-guy, white-hat hackers. But it makes you wonder how many other MFA solutions are susceptible to brute-force attacks and how many malicious groups are exploiting them without publicly reporting them to get fixed. Luckily, to stop these types of attacks just takes awareness about them by the developers and some implemented controls.

# Unlimited Biometric Brute-Force

Attacks against biometric MFA solutions will be covered in Chapter 16, "Attacks Against Biometrics," but need to be mentioned here. Many, if not most, biometric solutions allow unlimited brute-forcing attacks and the ones that are not usually verbal.

For example, when I walk up to my fingerprint's entry system I can try over and over using my fingerprint. It will beep and tell me I've failed, but it doesn't lock me out. The same thing with a retina scanner I used at a previous company. I could put my eye onto the retina scanner and try over and over for days, and it would keep me trying. Why? Because shared-metric scanners can't tell the difference between a single person trying over and the next (different) person trying for the first time. They accept all tries as new. For many biometric implementations there is no such thing as "account lockout."

Additionally, most biometric scanners experience tens of false-negative cases, where your fingerprint (or retina or whatever) is read too early as you are placing your finger down on the scanner or moving your finger around to get it “situated” or placed for scanning. The scanner doesn’t know the difference between your finger almost being placed on the scanner and your finger being solidly on the scanner. Instead, it’s scanning over and many times a second just waiting for the right fingerprint image to be placed in the right way.

All biometric scanners are intentionally “de-tuned” to be less accurate. If they were tuned to be as accurate as possible, it would result in too many false negatives due to variations in legitimate fingerprints from normal wear and tear. This de-tuning, along with a lack of rate limiting, opens up the opportunity for an attacker to try many, many biometric attributes it hits or one “sort” of matches a legitimate one. Your fingerprint or retina may be unique in the world, but how it is stored and analyzed is a lot more susceptible to brute-force guessing.

# Defenses Against Brute-Force Attacks

The risk of brute-force attacks is exacerbated by the fact that most MFA solutions require input only numbers 0–9 and have a maximum number of 4-to-6 digit positions. In the regular password authentication world, common rate-limiting and back-attempt lockout controls against brute-force attacks have been the norm for decades—but not in every login portal. For some reason these controls, which have proven their value in the password world, just aren’t seen as important in the MFA world. In fact, brute-force attacks are often ignored until some hacker tries a brute-force attack and tells the vendor. It seems uncommon for a new MFA vendor to implement anti-brute-forcing controls from the very beginning. I don’t know why. Perhaps it is because the MFA vendors and their users think that MFA has mysterious property that makes them far less likely to be hacked using the normal, everyday methods. When the flaws are reported, the vendors, big or little, often take months to close the holes.

Here are some developer and user defenses against brute-force attacks. Most of the controls are developer-focused and the user’s risk is mostly controllable only by the MFA solution vendor.

# Developer Defenses Against Brute-Force Attacks

First, let’s explore developer defenses.

# Enforce a Maximum Number of Guesses in a Particular Time Period

Developers should allow a maximum number of bad attempts in a particular time period before making the attacker wait for a set of time to try again, require an unlock from a most trustworthy service or person, or permanently lock the device. How many attempts and in time period is up to the developer. I will say I was surprised to read that Instagram allowed 250 attempts. That seems insanely high to me, but they must have their reasons. A value of six attempts within minutes as the maximum without causing a lockout seems reasonable to me.

Why would Instagram, or any other vendor, allow hundreds of bad attempts before locking out an account? It may be because of their past experience or simply their personal belief about what the right settings are. For example, for decades Microsoft's default password lockout policy was that five guesses in 30 minutes caused a 30-minute lockout. After 30 minutes without any further password guessing, the counter would be restarted and bad attempts would be set to 0. The recommended defaults (which often varied with the default settings in their products) changed, starting with Windows Vista/2008, to many bad attempts before an account locked out. Today, Microsoft is not recommending a one-size-fits-all default policy for any organization (docs.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/securing/configuring-account-lockout). Why?

I can give you a couple of reasons. Microsoft felt that if an organization's policy was sufficiently strong—requiring at least eight characters and multiple character sets of complexity with a 90-day or shorter expiration period—then an attacker would be unlikely to successfully guess anyone's password before it expired. With a sufficiently strong password policy, it would take a hacker billions of guesses before they would succeed, and even with authentication, the password would be changed before the automated tool guessed anyway.

Account lockouts are used to stop guessing against passwords using online methods. Using online portals that allow the login name and password to be entered and the Enter key or OK clicked to send the login to the authentication system for verification. The rounding process of entering in a particular login name/password combination and waiting for the system to validate, either successfully or with a failure message, normally takes 2-3 seconds in most scenarios. So, automated (online) generators can guess at a single login combination only every 2-3 seconds per portal instance (usually). At 2 seconds per guess, at best would allow a guesser 3,888,000 attempts in a 90-day period with no account lockout or throttling enabled. That's a lot of guessing but far less than what it would take to randomly guess an eight-character, "complex" password (without any usable knowledge or birthday attack). If you hit passwords to get eight characters and not any bigger, you get 95% possible passwords. That's billions of billions, over six quadrillion possible passwords (i.e., 6,634,204,312,890,625). If all was allowed for 26 lowercase (2⁶), it would still be 808,287,056 possible different passwords. This is because of course the entropy issues discussed in Chapter 1 and premature birthday attack collisions. But since it to say, guessing at "complex" passwords at a time using online portal is nontrivial if the passwords are sufficiently "complex."

Second, Microsoft tech support had seen tens thousands of instances where legitimate scripts or tools caused big account lockout problems and operational issues because someone updated a user account password script or tool (as recommended by every password guesser and regulation) did not update it in the script or tool. So, the automated tool was trying to do legitimate, routine task and in the process unintentionally causing account lockout issues across the network.
