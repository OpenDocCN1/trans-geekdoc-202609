## User Defenses to Downgrade/Recovery Attacks

Now we'll look at user defenses to downgrade/recovery attacks.

### Education

Users should be given routine security awareness training to recognize common downgrade/recovery attacks and how to avoid them. The education should include the next three recommended defenses.

### Avoid Personal-Knowledge Questions for Recovery Where Possible

Users should avoid simple personal-knowledge questions whenever possible. I often get prompted to input them, but if I can get around using and answering them, I do.

### Give the Wrong Answers

If forced to use and answer simple personal-knowledge questions and answers for account recovery, give incorrect answers. Treat them like the popular game Jeopardy and must answer them thoroughly and correctly! Lie, seem to feel that they are on the popular game Jeopardy and when forced to use a personal-knowledge recovery password question, type something closer to a password or let the wrong answer. ### Protect Master Codes

If you have to have master codes as backup authentication, securely store them. I store them in my password manager, where I can easily access them on my phone if I need. If it printed out or downloaded, do not store the account identifier (e.g., email address) they are linked in full text along with the codes. That makes it too easy for unauthorized discoverers. At least make the attackers work for it.

## 14 Brute-Force Attacks

This chapter will cover brute-force attacks against the underlying technology of MFA solutions. Some of this material was covered in earlier chapters, but it's worth repeating and expanding on.

### Introduction

Brute-force attacks are considered the most primitive type of cyberattack. They don't require a lot of intelligence. You just attack a target over and over again, slightly changing or incrementing one value until you get success. If you have no defensive controls blocking all the tries, it's the one cyberattack guaranteed to eventually win.

As covered in Chapter 1, "Logon Problems," brute-force guessing passwords is one way to guess passwords. A brute-force guesser, with zero knowledge of the allowed minimum length of a targeted password, would start with a letter, let's say, 'a', and when that didn't work, try the letter b, and so on, until they had tried all the letters, numbers, and symbols in the possible character and come up empty. The password brute-force guesser would try aa, then ab, and then ac, and so on, moving through all the possible characters. Sequentially they would add more characters in each position, trying every possible combination, one at a time, until they found the correct combination of characters that made the targeted password. Not surprising, brute-forcing passwords or anything cyber-related is relatively slow. Computer automated guessing can seem very fast, but compared to more advanced methods, brute force guessing is still comparatively slow.

Most attackers, even guessing at passwords, like to add a little intelligence to their guessing, hoping to be successful. As discussed in Chapter 1, password guessers know that most people form passwords based on their native language and the natural formatting of that language. Certain characters, like the a, e, i, and o, will be used more frequently than others. Usually, most passwords will start with a consonant, followed by a vowel. If they are forced to use uppercase characters, usually it will be an uppercase consonant in the first character position. If they are forced to use numbers, they will usually choose 1 or 2, and it will be at near the end of the password.

Using a little intelligence, attackers find that most passwords (PINs) can be broken faster with fewer tries by using brute-force methods.

But if a defender defends their password with a less popularly character, say q or z, an intelligent password guesser is likely to be slower than an attacker using a brute-force method. The odds of two people sharing the same birthday actually become quite high. It takes 23 people for the odds of two people sharing the same birthday to become over 50%.

### Birthday Attack Method

Attackers using the brute-force method can have great increases in speed by randomly guessing among the total population. Traditional brute-forcing starts at the beginning and sequentially increments one value after another until it solves the problem. By simply randomly guessing among all the possible values, brute-force guessers can be successful. This phenomenon is known as the birthday attack (among other names).

It's widely known that the birthday method works because of simple experiment to demonstrate the logic in a mid-sized group. In the experiment, which is best performed with at least 20 people, each person is asked, one at a time, to announce their birthday (July 19th, September 22nd, etc.), without any. And any time someone guesses a birthday that someone else, the person with the matching birthday announces the match and the experiment ends.
