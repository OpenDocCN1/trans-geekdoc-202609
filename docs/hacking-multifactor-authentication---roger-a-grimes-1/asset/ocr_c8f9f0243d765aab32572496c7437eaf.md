On the opposite end of the identity spectrum you have truly anonymous identities (i.e., *anonymity*), where no holder of an identity account is verified. In fact, it's an intended attribute that the real holder of the identity is never known. Many times, the anonymity simply happens because the requirements of the IDM system are not assured at all. Anyone can create nearly any identity label (within the scope of the acceptable identity label format) and are not required to submit any qualifying information, and thus no submitted information is verified. The vast majority of Internet websites and services fall under this category. But the anonymity is not necessarily guaranteed or near-guaranteed and is an advertised feature of the system—it's a fact that real identity was not guaranteed or the IDM was intentionally leaky.

Other times the anonymity is intentional. The Tor browser ([www.torproject.org](http://www.torproject.org)), for example, was intentionally made to maintain users' total anonymity. There are also ways to track individual Tor users at times, depending on the scenario, but doing so is not intentional.

Total and complete anonymity projects and services are trying to give users as much anonymity as possible. They will not know the identity of the subject or any attribute about them. If they store information about the subject, they will often store it encrypted in such a way that even they, the IDM service, cannot determine the real subject's identity or location. A law enforcement agency could serve a legal warrant to obtain as much information as they could on the participating subjects and gain little to no information.

Total anonymity is seen by many people, including this author, as a human right. If people want total anonymity to conduct particular actions or scenarios, they should be allowed to have it. There are many legitimate reasons to have and allow anonymity, including political protests without fear of political reprisal, whistleblower reporting, and sensitive group meetings, such as for cancer, AIDs, and rape survivor groups.

> NOTE These types of anonymity are not to be confused with generic, built-in anonymous accounts used in some operating systems. For example, Windows has a built-in, hard-coded “anonymous”
