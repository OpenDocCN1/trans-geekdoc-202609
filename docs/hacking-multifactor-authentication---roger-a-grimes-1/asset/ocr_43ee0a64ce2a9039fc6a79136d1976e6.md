# Passwords in Plain View

And sometimes you do not have to guess or phish because passwords are written down somewhere where anyone can see them. Developers are notorious for leaving “hard-coded” passwords left in development code that they upload to a public, shared, repository, like github. Many large data breaches have occurred because of this single issue.

Many devices come with built-in, hard-coded passwords which either can be changed or are not forced to be changed. Either way, anyone can look up these built-in passwords and attempt to use them. Many bot programs run on the Internet looking for these devices and tens of compromised devices try using the built-in passwords.

It's not uncommon to see a public video where even to see password is typed in the video-taped user. Here is a fairly recent 2019 example of a US congressman typing in his cellphone's-password (a bunch of 7's): mashable.com/article/congressman-lance-gooden-has-a-terrible-phone-code.

There have been dozens of sometime news interviewing some interesting person of the day where shared password is written down in the background and clearly seen in the video. We laugh, but it happens all the time. I was recently giving a lecture on password attacks and on the presentation, laptop, in a general conference room in a state legislature that was freely accessible by the general public. On the laptop was a paper sticker label (see Figure 1.8) that showed the name and password. I chuckled, but I was there to speak about computer security and the laptop that I was to present had the password printed for anyone to use and document. I said to the host in a kindly way, “You know that printing out a login name and password placing anywhere one can see is the epitome of what you shouldn't do in computer security. I hope that at least is a stand-alone login and not a networked login that works across your network.” I responded, “If you want to tell yourself that makes yourself feel better, sure.”

I hesitated this specific example in my book I do not want to show that particular customer. They obviously have a real need for the password to be. They've done the cost-benefit analysis for that IT asset and the laptop is in a place where malicious hacking is unlikely, or hopefully, they have the login locked down to that laptop and everything besides presenting was disallowed by default. Of course, I'm just saying to make yourself feel better.

But more importantly, it happens all the time. That customer was far from unique. It points to an inherent issue with passwords and how easy they are to use, and at the same time, how inconvenient they can be for shared assets. I travel for a living, and if I could get a dollar for every hotel check-in lobby desk where I could physically see a documented password from the lobby, I'd have a nice house on the water in the Florida Keys.
