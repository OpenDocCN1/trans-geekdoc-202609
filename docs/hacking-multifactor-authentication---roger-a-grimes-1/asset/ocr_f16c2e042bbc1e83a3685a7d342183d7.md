# Authentication Problems in the Real World

We have all sorts of troubling authentication problems in the real world. Authentication hacks aren't just a problem for the Internet. Our world has long been filled with identity fraudsters who cash checks that aren't authorized, pretend to be people and organizations they aren't, and lead victims to performing actions harmful to themselves. Much of real-world crime is related to authentication issues, and we need to fix those all.

Examples of these real-world authentication attacks attack around and are very common. The most common are the 'fake' from Microsoft and calling to help you with a virus on your computer' phone calls. Just as common are fake calls from the Internal Revenue Service telling the victim that they must pay hundreds or thousands of dollars for past tax returns or they will go to jail. So however they will be happy to take that payment in Walmart gift cards.

As we start to fix the authentication issues in our online world, we also focus on fixing those issues in our real world. We need to update telephone networks so that fraudsters can't fake caller telephone numbers, known as caller ID spoofing. In the United States, the new SHAKEN/STIR (Signature-based Handling of Assorted Information Using to toKENS and the Secure Telephone Identity Revisited) protocols are proposed to minimize or even possibly stop all caller ID spoofing.

But people happen in more places than just over the phone. We need ways to quickly and automatically identify whether someone interacting with me in the real world is who they say they are. It is clear that as we fix the authentication issues in our online world the keys to fixing the authentication problems in the real world will also be found. When we fix one, we can fix the other.
