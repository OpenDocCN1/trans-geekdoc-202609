## How Biometrics Work

Each biometric system has its own strengths and weaknesses, but most work across a general, common set of phases (as graphically represented in Figure 16.5). I'll cover each step in more detail in the following sections.

## Acquisition

Collection of the biometric trait starts the process. If it is the first time someone is being registered to a system for future use, it is part of the enrollment process. Or it can be collected to compare the current subject against previously stored images as a part of identification or authentication. The collection can be unimodal (a biometric trait collected or using only one sensor) or multimodal (multiple sensors and/or multiple types of biometric traits collected together once). The user involved may or not know that the biometric trait is being collected or asked to give permission, such as when a crowd of people walk past a facial identification camera set up in a public area.

> The National Institute for Standards and Technology (NIST) has a document dedicated to the collection of biometric traits: NIST Special Publication 800-76-2 (csrc.nist.gov/publications/detail/sp/800-76-2/final).

## Normalization/Cleanup

Every collection includes preprocessing to clean up the collected trait. Many types of images are "right-aligned" so that comparisons can be made at the same angles and direction. Each biometric system has its own way of determining what the "right" direction and position is based on biometric trait characteristic that is used to center and align the collected.

Unwanted noise and artifacts are rectified and eliminated. You don't want a tiny spec of dust invalidating an entire fingerprint. You don't want shot eyes or eyeglasses invalidating an eye print. You don't want a dark hair curl to invalidate a face. You want faraway background noise or cold voice print. Wanted traits may be enhanced. For example, a submitted fingerprint image often has lighter and darker friction lines. A fingerprint reader will lighten and darken the image and sometimes individual ridges as needed for more of the fingerprint detail to be distinct. Then, the normalized image is submitted for data collection.

## Selection of Data Points

All biometric traits are converted into digital form. Most biometric systems contain algorithms that determine which parts of the submitted biometric trait ultimately be identified, named, manipulated, and stored. For example, a facial recognition reader may use 30,000 separate points of light to capture a face, but the algorithm breaks up the face into only a few hundred points. These are the eyes. These are the lips. This is the nose, and so on. Measurements are made on the individual parts and between the parts. Math is used to create new data points.

## Storage

The biometric images and data points are stored locally in a database located on the same device on which the biometric measurement is made and/or in a remote location. To increase speed, commonly accessed data points may be uploaded to remote device or local memory for faster local processing. Wherever the storage is, it needs to be securely stored on disk, in and during transmission.

## Comparison

When requested, comparisons are made between the newly acquired traits and the stored biometric traits. Comparisons are done during initial enrollment, to make sure the new acquisition doesn't match an existing store, different subject, and during subsequent authentications.

It is always done using data analysis and is not a direct comparison of acquired optical images or sound. For example, when two faces are compared it doesn't done image as a human would analyze it. Instead, it's done using data points and measurements of the new candidate image of the same data points.

Another good is fingerprints. The actual fingerprints, even if stored and available for later retrieval, are not used in the comparison. Collected fingerprints are specific points where the friction ridges begin end, along with identified shapes and artifacts. Structured fingerprints more like a star constellation (e.g., Orion's belt or the Big Dipper) than a picture of a fingerprint. The more points are compared and found to be the same, the more accurate the fingerprint match will likely be. Unfortunately, it is not always possible to collect all that the fingerprint recognition system would collect during acquisition, because people are always placing differing areas of varying points during each fingerprint submission. Why many initial fingerprint collections will ask the submitter to press their fingerprint several different times in different ways—collect as many different data points as they can to better represent the overall fingerprint. A fingerprint collection system is essentially comparing “star constellations” looking for partial overlapping matches where different data points match.

## Authentication

If requested, the system will make a determination of whether someone is authenticated on the preponderance of evidence over the various data points. If successfully authenticated, a person will be allowed to access the protected system or resource. If accessing a digital access control system, as used in an operating system or web, the authenticated user will be assigned an access control token, as you learned in Chapter 6, "Access Control Tokens."

## Problems with Biometric Authentication

Most of the world has come to believe that biometric authentication is the most accurate method there is. Biometric vendors say their biometric identification systems are accurate to 1:100,000,000 and can't be faked. They sound like antivirus industry trying to convince us that their products can successfully detect 100 percent of all malware when the thriving industry over 30 years old proves otherwise.

And it isn't just security newbies who think biometrics are some dare-to-dream, magic, Holy Grail authentication solution. Many computer security people think that too. I've been in the room of supposed-renowned computer security experts determining the future of online and in-person commerce told the room making the next international standards how they could not wait for the near-term magic of biometrics to make fraudulent authentication and transactions finally be a thing of the past. They sound like they've been waiting while their professional careers for biometrics to mature the point that online and computer crime would never be a problem.

They could not be more wrong! Biometrics are not great authenticators for a variety of reasons, including high false error rates and theft of biometric traits causing irreversible damage. There are other bothersome issues as well. Personally, I'm less impressed with biometrics than most other MFA solution methods. Here's why.

## High False Error Rates

Every authentication solution has a certain number of false positives and false negatives. A false positive is an authentication transaction determined to be successful when it should not have been. This is known as a Type II error or false-acceptance rates (FARs) in the biometric world. A false negative is a legitimate authentication denied that should have been. These are known as Type I errors or rejection rates (FRRs) in the biometric world.

If you've ever been in a company that uses biometric scanners, you can probably relate to the experience of having to wait for someone (or yourself) as the biometric scanner incorrectly doesn't accept the validly submitted biometric trait over and over until it finally works as it should done the first time. If you are in that type of work situation, it happens so often that many of us ever cognitively notice it. It's a way of life around biometric authentication devices. We are standing around waiting for legitimate people to get recognized. It happens to us personally if our cell phone has a fingerprint scanner as we try and over placing our finger on a phone scanner... wipe the sensor, wipe your fingertip, and try again, until it finally recognizes and unlocks.

As sensitivity of a biometric system is tuned up or down, these errors start to increase or decrease, often inversely to each other. There is also the crossover rate (CER), where Type I errors equal Type II errors. 16.6 shows this biometric error comparison.

False acceptance rates are clearly dangerous because the system is admitting someone who should not be admitted. That is a safety and security issue. In the password world, it would be like someone submitted the wrong password of finger instead of the correct password of frogger and still the authentication system accepted the incorrect password and authenticated the user. A false rejection in the password world would be like the legitimate user using the correct password of frogger and it still did not work.

In the biometric world, it's also not uncommon for a biometric trait being close enough to the legitimate trait that it is accepted (false acceptance). Perhaps a fingerprint is smeared in a comparison region and the fingerprint reader incorrectly marks it as a friction ridge change or whorl.
