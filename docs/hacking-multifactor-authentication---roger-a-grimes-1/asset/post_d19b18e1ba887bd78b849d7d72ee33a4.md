
## 362 Hacking Multifactor Authentication

We aren't worried about physical mechanical attacks. Wireless attacks can be a problem, too, in the physical category. In the nation-state area, electromagnetic pulse (EMP) attacks have worried the world's governments for over half a century. Most major nations are believed to have EMP weapons, nuclear and otherwise, which will be deployed in any major war to disrupt adversary electronic devices. Large electromagnetic pulses have been proven to permanently disable electronic devices. That means our computers, phones, radios, generators, lights, air conditioning, networks, power grids, cars—anything with an electronic component is thought to be susceptible. It is pretty much nearly everything in our world. The fear is that we would be back to bicycles, spheres, bows & arrows, and guns, if an EMP attack comes. Because of that, most major governments require anti-EMP defenses on their electronics and have national plans to mitigate the threat. There is a recent 2019 story on the Latest EMP defense package (www.forbes.com/sites/airesicolhen/2019/04/05/whitehouse-prepares-to-face-emp-threat).

## Example Physical Attacks

This section shows examples of real-world physical attacks against MFA solutions that haven't already covered in earlier chapters.

### Smartcard Side-Channel Attack

As discussed in previous chapters, a smartcard contains a specialized integrated circuit chip—the small, squish metallic chip (as shown in Figure 17.2). It contains a microprocessor, memory storage areas, an operating system, and distinct areas as "pins." Some of the pins are involved in powering up the chip, and others are data input/output (I/O) interfaces. The chip is the "smart" of the smartcard and is designed to securely protect secrets (which often include an asymmetric key pair, including a private key). It was specifically designed to protect authentication secrets, and in most normal circumstances, it does a pretty job at it.

However, at least since 1998, researchers and smart hackers have known that smartcard chips and the secrets they protect are vulnerable to side-channel attacks, particularly different attacks. Here is a 2002 paper on these attacks: www.cs.uic.edu/~slan/my-papers/ie-messages-proof.pdf. Smart attackers can essentially monitor the power differences between the ground pin and the true ground, and using a resistor and a digital oscilloscope, note voltage differences that equate to 0s and 1s. The key lesson is that even specialized hardware designed to protect authentication secrets can be beaten dozens of ways. It takes awareness of these specialized side-channel attacks and how they are accomplished. Sometimes if an attacker looks hard enough at the microscopic level, they can even defeat those defenses.

## Hacking Multifactor Attack

A typical microscope works using light, which is made of photon particles. When you want to measure very tiny things down to the nanometer scale and smaller, light doesn't work because the photons are bigger than the objects being measured. It would be like to use a yardstick to measure a pea.

For very small things, scientists and researchers use an electron microscope. Electron microscopes, not surprisingly, use electrons. Electrons are up to 100,000 times smaller than photons and can be used to measure microscopic subatomic objects of all types.

A new electron microscope (there are several types) can easily set back $3 million to $5 million, which you can buy in tens of thousands of dollars. Here's one marketplace for electron microscopes: www.electron-microscope.com. Of course, the purchase cost is only part of the cost equation. Electron microscopes consume power to create electron beams and are usually housed in environmentally controlled labs with teams of professional people using and maintaining them. Still, in the United States, many small communities and even high schools (www.hsemgroup.com/high-school/) have access to them. They aren't the rare commodities that nation-states have and use them.

An electron microscope can be used to search for and identify secrets at the molecular level. The first time this was publicly discussed was in 2010 (www.blackhat.com/articles/2010/02/02/black-hat-chip-crack-02010.aspx) by Christopher Tarnovsky. After nine months, using a focused beam electron microscope, Tarnovsky was able to find and retrieve the authentication secrets stored on an Infineon SLE66PE, a microcontroller chip, which carried the secret of Trusted Platform Module (TPM).

TPM chips are designed to prevent the unauthorized retrieval of authentication secrets. Microscope is having a TPM chip to protect critical secrets for all of enterprise levels of Windows hardware. Tarnovsky revealed that anyone with access to an electron microscope could get those secrets. Tarnovsky also found that the TPM chip had anti-tampering technologies built-in and that triggering any one of them would destroy the chip. He ended up destroying more four dozen chips in the process, but he learned how to get at the secrets without destroying the chip. Tarnovsky estimates he has reverse-engineered a thousand chips in his career, and he continues to attack chip protections to this day. He often shares what he does, including this 2019 talk: media.hardware.io/exposed-the-deep-secure-elements-of-smartcards-christopher-tarnovsky.
