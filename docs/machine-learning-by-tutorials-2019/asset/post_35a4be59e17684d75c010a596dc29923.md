
Even with those things going against it, using words still has one overwhelming advantage: Words convey meaning. Computers can't really understand what they read — not yet, anyway — but they can identify semantic relationships between words. The next section points out a few things you'll need to do differently when dealing with word tokens instead of characters, and it introduces a popular way to represent them: embeddings.

## Working with word tokens

Recall that neural networks require numerical inputs. So far, you've been one-hot encoding text to use it, but that essentially means your network sees mostly just zeros. What if you could provide more useful information?

It turns out, you can, with something called word embeddings, or word vectors; you'll see these terms used interchangeably. These are vectors that represent words in some theoretical n-dimensional space, and in the process, capture meaningful information about them. You can use these vectors as inputs to your networks instead of one-hot encodings. This essentially lets you provide information about a word instead of just a Boolean flag indicating the presence of a word.

But what's all that mean?

Several ways to create such embeddings and we won't go into the details here, but they all revolve around the same premise: Words are given positions in some n-dimensional space, and their positions are adjusted a bit time the word is used in the dataset. After processing a large text, words that are used in similar ways end up closer to each other in vector space.

One interesting side effect of this process is that the embeddings seem to encode relationships between words. ## Machine Learning Chapter 16: Natural Language Transformation, Part 2

Don't worry! With these tips and what you've already learned, you're well prepared to build models on your own. Consider it a challenge! We'll assume you're starting with pre-trained embeddings. The first thing you'll need to decide is how many words you want to include in your vocabulary. Just because you have an embedding for a word doesn't mean you'll want to use it in your model. Remember, vocabulary size affects model size, so you'll need to keep things manageable. But there is another reason you might not use all the embeddings: some of them may be garbage. Word embeddings are trained in an unsupervised manner with huge datasets scraped from the internet, not uncommon for bad tokens to slip through. For example, there are millions of tokens in the embeddings you can download at https://fasttext.cc/docs/crawl.html. These tokens like “English” and “Portuguese” would just take up space in your model without serving any useful purpose.

Important: The more embeddings you include, the larger your model will be. It's common to choose a few tens of the most commonly used words, but whatever you do, try to limit to a reasonable set for your task. An important step when dealing with word tokens is tokenizing your text into words. You've got different choices for how to do this. For example, the English contraction “don't” could make the tokens "don" and "'t". The first two options are the ones you're most likely to come across, but whatever you choose, you need to ensure a few things:

1. If you're using pre-trained embeddings, make sure you create tokens in the same way as the creators of the embeddings did, otherwise you'll end up with more OOV tokens than you should have. For example, if you parse the word “don't” as the token 't', but the pre-trained embeddings contain the tokens "don" and "'t", you'll end up treating every “don't” you encounter as an OOV token.
2. Tokenize in your iOS code the same way you do in Python. The Natural Language framework's NLTTokenizer doesn't let you configure its tokenization settings like you can with Python packages like nltk, so you have to write your own tokenization logic to produce the tokens your model expects.

![](img/5720be5c3cca252e1244e68d2_546_0.png)

For your special START and STOP tokens, you should add words to your vocabulary you know appear in the data, e.g., <START> and <STOP>. You'll need to deal with OOV quite differently too. You can't just remove them like you did with the characters, so you'll replace them with a special token, such as <UNK>.

You learned in the first NLP chapter that it's possible to tag text with parts of speech. Instead of using a single <UNK> token for all OOV tokens, you may get better performance if you replace words with part-of-speech-specific tokens, such as <UNK_NOUN>, <UNK_VERB>, etc. This gives the model additional context that might help when translating sequences that contain OOV tokens.

Once you've loaded your vocabulary of pre-trained embeddings, you'll need to add embeddings for the OOV token(s) too. Assuming your embeddings are in a NumPy array called `token_vectors`, and you have an OOV token in the variable `unk_token`, you could add a random embedding like this:

```python
token_vectors[unk_token] = 2 * np.random.rand(embedding_dim).astype(np.float32) - 1
```

This assigns an <UNK> token embedding filled with embedding_dim values in the range [-1,1). That's not necessarily the best range to use — you might want to check the pre-trained embeddings you've got to see the range of values you're already dealing with — but the point of using random values is to keep your OOV tokens from being too similar to any other words in the vocabulary.

You could try various options for the embeddings for your unknown token(s). For example, take an average of all or some of noun embeddings for an UNK_NOUN token, an average of verb embeddings for an UNK_VERB token, etc. It's unclear whether that would be helpful, but trying different ideas is part of the function of machine learning, right?

You'd identify which tokens are OOV by checking the tokens in your training set against your word embeddings. That is, if your training set contains a token that does not exist in your pre-trained embeddings, you'd remove that term from your training.
