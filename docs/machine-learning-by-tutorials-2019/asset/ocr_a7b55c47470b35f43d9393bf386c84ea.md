Because the data contained fewer samples of women or racial minorities, the model might be 90% accurate overall, but only 50% accurate for women or minorities.

Also, the data might be biased by the people who made the decisions in the first place: Loan officials might have been more tolerant of late payments from white men, or university professors might have been biased against certain demographics.

You can try to overcome bias in your model by explicitly adjusting your training data or parameters to counteract biases. Some model architectures can be tweaked to identify sensitive features and remove their effect on predictions.

# Explainable/interpretable/transparent AI

The algorithmic bias problem means it's important to examine how an ML model makes predictions: Which features did it use? Is it accurate or fair?

In the diagram earlier in this chapter, training was drawn as a black box. Although you'll learn something about what happens inside that box, many deep learning models are so complex, experts can't explain individual predictions.

One approach could be to require more transparency about algorithmic biases and what the model designer did to overcome them.

Google Brain has an open source github://github.com/google/svcca that can be used to interpret what a neural network is learning.
