# Chapter 12: Training a Model for Sequence Classification

Order and grouping matter — that's what makes them sequences! For example, if you're counting by — two, four, six, eight — and then randomly shuffle data — eight, two, six, four —you've lost the sequence and now the data is meaningless. Or worse, you may have accidentally reordered them into a sequence with a different meaning — eight, six, four, two —now the sequence counts by twos!

So the first rule for training with sequences: keep samples related to individual sequences grouped together and in order. Any shuffling or sampling you do should account into these groupings.

> Note: There can be situations where relationships exist between images in datasets meant to train classifiers, but those usually indicate mistakes that you should avoid. For example, if some images are identical or nearly so, as is common when dealing with large numbers of images, having some in the training set and in validation or test sets may mislead you into thinking your model generalizes better than it does. It's a tricky situation, because sometimes your in-production model will encounter examples that are nearly identical to those while training. For example, consider a model meant to identify product images from the internet — it's unlikely that you'll manage to create a good training set without also including some of the very images meant to recognize. But in general, to keep training and test sets as separate as possible, while realizing there are going to be times when some similarity sneaks in.

There's a second potential concern, relating to the sources of the sequences. Consider the case you've been working on this chapter — gesture recognition. There's certainly some variation each time you perform a gesture — after all, the app collects several floating point values from multiple sensors, many times per second, so basically impossible to get two identical recordings.

Different recordings of one person making a gesture are going to be similar to each other. That's not entirely bad — that similarity you want the model to recognize. You may find it easy to train a model that recognizes gestures from a specific person — it may not require many training examples. But it might not work as well when you use the model with someone else.
