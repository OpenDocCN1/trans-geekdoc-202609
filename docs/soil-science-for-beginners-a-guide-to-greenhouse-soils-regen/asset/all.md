# Introduction

John always had a green thumb, or so he thought. For years, he'd tend to his garden with such care, watering and weeding where he could and getting rid of pests as soon as he noticed something amiss.

For a long time, his garden provided fresh produce to feed his entire family. However, there came a time when it seemed like no matter what John did, he would have the same disappointing results. His lush, vibrant plants appeared to wither right before his eyes, neither water nor fertilizer seemed to help. It was a frustrating cycle, and it made him question his gardening abilities.

Little did John know that the root of his problem lay beneath the surface. The soil in his garden was the cause of this issue. But do you know who the main culprit was? It was John himself! He had been using the resources of his garden soil for so long, yet he failed to do what he was supposed to do in return. John discovered that his soil was compacted, nutrient-depleted, and teeming with harmful pathogens. It was only after he made this discovery and took some remedial measures that his gardening journey took a much different turn.

This is not the story of one gardener; the same happens to so many gardening enthusiasts around the world. Reason: They fail to understand the basics of soil science, the crucial functions that soil performs to maintain the balance of our ecosystem, and what we can do to ensure the long-term vitality of this indispensable resource.

## Why Soil Matters

Soil, which is often overlooked, is the foundation upon which plant life thrives. It's so much more than just dirt; it's a complex ecosystem packed with microscopic organisms that work endlessly to support plant life.

### The Key Role of Soil

Nutrient supply: Soil provides essential nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. These are all things that plants need to grow. These nutrients are slowly released as organic matter decomposes, which then gives a steady nutrient supply to your plants.

Water retention and aeration: Soil is a natural sponge, absorbing and retaining water. It also allows oxygen to get to your plant roots, allowing them to breathe. The structure of the soil, with the balance of air and water, is crucial for healthy, productive plant growth.

### Ecosystem Services of Healthy Soil

Natural carbon sink: Healthy soil with high organic matter content is a carbon sink, which can take carbon dioxide out of the air and store the carbon within. This helps mitigate climate change by reducing the carbon present in the atmosphere.

Water filtration: Soil naturally filters water, removing pollutants and preventing them from getting into water bodies. This helps maintain our clean and healthy water sources.

Biodiversity: Soil houses a diverse range of organisms like bacteria, fungi, and insects. These lifeforms play key roles in nutrient cycling, decomposition, and pollination.

### Food Security and Soil Health

Healthy soil is key if you want to have sustainable food production. This dirt provides all the nutrients and support plants need to grow and produce highly abundant yields. This is why, as growers, we need to focus on practices like crop rotation, cover cropping, and composting. This helps us keep our soil healthy and ensures we have plenty of food for several growing seasons.

Healthy soil can do that and much more, thus keeping our environment clean, our ecosystems maintained, and our food abundant and nutritious. The question is what do we do in return for all of these favors? Sadly, the stats related to this are not encouraging. According to the United Nations, approximately 40% of the world's soils are moderately or severely degraded. Moreover, if the current trend continues, this number could rise to 90% (Ong, 2023).

Is there a way to control that? Absolutely yes! The answer lies in soil management practices that ensure soil resources are replenished at the same pace at which they are being utilized. These methods are more commonly known as sustainable agricultural practices. The power of these practices needs to be understood. They can be a win-win for us, the environment, biodiversity, and the soil, as they ensure that soil quality is maintained and improved over time and the loss of soil nutrients is prevented. This, in turn, ensures that the soil remains productive and can offer all the benefits we have discussed above.

The popular agricultural practices that incorporate principles of sustainability are greenhouse farming, regenerative no-till farming, container and raised-bed gardening, and composting. We'll look into each one of these in this guide.

●  Greenhouse farming practices employ enclosed structures to provide optimal growth conditions for the plants.

●  Regenerative no-till farming  is the practice that ensures minimal soil disturbance and allows the forces of nature to rebuild and maintain soil health.

●      Container gardening involves the use of containers and pots to hold the soil and provide favorable conditions for the plants. A slightly similar concept to this is that of raised-bed gardening where the "soil bed" is raised a few meters above the ground.

●  Composting is another commonly used method in the world of sustainable farming. It involves the conversion of organic waste into nutrient-rich compost that can be fed to the soil to keep it nutritious and fertile.

This guide has been created to inform you about the basics of soil management practices that are relevant to all of these farming methods. The ultimate goal is to allow you to gain the most out of your growing spaces in a sustainable manner. Each farming practice has been discussed separately, with the basic growth principles, soil management practices, and tricks to enhance soil fertility elaborated on.

Once you finish this guide, you will understand that soil is much more than just dirt. By treating it as a valuable resource, you can significantly enhance your gardening results. My years of experience as a successful gardener have shown me that soil is a resilient entity, capable of thriving even in less-than-ideal conditions. However, when you start to manage it intelligently and provide it with exactly what it needs, your production can increase exponentially.

This book is the second installment in my series, where I share how I maintain healthy, rich, and thriving soil in various areas of my garden, including my greenhouse, raised beds, container gardens, and non-till areas. Through my soil management practices, I have been able to provide fresh produce for my family over the past few years, and I want to help you achieve the same success.

It's time to dive in—let's get our hands wet and muddy.

# Chapter 1:  
Understanding Soil Basics

When you step onto soil, you are stepping onto one of the most incredible forces on the planet. This powerhouse sustains life on Earth. While it's not as majestic as the trees that tower overhead or the mighty water bodies that make up most of the planet, soil is the humble substance that lies at our feet. It's often overlooked, but it really is the foundation of our ecosystems, the provider of food, and the regulator of our climate.

This unsung hero of our planet plays a role that's just as critical as the sun and rain. It is a complex ecosystem that is teeming with billions of tiny microorganisms working endlessly to support plant life. They break down organic matter, cycle nutrients, and keep our soil fertile. Without all these microscopic workers, our planet would look more like a barren wasteland. But why do you need to know any of this? It's because if you are going to work with soil, you have to understand soil.

In this chapter, we are going to uncover so much information about the world of soil. You will explore its composition, properties, and functions. You'll unlock secrets about soil formation, understand how nutrients keep this age-old dance going, and recognize the balance of microorganisms that make our soil an incredible resource. When you learn the fundamentals of soil, you gain a true appreciation of its importance and take the steps needed to protect what you have.

## What Is Soil?

Dictionaries generally define soil as the firm land or the upper layer of earth that may be dug or plowed and in which plants grow (Merriam-Webster n.d.). The U.S. Department of Agriculture (n.d.) defines it as a natural body comprised of solids (minerals and organic matter), liquid, and gases that occurs on the land surface, occupies space, and is characterized by one or both of the following: horizons or layers that are distinguishable from the initial material as a result of additions, losses, transfers, and transformations of energy and matter or the ability to support rooted plants in a natural environment.

Typically, people refer to the loose, dark-colored substrate of the earth as soil. However, scientists define soil based on three criteria: it must contain organic matter, be located within the upper few tens of centimeters of the surface, and be capable of supporting plant growth.

### Composition

This seemingly simple and homogenous substance is more complex than it appears. The diversity in soil and the varying properties among different soils require thorough evaluation. So, let's do that.

If I were to express the composition of the soil in a simple formula—although it is not that simple—it would be represented like this:

#### Weathered rocks + Organic matter + Water + Air = Soil

Let's see how each of these components enters the process of soil formation, how it gets locked in, and how much time it actually requires to convert an active mixture of these components into rich, fertile soil.

Weathered Rocks

Weathering refers to the breakdown of rocks and minerals by the forces of nature—water, air, and living organisms. This is a continuous process that has been occurring since the earth's surface formed. The pieces of rocks and minerals that are formed as a result are then transported to the place where the soil formation finally begins. Since rocks are the first component of the soil, they are often referred to as the parent material. The number of rocks and minerals in a soil sample can vary, but it is around 45% in an average soil sample (Panchuk, 2019).

Organic Matter

A general term used for soil's organic components is humus. We usually think of organic matter as the dead, decaying matter but that's not what organic matter truly is. I like to define organic matter as a mix of live, dead, and very dead entities. The living organisms in the soil are the most active component, followed by freshly dead organisms, and then those that have long been dead and thoroughly decomposed. Live organisms in the soil include bacteria, viruses, fungi, algae, and protozoa. The dead organisms include recently deceased earthworms, insects, microorganisms, old plant roots, crop residues, and recently added manures. The very dead organic matter in the soil refers to the components that have been present in the soil for a long time but microorganisms have failed to decompose completely. This form of organic matter resides in the soil for hundreds of years and releases essential nutrients into the soil in a gradual manner.

Although only 1–6% of soil comprises organic matter (Panchuk, 2019), this small amount plays a crucial role in soil fertility.

Air

The empty spaces in the soil comprise up to 51% of its volume (Panchuk, 2019). These voids are filled with air and water to varying degrees, and the air present is commonly referred to as the soil atmosphere. Proper aeration of the soil is essential, as it facilitates the exchanges that occur between plant roots and soil particles. Additionally, the chemical activity of soil microbes also relies on adequate aeration.

Water

Water is yet another vital component of soil, and it plays a role in determining the soil texture, water content, supporting microorganism activity, and plant growth. As mentioned earlier, water resides in the air pockets present in the soil. So, the larger these pockets, the more water is retained within the soil and the easier it is for the plants and microorganisms to access it.

### Soil Profile and Soil Horizons

The next concept to explore in order to understand the basics of soil is the soil profile. A soil profile is the vertical section of soil that extends from the surface down to the underlying rock. You can think of it like cutting a cake and examining its layers to see how many layers there are, which one is the thickest, and how distinct each layer is. A soil profile reveals several parallel layers of soil, which we refer to as soil horizons. You might have even conducted experiments in your school for soil profiling. This experiment involves collecting soil samples at different depths from a single location. The samples are then examined for variations in color and particle size. Students also create soil profile models by layering each sample in a transparent jar, one on top of the other.

Soil scientists commonly refer to three layers or horizons: A, B, and C. On top of these horizons is a Horizon O that can sometimes get buried too. There may also be an additional layer called the E-horizon between the A and B horizons.

Let's take a closer look at the different soil horizons.

●       O-Horizon: This is often found on the top of all layers. Its components include dried grasses, dead leaves, fallen tree parts, surface organisms, and other organic entities. The presence of all of these components gives this horizon a dark brown color.

●       A-Horizon: This is the surface soil or topsoil. It contains a mix of organisms like earthworms, bacteria, and fungi along with minerals, water, air, and other organic components. The soil in this horizon is particularly soft and porous. All of these conditions provide a perfect environment for the seeds to germinate.

●       E-Horizon: The "E" stands for eluviation. Eluviation is the process by which water moves from one layer of soil to another, carrying dissolved nutrients and minerals along with it. This explains the formation of this horizon. You guessed it right! The trickling of water—and nutrients—down from horizons O and A creates horizon E.

●       B-Horizon: This is also known as the subsoil or subsurface horizon because it is present below the topsoil. The amount of humus and other organic compounds in this layer is comparatively less than that in the topsoil, which is why it is slightly harder than A-horizon. However, mineral salts and metal compounds are found abundantly in this layer, which is why the color of this layer is light to medium brown.

●          C-Horizon: This layer is known as parent material (discussed under weathered rocks) and is devoid of any organic matter. It is made up of large pieces of bedrock.

Typically, the A-horizon is where the majority of plant roots thrive. This is due to the characteristics of this horizon, which support various growth stages of a plant, from seed germination to root and shoot development. The O-horizon replenishes the organic content as the plant matures. Additionally, the B-horizon gradually transfers essential minerals and metallic compounds to the A-horizon, meeting the plants' needs for these vital components to support their growth.

### Soil Properties

Our next focus in the basics of soil science will be on soil properties. These properties are crucial as they determine soil fertility, nutrient availability, and suitability for various agricultural uses. Let’s examine the major soil properties, including texture, structure, and pH.

#### Texture

When we talk about soil's texture, we are actually referring to its mineral components or the particles that make soil. There are three types of particles: sand, silt, and clay. The relative of each of these particles in a soil sample ultimately decides the soil's texture. The difference between these particles lies in their sizes.

●   Sand: It has a particle size of 2.0–0.05 mm and a low surface area. The coarse particles of sand give a gritty feel to the soil. A common test used to observe the texture of each of these particles is the ribbon test, in which a moist lump of these particles is pressed between the thumb and forefinger to determine whether it can be formed into a thin ribbon-shaped structure. Compared to the other two types of particles, sand has the lowest ability to form a thin ribbon. If you perform this test for sand, you will notice that the sand particles do not stick well to each other and crumble as soon as they are pressed beyond a certain point.

●     Silt: It has a particle size of 0.05–0.002 mm, giving these particles more surface area than sand particles but less than clay. In the case of silt, the ribbon formed is short, weak, and has a powdery feel to it. If you place a small amount of silt between your teeth, you'll feel it has a gritty texture.

●   Clay:  It  has  a  particle  size of 0.002 mm or less, meaning that clay offers the highest surface area compared to sand and silt. Clay is the easiest to distinguish among the three. This is due to its colloidal properties and sticky nature. It performs the best in the ribbon test where it readily forms a long, thin ribbon. When placed between the teeth, clay gives a smooth slick feeling.

Various combinations of these three soil particles can give us 12 classes of soil textures (USDA, n.d.-b). Due to the varying amounts of particles in each of these classes, we observe different water retention capacities, nutrient-holding capacities, and soil porosities. For instance, soils with higher ratios of clay and silt have higher water retention than sandy soils. Conversely, sandy soils are effective at absorbing moisture from the surface because their porosity (size and number of pores) is high. Nutrient management is another factor that is influenced by soil texture. This refers to how well the soil can hold onto nutrients and supply them efficiently to the plants that need those nutrients the most. Generally, the smaller soil particles (silt and clay) perform this function better. This is because these particles offer more surface area for the nutrients to adhere to. These nutrients are then transferred to the plant roots.

It is also interesting to note that soil texture changes with depth. Based on these criteria, soils are classified into one of these categories:

●  Uniform:    These  soils  have  uniform texture throughout the soil profile.

●    Texture contrast:   These soils exhibit drastic texture variations between topsoil and subsoil.

●      Gradational:  These soils have a gradual increase in texture down the profile.

It is always good to know what soil texture you are working with and how consistent it is down the profile.

#### Structure

The next concept that we will be exploring is soil structure. This is another important soil property that can help you understand the qualities of the soil you are working with.

Soil structure refers to the way soil particles (sand, silt, and clay) combine to form aggregates. In the world of soil science, these aggregates are known as peds. Depending upon the bonding of these particles (and organic matter), the peds formed can be very strong, large, and poorly structured or very weak, small, and well structured. But these are the two extremes; we cannot expect soils with these aggregates to perform well. Good soil always lies somewhere in between these extremes. It forms medium-sized peds (usually 1–60 mm) that have a well-defined shape (Queensland Government, 2013a).

After reading the section about soil texture, you can easily comprehend what role soil particles play in determining structure. Imagine a soil sample rich in sand and silt. Even if this soil has good organic content, we cannot expect it to have a good soil structure. Similarly, if the soil has a high clay content, the aggregates formed will be too hard and difficult to work with.

In addition to the size and structure of peds, the shape of peds can serve as the most distinguishing criterion for assessing soil structure. Soil technicians typically utilize four shapes for this purpose (FAO, n.d.):

●     Granular: These are weak aggregates of individual mineral particles and organic matter. Such aggregates are found commonly in the A-horizon.

●   Columnar: These aggregates are arranged in vertical columns that are separated by small vertical cracks. Columnar structures are common in the B-horizon where clay is present in a higher percentage.

●    Blocky:     These structures are formed when soil    particles are arranged to form a square or slightly angular geometry. They are abundant in the B-horizon of the soil.

●   Platy: Platy structures are created when soil particles aggregate into flat plates or sheets. These plates are often stacked parallel to one another. Platy structures are commonly found in the A-horizon of soil.

#### The Link Between Soil Structure and Porosity

Now, you must be wondering what the characteristics of soil peds have to do with plant growth. This is important because this factor alone decides how much water and air your plant is going to get and with what ease. In the world of soil science, all of this discussion is covered under the head of soil porosity.

You might already have a general understanding of the term porosity, but in the context of soil, it specifically refers to the spaces within the soil that are filled with air or water. These spaces can be found both within and between soil aggregates. Porosity is closely linked to soil structure, as the arrangement of the aggregates determines the amount of empty space between them and the effectiveness with which air and water can move through the soil. So, you can look at the structure of aggregates to determine the porosity of your soil.

●    Soils with granular structures have high porosity and, therefore, allow an efficient movement of air and water in all directions.

●    Soils with columnar structures have low porosity and facilitate poor water or air movement. This is because the aggregates are long, tightly packed, and have small cracks between them.

●   Like columnar structures, blocky structures have low porosity and offer limited penetration and water movement, especially when the size of the blocks is large and they have few cracks between them.

●    Platy structures make soils with the lowest porosity. This is because when plates are layered on top of each other, there is virtually no space left between them to allow for air and water movement.

### pH and Its Importance

pH is a topic that comes under discussion when we are dealing with solutions, but it is also a pertinent subject when it comes to determining the best soil to grow plants.

Soil pH is the measure of acidity or alkalinity of soil. Every soil has a natural pH value, which is influenced by the parent material involved in its formation. Additionally, factors such as climate, vegetation, and the area's physical characteristics can change the pH over time. Therefore, measuring the pH before you begin can provide valuable information about the suitability of the soil for plant growth. What else can soil pH tell you about the soil you are working with? It can

●    tell you about the nutrient availability of the soil. This is because the pH determines which nutrients dissolve in soil water and how much of them are absorbed by plant roots. Some nutrients require a mildly acidic pH to become available to plants, while others need a slightly alkaline pH. However, most nutrients show good availability when the pH is close to neutral (5.5–7.5). Strongly acidic or alkaline pH levels can be disastrous for plants because, at such pH levels, soils can develop nutrient toxicity or nutrient deficiency.

●    help you determine which soil amendments need to be added to make the soil suitable for your plants. This is especially important if you are planning to work with problematic soil (types of soil that have a high ratio of metals or salts).

●     help you understand the long-term impact of your agricultural practices on soil health. Most agricultural activities have the potential to alter soil pH over time. Moreover, the crops that are planted and the soil amendments that are applied to promote plant growth can also change soil pH.

#### pH Determination and Amendment

Determining soil pH is not difficult. There are a number of ways to determine the pH of your soil; however, the easiest and most convenient method is to use a field test kit. The pH field kits come with the required materials and a user manual with easy-to-follow instructions.

One way to check pH is by using an indicator solution. All you need for this method is to collect a soil sample and add an indicator solution to it to create a thick paste. This paste is then covered with a powder of barium sulfate. Barium sulfate changes color upon contact with the soil paste. The color is then matched with the indicator color strip to determine the pH value. Another method of pH measurement uses a portable pH meter. All you need to do is collect a moist sample of soil and dip the electrode of the meter into it. The meter displays a stable pH reading after some time. Both of these methods are not as accurate as readings taken in a lab, but they can serve as good indicators of your soil’s overall health.

Once you have determined soil pH and noticed that it is not within the desired range, you can employ amendments to modify it. When it comes to pH modification, there are several options available:

●  One of the most straightforward solutions for adjusting soil pH is using fertilizers. Sulfur-containing fertilizers and ammonium-containing fertilizers are the go-to choices for many who are dealing with high pH values and want to lower them to a normal range. Conversely, lime-based fertilizers are used to modify soils that exhibit low pH values.

●    In addition  to  fertilizers,  directly  applying minerals and mineral-based compounds can yield significant results. For example, lime or dolomite can be used to raise soil pH to the desired level. Baking soda can also serve this purpose. To lower soil pH, you can directly apply elemental sulfur or builder's lime.

●  There are also organic options available. Wood ashes, ground eggshells, and ground oyster shells, which contain up to 39% calcium, can be used to increase soil pH. To decrease soil pH, you might consider using pine needles or peat moss. While these organic methods may not produce immediate results, their natural composition makes them a desirable choice over synthetic alternatives.

Soils that have a high clay content or significant organic matter tend to be resistant to changes in pH. As a result, they require larger and more frequent applications of amendments. Additionally, the moisture content of the soil can affect how quickly a pH amendment is absorbed. Therefore, it is advisable to obtain a professional estimate to determine the correct amount of amendment needed for your soil unless you are an experienced gardener or agriculturist yourself.

### Soil Functions

We touched upon the essential functions that soil performs for the maintenance of entire ecosystems. Next, let’s discuss some of its functions that are pertinent to plant growth. Now that you have grasped the essentials of soil science, including soil texture, structure, and porosity, you will be able to understand how soil properties support these functions.

#### Water Retention and Drainage

In our discussion about soil texture and structure, we examined how soil particles are key factors in determining soil porosity. The size and aggregation pattern of these particles dictate the number of pores present between the particles and within adjacent soil aggregates. These pores play a crucial role in influencing both the water retention and drainage capacity of the soil. Regardless of the type of plant you intend to grow, a general indicator of healthy soil is that it should be well-drained while also retaining enough water for a sufficient duration. This allows plant roots to absorb the necessary moisture for growth.

From our discussion about soil porosity, you can easily deduce the water retention and drainage capacities of clay soil and sandy soil. Clay soils exhibit high water retention but have poor drainage capacity. Such soils may be suitable only for water-intensive crops like rice. Conversely, sandy soils exhibit low water retention capacity but excellent drainage. These soils are ideal for growing plants with strong root systems and those that thrive in dry conditions (for example, radishes, carrots, and beets). In addition to these types of soils, there is loamy soil, which contains equal proportions of sand, silt, and clay. Loamy soil is considered the best for most plants because it offers medium water retention and drainage.

It is important to note that these soil properties also influence our irrigation practices. If the soil has high water retention, you might not need to water it too often; however, soil with poor retention (and high drainage) will require more frequent watering. This is one of the crucial concepts that you need to consider when planning to create a sustainable garden.

#### Nutrient Supply and Storage

The role of soil pH and soil water in providing nutrients to plant roots was discussed briefly before. Now, let’s discuss the concept in detail. The important questions to consider here are how and from where the soil acquires these nutrients, how these nutrients are retained within the soil, and what the transfer process is from soil to plant roots.

All plants require a diverse range of nutrients to reach their full potential. These nutrients are broadly classified into two categories: macronutrients and micronutrients, based on the amounts needed by the plant. Soil fertility is closely linked to these nutrients. It refers to the abundance of nutrients, water, and air in the soil, as well as their transfer to plant roots through a combination of biological, physical, and chemical processes.

The process of nutrient storage and supply in the soil is complex, involving various organisms such as bacteria, fungi, and protozoa, which play crucial roles. This process includes long cycles of breaking down minerals and organic matter until the simplest and most active forms of these nutrients, known as ions, are produced. As plant roots grow deeper into the soil, they can access these ions. Soil water dissolves the ions and facilitates their movement from the soil air to the root hairs of the plants.

In short, the players that determine the fertility level of soil include the following:

●    Parent material—determines the natural nutrient content of the soil.

●     Organic matter—hold on to the nutrients until they become accessible to the plants.

●     Soil organisms—help extract the nutrients from organic matter.

●   pH—determines which nutrients are available to the plants.

●      Soil water—solubilizes the nutrients to make them accessible to the roots.

●   Fertilizers—replenish  soil  nutrients and improve fertility.

The extraction and supply of nutrients is not a unidirectional process; It is a cyclic one where nutrients are continually moved around between the soil, living organisms, and the atmosphere. Recall the nutrient cycles of carbon and nitrogen that we studied in school. These cycles occur all the time, thus maintaining the balance of nutrients between living and non-living components of the earth. For the part where these nutrients are residing within the land, soil plays the most important role in the breakage, simplification, and transport of these nutrients to living things and the atmosphere, thus maintaining the continuity of nutrient cycles.

#### Support for Plant Roots

Imagine a large tree with an extensive root network but without any soil surrounding it—just empty space. Do you think it could stand upright in such a condition? Certainly not. The soil is what anchors the roots firmly into the ground, providing essential support for the tree to stand tall for years or even decades. In this case, soil texture and fertility play significant roles. A favorable soil texture offers the roots of a tree enough accessible spaces to anchor themselves. Once in these pores, roots can exert greater force to penetrate the soil aggregates, allowing them to grow in all directions.

However, physical support is not all that soil provides to the roots. The soil-root interaction is much more dynamic than that. This interaction occurs between plant roots and the soil surrounding them. The name for this special area of interaction is the rhizosphere. The soil assists in the growth and elongation of the roots, as mentioned above, while the roots modify the soil by releasing specialized chemicals (exudates) to solubilize nutrients and create physical channels to access water. Microorganisms are most active in this zone and play a monumental role in maintaining the soil-root interaction for optimal plant nutrition and growth. Due to this interaction, plants are able to maintain their integrity even during times of stress, such as droughts, salinity, and metal toxicity.

Soil is a wonder in and of itself, capable of performing so many miracles with such precision and accuracy that it sustains life on Earth. To appreciate these roles of soil and use soil to optimize agricultural gains, understanding the basics of soil science is crucial, and that is exactly what we have done in this chapter. You are now better equipped to understand the specifics of soil management associated with common farming practices.

## Characteristics of Greenhouse Media

The selection of suitable growth media (or growth substrate) is the most important task when setting up a greenhouse. A quick question: Why can’t we just use regular soil for greenhouse plants? Growing plants in a limited space differs from growing them in a limitless patch of soil, where roots have access to all layers of soil to obtain nutrients and water. Moreover, topsoil is generally easily compacted, so if you use such soil in greenhouse pots, you will suffocate your plants in the long run. Thus, even if you want to utilize your local soil for this purpose, you will need to prepare and modify it to make it suitable for a greenhouse.

The characteristics that we need to look for in a suitable greenhouse media are high water retention, the ability to hold nutrients and actively exchange them with plant roots, a capacity for good drainage, and a good level of resistance to pests and diseases. Other factors that need to be considered while choosing a growing medium are the cost, availability, and requirements of the plants one wishes to grow.

### Soil Mixes vs. Soilless Media

The growing media for greenhouses is divided into two main categories: soil-mix and soilless media.

If you opt for using soil mix, you can simply procure soil from your locality and prepare it to suit your greenhouse conditions. A lab test for soil quality will tell you about its pH and nutrient content. You can also check the soil for its drainage capacity. Once that's done, you can make simple modifications such as

●   adding soil additives like vermiculite or perlite to improve drainage and aeration.

●     adding pH amendments to get the desired pH.

●  adding nutrient charge to improve nutrient exchange capacity.

●     adding compost to improve organic content.

●    adjusting  the ratio  of  sand,  clay, or silt to get the desired water retention and drainage.

#### Advantages of Soil Mixes

The following is a list of the advantages of a soil mix:

●       Low cost

●       Availability of the basic nutrients required for normal plant growth and development

●       Availability of beneficial microorganisms

●    Ability to adapt to the requirements of different crops

#### Disadvantages of Soil Mixes

The following is a list of the disadvantages of soil-based media:

●      They need disinfection to eliminate pathogens and weed seeds.

●      They may contain harmful soil pathogens that can ruin your plant and your entire setup.

●   The drainage and aeration may be difficult to adjust.

●   Compared  to  soilless  media,  this  media  can be difficult to move and transfer from one location to another.

●    Maintaining consistent media quality can be difficult in different seasons.

Alternatively, many agriculturists prefer soilless media, which, as the name suggests, does not contain any soil. Instead, this media comprises a variety of organic and inorganic components, each contributing unique qualities. The organic components include wood fiber, coconut coir, bark, peat moss, and rice hulls. The inorganic components consist of pumice, perlite, sand, vermiculite, and hydrogel. Hydroponics is a special type of liquid-based soilless media. These substrates can be used individually or mixed together to achieve a medium with desired properties.

Given the numerous options available, selecting the right media can be challenging, especially for first-time growers. However, a careful analysis of the characteristics of each component can aid you in choosing the best option for your setup. To avoid any confusion, let us first define the qualities of a good soilless substrate. According to Canna Research (2015), good quality soilless media has

●  a low bulk density to facilitate installation and transportation. Bulk density is a measure of compaction and an indicator of how much mass there is per unit volume.

●    a high porosity—between 50% and 85%.

●   a  homogenous  composition   that   allows   good drainage but also has high water and nutrient retention.

●  a slightly acidic pH, which can also be adjusted easily.

●     a low content of soluble salts.

●    sterility to prevent the growth of pests and pathogens.

●   a chemically stable substrate that shouldn't react with or hinder the movement of nutrient molecules.

●    longevity so that it can be used for several successive cultivation cycles.

●   ability to recover from growers' mistakes such as overwatering or overfertilization.

#### Advantages of Soilless Media

Considering the qualities of soilless media, we can infer their advantages:

●       They are free from harmful soil pests and pathogens, so the chances of viruses and pest attacks are extremely low.

●     The chemical stability (inertness) of the media allows for the maintenance of homogenous growth conditions for a long time.

●     The porosity and drainage are suited to the plant's requirements.

●    High water retention means fewer irrigation sessions are required.

●    They  allow  for  the cultivation of plants that are impossible to grow in local soil.

●       They require no extra labor for activities like tilling and fumigation.

●    In hydroponic systems, issues like water loss and nutrient leaching are completely eliminated.

●     These media allow better control over pH, ion exchange capacity, and nutrient retention.

#### Disadvantages of Soilless Media

Although soilless media offers several advantages over soil-based media, we cannot ignore its obvious downsides.

●    Balancing water retention with drainage capacity can be a difficult task. Most soilless media are so well-drained that they lose water rapidly and need frequent irrigation sessions.

●   Nutrients   are exhausted at a rapid pace, which then necessitates the addition of fertilizers or a total replacement of the media.

●      Media can become hydrophobic (water-repellent) if left dry for too long.

●    Some of these media (like hydroponics and hydrogels) can be costly and require trained personnel to install them.

●  Diseases   and  pests  that  survive in the soilless media can easily spread within the entire system.

To overcome these issues, several components of soilless media are combined to create a mixture that possesses the maximum number of desired traits and is cost-effective, long-lasting, and stable for supporting the growth of various plants.

### The Importance of Aeration and Drainage

The discussion of the characteristics of greenhouses cannot be complete without mentioning the importance of aeration and drainage. Both of these are perhaps the most important determinants of the success of a greenhouse.

Aeration allows plant roots to exchange gases, which is necessary for energy production in plant cells and the overall health of the plants. Aeration also prevents the media particles from getting compacted over time, thus allowing adequate space for roots to grow. Beneficial microorganisms also thrive in well-aerated soils. Without adequate aeration, plants can wilt, and roots can start rotting leading to stunted growth.

Drainage prevents waterlogging and salt accumulation issues. Additionally, good drainage allows better temperature regulation within the greenhouse. Without adequate drainage, media particles can get compacted and retain more heat, thereby stressing the plants and affecting their growth.

Obviously, these issues are not the primary concern in an in-ground plantation, as the soil is inherently capable of performing these functions on its own; factors like tilling and crop cover can also assist with these functions. In a greenhouse setup, a grower must attend to these aspects. So, how can we achieve and maintain good aeration and drainage capacity in a greenhouse media? Well, there are a couple of things that you can do to achieve that. For instance, you can

●       use a soil mix that has equal quantities of sand, clay, soil, and organic matter to improve aeration.

●    use containers that have drainage holes at the bottom.

●     use gravel and coarse sand to raise in-ground beds—both of these materials can help you create raised beds with suitable aeration and drainage capacities.

●    avoid overwatering and ensure water doesn't pool around plants.

●   use water-efficient irrigation methods like drip irrigation or sprinkler heads that prevent chances of overwatering and waterlogging.

●    add organic matter like well-rotted manure.

●     create small slopes on the greenhouse floor to assist drainage.

●       incorporate gravel at the bottom of the containers/beds to ensure efficient drainage.

●       use materials like perlite or vermiculite to enhance the aeration of the media.

●    periodically till with a gardening fork to enhance aeration especially if you are using soil media.

## Choosing the Right Medium

Selecting the right growing media for a greenhouse is crucial for ensuring success and maximizing yield. To make this selection process easier, it’s important to understand the different options available, along with their advantages and disadvantages, as well as possible combinations. So, let’s explore the various substrate options. However, if you find the selection process challenging, consider using a pre-mixed media offered by many commercial growers. Alternatively, you can experiment with two or more components to create your own custom growing medium. Don’t worry; we’ll also discuss a simple recipe for an ideal growing medium that you can follow.

### Components and Their Properties

As I mentioned earlier, there are two broad categories of these components: inorganic and organic. Let's first discuss a few inorganic options.

#### Vermiculite

Vermiculite is a mineral rich in iron, magnesium, and aluminum. Its texture is similar to that of mica. It is available in small worm-shaped structures. Properties that make vermiculite a desirable option for greenhouse media are that it

●   is lightweight, which makes handling and transporting it easy.

●    has a high ion exchange capacity, which makes it a good medium that gradually releases nutrients to plants.

●      has chemical stability, which means that it doesn't react with other media components.

●      is highly resistant to pH changes.

●       has high water absorption (3–4 gal per cubic feet).

The only shortcoming of vermiculite is its poor performance upon wetting. Once wet, the particles are easy to compress and collapse under little pressure. Moreover, this damage is permanent, and particles do not recover even after completely drying.

#### Perlite

Perlite is a silica-based mineral that is extracted from volcanic rocks and then exposed to high temperatures to convert it into a crushed powder consistency suitable for growth media. Its desirable qualities are that it

●       is lightweight

●       has a uniform consistency

●    has high porosity that ensures drainage and aeration

●       has high water retention

●       is chemically stable

●       has a neutral pH level

Due to its powdery nature, perlite tends to flow on top of water during irrigation. Additionally, it has been shown to contain toxic levels of fluorine, which is not a desirable component in growth mediums. For these reasons, people prefer to use it as an amendment to improve drainage and aeration and in media mixes, rather than using it as the sole media ingredient.

#### Calcined Clay

This media component is created by heating clay minerals at extremely high temperatures. The particles obtained after this procedure are extremely hardened and indestructible. This makes calcinated clay

●       highly porous

●       well-drained

●       highly aerated

These properties make calcinated clay a desirable amendment and potting substrate. It is also a preferred alternative to perlite. The only downside to using calcined clay is its high water demand.

#### Sand

Sand is the most common and inexpensive inorganic media component. As discussed in the previous chapter, sand has a coarse texture and a highly porous nature. These properties can make sand

●       well-drained

●       well-aerated

●   good at providing mechanical support to plant roots.

Remember, we are referring to the large and medium-grain sand here. Fine textured sand doesn't offer these properties and can even reduce drainage and aeration of the media. An issue with sand, especially the form derived from limestone, is its high pH levels. However, this won't be a problem if you are growing plants that have been adapted to local soils and can tolerate high pH levels. The low cost of sand also makes it the most common media amendment.

#### Polystyrene Chips

These chips are the by-products of polystyrene processing. It is

●       inexpensive

●       lightweight

●       well-drained

●       well-aerated

●       resistant to decomposition

It can be used as an alternative to sand. The drawbacks of using polystyrene are extremely low water retention and limited ion exchange capacity. Polystyrene chips cannot tolerate high temperatures and the chemicals present in disinfecting agents.

#### Rockwool

Rockwool is a unique media component that makes an ideal substrate for hydroponic systems. It is obtained by spinning molten rocks into thin fibers. The material obtained as a result has

●       high water retention

●       excellent aeration

●       a uniform texture

●       chemical stability

Rockwool is also resistant to pests and pathogens. Moreover, it is reusable after thorough cleaning. The main drawback is its low cation exchange capacity and requirement for pH stabilization.

When it comes to organic substrates, we have the following options:

#### Peat Moss

Peat refers to plant debris that decomposes in humid conditions in the absence of oxygen to form peat moss. Not all peat deposits are suitable for media usage, it actually depends on the type of plant that has caused the accumulation of peat deposits and the degree of decomposition that it has gone through. There are several kinds of peats, but the distinct ones are Sedge, Hypnaceae, Sphagnum, and Muck. Out of all these options, Sphagnum moss is the most preferred option. According to Aggie Horticulture (n.d.), it has

●       an absorption power of 10–20 times its weight

●       a good aeration level

●       resistance to decay

●       moderate cation exchange capacity

●       no salts

Sphagnum moss fiber length can be adjusted for different plants and various growth stages.

#### Wood Residues

Like peat moss, wood residues is a big category with several available options. Its availability and price is not an issue because it is a by-product of the lumber industry. Let's take a look at the main options here.

●     Sawdust: The price and characteristics of sawdust depend on the tree from which it is derived. It has low bulk density and similar water retention to that of Sphagnum peat moss. High amounts of cellulose and lignin are a downside of using sawdust as a medium because these components make the media prone to nutrient depletion.

●   Leaf Mold: Leaf mold is prepared by composting soil with the leaves of oak, maple, or sycamore for 12–18 months (Aggie Horticulture, n.d.). The product obtained as a result has excellent water holding capacity, aeration, and drainage.

●      Barks: The barks that are commonly obtained from the lumber and paper industries are of varying textures and sizes, but a simple hammer milling and screening provides the pieces that are suitable for growth media. The physical properties of this media are similar to that of Sphagnum peat moss.

The biggest problem with wood residues is nitrogen depletion by microorganisms. The process can rob the plants of much-needed nitrogen. However, this problem can be avoided by employing frequent applications of supplemental nitrogen.

#### Coconut Coir

Coconut coir is derived from the husk of coconuts and is available in various forms, including compressed dust, fibers, and chunks. When wetted, it can expand up to five times its original volume (Buechel, 2014). The total porosity of coconut coir is around 80%, and it has a moderate to low cation exchange capacity (Buechel, 2014). One of the main advantages of coconut coir is its durability; it can be reused for several growing cycles if properly sterilized, as long as it doesn't start to decompose.

However, coconut coir does have some drawbacks. It is naturally high in salts, so it is recommended to use a quick leaching protocol to remove excess potassium and chloride before use. Additionally, coir tends to have a slightly acidic pH level, which means it should be mixed with some lime for optimal results.

#### Rice Hulls

Rice hulls are the main by-product of rice processing. The primary properties of rice hulls are their

●       large particle size

●       high porosity

●       high drainage capacity

●       resistance to decomposition

Rice hulls are an excellent choice for growing media because they enhance drainage and porosity. If you have a medium that has become compacted over time, adding rice hulls can immediately improve its porosity and drainage. However, it's important to note that media mixed with rice hulls may experience nitrogen depletion.

#### Urea Formaldehyde

The properties of urea formaldehyde foams are comparable to that of inorganic polystyrene chips. The water holding capacity, aeration, and drainage are similar to polystyrene. A single application of this amendment can provide a year-long supply of nitrogen. Another desirable trait of urea formaldehyde is its light weight, which makes transportation and handling very easy. The only issue is that it can release formaldehyde, which is harmful to the environment. However, it has been noted that formaldehyde emissions are higher in areas with elevated temperatures. In regions that experience frequent rainfall, as well as in greenhouses that are regularly irrigated, it can be used without significant concerns about its release.

#### Animal Manure Compost

Animal manure compost is certainly not the best option; however, it can be added as an amendment in small amounts. The trick is to compost animal manure for a long period with straws or wood chips. This gets rid of the odor and water content. But even with well-aged animal manure, there is always the danger of nutrient overload, salt damage, and nitrogen damage. Moreover, it needs pasteurization and fumigation to remove pests, weeds, seeds, and nematodes.

Due to the variety of components available, selecting and combining the most appropriate ones can be challenging, particularly for first-time growers. However, if you take the time to study the properties of these components and understand your requirements for greenhouse media, the selection process can become much easier. Here are some tips to get an ideal media composition:

●    Select components from both categories—organic and inorganic—because they complement each other very well.

●    If you are mixing a component that has an acidic pH, make sure to add some lime as well.

●   If  your  media components are prone to causing nitrogen depletion, ensure that you add some nitrogen supplements.

●  Ensure that your final media has good physical characteristics so that it can hold water and nutrients and resist pH and temperature changes.

●       If you suspect that a component needs sterilization or fumigation, get it done immediately; you cannot risk ruining your entire media.

●     Test the pH and wettability before use.

●   Thoroughly mix the components to achieve even growth throughout the substrate. Uneven mixing can result in a wavy plant appearance due to irregular growth. Gentle mixing is recommended if you are using vermiculite or other slow-releasing fertilizers.

●   Your media should be lightweight, easy to transport, and easy to work with.

●     Do not allow mediums that contain a high amount of peat moss to dry out.

●    Store fully prepared media in closed bags to avoid contamination.

### Choosing a Medium Based on Plants' Requirements

So far, we have discussed how we can obtain a growth medium that fits the general definition of "suitable greenhouse media." However, there is one additional factor that must be kept in mind while selecting or preparing a growth medium: The requirements of the plants you are planning to grow in it. Now the DIY media recipes you get online or the pre-mixed media you purchase may vary in components and their relative percentages; however, there are general compositions that you can refer to when you are looking for media for different plant growth stages and plant families. Some recommended compositions by Sela (2024) are as follows:

#### Seed Starter Mix

It can be made using coconut coir and perlite in a 1:1 ratio. In this mix, coconut coir imparts high water retention and provides a neutral pH level, both of which are crucial for germination and early root development. Perlite maintains aeration and drainage, thus preventing seed rot.

#### Potting Mix for Container Gardening

It can be made by mixing 60% peat moss with 40% perlite. In this mix, peat moss imparts high water retention and provides moisture. Perlite maintains aeration and drainage. This mix is suitable for growing flowers, vegetables, and herbs.

#### Growth Mix for Succulents

This medium is suitable for growing succulent plants (such as aloe vera, asparagus, and dragon fruit) or others that need well-drained soils to thrive. Both of these components act together to provide excellent aeration and drainage to the growth media.

#### Hydroponic Mix

This mix is suitable for growing leafy greens and herbs. It can be made using 70% rockwool and 30% perlite. These two ingredients provide perfect control over moisture and drainage levels, which is exactly what a hydroponic system needs.

#### General Soil Amendment Mix

This medium is a perfect soil amendment for improving drainage and aeration, decreasing compaction, and supporting root health. It can be made by mixing peat moss and red ash in a 1:1 ratio.

## Managing Soil Health in Greenhouses

Once a greenhouse system is up and running, there are two main factors that you must manage to ensure that the medium remains healthy and that you achieve the maximum yield from your greenhouse: Managing nutrients and preventing pests, as well as controlling diseases. These two factors alone can determine the success of your greenhouse.

### Nutrient Management

In in-ground plants, the root system is deep and extensive, allowing it to easily access the nutrients present in the soil. In greenhouses, however, the roots are limited to the substrate and can only access the nutrients that are added to it. Since nutrients are provided in the form of nutrient solutions mixed with irrigation water, several considerations must be kept in mind to ensure that the nutrients and water provided to the plants are well-balanced and precisely matched to plants’ needs.

Here's a list of steps that will help you monitor and manage the nutrient levels and chemistry of your greenhouse media:

#### Monitoring Growth and Soil Health

Before using methods that involve lab tests and complex equipment, you can start by using basic observation to assess the health of your media. Look for the physical characteristics of growth media and check whether the texture and aggregate structure are maintained after irrigation sessions. Additionally, observe the health of your plants; symptoms such as yellowing leaves, stunted growth, and discoloration are clear indicators that there may be issues with the nutrient mix you are using.

#### Media Sampling

If you notice something unusual about your growth media and want it tested, you will need to obtain a medium sample. It is essential to get a test done as soon as any odd signs appear, as detecting and addressing the issue can take 2–4 weeks (Zhang et al., 2021). Additionally, it is advisable to use the same lab for testing each time to ensure consistent results. Before taking a medium sample, you can consult the lab for instructions on sample collection. Sample collection protocol may vary between labs. However, the general guidelines include the following:

●     Avoid taking samples immediately after irrigation or fertilizer application; it is recommended to wait at least one week.

●  Collect separate samples from plants exhibiting both normal and abnormal growth, as this comparison can aid in diagnosing the problem.

●    If slow-release fertilizers are present, they should be removed as much as possible to prevent the overestimation of fertility.

●     Samples should be taken from the root zone or the bottom of the media, as salts and nutrients are typically higher in the upper layers.

●    Provide the lab with all relevant details, including the condition of the roots, the growth stage of the plants, and the information on the fertilizer. This will help them better detect any toxicities or deficiencies in your media.

Even if everything appears to be going well, it is recommended to get a lab test once halfway through the production cycle.

#### Media Testing

Although there are pH meters and electrical conductivity (EC) meters that can allow you to measure these parameters independently, getting a soil test from a reputable lab is going to provide you with the most reliable values of pH, EC, soluble salts, macronutrients, and micronutrients. Most labs perform these tests on nutrient solutions that are extracted from the submitted samples.

#### Interpreting Results

Some labs offer consultations with their experts to help you with your nutrient management strategies, but most of the time, the results are accompanied by a result interpretation fact sheet designed to help growers interpret their media reports. The interpretation of these results may not seem like an important task, but it is actually the most crucial step in nutrient management. Remember, the interpretation fact sheet of one lab is specific to its own test results and you cannot use this fact sheet to interpret the results from another lab.

A typical report lists normal ranges for every parameter along with your sample values. You can compare your values to the normal ranges to determine what is wrong. A distorted pH value is the first sign of nutrient imbalance. EC and individual nutrient values that are above normal indicate nutrient toxicity and over-fertilization. If the values are lower than normal, it indicates a nutrient deficiency.

#### Adjusting Nutrient Levels

When it comes to managing nutrient deficiencies and toxicities, there are a number of strategies you can employ. Let’s take a look at a few of them.

●   If  there  is  a deficiency of nutrients, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, or sulfur, it can be easily remedied by applying a post-plant fertilizer. These nutrients are administered in the form of liquid fertilizer.

●      Nutrient toxicity can also be addressed by leaching out the excess nutrients and then applying a nutrient solution containing only the desired nutrients.

●      Sometimes, nutrient toxicity may be caused by the water you are using. Filtering the water through procedures like reverse osmosis can be helpful in this situation.

●     If there is a toxicity or deficiency of a micronutrient, adjusting the pH level of the growing media to the desirable range (5.5–6.5) can rectify the issue. The imbalance of these nutrients occurs because drastic pH level changes can render micronutrients insoluble or excessively soluble; therefore, these issues can be resolved by stabilizing the pH level within a normal range.

●       Lastly, fertilizers are an important tool for addressing nutrient deficiencies. If you already have a fertilization program, consider making adjustments by incorporating the nutrients that your soil lacks. You can also use nutrient mixes, such as the soluble trace element mix or STEM, to correct minor nutrient deficiencies.

### Disease and Pest Control

In a closed environment where each step, from media selection to structural design, is carefully implemented to avoid problems like soil-borne diseases and pests, such issues can still arise. However, they are less common and not as severe as in other cultivation practices.

The environment, the placement of the growth beds, and even the chemistry of the substrate play a crucial role in preventing the spread of soil-borne diseases and pets. Moreover, there are some crucial steps that growers can take in the post-plantation phase to ensure the safety of plants from diseases and pet attacks. The following are examples of some steps that can be taken:

●   Ensure that the humidity within the greenhouse can be regulated to prevent the conditions that are conducive to the growth of pathogens.

●      Ensure that the tools used in the greenhouse can be disinfected from time to time to prevent the spread of disease if there is any.

●   Ensure   that irrigation patterns are adjustable so foliage can stay dry during the night.

●      Ensure that in-ground soil beds can be treated with steam to kill disease-causing organisms.

There are several preventative practices like these that can minimize the chances of diseases in your greenhouse; however, when it comes to the active management of cultivated greenhouse soils, the ability to identify the early signs of diseases and pest attacks is the most important step on the grower's part.

For this, your knowledge of the common greenhouse pests and diseases should be good enough. You should be able to differentiate between different symptoms and know the best possible remedies to tackle the problem. Common diseases in the greenhouses are Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Phytophthora, and Thielaviopsis, which can cause problems like root rot, blight, and stem browning.

Pests that are common in greenhouses are whiteflies, spider mites, aphids, and thrips. You should be able to identify these pests and diseases in a timely manner and apply suitable classes of fungicides, bactericides, and pesticides as soon as possible. The method and schedule of application should be adjusted in a way that the problematic agent is not only eliminated but any future attacks are also prevented.

An oft-discussed program for pest management in soil science is integrated pest management (IPM). It is a holistic approach to pest management that takes into account both the environmental aspects as well as human health. It aims to prevent, eliminate, and reduce pests using nature-based methods. The four-tiered system of IPM consists of the following:

1.  Identification and monitoring of pests
    
2.  Setting of action thresholds
    
3.  Prevention
    
4.  Control
    

Encouraging pest predatory species, growing a variety of plants, using botanical extracts to repel or kill pests, and using physical traps or barriers to prevent the entry of pests are all a part of IPM.

The topics we have discussed so far highlight the crucial role of soil management in the success of a greenhouse. This chapter has addressed various aspects of greenhouse soil management, including media selection, nutrient management, and pest and disease management. While these topics are not exhaustive, they provide a solid foundation for understanding the unique characteristics and challenges associated with greenhouse soils. By grasping these concepts, growers can implement targeted strategies to maintain soil health effectively.

Regenerative No-Till Farming

What will you imagine if I ask you to picture a field ready for sowing? Most probably, a freshly tilled ground with an uneven and somewhat rough surface and visible furrow will come to your mind. Well, that's because it is how we have done farming for so long without even thinking about the possible outcomes of this supposedly harmless act. We have done this without realizing that through this act we are exposing the soil and the life residing within it to the brutal forces of the atmosphere. Over time, this practice has caused significant damage to the topsoil. However, there has been a recent increase in interest in no-till farming, which has led to both research and advocacy from soil experts and sustainability advocates.

In the realm of agriculture, regenerative no-till farming stands out as a leading sustainable practice. This method not only produces high crop yields but also nurtures the health of the soil. ## Principles of Regenerative Agriculture

Regenerative agriculture is a multifaceted concept, but its basic definition can be understood by understanding the practice of no-till farming. No-till agriculture is a method that avoids turning or disturbing the soil, allowing nature to handle much of the farming process. Essentially, this means steering clear of any practices that damage or disrupt the topsoil.

In the absence of tilling practices, organic matter is what keeps the soil fertile. When a crop is harvested, its waste and residues are left on the ground where they eventually decompose, forming a thick layer of organic components. This organic matter gradually becomes part of the soil, improving its texture and water-holding capacity and enhancing the natural soil ecosystem. Ultimately, this process creates exceptionally fertile soil for the next farming sessions. This is just one aspect of regenerative farming. There is much more to this incredible concept that makes soil management easier, more cost-effective, and more productive for growers; we will explore these aspects later in the chapter.

The following are the main principles of regenerative farming that differentiate it from conventional farming practices.

### Minimum Soil Disturbance

The most prominent aspect of regenerative farming is ensuring minimum soil disturbance. This concept has been derived from ancient farming practices in which farmers used a stick to make holes in the soil, placed seeds in them, and covered the seeds with the same stick. In the past, however, this was the standard practice because they were unaware of other methods to assist crop production. The recent popularization of the no-till idea was prompted by research that revealed the repercussions of tilling, as well as events like the Dust Bowl that eroded much of the topsoil in America and Canada.

The idea of regenerative farming is centered on the belief that minimizing soil disturbance will allow natural soil chemistry to thrive, thereby facilitating the regeneration of the soil damaged by our agricultural practices over the years. Minimizing soil disturbance does not only mean limiting physical agents that disrupt the soil; it also encompasses the minimal use of chemical and biological agents. Essentially, the concept is to restrict all kinds of practices, such as

●      use of machinery like tillers and ploughs

●      overgrazing

●     application of chemical fertilizers and every other soil application that can alter soil chemistry and the profile of the organisms living in it

●   continuous cultivation of the same type of crops that can strip the soil of essential nutrients

### Use of Crop Covers

You can think of this principle as an add-on aspect of regenerative farming. The main principle (mentioned earlier) focuses on preserving the indigenous soil chemistry, while this one aims to replenish the nutrients in the soil and protect it from undesirable physical, chemical, and biological agents. The idea is to never leave agricultural land without living plants. To implement this principle, an alternating planting of the main crop and cover crop is used. When one crop is replaced by another, the residues from the previous crop are left on the ground. These residues cover the soil and act as a shield for the soil surface. This shield protects the soil against weathering and erosion. Over time, these residues decompose, adding a significant amount of organic matter to the soil, thereby reducing the need for additional organic matter. The continuous presence of living roots within the soil also provides an ideal environment for beneficial soil microbes and worms to thrive. The maintenance of living roots is another key principle that deserves a separate mention, so we will address that later.

In the long run, crop cover improves soil structure by

●       regulating the pore structure.

●       enhancing aggregate formation.

●       promoting the movement of water within the soil.

### Maintenance of Living Roots

Before diving into the basics of this principle, it's important to understand a concept called carbon sequestration. This is the process through which atmospheric carbon is captured and stored in living organisms and the soil in various forms. Several natural processes continuously sequester atmospheric carbon, and biological sequestration is just one of them. Living plants utilize carbon during photosynthesis to create food for themselves. However, when these plants are no longer present, the soil breaks down organic matter and releases carbon back into the atmosphere.

Regenerative agriculture employs this form of sequestration to combat global warming. The idea is that the more plants and active roots there are, the more carbon dioxide they can sequester and store in the soil. Additionally, having living roots in the soil means that there's an active community of microorganisms that aid in carbon sequestration and nutrient cycling. This principle is put into practice through the use of cover crops and by ensuring that land is not left fallow at any time during the year. Due to this aspect of regenerative farming, it is often referred to as carbon farming. The roots also help hold soil particles together, thus preventing soil erosion.

Furthermore, living roots support the formation of a communication network between plants, soil, and microbes. This network positively influences soil nitrogen fixation, nutrient retention and supply, and the stress tolerance of agricultural soil. We'll explore this communication network further later in this chapter. Overall, maintaining living roots within the soil serves several important functions, including the following:

●       Minimizing erosion

●       Sequestering carbon

●       Building up organic matter

●       Mixing nutrients within the soil

●  Protecting against pathogens, pests, and other stresses

### Use of Livestock

At this point, you must be wondering if there is anything (of course other than tilling) that can be done in regenerative farming to assist with nutrient cycling and the dispersal of organic matter. Well, there is. It can be accomplished through the intelligent use of livestock.

While restricting overgrazing is one of the crucial aims of regenerative agriculture, a carefully planned system of grazing sessions can still be implemented in this approach. This can be achieved by using short, high-impact grazing sessions followed by long recovery periods. The successful application of this principle hinges on the careful selection of timing, intensity, frequency, and duration for these sessions. Clearly, this system is not applicable to all modalities of farming, as livestock cannot be utilized everywhere. However, the use of animal-based manures in the form of slurry can still be beneficial, as it can be considered a contribution of livestock to regenerative agriculture.

Prominent benefits of well-planned mob grazing sessions include the following:

●       Stimulation of plant growth

●       Increased soil fertility

●       Increased activity of insects and microbes

●       Better carbon sequestration within the soil

### Reliance on Beneficial Soil Organisms

Regenerative agriculture relies on beneficial soil microbes such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, and protozoa to support soil health. These microbes contribute in numerous ways. First, they enhance nutrient cycling by decomposing organic matter at a rapid pace and thoroughly mixing it with the soil. Second, they improve soil texture and increase its water retention. Additionally, the toxic waste that plants release into the soil is also broken down by these microbes. Lastly, the competitive environment created by these microbes makes it nearly impossible for pathogenic species to thrive, thereby eliminating the threat of disease development. Can you see how ingenious that is? When beneficial soil microbes perform so much heavy lifting to ensure soil health, there is no need for tilling or the application of chemical fertilizers and pesticides.

Regenerative agriculture can support the growth of these beneficial microbial species via several strategies such as

●      cover cropping

●      mixed cropping

●      no-till farming

●      use of composts

●       use of indigenously sourced microbe-friendly solutions

### Maximization of Crop Diversity

This principle focuses on maximizing community diversity to promote the diversity of beneficial microbes living within the soil, manage pests, and prevent nutrient deficiencies that may occur from the prolonged planting of single crops for too long. This diversity also decreases the occurrence of pests and diseases. To implement this principle, various regenerative practices such as cover cropping, rotational cropping, and companion cropping can be utilized. Multispecies grazing can also be employed to promote diversity.

What does crop diversity do for regenerative agriculture?

●     It boosts the ecosystem function because different plant species perform different functions in it.

●   It  reduces  the chances of crop damage that can occur due to drought and floods.

●      It boosts soil fertility.

●    It boosts the nutrient cycling and water retention function of the soil. Research reveals that compared to monocrops, the diversity of crops lowers disease risk by 73% (Singh et al., 2023).

All of these principles of regenerative agriculture work toward the creation of an ecosystem where

●      soil health is maintained.

●     the rate of nutrient replenishment is almost equal to the rate at which they are being utilized.

●      the soil's chemistry is preserved and maintained.

●   the  microbial  communities residing within the soil are protected and actively used to maintain soil health.

●     the crops grown are free from the effects of chemical fertilizers.

●   the crop production is turned into a simple and cost-effective process for the growers.

However, the biggest advantage of regenerative agriculture is that it works for climate remediation. It is aimed at lowering the carbon burden from the atmosphere and enhancing land use efficiency, resulting in higher yields from minimum efforts. The soil that results from continued use of these practices is more fertile and resilient. It is able to withstand extreme climate events like floods, droughts, and heatwaves leading to better quality yield and higher food security for everyone.

## Benefits of No-Till Farming

Let's understand how no-till farming works and how is it a better farming method than conventional farming practices.

### Preserved Soil Structure

When we are using no-till farming, we do not use any procedure that disturbs the soil's natural composition. As we have discussed at the start of this book, soil formation is a complex and slow process that takes a lot of time to reach its best version. In scientific terms, we refer to this as soil succession. When soil reaches its highest succession level, it has a suitable ratio of bacteria and fungi. Moreover, it is the best one texture-wise. Certainly, we wouldn't want to disrupt this succession level, but this is exactly what happens with tilling. No-till farming resolves this problem by preserving soil with its inherent properties.

### Reduced Risk of Soil Erosion

This is by far the biggest benefit that no-till farming has to offer. If we convert all of our agricultural practices to no-till farming, we can recover the topsoil that we have lost due to rapid industrialization, urbanization, and climate change. One study has found that if the Midwestern US entirely adopts low-till or no-till farming practices, topsoil erosion can be decreased by 95% within the next century (Advancing Earth and Space Sciences, 2023).

### Enhanced Biodiversity

No-till farming when combined with other sustainable practices like crop cover and mulching provides the soil a stable environment where beneficial bacteria, fungal species, and insects like ants, spiders, beetles, and earthworms are able to thrive. Research and studies have proven that good soil management practices using a combination of no-till farming and habitat and crop diversification significantly impact the preservation of soil biodiversity (Mamabolo et al., 2024).

### Better Water Retention

Water loss via evaporation and run-off are common in agricultural lands. No-till farming provides an easy solution to both of these problems. A lack of tilling keeps the soil aggregates together meaning that the loss of water that would normally occur is prevented. Also, when the surface structure of the soil is kept intact and covered with plant residues from the previous crop, the loss via evaporation is minimized. A recent study has revealed that no-till farming of rice, which uses direct seeding, saves up to 15% of the water compared to the conventional water-seeding method (California Rice News, 2024).

### Savings on Investment

Growers can also obtain significant savings by resorting to no-till farming. Think about the bucks that can be saved on plowing equipment, fossil fuel inputs, and labor costs. Sophisticated machinery can be employed instead of planting seeds on untilled soil. According to estimates by Tadjiev et al. (2023), no-till farming can reduce a household's production cost by around 15%.

### Climate Remediation

No soil does carbon sequestration like soil that is used for no-till farming. Since this soil is always swarming with nutrient-cycling microbes and living roots, it is always fixing and storing carbon. This solution has the potential to reduce atmospheric carbon ratios and alleviate our climate issues. According to Lal et al. (n.d.), adopting soil management practices like no-till farming and cover cropping in the US alone can lead to the sequestration of around 83–270 million metric tons of carbon per year. This means that around 650 million metric tons of CO2 can be removed from the atmosphere each year.

The no-till farming method also enables the soil to replenish its nutrient content (via cover crops and green manures), which minimizes the need to apply chemical fertilizers. This aspect of no-till farming has the potential to reduce water pollution.

## Implementing No-Till Techniques

After learning about the benefits of no-till farming, you must be interested in trying it. However, you might also be wondering how easy it is to transition from traditional tilling to a no-till method. You may have questions about how long it takes to fully implement this method, whether you will need specialized equipment, and what are the steps to get started. In this section, we will address all of these questions.

First, the implementation of the no-till method can begin in your very next cropping season. In the meantime, you can prepare your soil and manage your existing vegetation in a way that will make them suitable for no-till farming. I recommend making a gradual transition by incorporating one element at a time. This approach will help you achieve optimal results and tackle potential challenges one by one. As far as the equipment and machinery are concerned, you will need to acquire a few items specifically designed for this purpose. I recommend purchasing second-hand equipment; however, if you prefer to buy everything new, that's okay too. This may feel like a significant investment, but you will be able to recover this cost in the future through higher yields and savings on fuel and labor. Additionally, if you are making a complete transition to no-till farming, you can sell your tilling equipment and use those funds to acquire no-till equipment. No matter what you do, rest assured that you will gain immensely from this initial investment, and your soil will thank you for making this transition.

#### Step 1: Accessing Soil Chemistry and Texture

Just like with any other method of farming, you will begin with a soil health check. This will include checking its pH and nutrient profile. This assessment will guide your future steps to maintain soil health. More importantly, you will need to evaluate the degree of compaction. If your soil is extremely compacted, you’ll need to aerate it first. While this may not seem like the best way to start no-till farming, if there is an immediate need for aeration, you can use a low-impact tilling tool or machine for that purpose. Remember, this step is only necessary if you believe your soil is excessively compacted.

#### Step 2: Adding Organic Content

This is where no-till farming truly begins. At this point, you will focus on adding organic matter to the soil using anything organic and decomposable that you can find. This process can begin by cutting down the vegetation that is already growing on the land. This is where the concept of green manure comes in. Green manure refers to the use of live crops to add organic matter to the soil. Moreover, adding layers of nitrogen-rich components, cardboard, newspapers, dried vegetation, and compost can help build a thick layer of organic matter in the cultivation area. We will discuss this step in detail later on.

#### Step 3: Seedbed Preparation and Seeding

This step involves preparing the ground for sowing, ensuring that the temperature and moisture are at the desired level, and then planting in a way that minimizes soil disturbance.

#### Step 4: Mulching After Seeding

Mulch basically refers to materials that you lay on top of the soil. The purpose of adding mulch after seeding is to provide a protective layer to the layers beneath it, add more nutrients, and provide a stable environment for the germinating seeds.

#### Step 5: Maintaining Fertility and Soil Health

At this stage, all you have to do is monitor your plant growth and ensure that no soil diseases, pest attacks, or nutrient deficiencies arise. To promote soil health at this stage, using compost teas, foliar sprays, and other integrated pest management strategies can yield excellent results. These methods allow you to supply nutrients to the soil, reduce diseases, and deter pests.

#### Step 6: Cover Cropping

This is a step following the harvest of a crop. As the name suggests, cover cropping covers the land, protects and nourishes it until it is ready for the next cropping season. There is a wide variety of crops with various functions that you can choose from based on the requirements of your soil.

#### Step: 7: Crop Rotation

The last crucial step in no-till farming is the use of crop rotation. Crop rotation is a key component of IPM. Crop rotation simply means not growing the same plants repeatedly in one area and rotating these growth patterns to ensure that no pest attacks occur and the soil stays fertile and aerated.

Let's discuss the essential steps in detail. Before we begin, it's important to understand that there is no single formula for success in no-till farming. Different methods can be applied depending on soil type and environmental conditions. The only constant principle to remember is to resist the urge to till your land.

### Soil Preparation and Planting Methods

The process begins with soil preparation. The aim here is to prepare the soil such that it can support plant growth throughout the season, hold moisture for an extended period, and suppress unwanted elements such as weeds and pests. The method of soil preparation may vary depending on the condition of the soil and the season.

It is better to start soil preparation with the harvest of your spring crops. This gives you ample time to add organic content to the soil. If you start at such a point and notice that the soil is compacted, one last low-impact tilling would be beneficial. Alternatively, you can plant tillage radish or daikon radish as a cover crop. These cover crops will provide excellent tillage for your soil. You don’t even have to pull them out; just leave them there. Chopping and dropping their green parts on the soil surface will further add organic content. You can then add compost and a thick layer of mulch to fully prepare the seedbed for the next season.

If you get inspired to go no-till near the start of the spring season and want to prepare no-till garden beds, you can conduct a quick soil health check. If the soil is compacted, till the upper few inches. If there are weeds, do not pull them out; just chop and drop them in the same spot. They will act as mulch for your soil. After that, add a thick layer of finished compost; ideally, a 6-inch (≈15 cm) layer is preferred, and then cover it with mulch (Dziak, 2023). This quick prep no-till soil can be cultivated; however, it is not as effective as soil that has been prepared since the last season. You will need to add fertilizers and organic inputs, as well as perform weeding to support plant growth in such soil during the first season. Things will improve as the land fully transforms into no-till soil.

If you are preparing no-till soil from scratch and want to add organic content to it gradually over the winter, here's what you can do.

1.  On slightly wet soil, chop and drop the vegetation that is already present there. Use a pitchfork to aerate and gently mix this layer of greens with the soil.
    
2.  Add a layer of nitrogen-rich components. These components may include manure, leaves, pine needles, coffee grounds, crushed eggshells, and fruit or vegetable peels.
    
3.  Add a third layer of wet newspapers and cardboard. This layer will eliminate weeds and spur the decomposition process.
    
4.  Add more nitrogen-rich scraps on top of this layer and cover it with a layer of dried leaves, straws, and grasses. These two layers will eventually mix with the layers below the cardboard, thus adding a bulk of organic matter to your gardening beds.
    
5.  Once all of these layers have decomposed and mixed well with the soil below, you can add a layer of compost and cover it with mulch just before you sow seeds.
    

When you are ready to sow seeds, you will need to check for two things: whether the ground has a high moisture content and whether there are any grasses or weeds poking out of the mulch. If the moisture content is low, a good watering session will do the trick. The presence of weeds and grasses is unlikely; however, if there are any, you can remove them by hand or use a mower set to the lowest setting. Add more mulch if any of it becomes displaced during this process. During the planting process, ensuring that the soil remains undisturbed should be your top priority. Here are some tips to ensure a smooth and effective plantation on a no-till area:

●    Use seeds that have been pre-treated with insecticides and fungicides.

●   Use special drilling equipment to create holes for placing seeds in the soil. Alternatively, you can sow them by hand. You can push mulch aside to create long, shallow trenches. Once the seeds are placed in the ground, properly cover them.

●   Seed placement should be done in a way that ensures a consistent depth for all the sown seeds so all of the plants emerge at the same time out of the ground.

●     Don't use heavy mulching material or apply a thick mulch layer to cover the seeds, as this can hinder seed germination and emergence of the seedlings.

Make sure you mulch the ground periodically as the season progresses. Anything including compost, dried leaves, and grass clippings can be added to ensure nutrient replenishment and moisture retention. This will not only help the existing plants but also build the soil for the next cropping season.

### Cover Crops and Green Manures

Our discussion about no-till farming cannot be completed without appreciating the wonders of cover crops and green manures. These two components are often used with other farming methods; however, in no-till farming, their role is that of the pillars that make no-till farming successful.

As mentioned above, cover crops and green manure are the two main ingredients that can be used for preparing the land for no-till farming. From our discussion above, you can easily infer the main benefits of cover crops. There is prevention of soil erosion, tilling of compacted soils, and provision of organic matter. However, there is much more that cover crops have to offer. For instance, they

●   stop fast-growing weeds from accessing the sunlight, essentially eliminating them from the soil.

●   shade  the  soil, thus preventing the rapid oxidation of organic matter and the evaporation of water from the topsoil keeping its water content maintained.

●   feed and protect the soil microbial communities during the non-cultivation period.

●     balance out nutrient toxicities and effects of fertilizers if over-application of fertilizers has been done during the previous cultivation season.

Green manures are a crucial component of sustainable farming because they perform the same functions as chemical fertilizers, but their environmental impact is negligible. Any type of crop that is applied to the soil, while it is still green, to enhance soil fertility is labeled as green manure. So technically, cover crops that are chopped and dropped on the ground to enhance the organic content of the no-till soil are also a form of green manure. Corn stalks and freshly mown grass can also be spread on fields as green manures.

What is interesting about cover crops and green mulches is their ability to eliminate weeds. In any no-till farming protocol, it is often recommended to apply herbicides before sowing as a precautionary measure because you never know when a few weeds may emerge from the multiple layers of organic matter and mulch that have been applied to the ground. However, recent research on the effects of green manures and cover crops has revealed that these two components have the potential to completely remove weeds from no-till soils. With the use of these two methods, the application of herbicides on no-till lands can be entirely eliminated (Northern Virginia Soil and Water Conservation District, n.d.).

#### Selecting Suitable Cover Crops

As mentioned above, there is a variety of cover crop options, and each one offers unique benefits beyond adding organic matter to the soil and protecting it from environmental changes. Some cover crops add nitrogen, some absorb excess phosphorus, some till the soil, and some provide livestock feed. Therefore, the choice of cover crops should be made based on your specific needs.

Take a look at popular cover crops and their unique properties.

●  Grasses (Buckwheat, oats, barley, and rye)—good at accumulating nitrogen and providing livestock feed.

●  Cruciferous Varieties (Leafy greens, broccoli, and Brussels sprouts)—good at fixing phosphorous toxicity.

●   Leguminous Varieties (Chickpeas, alfalfa, and lentils)—efficient nitrogen fixers and best green manure.

●  Tillage Varieties (Radishes, turnips, carrots, and sunflowers)—have deep root systems that aerate without disturbing the soil organisms.

The timing of cover crop plantation also matters. The best time to plant one is early fall, just after the harvest of the summer crop. This allows it ample time to grow during the winters, and then you can cut it to add green manure to your soil. However, for people who want to grow a winter crop, planting it before the harvest of summer crops is the best option. This allows the seeds to germinate and seedlings to emerge while you are harvesting the main crop. These plants can then be removed just before winter cultivation.

## Maintaining Soil Health and Fertility

Soil health maintenance and fertility in no-till areas is a completely different story compared to till-based farming practices. The reason is clear: When you find something problematic on your farm, you take a chemical, add it to the soil, mix it well, and wait for it to resolve the issue. All of this cannot be done in no-till farming. So, you may ask what can we do to ensure soil fertility in no-till areas?

The answer is simple: You add whatever you can from the top without disturbing the soil, keep the soil microbes happy, and trust them to do the rest of the work for you.

### Building Organic Matter

By now, you already understand the importance of organic matter for a no-till setup, and you may be wondering why we are discussing this again. The point is that the addition of organic matter is not just something that needs to be done during the initial phase; it is a continuous process and an integral part of no-till farming. So, if you want to maintain a no-till farm and maximize its productivity, you will have to continue adding organic matter to it.

This might seem like a laborious task, but in reality, it's not. Once you transform your farming practices completely to no-till or prepare a special no-till garden by carefully layering greens, browns, and compost, you won't have to do everything from scratch before every cultivation season. However, you will have to continue adding more organic components as mulch. This can be in the form of finished compost, grass, and straws. After your produce has been harvested, you will also have to plant a cover crop to allow the soil to recover and prepare itself for the next crop. These practices will continue enriching your soil while also suppressing the growth of weeds.

To meet your compost needs, I suggest starting a compost pile as soon as you begin preparing no-till soil so that it is fully ready when you need it during the cultivation season. Don’t worry; we’ll discuss everything about composting in Chapter 5. For procuring components for mulch and compost, there are several options, including the following:

●   the waste products of other farming practices on your farm

●     kitchen waste

●     garden scraps from the neighborhood

●     animal manure from nearby barns and stables

●     commercial products of compost and mulch

### Using Compost Teas and Foliar Sprays

These two are important for maintaining soil health as well as supporting plant growth. Compost teas are liquid extracts prepared by adding water to finished compost. Since their chemical profile is almost the same as that of solid compost, they can be an ideal input for no-till soils where you want to add nutrients without disturbing the soil. They can easily seep into the soil and help increase soil fertility severalfold.

Foliar sprays, as their name suggests, are compositions that can be applied directly to the leaves (foliage) of plants. They can be used to promote plant growth, repel pests, and control diseases. Some fertilizers can also be used as foliar sprays to promote fruit production and help plants under stress. Weed teas and herbal teas are additional examples of foliar applications. Compost teas can also be used as foliar sprays. When such an application is made, the nutrients released by microbes (in compost tea) are absorbed through the pores on plant leaves. It is important to remember that foliar sprays are meant to support plant growth; however, their effects are not equal to those of organic matter added to the soil.

### Soil Microbial Life and Its Role

As I mentioned before, keeping the below-soil microbial communities happy can be the next best thing you need to do to succeed at no-till farming. Now, obviously, you cannot do anything in particular to provide them with a nice, cozy home; it’s no-till farming, remember? In fact, when it comes to protecting beneficial soil communities, it is not about what you should do to help them, but rather what you shouldn’t do. The role of soil microbes in no-till farming is crucial, and understanding it demands a deep dive into their relationships and workings. So, let’s do that.

The soil contains several microbes (and other organisms) that are divided into classes, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Each of these classes of organisms plays a crucial role in nutrient cycling and soil fertility maintenance. Nematodes and some arthropods initiate the breakdown of organic constituents by shredding them into tiny pieces. These pieces are then attacked by fungi and bacterial cells, thus starting the decomposition process. Nematodes and protozoa feed on fungi and bacteria, releasing nutrients into the soil. Then there is a special class of these organisms called grazers which help mix organic matter with soil particles. There is also a specific class of bacteria that fixes atmospheric nitrogen and converts it into a plant-ready form. Plants acquire these nutrients by releasing root exudates. Based on the chemical composition of these exudates, specific bacteria and fungi are attracted to the plant roots. The roots engulf these bacteria and fungi, extract the nutrients present within their bodies, and transport the nutrients to the upper parts of the plant. Note that this process is not specific to no-till soils—the same plant-microbe interactions occur everywhere in the soil; however, this interaction is more important for no-till farming where topsoil tilling and the addition of soil substitutes is limited.

This process is much more complex than I mentioned earlier, but that’s not the point. The important aspect to understand is that this harmonious flow of nutrients occurs because the right type of microbes can aggregate in the appropriate amounts and perform functions that ensure soil fertility, providing you with the productivity you desire. These soil communities require extended periods to establish themselves in the soil, and keeping them thriving is the goal. The question is what can we do (and not do) to ensure that these microbial communities are able to thrive?

Here are my top five tips:

●       Do not disturb! (minimize soil disturbance as much as you can)

●       Add compost and compost teas

●       Mulch

●       Use cover crops

●   Plant a variety  of crops (Hint: Implement crop rotation)

No-till farming requires a complete shift in your farming techniques. It involves minimizing soil disturbance and adopting a "sow and let it grow" strategy. At first, this approach may seem very unusual, and you might doubt its effectiveness. However, with a little persistence and continued addition of organic matter to the soil, the results you achieve will astonish you. After all, it's a win-win situation for both you and your soil.

# Chapter 4:  
Container and Raised Bed Soils

We briefly touched upon the dynamics of maintaining soils in containers and raised beds in the greenhouse soils section, but container and raised-bed cultivation is not limited to greenhouses only. They are distinct cultivation modalities that require separate discussions. The reason for the popularity of these two methods is their adaptability and sustainability in a variety of growth conditions around the world. However, along with these advantages, there are some downsides to these methods. To address these downsides and maximize the benefits, we need a thorough understanding of container and raised-bed soils.

## Container Soil Basics

Container gardening, or pot gardening, is an ancient method that dates back to 5000 BCE; however, there has been an exponential increase in the popularity of this method due to rapid industrialization and urbanization (Catalano, 2023). As a result of these events, the soil all across the world has faced a drastic decline in fertility levels. Additionally, people are increasingly finding it difficult to locate in-ground planting areas, so resorting to container gardening has become the most appealing solution for them. Today, you can find plants growing in containers on the smallest patios and in the narrowest alleys.

### Advantages of Container Soils

Container farming, like any other farming method, has its own set of advantages and disadvantages. A thorough understanding of these factors can help you decide how you can incorporate container farming into your set-up to maximize your gains.

#### Portability

With container gardening, the biggest advantage is the plants can be easily moved from one place to another, depending on their light and shade requirements. Moreover, during extreme weather conditions, plants can be easily covered or moved indoors.

#### Flexibility

They can be placed anywhere based on the available space. Stacked gardens and vertical gardens are two concepts that take this flexibility to the next level. They require limited space but allow for several plants to be planted together.

#### Protection From Diseases and Pests

Since the soil from one pot is rarely mixed with the soil from another pot, the chance of spreading a soil-borne disease is minimal. The same applies to pests. In case of a pest attack or disease, quick remedial action and separation of the affected pot from the other pots can efficiently stop and control the problem.

#### Ease of Soil Maintenance

The maintenance of soil contained within the pot is comparatively easier than the maintenance of soil in in-ground farming. Soil texture, drainage, and aeration can be easily adjusted in containers.

### Challenges of Growing in Container Soils

The limited growth space in container gardening is both an advantage and a disadvantage. For plants with deep root systems, you need to make special adjustments to grow them in containers; otherwise, they will outgrow their pots, leading to stunted growth. Having a limited soil volume also means frequent watering requirements because the soil is unable to retain moisture for long.

Container soils are also more susceptible to temperature changes, meaning they cool down or heat up more easily than in-ground soils. Lastly, soil maintenance in pots is straightforward owing to their small size; however, container soil is not a self-sustaining system, which means that it requires frequent maintenance sessions and the addition of soil amendments.

### Recommended Soil Mixes

Since container soils are completely isolated growth media, they must have all the properties of an ideal soil to support the growth of the plants. The main properties that a potting medium must have include the ability to

●   retain  moisture  and  nutrients  long enough for roots to absorb them.

●      provide sufficient aeration.

●      provide sufficient anchorage to the roots.

Therefore, the selection of potting media needs to be made after proper consideration. There are two main media options: potting soil and soilless media.

#### Potting Soil

You cannot simply dig up soil from your backyard and use it for container gardening. That soil is often compacted and heavy. We need soil that is well-aerated, light, and rich in nutrients. Therefore, potting soils need to be prepared specifically by mixing them with other components to get the desired texture of the growth media. You can buy potting soil from local gardening stores or prepare custom mixes using soil and inorganic and organic components.

Some farmers prefer using soil in potting mixes because it helps plants adapt and establish themselves more easily while accessing readily available nutrients. This approach allows growers to worry less about the nutritional profile of the media. However, using soil comes with challenges. One significant issue is compaction, which can be managed by adding other components. Another major concern is the presence of soil-borne pathogens, which can be addressed through heat treatment of the soil. Even after removing soil pathogens and improving its porosity, soil particles can compact again and lead to problems in the future, so experts recommend keeping the percentage of soil very low in these mixes (Container Soils and Soilless Media, n.d.).

Take a look at the recipe for potting soil below. Note that this is just a recipe that includes most of the ingredients that provide a normal level of drainage, aeration, and nutrition to the plant. You can obviously make changes to the quantity of these ingredients if you think your media needs more or less of any ingredient.

For this mix, you'll require 37 cups (2 gallons) each of garden soil, peat moss, compost, and perlite. You will also need half a cup each of kelp powder, rock phosphate, greensand, dolomitic limestone, and soybean meal (Reich, 2014). You will have to fumigate and pasteurize soil to remove fungal and bacterial cells. You can start by sifting and mixing soil, peat moss, and compost. Finally, all of the other ingredients can be added along with some water if you think the mixture seems too dry. This medium is suitable for growing all kinds of container plants.

You can also amend this media or any other potting soil with ingredients that are known for specific properties. These ingredients include the following:

●   Sharp sand—has a large particle size and can be used to enhance porosity.

●   Sawdust  and  wood mulch—have a high carbon content and are best for growing vegetables.

●      Coconut coir—has high water retention and is good for growing succulents.

●      Leaf mold—has a high nutrient content and can be used to enhance the fertility of the potting mix.

#### Soilless Media

We have discussed the properties of several inorganic and organic components for soilless media in Chapter 2. The same components can be used to create soilless media for container plants. Normally, a blend of one organic and one inorganic component gives an excellent potting media. To further enhance its composition, fertilizer can be added. The following are the most common ingredients for soilless media:

●     Peat moss—provides moisture and nutrient retention.

●   Pine  bark—provides nutrients, anchorage, moisture retention, and air space.

●   Perlite and vermiculite—provide most of the air space in the media.

There are several recipes for creating potting mixes with each one offering special properties like high-water retention, quick-drainage, and light weight.

Consider the recipe below:

●       ½ pound dolomitic limestone

●       1 bushel (1 bushel ≈ 9.3 gallons) perlite or vermiculite

●       1 bushel peat moss

●       1½ ounces 20% superphosphate fertilizer

●       1 pound 5–10–5 fertilizer

20% superphosphate fertilizer is an ideal fertilizer that supports all kinds of fruit, root, and seed crops while 5–10–5 fertilizer refers to a general-purpose fertilizer that contains 5% nitrogen, 10% phosphorus, and 5% potassium. Since all of these ingredients are dry, you'll have to moisten them by adding a small amount of water during mixing. You can also store this mix for later use. This recipe was originally created by the experts at Cornell University and is used today for commercial preparation of potting mixes. So, if you source all of these ingredients and prepare a medium with the exact composition, you will have a standardized soilless potting mix for your garden.

I would like to mention compost here, especially because it can serve as a perfect soilless medium for container plants on its own. Finished compost that has been made using a variety of organic components can provide all of the required nutrients for the plants. Additionally, it is light and well-aerated. However, if you decide to use 100% compost as a potting mix, you will have to add fertilizer occasionally because the nutrients in the compost are released slowly.

## Raised-Bed Soil Composition

Similar to container gardening, raised-bed gardening is a concept that involves growing media that is separate from the in-ground soil. Unlike the pots and containers in container gardening, the frames in raised-bed gardening are bigger, sturdier, and do not offer much portability. These features of raised beds offer a certain set of benefits in terms of soil maintenance. Let's take a look at these benefits.

### Soil Quality Control

The biggest advantage of raised garden beds is that they allow for better control over soil quality. Some soils are naturally unsuitable for farming due to poor texture, toxicity from certain elements, or problematic microbial profiles. Raised-bed gardening provides an ideal solution for these situations because it enables you to tailor the soil composition to your specific needs, choosing each ingredient carefully. Additionally, it helps maintain soil quality over the years, ensuring a healthier environment for plants to thrive.

### Water Control

For soils that are either waterlogged or extremely poor at retaining water, raised-bed gardening offers the solution. With a carefully constituted raised-bed medium, you can control the level of drainage you want for your plants.

Adjusted drainage also means that the irrigation water is not lost to the ground; instead, it is used sustainably. There are options like drip irrigation and sprinkler systems that ensure that water is delivered to the plant roots and foliage with precision, preventing any loss to the ground below.

### Pest Protection

Raised-bed gardens are a huge success in areas that are generally swarming with pests. This is because they can easily be designed in ways that prevent the entry of pests from both the top and bottom of the soil. Mesh screens can be installed at the bottom of the bed frames, while row covers can be used to protect the plants from above. This way, every pest, from small flies to the largest moles and garden mice, can be kept out of your precious plants.

### Season Extension

Compared to ground soils, where cropping seasons are strictly dependent upon the climate conditions, raised-bed gardening allows for year-round planting. This is because the soil temperature in raised beds does not rise or fall as rapidly as it does in in-ground soil. Moreover, with the addition of simple tunnels and cold covers, plants can continue to thrive well into late fall. In the spring, the soil in raised beds warms up at a faster rate, allowing growers to start their spring planting earlier.

Moreover, with raised garden beds, several plants can be planted together. This maximizes growth space and provides you with diverse produce throughout the years.

### Building a Balanced Soil Mix

Like any other gardening method, the quality of the growing media is important in raised-bed gardening as well. The top considerations here are drainage, nutrient content, and texture. The drainage level needs to be adjusted so that water is retained for a duration long enough for the roots to absorb it, but rainwater should percolate quickly out from the bottom. The nutrient content should be very rich in macronutrients, micronutrients, and organic matter because the number of plants that are grown in a raised bed at one time is high, and the soil should be able to support their growth all at once. In terms of texture, the media should be soft and crumbly.

Compared to the variety of components used in the composition of other growth mediums, the media for raised beds has only two main components: topsoil and compost. Sometimes, other organic amendments, such as leaf mold, worm castings, and wood chips, can be added.

Now, you may be wondering why only these components are used for raised-bed gardening and not others. Additionally, what about soilless media? The answer is simple: The reason these components are the only ones used is that research and growers’ experience have proven that this combination is the only sustainable media for raised beds. As for soilless media, it is common for first-time growers to purchase bagged, soilless media; however, the downside is that these media experience a drastic decline in quality and productivity after just one or two seasons (Schalau, 2018). Therefore, using soilless media is not a recommended practice for raised-bed soils.

#### Topsoil

Topsoil is considered the best starting material for raised beds. It provides a favorable texture, drainage, and microbial community to support plant growth. Ideally, the topsoil should have a loamy texture that contains all of the basic soil components, including clay, sand, and silt.

You can source the topsoil from your own property if its texture fits the criteria. Locally sourced soil will also have an established community of plant-friendly bacteria and fungi, which is again a desirable feature. The removal of gravel and large pieces of rock is recommended before filling the soil into the raised beds. If the topsoil from your own property is not of the desired texture, you can source the soil from a supplier. When purchasing topsoil from a supplier, it is always good to look for unamended topsoil because this allows you the flexibility to amend it with the ingredients of your choice and based on the plant's requirements.

According to the University of Maryland Extension (n.d.), the quantity of organic matter in raised-bed soil should be 25%–50% by volume or 5%–15% by weight. Overall, the percentage of topsoil in the mix should be 50% (Pearce, 2022). Once you have added the soil to the raised bed, you can measure the quantity of organic matter by obtaining a lab test. The lab results will guide you regarding how much more compost and amendments you need to add.

#### Compost

The topsoil that you add to the mix might not provide everything that the plants need, so we need to add compost. Since compost is made from decomposed organic constituents, it is extremely rich in macronutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. The following are some other roles of compost:

●     Provision of micronutrients that might be deficient in the topsoil

●     Maintenance of drainage

●      Improvement of soil's water-holding capacity

●   Maintenance of soil temperature (compost helps keep the soil warm)

●  Addition  of  soil-friendly  bacteria, fungi, and worms to the soil

In ideal raised-bed soil, compost should make up around 30% of the volume (Pearce, 2022). Just like topsoil, it is best to procure compost from your own farm. However, this is not easy because a large amount is required. Therefore, you can obtain compost from a local supplier. However, you can start your own compost heap to provide you with the small amount of compost that is needed periodically for the maintenance of media quality.

#### Organic Matter

This is the last ingredient of raised-bed soil. The recommended amount for this is 20%, which is not as high, as the other two components are equally important for the mix. Topsoil and compost already contain a good amount of organic matter, but the reason for adding more is that we want to enhance the nutrient profile and structure of the soil. Organic matter, such as wood chips, bark, well-rotted horse manure, and grass clippings, may take some time to decompose completely and add nutrients to the soil; however, they instantly improve the texture of the soil. Their large-sized particles create tiny pockets in the soil, making it easier for the soil to hold water and air. This, ultimately, improves the workability of the soil and stimulates root growth.

Besides the basic mixing method that involves combining all three of these ingredients, there are some unique strategies that can be used to prepare soil on raised beds. Lasagna and Hügelkultur gardening are two strategies worth mentioning.

### Lasagna Gardening Method

This media preparation method makes little to no use of topsoil. Instead, it relies solely on components rich in organic matter that decompose easily. It is popular among growers who want to incorporate sustainable and resource-conservation practices into their setups. Lasagna methods use all of the ingredients that would otherwise end up in a landfill. This helps create a medium that requires minimal grooming and watering during the growing season because the materials decompose gradually, releasing nutrients slowly for the plants. Additionally, the water retained within these materials is sufficient to sustain the plants.

Here's a quick guide to preparing raised-bed soil using this method:

1.  Locate an ideal spot to place your raised bed frames. For this method, I suggest you place them far enough from your house because decomposing materials may smell, and you wouldn't want to smell any of it in your house.
    
2.  Collect high-carbon and high-nitrogen sources. You will need to create a 4:1 combo between carbon and nitrogen-rich components. For carbon sources, you can use browns like dried leaves, sawdust, and straws. For nitrogen, greens like vegetable scraps, grass clippings, and ruminant manure can be used.
    
3.  Cover the bottom of the bed with newspaper or cardboard and wet this layer properly. This layer will eliminate weeds.
    
4.  Add a carbon layer keeping the 4:1 ratio in mind.
    
5.  Add a nitrogen-rich layer.
    
6.  Keep in mind that you have to fill the bed by adding multiple layers of carbon and nitrogen-rich components, so your initial layer should be thick enough to fill the entire raised bed.
    
7.  Keep on layering until you reach the desired height.
    
8.  Allow the lasagna to cook so that it gets completely decomposed and all the layers are thoroughly mixed together. However, you can also cultivate it immediately. Just make sure that the top layer (where you sow the seeds) consists of finished compost or any other composted material so that seedlings don't get burned.
    

### Hügelkultur Gardening Method

Hügelkultur is a term of German origin that means “mound culture.” This technique involves creating a mound of growth-friendly components that can regulate factors such as water retention, drainage, and temperature management. The three main ingredients in this method are topsoil, compost, and wood components. As the mound decomposes slowly, it provides a continuous supply of nutrients to the plants while keeping the soil heated. The shape of the mound also allows for maximum utilization of rainwater runoff, which eliminates the need for frequent watering sessions.

Here's a quick guide to designing a raised-bed media based on this method:

1.  Start by adding an initial layer of topsoil on the raised bed.
    
2.  Use a stick or a pointed tool to trace the location and width of the mounds that you wish to create. I recommend making several long wavy mounds that are parallel to each other. This is the base shape as it allows you to utilize the raised bed efficiently.
    
3.  Layer the bottom and the boundary of each mound with twigs and broken branches.
    
4.  Add a layer of wood chips.
    
5.  Add a layer of manure.
    
6.  Add a layer of small twigs.
    
7.  Add a layer of grass clippings and manure.
    
8.  Top it off with topsoil and compost.
    
9.  Allow the mounds to decompose over the winter, but if you want to plant right away, that's okay too.
    

Note: Hügelkultur basically involves the same layering method as in the Lasagna method. The only difference is the ingredients and placement/shape of the mound. Moreover, there is a wide collection of mound shapes that you can make, including ones with borders, without borders, with wooden borders, and with stone borders. The one I have mentioned above is for the design with wooden borders.

### Managing Soil Depth and Drainage

An important question that arises when filling raised beds with soil is how much media should be added. What depth is desirable for a specific plant family? Should we fill raised beds to the top, or should we leave some space empty?

In the farming world, the minimum recommended depth is 8–12 inches or ≈20–30 cm (Burke, 2023). However, the main aspect you need to consider here is the type of plants that you wish to grow on your raised beds. For different plants, there are different recommended depths. The following are some examples:

●   Shallow-rooted plants including leafy greens and herbs need a depth of around 6 inches (≈15 cm).

●     Root vegetables and peppers need around 12-inch (≈30 cm) depth.

●    Deep-rooted plants like kale, squash, and zucchini need a depth of 18 inches (≈45 cm).

The question of whether to fill the raised bed to the top is also very simple. If you fill the bed to the top, you will be exposing your precious media to the forces that cause erosion. Every time you water the beds or it rains, you will lose the top layer of your media. Therefore, it is always better to leave at least 1–2 inches (≈2.5–5 cm) of the raised bed empty (Lamp’l, 2018).

The maintenance of soil texture to improve drainage and prevent waterlogging is another important consideration that growers need to keep in mind when creating raised-bed soils and filling them into the beds. If the drainage factor is ignored during the initial phases of the raised-bed setup, severe drainage and waterlogging issues can occur during the rainy season. The most important thing that you can do to prevent these issues is to incorporate a lot of organic components into the soil mix. Both greens and browns are important in this regard as they help maintain a perfect balance of bacteria and fungi that carry out decomposition. With an optimum rate of decomposition, enough moisture (and nutrients) is released into the media required for plant growth. The presence of organic matter and compost also provides the necessary crumbly, porous texture to the soil that maintains drainage at the required level. Here’s what else can be done to maintain the desired drainage level:

●       Add extra depth to the soil in the raised beds if they are placed on a hard surface that cannot absorb water readily.

●      Add a base layer of sand at the bottom of the raised beds before adding anything else.

●      Add broken rocks or small pebbles at the bottom of the raised beds. This way, you won’t have to fill the entire bed with soil, and you will also achieve improved drainage.

●   Add  an  extra layer of wood chips on top of the plants and mix them gently with the soil before a heavy rain spell.

## Maintenance and Care

When it comes to the maintenance of soils in containers and raised beds, the process is not very complicated or time-consuming, at least when compared to greenhouse soil and in-ground soil. The reason for this is the size of these systems. Container plants are easily moved when weather conditions are extremely unfavorable, which eliminates the need to create complex maintenance systems. The raised beds are not as portable as container plants but are still small setups that can be easily tended. However, I believe that being small is both an advantage and a disadvantage of these systems. They require frequent attention in terms of watering and fertilization.

### Watering and Fertilization Practices

Since plant pots have a hole at the bottom that allows for the continuous removal of excess water from the soil, the soil itself is unable to retain water for long. Therefore, frequent watering is required. However, that doesn’t mean that you should seal the bottom of the container to retain water. Container plants cannot thrive in soggy soil; they need well-drained and adequately watered conditions. Here are my top tips to ensure that container soils have just enough water without becoming waterlogged:

●      Make sure you water your container plants at least once daily.

●    You can go for two watering sessions if your containers are large and the material is quick draining (Sawyer, n.d.).

●  Two  watering  sessions  are also recommended when the weather is hot and dry.

●     Add mulch to minimize water loss due to evaporation. Mulch will also prevent soil from splashing onto the leaves during watering sessions and rainfall.

●      You can use grass clippings, hay, or even shredded newspaper as mulch for container soil.

●     If you move your container plants indoors during an extreme weather event, make sure you adjust the watering frequency to avoid overwatering.

For raised-bed soils, you can make your watering sessions a breeze by installing water-efficient systems like drip irrigation and sprinkler systems; however, the watering requirements are frequent because the medium is quick draining. Surely, the high amount of organic matter and compost in these soils does play a role in keeping the soil humid; yet, watering is still required since several plants are growing close together in a limited space where competition for resources is very high. Here are some tips for watering raised-bed soil:

●     Water early in the morning to prevent quick evaporation.

●    If you are growing plants with deep root systems, use soaker hoses to deliver water directly to the roots.

●      Add a layer of mulch on top during the summer to prevent water loss through evaporation.

●     If you are using a hose to water the soil, be sure to position the hose head very close to the soil to ensure that no soil splashes onto the leaves. This can transfer soil-borne pathogens to the upper parts of the plants.

The fertilization needs for container soils are also frequent because watering can leach nutrients out of the soil. However, in the long run, these frequent yet small applications can keep the soil productive without causing any problems. You can start regular fertilizer applications within two to six weeks of cultivating the potting media (Sawyer, n.d.). Nothing specific is needed for container soils; an all-purpose fertilizer does a good job. During the flowering and fruiting periods, you can switch to fertilizers with high ratios of phosphorus and potassium. When it comes to choosing between soluble and fast-release fertilizers, soluble fertilizers perform better in container soils. They provide nutrients rapidly to the plants. Therefore, soluble fertilizers are best for post-cultivation applications. You can use slow-release fertilizer when creating the potting mix. Over-fertilization is also common in container gardening. To avoid this, fertilizers should always be applied in small quantities. Small applications in frequent sessions are always better than large but occasional applications.

For raised-bed soil, compost and organic matter are sufficient for keeping the soil fertile. The constituents added during the preparation of raised-bed soil are sufficient for at least one cropping session. However, we cannot ignore the fact that the decomposition rate is high in raised-bed soils, which can cause the soil level to fall over time. To maintain the levels and keep the soil thriving, 2–3 inches (≈5–7.5 cm) of compost should be added, each fall as a top dressing (Tips for Fertilizing in a Raised Bed?, 2022). Additional fertilizer needs strictly depend on the types of plants you are growing. Bloom fertilizers can be applied just before the flowering or fruiting period, but for most plants, there is no such requirement.

### Dealing With Soil Compaction and Erosion

Soil compaction and erosion are two important issues associated with container and raised-bed soil. The main causes of these problems are excessive watering and tilling that these systems receive. The rapid loss of organic matter in raised-bed soils is another significant contributor to soil compaction. What can be done to address these issues? Well, there is a lot that can be done to prevent and manage them effectively.

To prevent soil compaction, foot traffic on garden beds needs to be minimized. A path covered with wood chips can be created between adjacent raised beds to avoid stepping directly onto the bed soil. Pets and other animals should not be allowed to sit or walk on the soil. Bare soil is susceptible to compaction and erosion; therefore, planting cover crops and using mulch is the best way to prevent these issues. Wet soils are also susceptible to compaction; therefore, the soil should not be disturbed when wet. The use of heavy machinery should also be avoided. Instead, small hand-held tools should be used. If the soil is already compacted, a layer of compost followed by a quick tilling with a garden fork can do a lot to minimize the compaction. If the soil is heavily compacted, tillage radish should be planted as a cover crop; it should fix the issue.

For soil erosion, it's nothing new. Just avoid splashing water during irrigation sessions and use cover crops and mulch to keep the soil covered at all times. Another method is to place wood chips or stones as retaining walls at the boundaries of containers or raised beds to prevent erosion.

Composting

My family and I have tried various farming practices and tended to different types of land, but I feel that composting is the most rewarding activity I have participated in so far. Let me explain why. In farming, you work the soil, sow the seeds, care for the plants, and eventually harvest the fruit. Composting, however, is quite different. You take what would normally be considered waste (with some planning, of course) and transform it into something incredibly valuable—nutrient-rich compost for your soil. For someone like me, who is passionate about sustainable land-use practices and resource conservation, composting is a deeply satisfying endeavor. It's the perfect way to recycle nutrients back into the soil while also helping to improve the environment.

You might be wondering if composting is a difficult task that requires a lot of experience and skill. It’s nothing like that. Anyone who understands the basic principles of composting and knows about the fundamental mix of ingredients can do it. In fact, home composting can be done by anyone. In this chapter, we will cover everything related to composting, including its benefits, types, preparation methods, and application techniques. This will enable you to start your own compost pile and help you maintain it to meet your composting needs for your farm.

## What Is Composting?

Merriam-Webster (n.d.) defines compost as a mix of decayed organic matter that is used for fertilizing and conditioning the land, while the USDA. (n.d.-a) defines composting as the process of converting organic materials into amendments that can be fed to the soil and plants to maintain their health and productivity. Note that composting doesn't just refer to the human-assisted conversion of organic constituents into compost. It is a natural process that is occurring all the time on Earth. However, we are just discussing human-assisted composting here that aims to provide a favorable environment to the composting organisms so that they can carry composting at an exponential rate.

### Benefits of Composting

Before diving into the how-tos of composting, we’ll first discuss some of its uses and benefits. This will help you understand the role that composting can play in creating zero-waste farming systems and communities in general.

#### Adds Nutrients to Soil

This is perhaps the most important use of compost. When compost is added to the soil, it instantaneously boosts the cation exchange capacity (CEC) of the soil. Remember what we discussed about CEC in Chapter 2; it is the ability of a medium to hold (and exchange) charged nutrient ions. With improved CEC, the soil is better able to hold nutrients and provide them to the plants. Finished compost adds a significant amount of nutrients to the soil. Although this amount is not as high as that of chemical fertilizers, frequent application of compost made from mixed components can provide a concentration of nutrients comparable to that found in fertilizers. Compost acts as a slow-release fertilizer for the soil, gradually releasing nutrients over time. Therefore, the effects of a single application of compost are noticeable even after 6–12 months (US Composting Council, 2024).

#### Promotes Soil Microbial Diversity

Compost achieves this by adding nutrient-rich organic matter to the soil, which soil microbes can feed on to grow and multiply. Beneficial soil microbes and worms are also present in the compost, and when added to the soil, they further enhance its microbial diversity.

#### Improves Soil Density

The availability of just the right soil density is important for plant growth. Compost does that by modulating soil texture. If it is too tight, compost loosens it up, and if it's too loose, compost clumps up the soil, thus creating a perfect growth environment for plant roots.

#### Prevents Soil Erosion and Locks in Moisture

Compost enhances the soil’s ability to resist erosion by binding soil particles together. It does this by producing a material called humus in the soil. Humus is a black-brown material formed by the microbial decomposition of plant and animal residues. In soil, humus acts as a glue that provides structural rigidity, allowing the soil to resist erosion. Even when used as a soil cover, compost protects the topsoil from being washed or blown away.

#### Suppresses Pests, Diseases, and Weeds

Soils that are regularly treated with compost show a better response to pest attacks, soil-borne diseases, and weeds. The reason is simple: Plants that are commonly affected by pests are those that experience nutrient imbalances. Since compost helps address nutrient deficiencies, these plants are better able to resist pest attacks. Disease-causing agents are easily eliminated from compost-covered soils due to the elevated temperatures of these soils. For weed elimination, the two aforementioned factors again play a role. Compost corrects nutrient imbalances in the soil, which are usually conducive to weed growth. Additionally, the temperature of compost-treated soil is high enough to effectively suppress weed growth.

#### Reduces Waste

Composting is certainly a major technique for converting trash into nutrient-rich food for your soil; more importantly, it is a way to reduce waste. This is especially crucial for those who manage a garden or a farm, as these activities produce a significant amount of organic waste. Composting provides a simple and productive method for managing this waste. In the long term, it offers numerous benefits for the remedy and protection of the environment.

When incorporated into a farming system, composting can offer so much more than we have discussed above. This can save cost on buying compost from commercial sources, so you can have an unlimited supply of compost to feed your raised beds, container plants, and in-ground soil and have an efficient system to get rid of all the garbage that you produce on your farm or home. It's honestly an incredible way to reduce soil maintenance costs while also practicing sustainability and environmental efficiency.

### Types of Composting: Aerobic vs. Anaerobic

When it comes to the basic types of compost, there are two distinct categories based on the methods used to create them: aerobic compost and anaerobic compost. Knowing the major differences between the chemistry of each type and the methods involved in their production is crucial because it can help you decide which method suits which farming system best and is most convenient for you.

#### Aerobic Compost

The word aerobic means "with oxygen," so aerobic compost is the type that is made in the presence of oxygen. More specifically, the microorganisms involved in this composting process require oxygen to grow and multiply. Therefore, in aerobic composting, the pile needs to be turned and mixed regularly to assist the aeration process. The good thing about this process is that there is no production of methane gas. Only water, carbon dioxide, and heat are produced as the main by-products of this process. The water can be drained out of the system very easily. As for heat and carbon dioxide, it is often debated whether these two components are eco-friendly; however, when viewed objectively, both of these components serve a purpose, especially when placed on or near agricultural land. The heat eliminates weed seeds and pathogens that can cause future problems for the soil. Carbon dioxide is abruptly absorbed by the surrounding vegetation for its fuel-generating activity. Therefore, aerobic composting can be considered a problem-free process, barring the need for it to be continuously or regularly turned.

The following are the required conditions for aerobic composting:

●    Temperature between 54 °C and 65 °C (129.2 °F and 149 °F)

●      Humidity between 50%–80%

●      Air-filled volume (porosity) between 25–35%

●    A proper balance of carbon-rich materials and nitrogen-rich materials

#### Anaerobic Compost

Anaerobic (without oxygen) compost refers to compost that is prepared in the absence of fresh air by a group of microorganisms that thrive under such conditions. Unlike aerobic compost, which needs to be turned regularly, anaerobic compost performs well even under low-maintenance conditions. There is no need for temperature and moisture regulation. All you have to do is dump the organic waste and leave the rest of the work to the microorganisms. The one downside of anaerobic compost is the production of methane gas, in addition to other by-products of composting. Due to this, an anaerobic pile cannot be maintained near houses or other inhabited properties.

The following conditions are required for anaerobic composting:

●      A balanced carbon-to-nitrogen ratio.

●      A tight-fitting lid or sealed container.

●   Sufficient  moisture to  facilitate the breakdown process.

Let's make a quick comparison between both methods of composting.

## Starting a Compost Pile

Starting a compost pile doesn’t require much, but it certainly necessitates that you understand and decide on three major factors of composting. These factors include whether you want your composting system to be indoors or outdoors, whether it will be hot or cold, and whether you prefer a batch system or a continuous system.

The first factor is already determined when you choose between aerobic and anaerobic composting. Obviously, it is impossible to maintain an indoor compost pile that is decomposing anaerobically. So, if you are starting with a small system using your kitchen scraps, you'd obviously have to go for an outdoor pile that is maintained aerobically.

Next, there is a choice between hot and cold composting. Hot compost requires more work in terms of moisture, temperature, and aeration maintenance, but they decompose quickly. Cold compost is, well, just cold. You don’t need to do much to maintain it; it also decomposes slowly and takes a long time to prepare. One thing to note here is that hot compost is only maintained aerobically (it’s the oxygen that keeps the pile heated), so if you have already opted for anaerobic composting, you can only have cold compost.

Last, you have to choose between a batch system or a continuous system. A batch system is where the materials for composting are added once to the pile, and the finished compost is retrieved only when it is complete. Finished compost looks and smells very much like dark-colored earth. It has a high ratio of decomposers that have thoroughly consumed the organic matter and completed their life cycles in the compost pile. In contrast to a batch system, a continuous system operates continuously, where you keep adding new scraps from the top and removing the finished compost from the lower sections of the pile. The choice between these two systems strictly depends on how often you need compost. If you want a small but continuous supply, you can opt for continuous composting. For large batches that you need once every few months or once a year, you can select batch composting.

### Materials to Use

Once you have decided on the method you will use for composting, the next main question is what you can put in a compost pile. It is crucial to remember that while anything organic is decomposable, not all organic materials are compostable. Consider meat and dairy products for example: They will decompose fairly quickly in a compost bin, but do you think they won’t invite pathogenic microbes into the pile? Certainly, they will. Additionally, small backyard composting systems never sit for long enough or reach temperatures sufficient to decompose these materials adequately. The same goes for items like diseased plants and chemically treated wood. These items will decompose but will introduce soil-borne pathogens and harsh chemicals into the compost. Therefore, the notion that anything organic can be added to the compost pile is incorrect. There are three main elements that can be used: The browns, the greens, and water.

#### Greens

The greens refer to the green parts of plants, herbs, and shrubs, including grass clippings, fresh leaves, plant trimmings, fruit and vegetable scraps, fruit and vegetable peels, tea bags, and coffee grounds. These sources provide nitrogen to the mix.

#### Browns

The browns refer to the brown parts of vegetation, those that have turned brown upon drying, as well as wood-derived products. These include twigs, bark, branches, dried leaves, straw, sweetcorn cobs, sawdust, cardboard, and paper. Cotton wool, vacuum cleaner contents, and eggshells are also considered browns. The browns are important for a compost pile because they add carbon to the mix. Based on this criterion, animal manure, such as that from cows, sheep, goats, horses, rabbits, and chickens, can also be considered browns. Note that only the manure from these animals is compostable. Manure from any other animals or from humans should not be added to a compost pile.

Both browns and greens are considered important for a compost mix because, together, they provide an ideal meal to feed the microbes that carry out decomposition. An ideal browns-to-greens ratio for compost is 3:1 (Weikel, 2020). This means that you should add three times more browns than greens. It's not like the browns do not contain any nitrogen at all. Their carbon percentage is higher, but they still contain some nitrogen, and taken together, the nitrogen percentage can become too high in the mix. To balance this, we add a larger amount of carbon-rich browns to maintain the appropriate carbon-nitrogen levels in the mix.

#### Water

Water is a crucial component of the compost pile because it helps maintain a humid environment for the microbes where decomposition is carried out at a fast pace. The content of the compost pile should be soggy, but they should be moist enough to keep the composting pile wet. The ideal moisture range for efficient composting is 40–60% (Cornell Composting, n.d.).

### Building and Maintaining the Pile

Based on the factors mentioned above, there can be slight variations in the construction and management of composting systems; however, the basic steps are the same. Let's take a look at these steps.

#### Collection and Storage of Greens and Browns

This step involves the collection and storage of compost-suitable items in a designated area before they can be added to the compost pile. The aim is to ensure that all of these items maintain their quality; we don’t want them to start rotting before entering the compost pile. For storing greens, you can use a closed container that can be placed in one of your kitchen cabinets or in your backyard. If you have sufficient space in your fridge, you can store them there as well. For the browns, you can select a sheltered, dry area in your backyard, garage, or a designated spot in your greenhouse. Greens can be collected year-round, but browns are only abundantly available in the fall. Therefore, it would be a great idea to collect most of your browns during this time and store them for later use.

#### Creating a Site for Composting

This depends on where you decide to place your compost. If it’s an indoor composting setup, you will obviously have to select a compost bin that can retain moisture and heat but also has perforations at the bottom to allow for the drainage of excess fluids. You can either buy a compost bin from a local supplier or construct one of your own using materials like wood, wire, cinder blocks, or plastic.

For outdoor composting, you can either choose an open-air site or create an underground composting trench. Underground composting is mainly suitable for large-scale composting in farming areas where anaerobic composting is the preferred choice. For open-air composting and for the outdoor placement of a compost bin, make sure that the site is easily accessible and has good drainage. The site should also be away from water sources; you wouldn’t want the fluids from the compost pile to mix with your water source.

#### Preparing Compost Ingredients

Before adding the ingredients to the pile, you will have to chop or break larger components into small pieces. This step will ensure that all the ingredients you add to the compost pile are roughly equal in size. Smaller ingredients have a larger surface area, which means that a greater number of microbes can latch onto them and break them down. This step is important for tough kitchen scraps and large pieces of browns, such as branches and twigs.

#### Layering the Components

This is the most crucial step for starting a compost pile. Make sure you have your greens and browns in a 3:1 ratio before you start layering. Layering these components properly will ensure that the microbes receive all the right ingredients—carbon, nitrogen, and water—for growth and activity.

You will begin by placing a 4–6 inch (≈10.2–15.2 cm) thick layer of browns at the bottom of the pile. This layer will provide elevation to the pile, absorb and drain excess fluids, and allow air to circulate within the lower layers of compost. The browns you can add to this layer include twigs, logs, and wood chips. After this, you will layer browns and greens using the lasagna method, which involves adding alternating layers of greens and browns. Lastly, cover everything with another 4–8 inches (≈10–20 cm) of sawdust, straw, dry leaves, or other browns (United States Environmental Protection Agency, 2018).

#### Maintaining Temperature and Moisture

Maintenance of temperature and moisture are two main components that determine the end-product of composting. Aeration is also important in aerobic piles.

For temperature regulation and thorough aeration of aerobic composts, you must rely on frequent aeration. This will help mix the layers, speed up the decomposition process, and prevent the pile from becoming too hot. You can use a garden fork to turn the pile. To gauge the moisture content of the pile, you can use the squeeze test. Squeezing some of the compost contents will indicate whether the moisture levels are within the desired range. In a well-maintained pile, the texture of the contents feels like a wrung-out sponge.

For anaerobic piles, you won’t need to mix them, but you will certainly need to monitor temperature and moisture levels to ensure that decomposition occurs at a steady rate and that anaerobic conditions are maintained.

#### Making Adjustments

A part of maintaining desirable conditions in a compost pile is recognizing problematic signs in a timely manner and making adjustments to ensure that microbes in the pile stay active and happy.

Here's a list of steps that you can take to adjust the conditions in a pile composting aerobically:

●      If the moisture levels are low, moisten the pile, turn it, and add a few kitchen scraps that have a high moisture content.

●       If the pile is starting to smell (which indicates poor aeration or excessive water), add some browns, such as dry leaves or sawdust, and mix the pile.

●  If  the temperature  of  the pile is lower than required, add a few nitrogen-rich components (greens), and turn the pile.

For adjusting the conditions of an anaerobic pile, you can try the following tricks:

●      If the temperature is not within the desired range, add a few more nitrogen-rich scraps. Low temperatures in an anaerobic pile indicate poor compaction, so adding more scraps that eliminate the air present in the pile will help raise its temperature.

●      If the pile starts to smell too bad, it is an indication of high moisture content; adding some high-carbon browns will help. Note that anaerobic piles are bound to smell, but if the smell is extremely pungent, that is a sign of trouble.

●   If  the  contents of the pile are getting slimy, that is another sign of too much water; adding dry, carbon-rich scraps can help.

#### Harvesting Finished Compost

You can tell when a compost pile is fully cured and ready for use by looking at its physical characteristics. A finished compost has

●   a temperature close to that of the surrounding environment.

●       a volume one-third of the original pile size.

●       a rich dark-brown color.

●       a crumbly texture.

●       an earthy smell similar to the forest soil.

If, however, you notice that the pile is still hot, has recognizable scraps among its contents, and smells like ammonia, you’ll have to give it more time to cure. Compost that is well-maintained and turned frequently is usually ready in about three to five months (United States Environmental Protection Agency, 2018). Anaerobic compost takes longer than that to be ready for use.

Once your compost is ready, all you have to do is dig it out into a wheelbarrow and use it wherever you need it. However, instead of taking it all out in one go, I recommend removing some from the top and the sides into a separate container. This is because the upper layers may not have attained temperatures high enough to kill weed seeds and soil-borne pathogens. If you apply this to your soil, there is a chance that weeds might erupt with the slightest soil disturbance. Therefore, I recommend removing a thin layer from the top of the compost and a little from the sides of the pile as well and adding it to your next batch.

You can sift the rest of the compost to remove the components that have failed to decompose or have only partially decomposed. This will include items like fruit pits, twigs, eggshells, and plastic (which have accidentally mixed in with the other components). You can put compostable items back into the compost pile for the next batch.

## Using Compost in Gardening

Using compost intelligently on your soil is another important soil management practice. This trick is to figure out how much of it can be used on which part of your garden or farm. Let's consider some of these uses.

### As an Amendment

Compost can be your go-to soil amendment whenever you feel like your soil is losing its vigor. It can be used to amend soils in greenhouses, raised beds, container gardens, and in-ground soils. The United States Environmental Protection Agency (2018) recommends applying 2–4 inches (≈5–10 cm) of compost to the top 6–9 inches (≈15–22 cm) of your soil.

### As Mulch

Mulch is required in most farming practices, and compost can be used as mulch everywhere. In soils that need to be kept covered, such as raised beds and container soil, compost serves as an excellent mulch. Compost can also be added to the borders of raised beds, container plants, and large trees to prevent soil erosion and retain moisture. Even if the compost is not fully cured and contains some undecomposed scraps, you can add it to the soil and allow the scraps to decompose in place. They will eventually break down, adding organic matter to the soil. The best way to incorporate compost into the soil is by loosening the top 2–3 inches (≈5-7 cm) of soil and applying a 3-inch-thick (≈7 cm) layer on top of it.

Compost can serve as an ideal soil cover during the winter season when the soil often lies barren. Adding it to the soil and allowing earthworms to mix it thoroughly will provide you with the perfect medium for planting your next crop at the end of winter.

### As a Growth Medium

Compost can also serve as an ideal growth medium on its own, provided that it is perfectly cured and free from all types of problematic agents. For instance, it can replace peat-based potting media and also be used for planting certain indoor container plants.

### Uses of Compost By-Products

Compost is not the sole product that you obtain at the end of the months-long composting process. There are several other by-products and many additional uses that you can obtain from them. Remember the compost teas that we discussed in Chapter 3? They are an incredible source of nutrients for no-till soils and container plants. There is a slightly different compost product known as compost extract. It is a quicker version of compost tea and contains fewer nutrients and microorganisms than compost tea. Compost extracts can also be used as foliar sprays and as direct soil applications to boost soil and plant health. Additionally, we have biochar, which is a form of coal created by heat-treating crop-based residues in a low-oxygen environment. Biochar can be applied to soil to improve fertility, moisture content, and texture.

Compost and all of its by-products are easy to make and apply to the soil, but most importantly, they are eco-friendly and offer a sustainable method for farmers to dispose of the organic waste that is frequently produced on farms. Learning the composting techniques and implementing them correctly on your farm, however, is key to ensuring that you are putting your organic waste to good use.

Even when we treat our soil in the best possible way and take care of all its characteristics, it can still encounter certain problems. The reason is not that our practices are flawed but that soil is a delicate entity that is not present in a vacuum; it is surrounded by dozens of biotic and abiotic factors. No matter how cautious we are in our soil management practices, soil can sometimes run into issues. Therefore, part of soil management involves being able to recognize these problems and take steps to remedy them.

## Salinity

In general terms, soil salinity refers to the amount of salts that are present in dissolved form in the soil. Typically, these salts are present only in amounts that are beneficial for plant growth; however, problems arise when these salts begin to accumulate in the root zone. When water comes into contact with salt, it dissolves and either accumulates in the root zone or is brought up to the surface of the soil. This affects the movement of water and nutrients in the soil, and consequently, soil health and plant growth are impacted.

There are various reasons for salt buildup in the soil, but we can divide these causes into two main categories: naturally occurring salinity and human-induced salinity. Naturally induced salinity is caused when soil is

●      waterlogged.

●      submerged in seawater.

●      covered with salt blown in from coastal areas.

●      affected by drought.

Causes of human-induced salinity include

●      usage of salty irrigation water

●       irrigation in the absence of proper drainage conditions

●   excessive irrigation of heavier, less porous soil in dry areas

●      compaction of soil

### Symptoms

Indications that soil might be affected by salinity include

●       soil compaction followed by crusting

●       white crusts on soil

●       soil that fails to drain even days after rain

●       inhibited seed germination

●       water-stressed plants

### What Can You Do?

Well, the best way to protect your soil from the wide-ranging consequences of salinity is to focus on promoting its structure and texture. Soils with good texture are generally effective at resisting salt accumulation. Recognizing early signs of salinity is also crucial. The earlier you treat the soil, the greater its chances of full recovery. Here’s what you can do to treat soils affected by salinity:

●   Add compost teas and other organic matter–rich components to the soil to promote the growth of salt-tolerant, plant-growth-promoting bacteria and other microbes. Salt-tolerant microbes are the best solution for addressing salt stress and promoting plant growth in saline soils.

●    Flush the soil using surface and subsurface irrigation methods. This can help dissolve and remove the salt that has accumulated on the surface.

●   No-till practices can also help, as these practices protect and promote the growth of beneficial, salt-tolerant soil microbes and can improve drainage.

●    Deep tillage can help in extreme cases. Tilling can enhance drainage and help remove excess salt with rainwater.

●       Adding compost as mulch can improve soil texture and microbial diversity, thus making the soil more resistant to salt stress.

## Erosion

As we discussed before, erosion is a natural process that causes the removal of topsoil at all times due to the action of forces such as wind and water. However, it becomes a problem when it occurs at an unprecedented rate on agricultural soil, resulting in the loss of soil resources. We have discussed how our agricultural practices cause the loss of topsoil in Chapter 3. Here, let’s focus on its most common signs and remedies.

### Symptoms

Soil erosion can be hard to recognize especially on small plots and growth patches, but the following signs can help in the early diagnosis of the problem:

●   Sediment formation on the soil surface that was previously leveled

●  Landscape changes including the appearance of rocks that were not visible before

●       Appearance of bald spots in areas that are normally covered in vegetation

●     The muddy appearance of water in streams and irrigation canals

●   Appearance of cracks and little rivulets along the bank of streams and irrigation canals

●      Exposed roots on the surface of the soil

### What Can You Do?

Topsoil is the most precious resource for any growers, and to protect it from erosion, the following steps can play a huge role:

●       Cover cropping during off-season

●       Adding mulch to soils that are prone to erosion

●       Using green manures

●    Using coir logs and wattles on sloppy areas and borders of fields. They consist of tubes filled with tightly packed coconut fiber

●      Irrigating the soil using methods that do not splash water onto the soil

●      Adding compost to the soil frequently as it acts as a protective cushioning layer

●     Farming land using no-till practices

●  Planting  rows  of plants and shrubs as windbreakers that can break the speed of wind, thus lowering the chances of erosion

## Compaction

Recall our discussion about soil structure, where we learned about the characteristics of an ideal soil type. An ideal soil texture is porous, crumbly, and has a balanced ratio of soil particles. Compaction ruins all of this, turning perfectly healthy soil into a rigid, infertile medium. In a healthy soil sample, porosity is around 50–60%; however, in a compacted one, it is reduced to around 30–40% (Testbook, 2018). This results in a significant decrease in the number of macropores and micropores, which are responsible for allowing the movement of air and water, the growth of roots, and the retention of extra water for plants and microbes.

In commercial farming, the use of heavy machinery is considered the primary cause of soil compaction. In small-scale farming and gardens, the role of heavy machinery is less significant, but it is still one of the main contributors to these problems. Additionally, foot traffic, excessive tilling, and untimely field operations also play a significant role in causing soil compaction.

### Symptoms

Soil compaction is easy to detect. The following are the main signs of compacted soil:

●     Swollen and malnourished root tips

●  Poor water absorbance and drainage (noticeable after rainfall)

●      Stunted plant growth

●      Yellowing of leaves

●      Bald patches on land (where grasses are struggling to grow)

●   Increased  growth  of  weeds that can resist soil compaction (those like knotweed, goosegrass, and bluegrass)

### What Can You Do?

Since most compaction results from the use of heavy machinery, reducing the reliance on such machinery and opting for lighter versions is the best way to prevent and eliminate this problem.

Reducing foot traffic on garden beds is the next best strategy to avoid compaction. I recommend creating designated walking paths using stones or gravel and covering the rest of the garden with mulch to discourage people from stepping on the soil. When stepping onto the soil is unavoidable, you can use wooden planks as a temporary walking path. You can also use tools with extra-long handles to work the soil from a distance. These include tools like long-handled cultivators and weeders.

Working on wet soil should always be avoided, and cover crops should be planted during the off seasons to allow the soil to recover its structure and heal from compaction. Cover crops, known for their tillage properties, should be planted in such soils. Lastly, frequent addition of organic matter and other organic amendments that promote and protect soil microbes is a great way to avoid and deal with compaction issues.

## Nutrient Deficiencies and Toxicities

The soil’s nutrient profile is a crucial determinant of its overall fertility. This profile dictates the amount of each nutrient accessible to plant roots and the quantity that can actually enter the plant. Therefore, maintaining nutrient levels in the soil is extremely important for keeping it healthy and fertile. However, soil nutrient availability is a complex topic, as it is not merely a straightforward process of nutrients entering the roots from the soil; several factors influence this process.

First, there is a concept known as the total concentration of a nutrient, which refers to all forms in which a nutrient exists within the soil. Even in infertile soils, the total nutrient concentration can be high. The available nutrient pool is a term that refers to the amount of a nutrient that is actually usable by the plant. This pool of nutrients is generated by the breakdown of soil minerals, organic matter, and chemical exchanges between minerals, organic matter, and soil solution. The ease of availability of this pool is further influenced by soil pH, soil moisture content, soil microbes, and certain chemical species present within the soil. Now, obviously, we cannot influence all of these factors to ensure that the entire pool of nutrients enters our plants, but we can certainly do some things to make sure that plants access what they need. Care needs to be taken here because we also don't want our plants to face nutrient toxicities.

Let's first discuss the common signs of nutrient deficiencies and toxicities.

### Signs of Nutrient Deficiency

Common signs of nutrient deficiencies in the plant include the following:

●   Leaf color changing from green to yellow, brown, red, and even purple

●     Delayed or stunted plant growth

●     Poor or delayed flowering and fruiting

●     Short and slender stems

●     Bottom leaves dying prematurely

### Signs of Nutrient Toxicity

The following are some common signs that show the toxicity of nutrients in plants:

●  Plants growing excessively or showing stunted growth

●      Loss of green color in the leaves (chlorosis)

●      Premature death of roots and leaves

●      Plants with reduced branching

●   Black or brown spotting on leaves (necrotic spotting) that indicates cell death

These signs and symptoms are clear indications of nutrient imbalance in the soil, but you don’t have to wait for these signs to appear before taking any remedial steps. If you are diligently monitoring your soil and soil testing regularly, you can predict such problems by examining the values of the total nutrient concentration, pH, and CEC of the soil. The best time to get annual soil testing done is early spring (Montana State University, 2019). For this test, on-field chemical testing kits can be used or a professional soil analysis can be ordered at a nearby lab. However, for confirmation of nutrient imbalances, a plant leaf analysis is required. With this analysis, you can determine the exact course of your correction plan.

### Correcting Nutrient Imbalances

For the correction of minor nutrient deficiencies, growers often resort to the application of compost and manure; however, this cannot correct the problem if the deficiency is severe. To treat such nutrient deficiencies, carefully planned applications of fertilizers and amendments are necessary. Take a look at a few of these fertilizer and amendment options.

| Type of Nutrient Deficiency || Recommended Fertilizer/Amendment |
| --- | --- |
| Basic Nutrient Deficiency (of elemental N, P, and K) || Alfalfa Meal and Worm Castings |
| Nitrogen Deficiency || Urea, Ammonium Sulfate,  
and Blood Meal |
| Calcium and Trace Element Deficiency || Bone Meal |
| Iron Deficiency || Chelated Iron |
| Magnesium and Sulfur Deficiency || Magnesium Sulfate |
| General Micronutrient Deficiency || Fish Powder or Fishmeal Emulsion |

The application of calculated amounts of these fertilizers and amendments can play a significant role in treating the soil. Before application, factors such as nutrient uptake timing and nutrient removal rates need to be taken into account, as we want all of these products to be absorbed by the plant roots and not lost to surface runoff. More importantly, the calculated application of fertilizers is also necessary because we do not want to create nutrient toxicities, which can occur if we apply too much of an amendment.

Dealing with nutrient toxicities is not as challenging as dealing with nutrient deficiencies, as most of the time, the excess nutrients accumulate in the soil and are not made available to plant roots due to the soil’s natural chemistry. However, sometimes, toxicities can create problems by hindering the availability of particular nutrients to the plants. For instance, potassium toxicity can disrupt the supply of magnesium and calcium to the roots.

Prolonged application of fertilizers in excess can also change the pH of the soil, thus creating a myriad of soil problems. To avoid such nutrient toxicities in the first place, the amount and duration of fertilizer application need to be decided carefully. If, for some reason, toxicity develops, the soil should be flushed with water to leach the excess nutrients. Checking whether the problem-causing nutrient is present in the irrigation water is also important, and if that truly is the problem, changing water sources or filtering it before use could be helpful. Additionally, implementing cover crops during the off-season and practicing crop rotations can help the soil recover from nutrient toxicities by diversifying nutrient uptake patterns.

## Testing and Improving Soil Health

Soil is an ever-changing entity. Topsoil is always under the influence of weathering forces. Its internal chemistry fluctuates with changes in microbial communities, but most importantly, our farming practices are constantly altering its chemistry and biology. As growers, we must acknowledge these changes and learn ways to keep the soil fertile under these fluctuating conditions. I strongly believe that troubleshooting soil problems should be a proactive strategy rather than a reactive one; therefore, checking soil characteristics should be a common practice in all farming systems, regardless of their scale.

### Soil Testing Methods

Take a look at some popular soil testing methods and the role they can play in keeping growers informed about soil. Remember that soil pH testing (discussed in Chapter 1) and mineral analysis (discussed earlier in this chapter) are two of the most important tests when it comes to determining and maintaining soil health. The following tests are easy compared to these two, and anyone can conduct them and interpret their results.

#### The Soil Jar Test

This is perhaps the easiest soil test. Remember the soil texture we discussed in Chapter 1? This test helps determine that. All it requires is a clear mason jar, a little dishwashing liquid, a soil sample, and some water. By mixing all the ingredients in the jar and allowing it to sit for 24 to 48 hours, the soil particles will separate into distinct layers. You can then determine the composition and texture of your soil based on the thickness of each layer and the turbidity of the water.

●     If  the water in the jar is almost clear and the bottom layer (composed of sand) is the thickest, it is clear that your soil is sandy.

●     If  the water in the jar is extremely turbid and two or three thin layers of particles have collected at the bottom of the jar, your soil is clay soil.

●   If the water in the jar is clear and three separate layers of sand, silt, and clay have formed at the bottom, your soil has a loamy texture.

#### Soil Microscopy

It’s no secret that soil organisms are the heroes of soil that transform it from mere dirt into a fertile, living entity. The complex interactions that exist between these organisms, soil aggregates, and plant roots ultimately determine the productivity of the soil. Therefore, keeping a close eye on the biology of the soil is necessary in order to maintain soil richness and fertility.

For the analysis of microorganisms, an examination of a soil microscopic slide with a microscope of at least 400x magnification is required. You can either purchase a microscope or consult a laboratory to conduct this analysis for you. Collecting multiple soil samples from different spots in your garden or farm at a depth of 2 inches (≈5 cm) will provide a comprehensive analysis of the microorganisms present in your soil. You will be able to observe four types of microorganisms: bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and microscopic animals.

Bacteria appear as small, rounded, single-celled entities. Fungi appear as single-celled organisms with long, thread-like bodies (mycelium). Protozoa include amoeba, ciliates, and flagellates, while microscopic animals include tardigrades, rotifers, and nematodes. In a healthy soil sample, bacteria are the highest in number (hundreds to thousands can fit on a microscopic slide). Fungi and protozoa are few in number, and microscopic animals are found only occasionally.

According to Short Guide to Soil Microscopy 2 (n.d.), this is what you can infer from soil microscopy:

●     If the number of bacteria is high but there is little diversity among them, the soil has been cultivated for a long time and needs thorough regeneration.

●   If the number of ciliates is high, the soil is compacted and lacks oxygen.

●      If fungal mycelium is absent, the soil lacks organic matter.

●     If a mix of all microorganisms is present, your soil is perfectly healthy.

#### Water Availability Test

This is an easy test to determine how your soil is performing in terms of holding water and providing it to plant roots. This can help you ascertain the watering requirements of your plants and how your soil’s irrigation needs are changing over time. All you need for this test is a good rain spell. You will then notice how long your soil stays wet and how your plants remain hydrated before there is an actual need to water them again. You can also dip an ice cream stick to check for soil moisture levels. If the stick comes out relatively clean, the soil has lost most of its water; if it comes out dirty, it still contains sufficient moisture.

To interpret the results of this test, you will need to gather knowledge about the normal irrigation requirements of the soil in your area. If you feel that your soil requires frequent watering sessions that are atypical for your region, then your soil may be problematic. There may be some issues with soil porosity or its texture.

### Amendments and Treatments

Based on the results of these tests, you can determine the issues your soil is currently facing and then identify the most suitable solutions to address them.

If your soil jar test indicates an undesirable soil texture, you will need to take immediate action to remedy it. Amending soil texture is not possible once the soil has been cultivated. If the soil is sandy, you will have to amend it to improve water retention, either by adding more clay or, if that’s not possible, by incorporating organic matter such as manure, compost, pine bark fines, and coir. For clay soils, the addition of coarse builder’s sand and organic matter can be beneficial.

To improve soil microbial diversity, soil conditioners and amendments like humus and compost teas can be highly effective, as both contain large populations of microbes. The application of mycorrhizal fungi can help increase beneficial fungi in the soil. Commercially available nitrogen-fixing bacteria inoculants can also be used to address this issue. For soil that demonstrates poor water availability, peat moss and blended topsoil can be mixed in to enhance soil structure and water retention. Compost and humus are also excellent options.

The only thing you must keep in mind while addressing these problems is the nature of the amendment or fertilizer. Both organic and inorganic solutions are available, but as responsible growers, our inclination should always lean toward organic solutions. Inorganic amendments may be inexpensive and readily available, but organic options tend to have the least environmental impact and offer the greatest benefits for the soil.

# Glossary

●       Bulk density: A material property that is measured by dividing the mass of its particles from the total mass of the material.

●   Carbon Sequestration: The capturing of the free carbon in the atmosphere and its conversion into organic compounds that are then stored in soil and living things.

●  Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC):  The inherent ability of soil or a growth medium to hold exchangeable ions (cations) so that they are available to the plant roots for uptake.

●   Cover Crops: The plants grown with the aim of covering the soil and adding organic content to it.

●       Cropping Season: A period within which crops are grown and harvested.

●    Crop Rotation: A sustainable agriculture practice involving the growth of different plants in the same area over the course of several cropping seasons.

●      Eluviation: A process through which water moves through soil layers, carrying other minerals and salts with it.

●     Erosion: The removal and transportation of rocks, minerals, and other particles from one place on the Earth’s crust to another due to the action of natural forces such as water, wind, and other agents.

●   Exudates: The chemical compounds released by plant roots into the soil to influence plant growth and soil chemistry.

●  Finished compost: A crumbly, earthly-smelling compost that has been thoroughly composted by the decomposers and contains a high number of dead microorganisms.

●      Fumigation: The process of disinfecting an area or a material using the fumes from a chemical.

●       Green Manure: A crop grown and then plowed into the soil in order to improve its texture and fertility.

●   Humus: A finely divided form of organic matter that is formed after the decomposition of animal and plant residues in the soil.

●  Integrated Pest Management (IPM):  An environmentally responsible approach to pest management that employs a combination of chemical, physical, and biological components to target and eliminate pests without disturbing other organisms or harming the environment.

●   Leaching: A process that involves washing away soil nutrients and salts from the upper soil layers to the lower layers or into nearby water bodies due to factors like heavy rain or irrigation.

●      Mulching: The practice of covering soil with a material (mostly of organic nature) to maintain soil fertility and characteristics.

●     Peds: The clumps of soil particles or aggregates of soil.

●   Soil succession: The process through which soil and plant communities change and develop over time on barren land to convert it into fertile soil with diverse communities.

●       Weathering: A breakdown of rocks that occurs due to rain, biological activity, and extreme temperatures.
