4. The social and psychological impact of cyberattacks

# Learnt helplessness

Findings show that less than 1 in 10 people (9%) claim that they feel ‘very’ safe online. Also, only half (51%) of adults aware, would change the way they behave online if they became a victim (Symantec, 2010). This provides an interesting comparison to earlier discussions. People might accept a situation, even if it feels unpleasant just because they cannot understand it or not know enough about it. Following this point, one may argue that people accept cyberattacks because of a sense of ‘learnt helplessness’.

Due to a sense of learned helplessness (for more on the term, see: Seligman, 1975; Hirtz, 1998) and a lack of knowledge about online attacks and ways to resolve an incident, users may simply accept the possibility of being victims. Indirectly, a key question becomes, whether they also accept the reality of impacts and how the severity is low. The anonymous nature of cybercrime can lead to an acceptance that one (e.g., an individual, industry, government) will become a victim of cybercrime at some point. Moreover, the sense of learned helplessness can potentially also result in a low uptake of protective security behaviours.

Users are called upon to make many security-related decisions every day which can cause anxiety. These behaviour include: (a) not opening an email from a sender they do not recognize, (b) not accessing attachments, (c) not downloading and running programs from trustworthy sources, (d) the use of antivirus software and security software (e.g., firewall) and creating regular backups. Some of these decisions can also cause the user feelings of anxiety due to a lack of knowledge about the possible implications of making incorrect decisions.

Members of the public have often reported a lack of knowledge about a number of key areas within the cybersecurity domain. A few examples are lack of knowledge about how to use security packages, how to secure their technology devices and the threats online. When these individuals are aware of the threats, they may report that they do not understand them (Gross, Canetti, & Vashi, 2017). These low levels of public understanding of cyber threats and security practices could lead to a lack of public engagement with security issues and a general loss of confidence in cyber and/technology. This also has been seen in the domain of information privacy in the context of new forms of technology, where some users now consider privacy ‘the boring bit’ (Williams, Nurse, & Creese, 2017). These issues characterize the broad social impacts.
