This suggests the notion of FOMO could be used to explain why individuals spend more time online, due to the fear of missing out rewarding experiences. Those with increased negative emotional experiences are likely to experience greater FOMO (Prybylski et al., 2013), which can increase cyber victimization in the home and social environment (Alt, 2015). In relation to cybercrime, research has found an association between time online in the home setting and increased risks of cyber victimization (Song, Lynch, & Cochran, 2016). This was especially true for those using the Internet when they were unemployed or engaged in online shopping, online banking, and in cases, sexting and exploitation (Song et al., 2016). For example, users who spend more time online, particularly through online shopping, were more likely to be subject to cyber fraud victimization (Pratt, Holtreifer, & Reig, 2010). In addition, users who spend time online for Internet banking are 50% more likely to experience cyber victimization through identity theft (Ryns, 2013). This highlights the propensity for cyber victimization to those whose spend time in the online domain. Government and company organizations can ensure employees are aware of the risks and dangers associated with increased online use, to promote increased awareness and protection through additional security measures.

The school, home and family structure has also been explored as a factor for cyber victimization acrossbullying, cyber threats and crimes. For example, parents who exhibit high levels of control for technology use and mediation were less likely to be subject to cyber victimization in the home environment (Aoyama, Utsumi, & Hasegawa, 2012). Additionally, parents who took a greater interest in their children's online activity through discussions and moderation of time spent online were less vulnerable to cyber threats and attacks (Elsasser, Russell, Ohnessian, & Patton, 2017; Taiariolo, 2010; Floros, Siomos, Fisoun, Daafouli, & Geroukalis (2013) examined family and protective factors for cyber victimization showing that adolescents who experienced a greater parental warmth, closeness and support from parents were less likely to experience cyber victimization. Considering the family structure, research has shown increased cyber victimization in single-parent settings compared to other family structures. This suggests additional support should be tailored to ward single-parent families. Reflecting on the educational setting, consistent research has identified an increased positive school climate, quality and perceived safety act as protective factors for future cyber victimization (Bevilacqua et al., 2017; Kowalski et al., 2014; Williams & Guerra, 2007).
