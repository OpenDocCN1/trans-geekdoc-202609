
# Cyberspace capabilities and the offence—defense balances of the United States, China and Russia

## The United States

The United States makes a clear distinction between its offensive and defensive capabilities. Although information on the United States's cybersecurity weapons and capabilities is relatively limited, behavioral precedents, political debate and statistics reveal the United States's offensive–defense balance in cyberspace.

The stated goals of the US Department of Defense demonstrate that the development of offensive and defensive cyberspace remains a top security priority. Consistent increases in the cybersecurity budget since 2015 confirm this priority. According to Admiral Rogers, former director of both the National Security Agency (NSA) and the US Cyber Command, the United States's commitment to bolstering its offensive and defensive capabilities was encouraged by increases in activity and aggressive behavior in Russia, China, Iran and North Korea's digital spaces (Gerden, 2016).

In 2017, spending on offensive and defensive operations in the general defense budget increased by 6.7 million dollars. In 2017, the cybersecurity budget reached 900 million dollars. Despite reductions to some defense budgets in 2018, cybersecurity remained a budgeted priority with an allocation of 971 million dollars (Office of the Under Secretary of Defense, 2017).

The United States Cyber Command, the United States's official cybersecurity force, was established in 2009. In 2017, the US Department of Defense assigned 133 units (approximately 5000 armed force members) to the Cyber Command. By the end of 2018, the Cyber Command is expected to have 6200 service members (DoD, 2017). A reform of the United States's cyber forces in 2017 to distinguish between the functions of the Cyber Command and the NSA. The US Cyber Command is a military formation. Therefore, it requires isolated infrastructure to collect intelligence on (and when necessary or destroy) the cyber capabilities of the United States' adversaries. According to former director of the NSA, Michael Hayden, the NSA does not foresee American offensive activity in cyberspace as cyberspace is more operationally and technologically complex and requires the professional infiltration of information networks, an inescapable takeover and the destruction of data (Pomerleau, 2017).

Although the United States has the capacity to counteract or execute extensive offensives, it prioritizes restraining escalation in cyberspace. This emphasis on restraint was illustrated by the President Obama's reactions to Russian cyberattacks in 2015 and Russia's 2016 cyberattacks on interference in the US presidential elections.

According to John Brennan, a former director of the CIA, President Obama decided to confine the United States' response to verbally warn Putin when sufficient evidence surfaced Russia's interference in the United States' internal political processes. The US security services created several potential responses, including an offensive attack on Russia. Obama rejected offensive-attack scenarios, as he did not want the escalation of the cyberspace conflict to lead to war (Miller, 2017). This situation demonstrates that at least during the Obama administration, the use of the United States' offensive cybersecurity capabilities was assessed with extreme caution, and the risk of a cyber conflict was perceived as both real and threatening.

The United States tends to develop its offensive and defensive security capabilities equally. The United States' large cybersecurity budget and the precedent United States set in the malicious computer worm Stuxnet demonstrate that the United States is capable of executing effective offensive cyberattacks. Until 2016, the United States' security culture and cyberspace politics were dominated by Obama's political restraint regarding the country's offensive cyberspace capabilities, and priority was to establish a defensive advantage.

## China

China makes a clear distinction between its offensive and defensive capabilities. An analysis of China's strategic security culture, specific behavioral precedents, cyberspace capability structures and primary actors reveal that offensive dominate China's cyberspace policy.

China's actors, who perform both offensive and defensive operations, are divided into two groups.
- The first group consists of professional hackers, contracted by the PLA.
- The second group consists of patriotic hackers, who periodically perform cyberattacks ordered by the state.

A general department of the PLA serves as China's cyber coordination center. It functions similarly to the United States' NSA and comprises of more than 13,000 experts. China has the largest cybersecurity force in the world (see Table 6.4). Each of its departments, PLA Blue 61398 (revealed by the American information technology company, Meiant) employs experts in the fields of information technology, electrical engineering, mathematics and linguistics. China's main task is to collect intelligence on foreign countries' military and technology innovations and industries. Intelligence on unconfirmed anti-China security, doctrine and defense strategies are frequently collected as well (Kozlowski, 2015).

## Russia

Although Russia does not make a clear distinction between its offensive and defensive capabilities in its strategic documents, Russia’s behavioral precedents provide insights into its defensive–offensive balance.

Since 2011, the Russian Army has prioritized its ability to carry out 'informational confrontation'. This concept reflects Russia's emphasis on securing an informational advantage that is perceived as a collection of diplomatic, economic, political, military and cultural measures aimed at creating technical and psychological impact. According to a Defense Intelligence Agency assessment (2017) of Russia's military power, Russia is attempting to use its information and psychological operations to prevent its enemies from acting. Given this, the principle of informational confrontation implies the weaponization of information policy, which a priori generates offensive-oriented policy. Furthermore, by conducting psychological operations during peacetime, Russia demonstrates an offensive information space. In other words, both cyber and psychological operations in information space should be seen as exclusively offensive.

Although Russia's government denies its affiliations with hackers, hacking groups are among Russia's most effective information warfare tools. Because it is relatively easy to navigate cyberspace anonymously, assigning responsibility for cyberattacks is problematic, yet there are a number of groups known to serve Kremlin's political goals. Russia's intelligence officers and hired cybercriminals often carry out cyberattacks jointly. In March 2017, the US Department of Justice indicted two members of Russia's intelligence agency, FSB, and two hackers hired by Russia's government for their coordinated roles in the heist of 500 million Yahoo user accounts against Russian government officials. In July 2018, Special Counsel Robert Mueller indicted twelve intelligence officers for hacking the Democratic National Committee and Hillary Clinton's presidential campaign in 2016.

Although Russia's official information security budget is much lower than the United States or China's, Russia prioritizes the development of its offensive capabilities. In 2015, Russia has spoken facto of strengthening its offensive information space capabilities to create an effective system of cyber defense. Russia plans to allocate between 200 and 250 million dollars for this purpose (Gerden, 2016).
