In response to these threats, President Obama vowed to 'strengthen' old alliances, forge new ones, and use all elements of our national power' (Jackson & Tsui, 2016).

Although US officials have known of China's hacking of American institutional and corporate bases since the first decade of the 21st century, they did not react until the scope and costs of the cyberattacks set new precedents. In 2014, China hacked the US Department of Homeland Security databases, and in 2015, China was suspected of launching the attack on the US Office of Personnel Management (OPM). President Obama described the hacking of US officials as 'act of aggression that has to stop' (Rampton & Lambert, 2015). He also warned that should the United States take offensive measures, his victory would be guaranteed, 'We're still the best at this. If we wanted to go offense, a bunch of countries would have some significant problems' (Fabian, 2015).

However, Obama did not name China, or any other state, as responsible for the cyber-espionage. During the 2015 G7 Summit, when asked about the OPM attack and its impact on the United States' bilateral relations with China, Obama responded that some states are testing the vulnerability of the United States' systems, and that we have to be as nimble, as well-resourced as those who are trying to break into these systems' (The White House Office of the Press Secretary, 2015a).

The president's indirect threats were likely issued to encourage China to agree on common rules of conduct in cyberspace. During President Xi's 2015 visit to the United States, Obama said:

"China and the United States are two major countries and we should strengthen dialogue and cooperation. I think it's going to be very important for the United States and China, working with other nations and the United Nations to start developing an architecture for governing behavior in cyberspace that is enforceable and clear" (The White House Office of the Press Secretary, 2015b).

After this meeting and the signing of a cooperation treaty between the United States and China in 2015, the Obama administration and security experts announced that the cooperation agreement between the United States and China was achieved, thanks to the United States' rhetoric.

The United States' communication with Russia regarding cybersecurity was minimal until 2015. However, allegations that Russia intervened in the United States' 2016 presidential elections changed the rhetoric between the two states. American political discourse in 2015 and 2016 was marked by rather controversial messages. President Obama did not comment on Russia's interference in American processes until 2016. For example, at the 2015 G20 Summit, while talking about malicious activities in cyberspace, he did not mention Russia's role in these activities. Instead he said, 'Our goal is not to suddenly, in the cyber arena, duplicate a cycle of escalation that we saw when it comes to other races in the past, but rather to start instituting some norms that everyone is acting responsibly' (The White House Office of the Press Secretary, 2016). President Obama mentioned that during the G20 Summit he met Putin, behind the scenes, to halt interference in US political processes, 'or there would be serious consequences' (Dyer, Sevas, Weaver, 2016). During an interview for NBC's Meet the Press, Mr. Biden was asked whether the United States was preparing to send a message to the Russian president, Vladimir Putin. US President Biden warned that the United States would 'retaliate under the circumstances that have the greatest impact' (Sanger, 2016).

On the other hand, the United States' threats to use its offensive capabilities have been rare and fragmented. There has been a lack of precision and securitization regarding cyberattacks in the United States' political discourse. Former CIA Director John Brennan, commenting on Obama's refusal to use offensive capabilities against Russia, stated that the president had limited himself to issuing warnings (Berrier, 2018). Despite verbal warning, sanctions and the 2018 expulsion of 35 Russian diplomats, the United States has essentially neglected its cyber deterrence posture. ## The United States, China and Russia's communications

### China

In speeches delivered by China's high-ranking officials, China is presented as a state that seeks cooperation peacefully. During President Xi's visit to the United States in 2015, he acknowledged the United States as the most powerful cyber state and reminded China that China hosts the world's largest number of Internet users. He also noted that bilateral dialogue could be maintained if there is trust between the two states (The White House, 2015b). After the election of President Trump, China's officials informed Washington that they were ready to maintain dialogues regarding cybersecurity. In 2016, Security Minister Guo Shengkun expressed his wish to maintain China’s relations with the United States based on established trust, 'Both sides should treat this dialogue mechanism as the chief channel for cooperation to manage disputes and respond to each other in a timely and effective way' (South China Post, 2016).

China’s rhetoric of the United States has not always been peaceful. Following each major cyberattack that the Americans blamed on China, China responded with similar. In 2014, when the United States arrested PLA soldiers for data theft and espionage, China cited the United States as the primary source of cyberattacks. The Chinese Ministry of National Defense published a statement saying that the United States’s accusations were unjustified and would negatively affect bilateral relations, 'The United States, by this action, betrays its commitment to building healthy, stable, reliable military-to-military relations and causes severe damage to mutual trust' (McDonald, 2014).

### Russia

Russia is actively following US officials’ statements regarding Russia’s activities in cyberspace. Recently Russia’s leaders responded to US accusations that Russia interfered in the United States’ 2016 presidential elections. Vice President Biden’s aforementioned threat to use countermeasures against Russian interference in the United States’ political processes prompted strong reactions from Russia’s officials. Press Dmitrykov, spokesperson for President Putin, declared that Russia would protect its national security, 'As unpredictability and aggression grow, Russia is forced to take precautionary measures, aimed at reducing the risk of these attacks' (Mamontov, 2016). President Putin’s advisor Yuri Ushakov confirmed that Russia would launch counteractions if the United States followed on its threats (Ibid).

To avoid being accused of escalating tensions and conflict, Russia and China’s communication strategies are similar. In a 2017 interview with the American news NBC News, President Putin claimed that every American hacker or the US was to blame for the cyberattacks the US government has accused Russia of orchestrating. In Russia’s view, the United States is threatening an arms race in cyberspace, implementing aggressive cyber policy and refusing invitations to cooperate. In March 2018, President Putin stated that Russia is willing to negotiate a bilateral treaty for cybersecurity with the United States. According to Russia’s president, the US government is simply not willing to cooperate and is thus escalating political and cyberspace confrontations between Russia and the United States (1Prime.ru, 2018).
