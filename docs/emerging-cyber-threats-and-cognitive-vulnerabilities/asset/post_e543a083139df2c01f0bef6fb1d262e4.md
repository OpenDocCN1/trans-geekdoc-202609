
## 1. Factors leading to cyber victimization

Cyberbullying literature produces an inconsistent overview in terms of gender and age profiles. A number of studies concerning young people indicate females are disproportionately represented as victims of cyberbullying (Gorgiz & Olsson, 2013; Lasher & Baker, 2015) which have found equal victimization prevalence rates for males and females (Hinduja & Patchin, 2008; Tokunaga, 2010). Fewer studies have considered cyberbullying in adulthood or the workplace; a metaanalysis of victimization literature concluded no substantial evidence for gender differentiation (Lund & Ross, 2017). A multi-national study of 3500 participants aged 15–30 years found no age bias results were statistically more likely to be male and the most experience to be defamation of character and threats of violence (Nasi, Oksanen, Keipi, & Räsänen, 2015). Cyber-victimization has been demonstrated by Kowalski, Toth, and Morgan (2018) to continue after adolescence. Compelling results indicate cyberbullying experiences are more likely to be reported than traditional acts of bullying in adulthood and in the workplace. From these findings, it is clearly paramount for future research to consider cyberbullying from the perspective of a wider field of age groups.

There is scarce amount of literature available concerning cybercrime and minority groups with findings being inconsistent and contradictory. A narrative systematic review of cyberbullying and minority groups found varying for high involvement of victimization for minority and majority groups, with no difference found between the groups (Hamm et al., 2015). Despite these findings, analysis form Tynes, Reynolds, and Greenfield (2003) indicated that ethnic and racial differences were significantly apparent at prevalent within unmonitored rooms, suggesting that negative attitudes are prevalent within the online context without social controls. Moreover, in terms of cyber-dependent crimes, ethnic minority groups have been identified as being more vulnerable as are less likely than majority groups to have Internet security software and are potentially more exposed to cyberattacks (Ipsos MORI, 2013). Scant research focus has been given to ethnic minority groups and specific cyber-dependent and enabled crimes as well as online sexual harassment, with literature still within the phase of considering homogenous groups. Future research must aim to answer questions heterogeneous groups (Hinduja & Patchin, 2010).

Yar (2005) considers the Internet to a disorganised environment due to the efficiencies the digital world provides which themselves present potential enhanced risks. Leukfeld (2014) concludes that everyone is at risk in terms of cyber-dependent and enabled crimes. Despite this, specific research conclusions have identified some characteristics such as users with high trust scores (Wright, Chakraborty, Basoglu, & Marett, 2010), high submissiveness (Alseadoon, Chan, Foo, & Gonzales Nieto, 2012) are more susceptible to phishing crimes. Research focusing on personality characteristics portray a profile of a potential victim. For instance, individuals who are impulsive or have low self-control (Holt, Bossler, Malinski, & May, 2016), low emotional control (Hempehill & Heerde, 2014) and low self-esteem (Brewer & Kersklake, 2015; Mobin, Feng, & Neudorf, 2017) are more likely to be victimized by cyberbullying. Festl and Quandt (2013) reported personality factors such as conscientiousness and openness to experiences of cyberbullying. Identifying vulnerable individual characteristics enables intervention to target areas of development. However, there is a need for more longitudinal research to consider direction of the association between individual characteristics of victims of cyberspace.

It is important to take into account additional risk factors beyond an individual's characteristics for targeted individuals, groups, companies and the wider community. In particular, how these risk factors impact across different settings including the home and school environment, and neighbouring community need to be acknowledged.

Peer relationships and group status has been recognized across an abundance of research as a strong risk factor for cyber victimization within cyberbullying, cyberthreats and cybercrimes. For example, individuals who experience dissatisfaction with existing friendships/or experiences difficulties creating new ones are likely to experience loneliness and depressive symptoms associated with changes in mood levels, which can predict subsequent cyber victimization (Olenik-Shemesh et al., 2012). Those individuals who exhibit difficulties with social interaction may be struggled to satisfy with peer group relationships and as such experience a higher amount of peer rejection, with increased risk of cyber victimization. These group relationships be an important interaction during adolescent development, and in particular can create challenges in the school environment as adolescents fight to retain and establish power in the peer group. Research shows that adolescents associated with peer rejection from peer groups were more likely to show cyber aggressive and cyber victimization experience (Wright & Li, 2013). As cyber victimization can lead to unpleasant feelings and delinquent behaviour (Mitchell, Ybarra, & Finkelhor, 2007), educational practitioners should work to develop adolescents' cognitive and emotional empathy, to promote stronger peer-group support and friendships, a recognized protective factor for cyber victimization (Frid-Lindström, & Rosvall, 2015; Williams & Guerra, 2007). Those who experience negative emotional reactions from peer rejection are likely to spend more time online and trigger increased social network use. For example, those who higher levels of depression and/or anxiety would spend increased time online and online communications, acting as a risk factor for future cyber victimization (Oberst, Wegmann, Stodt, Brandt, & Chamarro, 2017).

While young people can enjoy the benefits of digital technology, they also recognize the propensity to spend a time online, creating a sense of attachment and scope to interact with others online can cause vulnerabilities (Betts & Spenser, 2017). The accessibility and scope to interact with others who actively portray their real-world life in the online environment for, individuals can be vulnerable to cyber victimization as a result of prior actions. In particular, individuals may regret certain comments or online actions in the future as they are unaware of the long-term wider ramifications (Betts & Spenser, 2017). While children, young people and adults use online communications on a daily basis, adolescence is a period of development that has been associated with increased online communication and interaction (Marcum, Higgins, Roberts, 2010; Ybarra & Mitchell, 2007). The time spent interacting with others has been shown to be a significant risk factor for cyber victimization, as increased time online increases exposure to a potential victim. For example, adolescents who spend more time for non-school work were more likely to engage in perpetration/or victimization involvement (Ávarez-García, Núñez, García, & BarreiroCollazo, 2018). In addition, online users who spend more time online in terms of hours per day are more likely to be vulnerable to cyber victimization and cyber aggressive attacks (Mishna et al., 2012; Rice et al., 2015). In terms of cyberbullying, individuals who exhibited a higher frequency of online communications through social networks were more likely to be involved in cyber victimization and perpetration (Sticca, Ruggieri, Alsaker, & Perren, 2013). The online domain consists of numerous SNS, increasing the opportunity for users to engage in risky online behaviour and vulnerability to cyber threats. In particular, those users who had increased network groups were more likely to exhibit interactions that are not controllable (Buglass, Binder, Betts, & Underwood, 2016). Individuals who use SNS are at greater risk for having personal information exposed, and as such are more likely to experience cyber victimization (Mesch, 2009).

This account pertains to those individuals who perceive others are having rewarding experiences while they are absent. As such, FOMO could provide an explanation for cyber victimization.
