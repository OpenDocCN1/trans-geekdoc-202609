# 1. Factors leading to cyber victimization

Cyberbullying literature produces an inconsistent overview in terms of gender and age profiles. A number of studies concerning young people indicate females are disproportionately represented as victims of cyberbullying (Gorgiz & Olsson, 2013; Lasher & Baker, 2015) which have found equal victimization prevalence rates for males and females (Hinduja & Patchin, 2008; Tokunaga, 2010). Fewer studies have considered cyberbullying in adulthood or the workplace; a metaanalysis of victimization literature concluded no substantial evidence for gender differentiation (Lund & Ross, 2017). A multi-national study of 3500 participants aged 15–30 years found no age bias results were statistically more likely to be male and the most experience to be defamation of character and threats of violence (Nasi, Oksanen, Keipi, & Räsänen, 2015). Cyber-victimization has been demonstrated by Kowalski, Toth, and Morgan (2018) to continue after adolescence. Compelling results indicate cyberbullying experiences are more likely to be reported than traditional acts of bullying in adulthood and in the workplace. From these findings, it is clearly paramount for future research to consider cyberbullying from the perspective of a wider field of age groups.

There is scarce amount of literature available concerning cybercrime and minority groups with findings being inconsistent and contradictory. A narrative systematic review of cyberbullying and minority groups found varying for high involvement of victimization for minority and majority groups, with no difference found between the groups (Hamm et al., 2015). Despite these findings, analysis form Tynes, Reynolds, and Greenfield (2003) indicated that ethnic and racial differences were significantly apparent at prevalent within unmonitored rooms, suggesting that negative attitudes are prevalent within the online context without social controls. Moreover, in terms of cyber-dependent crimes, ethnic minority groups have been identified as being more vulnerable as are less likely than majority groups to have Internet security software and are potentially more exposed to cyberattacks (Ipsos MORI, 2013). Scant research focus has been given to ethnic minority groups and specific cyber-dependent and enabled crimes as well as online sexual harassment, with literature still within the phase of considering homogenous groups.
