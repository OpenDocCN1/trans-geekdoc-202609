In traditional and cyberbullying research, there are four categories of involvement; the victim, the perpetrator, the non-involved and the bully-victim (Kowalski, Limber, & Agatston, 2012; Olweus, 1993; Wong, Chan, & Cheng, 2014). The latter category entails a victim who also engages in perpetrator activity and is generally the minority group with reported low prevalence rates (Lovegrove & Cornell, 2014; Nansel et al., 2001). However, in cyberbullying the prevalence for cyberbullies who are also victims of cyberbullying is much higher (Mishna, Khoury-Kassabri, Gadalla, & Daciuk, 2012; Schultze-Krumholz, Schultz, Zagorsc, Wölfer, & Scheithauer, 2006), with strong evidence to suggest the bully-victim role is the majority group in comparison to the perpetrator and victim groups (Brack & Caltiano, 2014). Moreover, a meta-analysis of 131 studies by Kowalski et al. (2014) highly depicts cyberbullying perpetation as a leading factor for cyber victimization, clearly framing the impression that individuals who bully others are also victimied themselves.
