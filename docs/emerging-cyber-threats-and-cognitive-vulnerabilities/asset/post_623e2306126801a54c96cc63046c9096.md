## Sociocultural approach to the Russian sense of information security

Sociocultural approaches emphasize the interdependence of social and cultural processes in the production of knowledge (John-Steiner & Mahn, 1996, pp. 191–192). In this chapter, the feeling of safety, i.e. the sense of information security is understood as a sociocultural construct.

## 3. Closed, safe and secure — the Russian sense

‘Sociocultural’ refers to the Soviet roots of the social constructivist epistemology, e.g. Lev Vygotsky and the role of social context in the production of knowledge (John-Steiner & Hahn, 1996; Vygotsky 1962, 1978), i.e. all human functioning is socioculturally, historically and institutionally situated (cf. Daniels, 2017). Sociocultural gives an approximate theoretical framework for the production of information security (cf. Sjosted, 2010; Wagoner, 2014) in the Russian ‘unified information space’. In this, the sociocultural approach is used to illustrate how a space influences and shapes peoples’ experiences. This approach has been chosen as it emphasizes the role of the society and represents an original Soviet and Russian way of comprehending the production of knowledge.

In a sociocultural review and analysis are presented examples of the social, cultural and institutional features that can play a significant role in the production of knowledge (Cole, 1994; John-Steiner & Hahn, 1996). The aim is to show relation between individuals and the society as well as culturally created aspects in understanding the development of the sense of information as a collective phenomenon. In this chapter we have identified four examples of sociocultural production that provide a sense of security in the Russian ‘information space’. These examples include the historical fear-based template for the isolation rhetoric, celebrating and adapting ‘Russian way’ of doing things, cleaning together for safer information environment and the state project to control of ‘the national segment of Internet’.

In the sociocultural approach language as a system of expressions plays a fundamental role in the production of knowledge (John-Steiner & Hahn, 1996, p. 192). Language is used as a tool for thinking collectively. ## Historical contextualization of the sense

As noted earlier, in the Russian approach, information space is used by ‘other countries’ and hostile forces for the destabilization of Russia. This kind ‘besieged fortress’ (osazhdanna krepost) mentality characterizes Russian thinking for decades (sees, for example, Heller, 1988, pp. 108–109; Trenin, 2016, pp. 19, 36–39). It follows the very same fear-based template – the enemy is plotting to circle Russia, invade and overthrow the political system – on land, sea, air, and now in the cyber/information space.

Fear cannot exist without a cause to be frightened of (cf. Heller, 1988, pp. 99). After the Russian Revolution of 1917, the Soviet people were deliberately to think of themselves as being surrounded by enemies, the ‘imperialists’, who would crush them if they could. The perception of ‘enemy’ has been repeatedly deployed, to mobilize against actual external danger, and also to justify the struggle against a supposed enemy on domestic ground (Fateev, 1999, pp. 102–104; Solomesch, 2001; Gudkov, 2005, pp. 14–15).

Lev Gudkov (2005) has developed a so-called ‘theory of an enemy’ where the main function of enemy is that the enemy represents a threat to the very existence of the group, e.g. to a society or an organization, to any kind of group that identifies itself as a subject (Gudkov, 2005, p. 13).
