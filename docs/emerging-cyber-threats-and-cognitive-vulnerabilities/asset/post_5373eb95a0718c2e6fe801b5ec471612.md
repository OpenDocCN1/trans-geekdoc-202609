
Three defensive measures (fabrication, protect and withhold), which can be used by individuals, have been identified by other studies (e.g., Lwin, Wirtz Williams, 2007). Fabrication is when the user is trying to disguise his/identity using false information; protect is when the user uses technology to protect his/privacy and withhold when the user refuses to provide information or patronize websites. Another three privacy rules have been prescribed by Metzger (2007), which are withholding information, falsifying information and intention seeking. Furthermore, a simpler classification consists of two dimensions: protection and active protection by Dolnicar and Jordaan (2006) and Yao, Rice and Wallis (2007), respectively. Passive protection is depending on others such as government law to protect privacy, whereas active protection is when users take action to protect their privacy.

Researchers applied many theories trying to understand what affects the behaviour as PMBs, self-protection, fear appeal theory, etc. By reviewing the literature, we identified a gap that religious beliefs are commonly not considered in the study of social media.

Religion is a system of symbols which creates strong, extensive and permanent attitudes and motivation in people (Geertz, 1973). The attitudes and motivation lead to a diverse level of commitment to, which are called religiosity. Religiosity has no impact on individuals and results in a different effect. Accordingly, religiosity cannot be used as a measure by itself, but religiosity, which is the degree of commitment, practice and acceptance, can be used as a tool (Mukhtar & Butt, 2012), Khrain (2010) argued that religiosity is one of the powerful tools that can predict consumer behaviour.

By looking at the literature, the focus shifts from religion to religiosity because of the ability of religiosity to measure individual's commitment to, which conveys with behaviour (McDaniel & Burnett, 1990); Schneider, Krieger & Bayraktar, 2011; Swimmerberghe, Flurry & Parker, 2011; Vitell, 2009; Wilkes, Burnett & Howell, 1986).

McDaniel & Burnett (1990, p. 103) define religiosity as a belief in a God with a commitment to the set of principles for religion. Alongside, Worthingtonton et al. (2003, p. 85) claimed that religiosity is the degree of individual commitment and compliance to a certain religion value, practice and belief. These two definitions clarify the difference between religiosity and spirituality because spirituality engages in the exploration of ‘meaning, unity, connectedness to nature, humanity and the transcendent’ (Vitell, 2009, p. 156). Religiosity, on the other hand, grants faith, focusing on beliefs, attitudes and behaviours (Emmons, 2005; Vitell, 2009). Any person may carry a certain degree of religiosity, which makes it unique. However, it cannot be easily considered as a measurable variable (Abou-Youssef et al., 2011; Wilkes et al., 1986).
