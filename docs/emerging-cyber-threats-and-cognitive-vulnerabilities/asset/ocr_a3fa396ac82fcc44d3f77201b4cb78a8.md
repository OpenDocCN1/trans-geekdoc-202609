Research focusing on personality characteristics portray a profile of a potential victim. For instance, individuals who are impulsive or have low self-control (Holt, Bossler, Malinski, & May, 2016), low emotional control (Hempehill & Heerde, 2014) and low self-esteem (Brewer & Kersklake, 2015; Mobin, Feng, & Neudorf, 2017) are more likely to be victimized by cyberbullying. Festl and Quandt (2013) reported personality factors such as conscientiousness and openness to experiences of cyberbullying. Identifying vulnerable individual characteristics enables intervention to target areas of development. However, there is a need for more longitudinal research to consider direction of the association between individual characteristics of victims of cyberspace.

It is important to take into account additional risk factors beyond an individual's characteristics for targeted individuals, groups, companies and the wider community. In particular, how these risk factors impact across different settings including the home and school environment, and neighbouring community need to be acknowledged.

Peer relationships and group status has been recognized across an abundance of research as a strong risk factor for cyber victimization within cyberbullying, cyberthreats and cybercrimes. For example, individuals who experience dissatisfaction with existing friendships/or experiences difficulties creating new ones are likely to experience loneliness and depressive symptoms associated with changes in mood levels, which can predict subsequent cyber victimization (Olenik-Shemesh et al., 2012). Those individuals who exhibit difficulties with social interaction may be struggled to satisfy with peer group relationships and as such experience a higher amount of peer rejection, with increased risk of cyber victimization. These group relationships be an important interaction during adolescent development, and in particular can create challenges in the school environment as adolescents fight to retain and establish power in the peer group. Research shows that adolescents associated with peer rejection from peer groups were more likely to show cyber aggressive and cyber victimization experience (Wright & Li, 2013). As cyber victimization can lead to unpleasant feelings and delinquent behaviour (Mitchell, Ybarra, & Finkelhor, 2007), educational practitioners should work to develop adolescents' cognitive and emotional empathy, to promote stronger peer-group support and friendships, a recognized protective factor for cyber victimization (Frid-Lindström, & Rosvall, 2015; Williams & Guerra, 2007).
