# Fear-based template for the isolation rhetoric

Currently Russia has three different domains types:.ru,su, and .pdf (stands for Soviet Union). In the Russian press, Cyrillic domain was celebrated as ‘the end of the Latin script’s domination of the internet’ (Nocetti, 2015, pp. 121–122). Western commentators saw this leading towards segregated, heteric Russian Web that undermines the global spirit (Singer & Friedman, 2014, pp. 252–253; Vargas-Leon, 2016, pp. 175–177).

Consequently, the original meaning of RuNet is that the content of websites is available in Russian, i.e. it the Russian language community on the Internet (Schmidt et al., 2006, p. 15). A sense of security is created by looking at what is common to the group, how one can find the best way to relate to others. The Russian sense of information security begins in the linguistic therefore offered by RuNet, in sharing an understanding of the world with other Russian speakers.

Togetherness brings safety but it also offers a solid ground for self-attained isolation, especially if there is a shared historical experience that reinforces the ‘withdrawness’. In the following we present the historical contextualization of the Russian sense of information security.

# Fear-based template for the isolation rhetoric

As noted earlier, in the Russian approach, information space is used by ‘other countries’ and hostile forces for the destabilization of Russia. This kind ‘besieged fortress’ (osazhdanna krepost) mentality characterizes Russian thinking for decades (sees, for example, Heller, 1988, pp. 108–109; Trenin, 2016, pp. 19, 36–39). It follows the very same fear-based template – the enemy is plotting to circle Russia, invade and overthrow the Russian political system – on land, sea, air, and now in the cyber/information space.

Fear cannot exist without a cause to be frightened of (cf. Heller, 1988, pp. 99). After the Russian Revolution of 1917, the Soviet people were deliberately to think of themselves as being surrounded by enemies, the ‘imperialists’, who would crush them if they could. The perception of ‘enemy’ has been repeatedly deployed, to mobilize against actual external danger, and also to justify the struggle against a supposed enemy on domestic ground (Fateev, 1999, pp. 102–104; Solomesch, 2001; Gudkov, 2005, pp. 14–15).

Lev Gudkov (2005) has developed a so-called ‘theory of an enemy’ where the main function of enemy is that the enemy represents a threat to the very existence of the group, e.g. to a society or an organization, to any kind of group that identifies itself as a subject (Gudkov, 2005, p. 13).
