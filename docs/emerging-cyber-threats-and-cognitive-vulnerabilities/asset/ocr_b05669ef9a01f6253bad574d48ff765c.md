The establishment of 'Clean Internet' is related to the law 'On the Protection of Children from Information Harmful to Their Health and Development', but more commonly known as the 'Blacklist Bill' that passed in 2012 (Zakon, 2012). The 'blacklist' is instituted and maintained by Roskomnadzor with the stated purpose of blocking the sites related to child pornography, materials on drug abuse or production and suicide. It includes measures that allow Roskomnadzor to censor individual URLs, domain names and IP addresses. Additionally, the law allows censoring websites that would encourage 'mass riots in unsanctioned events (Soldatov & Borogan, 2015, p. 263–264).

According to their website "Clean Internet — Center for the protection of the rights and legitimate interests of children in the information sphere (Tsentr po zashcite prav i zakonnykh interesov grazhdan v informatsionnoy sfere "Chistyi Internet") is a non-profit Russian organization established by the initiative of the Minkomsvyaz in December 2012. 'Clean Internet' aims to develop mechanisms for self-regulation of RuNet controlling the distribution of illegal content and creating a safe internet environment (positionania internet-sreda).

Within the 'Clean Internet' project a voluntary association 'Safe Internet League' was established (Soldatov & Borogan, 2015, p. 298). According to its website, Safe Internet League is the largest and most reputable Russian organization fighting dangerous web content. It is a voluntary association of citizens devoted to helping law enforcement organizations. Its volunteers monitor the Internet for violations on behalf of law enforcement. The site posts detailed information on MVD Directorate K18 — the MVD component responsible for Internet crime and provides a direct email link for reporting violations. In the league's view, violations include child pornography, pornography accessible to children, promotion of drug and alcohol abuse as well as violent or 'extremist' content. Despite the prominent role actual social media. The league's website audits its members based on the social networking sites they identify that contain malicious content. In many opinions, the league is actually law enforcement's monitoring attempt to match social media’s expansion (Carr, 2011, p. 240–241; Gorham et al., 2014, pp. 189–190; Soldatov & Borogan, 2015, p. 202.).
