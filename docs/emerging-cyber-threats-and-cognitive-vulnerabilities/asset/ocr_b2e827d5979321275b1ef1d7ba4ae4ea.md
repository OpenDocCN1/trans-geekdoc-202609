# 2003–12: China's emerging cyber doctrine

In 2003, China issued its first official vision for information security and its first information security strategy in Document 27: Opinions for Strengthening Information Security Assurance Work. The 2006-approved Digitization Strategy for 2006 to 2020 outlines the direction of resources for China’s information policy and highlights protecting information technologies (Chang, 2014). During this period, information technology is perceived as both a source of economic opportunity and a challenge. China clearly understood and attempted to reduce its information vulnerabilities. In 2010, China’s White Paper on Security and Defense expressed concern about the expanding cyberspace capabilities of foreign countries and draws attention to the need for China to strengthen its cyberspace defenses (China’s National Defense, 2010). During this period, China’s information policy was isolated and dominated by a defensive position; its main objective was to ensure the security of national information technologies. On the other hand, China successfully digitized and matched the West’s technological progress. This process could be related to China’s expanding cyberspace espionage capabilities, which were increasingly used against the United States and other Western nations (Thomas, 2009).

# 2012–18: establishing the concept of ‘active defense’

Significant changes to China’s information policy doctrine took place in 2012 and 2013. In 2012, China’s Council of State Information Office published a new Concept of Information Security. As Giles and Hagestad (2013) note, this emphasizes strengthening civilian cyber defense. In 2013, the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) published China’s Science of Military Strategy, which reflected a more strategic view of cybersecurity (SMS, 2013). The document was not regarded as a military strategy, but it outlines new directions for the development of military doctrine. In the document, cyberspace is introduced, next to land, air, sea and space, as a defense and warfare domain. In 2015, the Chinese Ministry of National Defense approved a cyberspace military strategy (CMS, 2015). Although the strategy does not directly indicate the development of offensive capabilities, El Kania, the cybersecurity firm FireEye, asserts that China’s policy commitment to ‘stem major cyber crises, ensure national security and information security, and maintain national security and social stability’ imply the possibility of launching cyberattacks (Kania, 2015). China’s 2016 National Cybersecurity Security Strategy sends a clear message to foreign adversaries: actors who engage in destructive activities against China’s cyberspace and national security will be ‘lawfully punished’ (NCSS, 2016).
