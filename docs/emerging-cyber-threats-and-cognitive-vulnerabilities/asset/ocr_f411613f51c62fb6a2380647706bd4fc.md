Later, the Russian government began to plan top-to-bottom approach for technical disconnecting of the ‘Russian segment of the Internet’ from the global Internet. In 2014, a series of ex-ercises were conducted to test ‘national segment’s feasibility (Nikiforov, 2014; Demidov & Makhukova, 2016). During summer 2016, Russia declared that its ‘national segment’ would be disconnected from the global Internet by 2020 in order to protect the critical Russian infrastructure (Minkomsviaz 2016; Programma, 2014). Furthermore, a ‘road-map’ that Russia will be ‘digitively sovereign’ by 2020 was presented in 2017 (Programma, 2017). During the past years, Russia has launched strategies (Strategia 2015, 2017), doctrines (Doktrina, 2000, 2016), state programs (Programma, 2014, 2017) and action plans (Napravlenija, 2018) demonstrate clearly how the Russian Federation is preparing the infrastructural basis for national control of the Internet (cf. Kukkola, 2018a, 2018b; Kukkola & Rolistainen, 2018; Kukkola, Rolistainen, & Nikila, 2017). It is clear that the Russian approach to cybersecurity differs fundamentally from the ‘Western’ understanding of cybersecurity that is based on global and the open Internet (Ristolainen, 2017, pp. 113–114).
