# Cyberspace capabilities and the offense—defense 133

According to John Brennan, a former director of the CIA, President Obama decided to confine the United States' response to verbally warn Putin when sufficient evidence surfaced Russia's interference in the United States' internal political processes. The US security services created several potential responses, including an offensive attack on Russia. Obama rejected offensive-attack scenarios, as he did not want the escalation of the cyberspace conflict to lead to war (Miller, 2017). This situation demonstrates that at least during the Obama administration, the use of the United States' offensive cybersecurity capabilities was assessed with extreme caution, and the risk of a cyber conflict was perceived as both real and threatening.

The United States tends to develop its offensive and defensive security capabilities equally. The United States' large cybersecurity budget and the precedent United States set in the malicious computer worm Stuxnet demonstrate that the United States is capable of executing effective offensive cyberattacks. Until 2016, the United States' security culture and cyberspace politics were dominated by Obama's political restraint regarding the country's offensive cyberspace capabilities, and priority was to establish a defensive advantage.

# China

China makes a clear distinction between its offensive and defensive capabilities. An analysis of China's strategic security culture, specific behavioral precedents, cyberspace capability structures and primary actors reveal that offensive dominate China's cyberspace policy.

China's actors, who perform both offensive and defensive operations, are divided into two groups. The first group consists of professional hackers, contracted by the PLA. The second group consists of patriotic hackers, who periodically perform cyberattacks ordered by the state. A general department of the PLA serves as China's cyber coordination center. It functions similarly to the United States' NSA and comprises of more than 13,000 experts. China has the largest cybersecurity force in the world (see Table 6.4). Each of its departments, PLA Blue 61398 (revealed by the American information technology company, Meiant) employs experts in the fields of information technology, electrical engineering, mathematics and linguistics. China's main task is to collect intelligence on foreign countries' military and technology innovations and industries. Intelligence on unconfirmed anti-China security, doctrine and defense strategies are frequently collected as well (Kozlowski, 2015).
