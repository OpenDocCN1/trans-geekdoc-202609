This involves a user manipulating a targeted individual, group or community to sharing private information or knowledge. This type of cyberthreat can create large vulnerabilities across a variety of social networks. The fact the attack can be performed with relative ease means these social engineers can attack on a larger scale, creating a larger impact for those involved (Kromholz et al., 2015).

Willard (2007) created a typology of cyber aggressive acts and cyberthreats, distinguishing across seven recognized forms including flaming; harassment; denigration; outing and trickery; impersonation; exclusion and cybersecurity. Others include hacking, data privacy and stalking (Hasebrink, Görgüz, Haddon, Kalmus, & Livingstone, 2011). These cyberthreats can be perpetrated through emails, mobile phones and the many online communications or networks the Internet offers (Rivers & Noret, 2010), which can lead to detrimental consequences to the target and/industry. For example, these cyberthreats can lead to depression (Olenik-Shemesh, Heiman, & Eden, 2012), and in worst cases, victims of online are more likely to experience suicidal thoughts compared to users who perpetrate these cyberattacks (Hinduja & Patchin, 2010). Implications of these research suggest online companies should work together to manage the negative repercussions for those who are victimized.

The development and accessibility of the Internet has evolved social communication. The nature of online communication and networks mean users can post private or sensitive information online that can accessible to a wider audience (Kromholz et al., 2015). However, young people do show a desire to maintain privacy in the online environment, even if there is an expectation to be socially in the online community (Betts & Spencer, 2017). This suggests social media companies need to implement strategies to make young feel safer online. Online users who perceive they have a good awareness of controlling their information and privacy on SNS are likely to be victims of cybercrime than those who do not (Saridakis, Benson, Eizingarde, & Tennakoon, 2016). This illustrates how digital literacy and knowledge of Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs) can influence vulnerability to cyber threats and attacks. While some responsibility is down to each user on the social network, providers can focus to ensure any information published by the user is only visible to the intended audience through security settings.
