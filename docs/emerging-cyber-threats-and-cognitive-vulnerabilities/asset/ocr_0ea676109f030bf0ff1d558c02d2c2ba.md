International relations theories, particularly defensive realism, explain how and why security needs prompt cooperation between adversaries. Cooperation sought under these conditions, negative cooperation, is based on the need to minimize the risk of a direct conflict. Therefore, it is important to examine the United States, China and Russia's cyberspace interactions. Are these states moving toward a global conflict, which could become a world war? Or are they restraining themselves and regulating the weaponization of cyberspace?

This chapter explores the motives and conditions under which the United States, China and Russia are likely to cooperate on qualitative arms control in cyberspace to improve both their national and international security. The analysis focuses on the 20-year period spanning from 1998 to 2018. The premises of defensive realism serve as the theoretical background for the analysis... This analysis is based on Charles Glaser's *theory* (1995, 2010), which explains the conditions leading to negative cooperation among potential adversaries.

# Incentives for cooperation among rivals

Security cooperation poses a challenge for international relations theorists. Harald Müller (2013) writes, ‘States opting for security cooperation one security asset to gain after which they believe helps them to provide better for their security: the collaboration with their potential enemies and the pursuant agreements and organizations.’ Cooperation among states with a common enemy is explained in classic or structural realism. The formation of alliances is addressed comprehensively by Glen Snyder (1984, pp. 461–95, 1997), Stephen Walt (1987) and Randall Schweller (1994, pp. 72–107). According to the constructivist approach, with shared values and interests tend to increase their cooperation efforts and security communities (Adler and Barnett, 1998). Robert Jervis (1985, 1999) has also explored in incentives for cooperation among states from the theoretical perspectives of realism and neoliberalism. Yet, neither these nor theorists can explain how potential adversaries' interests and perceptions prompt them to enter into security cooperation and self-reflection.
