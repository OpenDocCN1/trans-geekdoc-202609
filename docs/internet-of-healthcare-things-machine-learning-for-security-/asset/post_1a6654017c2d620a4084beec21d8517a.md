### 3.4.1.1 HIPAA Compliance

HIPAA stands for Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. The primary purpose of this law is to make it easier for individuals to provide health insurance, to ensure the accuracy and security of health data, and to help the healthcare industry manage regulatory costs. This law has expanded requirements for the storage and confidentiality of patient information and has redefined the way health, safety and various organizations deal with health, safety and integrity issues. From an innovative perspective, HIPAA covers a broad range of areas including websites, pharmaceutical devices, medical imaging and electronic medical records.

### 3.4.1.2 HIPAA Five Rules

- **1) Privacy Rule**: HIPAA's Privacy Policy defines who can access Protected Health Information (PHI). These rules apply to all forms of communication, including oral, written or electronic disclosure. Mainly, patients must sign an association waiver. Without written consent, protected persons may not disclose personally identifiable information to anyone who does not fall under the Permitted Use and Disclosure section of HIPAA. Companies tracked include health insurance plans, healthcare providers, and health information centers.

Protected devices can disclose PHI to whom they share their data. Indoor devices may also use and disclose patient data to support operations, including treatment and payment procedures. Some caregivers have patient catalogues, so guests who ask for the patient's name will know where the patient is in the hospital and will provide their health status. Providers may use the patient's unintended consent to disclose information to people who want to pay for services or to make plans for the patient's death.

Confidentiality rules also allow clinical organizations to share data with public health authorities that have the legal authority to obtain information to prevent or manage illness, injury, or disability. As public health authorities deal with incidents, prepare for terrorism, and monitor programs, research on personal health data may be required.

- **2) Security Rule:** Describes the rules, specifications, and security cycles for electronic PHI. It defines how protected health information (PHI) should be communicated and not play a role in oral communication. Safety rules include specifications for administrative, physical, and technical controls. Identify specific individuals or groups to implement and monitor compliance within the organization. To comply with HIPAA, indoor organizations must use firewalls, encryption, modern software, and operating systems

- **a) Administrative safeguard**: There is a necessity to practice, create, and maintain policies. The policies and methods used to educate and support advocates to maintain PHI security must be implemented, created, and maintained. Some examples of administrative security measures include:

    - (i) Accept the use of the policy to help the mentor and representative gain authority and responsibility for the transfer of PHI.
    - (ii) A penalty policy is required to educate employees who violate HIPAA.
    - (iii) Access to information policy provides appropriate access to PC workstations, medical and shared registers, and various projects or cycles.
    - (iv) Safety training is required. In this way, employees are trained and assisted to remember policies and procedures through software updates, PC log checks, password updates and other important security measures.
    - (v) An emergency plan to ensure that appropriate policies and procedures are in place to prepare for emergency response. In the event of a fire, vandalism or other catastrophic events, you must develop, test and update your accident and emergency plans and designate all important exercises as owners.
