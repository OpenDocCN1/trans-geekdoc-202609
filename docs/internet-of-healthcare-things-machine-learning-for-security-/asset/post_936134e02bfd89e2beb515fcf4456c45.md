## Abstract
The potential risk posed by a vulnerable and security-compromised healthcare system, which deploys devices working with the Internet of Things (IoT), weighs extremely high on its consequential damages to the stakeholders – whether they be patients, doctors, other medical professionals, administrators, regulators, or vendors. The risk of harm outweighs the benefits of unbridled proliferation and requires comprehensive resolution. Cyber-attacks could lead to false claims being preferred over insurance companies with stolen personal data, fake data could be introduced with hard to measure consequences and botnets could infiltrate the network and cause havoc through DNS attacks or introduction of malware—all of which could potentially compromise healthcare systems. The purpose of using innovative applications like the internet of things is to enrich healthcare services, with enhanced quality in speed and delivery of a discernible range of services to the stakeholders, and the same stability is threatened when there is an attack using stolen data. The key to overcoming the challenge of intrusion into the system is to identify genuine users by implementing a robust and comprehensive mechanism of authentication (validating and detecting) and then authorization (allowing access to permissible activities). With the multitude of devices connected to the healthcare system in the IoT scenario, it is rather a formidable and daunting task; however, it is both doable and effective in neutralizing the challenges of hacking. Generic adoption of authentication and authorization is recommended, contingent on the type of use – routine data capture and recording vis-à-vis online procedure or without remote support.

## 2.1 Introduction
The main priority for healthcare organizations providing automated services beginning at registration, up to treatment and follow up, is to protect service beneficiaries and their data. This entails focused contribution from a range of development community supplying devices, applications and system integrators factoring issues security and privacy inbuilt into their design specially for the domain of IoT in healthcare. Intending to enable and provide optimal protection, one needs to understand the types of threats to which the network and its subsystems and individual components (devices) could be exposed.

Data from the Zion Market Research indicates that the value of global IoT healthcare market is anticipated to reach $14 billion by 2024 with a CAGR of 12.2% between 2017 and 2024. The increase is attributed to the increasing use of cloud computing in healthcare facilities. One of the principal drivers for the exponential growth envisaged in IoHT is the fast adoption of cloud computing and the accepted norm of storing data and hosting services through the cloud rather than the traditional practice of premises bound.

A survey conducted by McKinsey in 2015 [1], was found that breach of security scored extremely high on the list of concerns that could potentially impede the growth of IoT with its lack of confidence in the infrastructure and services. In the case of IoT, exposure to vulnerabilities arising out of low security may be the deterrent—a point underscored by the survey conducted in collaboration with the Global Semiconductor Alliance GSA. Any discussion on the potential growth opportunities in IoT is weighed when dealing with the issue of provisioning adequate security and the costs thereof.

Healthcare devices and applications collect, store and pass on personal healthcare data to provide the designed services. In case of internet, these devices, gadgets and wearables form the nodes of the network and are connected in such a way that they are both accessible and accessed anytime and anywhere as per connectivity requirements. The internet, such networks which have their terminals or origins on the internet are prone to hacking and attacks. It is necessary and important that the system designers and developers consider factor in protection elements and considerations while crafting the infrastructure and services to render IoT in the healthcare sector [2].

## VALIDATION MECHANISMS FOR IoHT
It is not uncommon to notice that the general requirements of security for any IT based system are equally applicable to the IoT environment; however, the scale and scope of the requirements stand enhanced due to the likely damage that could result in case of any compromise. Preserving confidentiality, including denial of data, by making them inaccessible, whether it is sensitive medical data, or the details of the patients, doctors, providers and supporting institutions to unauthorized users improves the level of security. Integrity and encryption ensure that data, whether it is patient-related or other sensitive data, is not changed or modified while in transit by any adversary action. More importantly, the integrity of stored data and content should not be compromised, claiming extra vigil and protection. Here, although the emphasis is on medical data, protection is equally applicable for other forms of data as well as its metadata.

The weakest node or link represents the most vulnerable point prone to attract the attention of attackers, and such weak nodes either in isolation or on a cumulative basis influence the overall security of the system. It is observed that the most vulnerable, and resistance-offering category of nodes, are devices that have IoT capability—yet nevertheless part of the end user device family. The vulnerability arises both from the hardware features of the devices with embedded IoHT capabilities, as well as user behavior, determining the willingness to follow and implement recommended protocols.

Protection in the form of authentication and authorization should be implemented at all devices, interfaces and links to ensure protection against attacks. Both of the protection mechanisms are needed when the device contacts the data transmitter, and when the latter interacts with the server designated to collect and analyze all the data (data aggregator). Entire infrastructure must be equipped with resilience in the form of walls or denial of service mechanisms at the data transfer level, to prevent any access to seekers/hackers other than the domain of authorized users.

## 2.2 Stakeholders in IoHT
The principal stakeholder is the patient, who requires examination and treatment, whatever admitted as an in-patient or treated as an out-patient with periodic investigations and medications. In the connected world of today, patients schedule their appointments and consultations by accessing the system of medical service provider through their devices: either by email, messaging, or custom-built apps. Educating, training, and making patients aware of the ways to use the IoHT apps, safely and securely, assumes a significant step toward their data protection, given that the devices and the apps at the edge are the most vulnerable elements in the value chain of IoHT.

Vendors’ group that develops applications to provide a range of medical services is the next level of stakeholder. This group should follow standards and protocols for communication, aligned with best industry practices, enabling secure functioning of the individual components as well as the sub-systems. Tendencies and practices that resort to adopting unlicensed and non-standard platforms, and overriding controls, should be avoided. It is the function and responsibility of the system integrator to establish qualification criteria for the selection of applications and devices that conform to, and comply with, secure list of standards and protocols. The often-observed painful practice among the developers is the claim that implementation and incorporation of security features within the applications would not be cost-effective, and the resources required to keep track of the updates and version changes is intense. The cost-effectiveness can be justified by the damage that could be done to the system; and ease updates, artificial intelligence may be deployed for scanning and making required changes.

Doctors and other medical professionals form the second important category of stakeholders in IoHT. Doctors are both users as well providers, who help craft the services’ list based on which applications are developed. Doctors and their associated medical colleagues are the most critical resources in the healthcare value chain, yet present themselves as vulnerabilities from a user’s perspective. Their behavior and knowledge of operating the IoHT devices is key to the successful protection of data and its security thereof. Importantly, their input and feed are of paramount importance to segregate data into three categories: sensitive data, personal data and non-personal data. Their judgement of response times and sense of assigning criticality can help design the appropriate security features of the IoHT.

Medical administrators usually make the call to authorize or restrain the budget for a system’s architecture, configuration, and security features. The tendency observed among such stakeholders is that cost saving is the driving force behind their measures: their consideration and decisions for system scoping and expenditure is guided by financial considerations. It is crucial for this group of stakeholders to be aware of the significance of incorporating security mechanisms right from design implementation, to ensure the long-term sustainability of it.

Policy makers, including governments, function as stakeholders who prime the IoHT landscape with appropriate rules and regulations.
