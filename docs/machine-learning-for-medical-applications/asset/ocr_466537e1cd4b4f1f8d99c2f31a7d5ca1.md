The major advantage of the quantum confinement effect lies in the fact that the dimensions of quantum dot (QD) devices are comparable to the de Broglie wavelength of electrons and holes. This confinement alters the energy states available to the charge carriers, making the performance of QDs significantly different from that of their bulk counterparts. As shown in Figure 1, this unique feature enables precise tuning of the emission wavelengths of QDs simply by modifying their size: smaller QDs emit light at shorter wavelengths (e.g., blue), while larger QDs emit at longer wavelengths (e.g., red).

QDs possess high photostability and brightness from the well-defined electronic structure and lower tendencies to photobleaching than traditional fluorophores. This stability makes them especially favorable in applications necessitating extended duration of imaging.

The synthesis and surface modification of QDs can be optimized toward better interaction with different surroundings, organic and aqueous inclusion. Problems associated with QDs’ solubility and biocompatibility can be solved by ligand exchange that expands the possible application field from medical diagnostics to environmental monitoring.
