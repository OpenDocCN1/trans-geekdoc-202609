# 2 Early developments in CNNs for image segmentation

# 2.1 Introduction to CNNs in computer vision

CNNs have significantly contributed to the development of computer vision by transforming the processing and analysis of visual input. CNNs are a distinct category of deep neural networks that are particularly engineered to manage the intricacies of image data and provide significant enhancements in the areas of feature extraction and pattern identification. Through a succession of convolutional layers, CNNs are able to automatically learn important characteristics directly from the input pictures, in contrast to typical machine learning methods that mostly depend on constructed features. This capacity to capture spatial hierarchies and relationships across different dimensions has made CNNs the backbone of several vision tasks, from object classification to picture segmentation.

The basic architecture of a CNN was built upon three core operations: convolution, pooling, and activation. A collection of filters was applied by convolutional layers. These then move over the input picture to create feature maps that emphasize significant patterns like edges, textures, and forms. Because the convolutional process maintains the spatial connections between pixels, CNNs are very useful for processing data that was arranged like a grid, like photographs. To lower the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, pooling procedures like max pooling and average pooling are used after each convolutional layer. By keeping important characteristics, this down-sampling aids in lowering the network’s computational complexity. CNNs are further enhanced in their ability to represent complicated visual patterns by the addition of nonlinear activation functions, such ReLU, which enable the network to capture complex, nonlinear correlations in the input.

One of the key advantages of CNNs was their ability to learn hierarchical representations of images. In the lower layers, CNNs typically capture low-level features such as edges and corners. As the network deepens, it begins to detect higher-level abstractions, including textures, shapes, and eventually entire objects.
