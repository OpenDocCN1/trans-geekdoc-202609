A problem that arises in this type of approach relates to when the data has many features, as it can cause issue with both clustering and classification. Since working with high-dimensional data the principal distances between the objects are less meaningful because of the “curse of dimensionality.” This problem can be solved by the projection for the lower dimensionality by common methods for example principal component analysis (PCA).

Integrating clustering with classification in order to detect anomalies seems highly feasible. This combined approach gives the capacity to detect irregularities that cannot be easily distinguished using the two methodologies. The application of clustering algorithm to discover the inherent structures of the data and classification models for definitive labeling of these discovered group structures lead to improved accuracy, flexibility, and robustness of the anomaly detection systems.

# 2.4 Enhancing robustness of anomaly detection through hybrid supervised and unsupervised learning

The combination of supervised and unsupervised learning approaches for anomaly detection has been a focus of much research interest in the past few years as it holds the potential to improve the general performance, and especially the robustness of a detection system in realistic problems. Figure 3 shows that while supervised learning models are very accurate when new labeled data is available, there are instances where they are hard to find. Indeed, the use of such techniques as clustering and outlier detection allows discovering new patterns without the provision of the training data set but it could also be problematic because of their inexact demarcation of decision regions. It is seen that through integration of ideas from both strategies, there is the likelihood of developing enhanced accuracy, flexibility, and robustness of the models used in anomaly detection.

Majority of the hybrid anomaly detection methods use a technique known as unsupervised learning to test the structures of the data. These known methods like the clustering algorithms (k-means, DBSCAN), etc., and dimensionality reduction (PCA, t-SNE), etc., can help in grouping similar data by features hitherto unknown. These unsupervised models aid in detecting regions of datasets that are most likely to contain outliers, given the fact that samples that do not belong to the learned clusters and have now fitted the assumed distribution are flagged by the algorithms. Yet, although these models can point to potential outliers, they do not have the capacity to classify these records definitively as outliers, without measuring specific features. That is where supervised models come into play.
