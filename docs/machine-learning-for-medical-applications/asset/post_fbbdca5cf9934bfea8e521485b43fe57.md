
## 5.3 Ultrasound imaging

A type of diagnostic imaging, sonography, also referred to as ultrasound imaging, is a common method of diagnosing illnesses that involves the use of high-frequency sound to produce images of internal organs. For this reason, the real-time imaging, mobility, and nonionizing radiation of this imaging technique are highly valued.

### 5.3.1 Principles of ultrasound imaging

The principles of transmission and reflection of sound waves form the fundamental basis of ultrasound imaging. High-frequency sound waves are emitted from the transducer head, travel through body tissues, and are reflected back at the interfaces where different tissue types meet. These reflected waves are then captured by the transducer, converted into electrical signals, and processed to generate accurate images of internal structures. ### 5.3.2 Clinical applications

Ultrasound imaging is applied in different fields of medicine because of its flexibility and noninvasive nature. It is employed for probing soft tissues, the liver, kidney, and heart, as well as for blood flow analysis and for the identification of diseases such as tumors and cysts. In obstetrics, ultrasound is utilized in assessing fetal growth, in diagnosing pregnancy complications, and in planning antenatal management. Also, musculoskeletal ultrasound is used to analyze joint, muscle, and tendon consistency, producing images that would be useful in sports medicine and orthopedics.

### 5.3.3 Advantages of ultrasound imaging

Real-time imaging allows for functional evaluation of organs and their movements, which is an advantage of using ultrasound imaging. The method is also of particular worth in terms of safety, since it does not employ ionizing radiation, making it most suitable for regular scans and for vulnerable groups such as children and sometimes pregnant women. In some cases, the equipment used in ultrasound is cheaper and more transportable than other imaging modalities such as CT, thus facilitating its use in clinical practice.

## 5.4 X-ray and other modalities

X-ray imaging is one of the main and most trusted imaging methods used in the diagnosis of patients. In this method, pictures of interior structures are made by passing X-rays through the body and then registering the extent to which they are picked up by a detector. Different tissues absorb X-rays differently, which results in the contrast shown in X-ray images: fatty tissues, for example, are darker than less fatty tissues, and materials like bones, which are very dense, appear white.

### 5.4.1 Characteristics and applications

- **Bone and joint imaging:** The ideal imaging modality is usually X-rays, which are particularly useful in visualizing bony structures and are often used in the diagnosis of fractures, dislocations, and degenerative bone diseases. Given that bone has a high attenuation value and soft tissues have relatively low attenuation values compared to bone, excellent depiction of the skeletal system is achievable.
- **Chest imaging:** Thoracic conditions are diagnosed using X-ray imaging as the standard form of diagnostics for the corresponding pathological states. It is used for the diagnosis of lung disease, including pneumonia, tuberculosis, and pulmonary edema, and for observing the heart and large vessels.
- **Limitations:** X-rays give useful diagnostic information about tissues; however, they are unable to distinguish between soft tissues; further, they are not as useful in staging early diseases. Radiation exposure is a critical concern in X-ray imaging, since it involves the use of ionizing radiation, which is dangerous particularly in children and pregnant women.

### 5.4.2 Computed tomography

CT is a very advanced imaging method employing X-rays that create cross-sectional views of the body in thin slices. CT scanners accomplish this by reconstructing many X-ray pictures from different orientations and creating a series of two-dimensional slices, which, when combined, create three-dimensional views of interior structures.

#### 5.4.2.1 Characteristics and applications

- **High resolution and detail:** CT imaging provides high spatial resolution and delineation of both cortical and trabecular bone as well as soft tissues. It is therefore of great use in the diagnosis and assessment of trauma, tumors, and internal hemorrhages.
- **Cross-sectional imaging:** Cross-sectional studies are superior for detecting and localizing abnormalities and for determining the overall extent of disease involvement. CT is often applied in oncology to stage tumors and in trauma surgery to assess the patient.
- **Limitations:** Compared with conventional radiography, CT imaging uses a higher dose of X-rays, which exposes the patient to the risk of radiation-induced effects, especially when scans are repeated often. As a result of these concerns, new methods and strategies for the reduction of radiation doses are routinely being developed.

### 5.4.3 Ultrasound imaging

Piezoelectric crystals transmit high-frequency sound waves to aid in the formation of images of internal organs. Waves generated by an ultrasound transducer are reflected from tissues and converted into pictures displayed on a monitor.

#### 5.4.3.1 Characteristics and applications

- **Real-time imaging:** Ultrasound allows for real-time imaging and subsequently facilitates dynamic evaluation of organs and structures. It is common in cardiology to determine patients’ heart function and in obstetrics to monitor the growth of the fetus.
- **Nonionizing radiation:** Ultrasound is not as dangerous as its counterparts such as CT, which create ionizing radiation. Due to this, the modality is safer for some patient groups, including newborns and pregnant women.
- **Limitations:** A major concern with the modality is that it has low penetration and cannot easily image through bone, which restricts its uses, and some areas of the body cannot be imaged. There are two factors that affect the quality of images: the operator’s expertise and the patient’s mobility.

### 5.4.4 Magnetic resonance imaging

MRI is another method used to produce images of the body by using magnetic fields and radiofrequency waves. MRI is particularly exceptional in imaging soft tissues and has high contrast compared with X-ray and CT.

The clinical practice is a field where various applications can be described to help better understand patients’ conditions.

Integrated imaging strategies are increasingly used in different clinical processes. The utilization of PET/CT is common in oncology, as it combines both tumor metabolism and its anatomical distribution in staging, treatment planning, and evaluation. MRI and fMRI are widely employed in neurology in combination to assess the structural and functional changes of the brain in patients with neurological diseases. Likewise, in cardiology, integrating MRI and echocardiography offers precise analysis of heart structure and function in evaluating cardiovascular disease.

#### 5.5.3 Challenges and considerations

Several difficulties arise in the integration of multimodal imaging data. Variation in spatial resolution, contrast, and the method and protocol of acquisition between modalities can sometimes make registration and fusion tasks challenging. Multimodal data add complexity, implying that they require sophisticated computational treatment and methods in order to be integrated correctly and to derive accurate meaning. Some of these challenges include data management and storage, as multisource and multistep capture and construction of multimedia data require sound solutions for acquiring, organizing, storing, and retrieving Big Data.
