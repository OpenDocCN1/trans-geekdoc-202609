### 3.4.4 Robotics and drone uses

Application of U-Net occurs in robotics and drones, where it is used for object detection and scene understanding. For example, U-Net has been used for objects and obstacles in images taken by drones for environmental monitoring, search and rescue operations, and precision agriculture. The details of the characteristics of the scene in practical robotics applications are provided by the U-Net, which helps in object manipulation and navigation. This unveils the robot's prospects in acing the mechanical interplay of operations with the surrounding space in order to accomplish intricate tasks independently.

### 3.5 Challenges in U-Net architectures

Although the U-Net design has become quite popular for its usage in biomedical image segmentation, it faces challenges. Despite the fact that U-Net has the encoder-decoder structure with skip connections and experiments prove its success to gather characteristics that are fine-grained and high-level at the same time, there are several challenges that influence how effectively U-Net works in different cases. It would be important to understand these hurdles toward building improved models and toward raising the level of segmentation.

### 3.5.1 Time and space complexity and time requirements

The main drawback associated with the proposed U-Net is that it has a very high computational cost. The number and depth of convolution layers, as well as the expenditure of feature maps, make the model demand high computational and memory resources, especially when used for high-resolution image data. Such computation becomes costly in terms of resources when applied in environments where there is limited availability of systems, hence calling for optimization solutions to handle such a challenge.

### 3.5.2 Overfitting and generalization issues

U-Net's ability to achieve high accuracy on training data does not always translate to superior performance on unseen data. Overfitting remains a concern, particularly in scenarios with limited annotated datasets, which are common in biomedical imaging. The model's tendency to learn noise and irrelevant details from the training data can lead to poor generalization and reduced effectiveness on new datasets. This challenge highlights the need for robust regularization techniques and strategies to improve model generalization.

### 3.5.3 Handling variability in input data

Biomedical images often exhibit significant variability due to differences in imaging protocols, patient demographics, and pathological conditions. U-Net's performance can be adversely affected by this variability, as the models struggle to adapt to diverse image characteristics. Addressing this challenge requires advanced preprocessing methods and adaptive learning techniques to ensure that U-Net models are resilient to the inherent variability in biomedical imaging data.

### 3.5.4 Loss of fine details and spatial resolution

While U-Net was designed to retain spatial resolution through its skip connections, there are cases where the preservation of fine details can still be suboptimal. The combination of downsampling and upsampling operations, along with the inherent pooling layers, results in loss of critical details, which is particularly problematic in tasks requiring precise delineation of small structures. Enhancing the model's ability to preserve fine details and improve spatial resolution remains an ongoing challenge.

### 3.5.5 Scalability and adaptability

The scalability of U-Net to large-scale datasets presents another challenge. While U-Net performs well on single-modal images, extending its application to multi-modal data, such as combining MRI and CT images, requires modifications to the architecture and additional training considerations. Adapting U-Net for new biomedical imaging modalities demands continuous updates and innovations to the model's design and training processes.

## 4 DeepLab family of architectures

### 4.1 Introduction to DeepLab architecture

Given the limitations of earlier CNN-based models, the DeepLab architecture represents a significant advancement in semantic image segmentation. Developed by Google Research, DeepLab integrates several cutting-edge techniques to enhance the precision and efficiency of pixel-level classification. As illustrated in Figure 5, one of the key innovations in DeepLab is the use of atrous (or dilated) convolutions, which expand the receptive field without increasing the number of parameters, allowing the model to capture more contextual information. This capability is essential for accurately segmenting complex and diverse visual scenes, enabling DeepLab to perform robustly across varied medical and natural image segmentation tasks.

### 4.1.1 Atrous convolutions and their impact

One major improvement in DeepLab was the use of atrous convolutions, also referred to as dilated convolutions. Dilations also increase the receptive field of the convolutional layers by adding zeros between the filters, thus widening the field of vision without leaving out high-resolution features in the maps. This design choice allows DeepLab to capture more contextual information at multiple scales, enhancing its ability to segment objects with varying sizes and shapes. The use of atrous convolutions addresses the limitations of traditional convolutions, which typically require downsampling to achieve a larger receptive field, leading to loss of spatial detail.

### 4.1.2 DeepLab's spatial pyramid pooling module

Consequently, integrating spatial pyramid pooling (SPP) module in DeepLab resulted in even better performance because of the multiscale pooling used to gather contextual information. The SPP module uses ASPP to apply atrous convolutions at different rates, effectively capturing context at multiple scales. This approach allows the network to combine features from different resolutions, improving the segmentation of objects that span various sizes. The integration of ASPP into the DeepLab architecture provides a robust mechanism for handling the diverse range of object scales and complexities found in real-world images.

### 4.1.3 Advantages of DeepLab architecture

The improvement of DeepLab architecture has some advantages over previous segmentation models. Atrous convolution and SPP reduce the problem of misaligned, nonuniform object sizes and the influence of background, thus enhancing its performance during segmentation. Because spatial resolution was kept uninterrupted over the network, DeepLab has an efficient mature ability to handle high-resolution images, as seen in our experiments. These enhancements turn DeepLab to be a state-of-the-art tool in numerous fields, including urban scene analysis and medical image analysis.

### 4.1.4 Evolution of DeepLab models

DeepLab framework has evolved over time, with successive releases having improvements and additions of different strategies. ASPP module was introduced in DeepLabv2 that enhanced the contextual aggregation to a very great extent. To extend DeepLabv3, the authors introduced global pooling to the design and improved the ASPP mechanism. DeepLabv3+ integration of the characteristics of previous models with an encoder-decoder structure made enhancements in boundary definition and segmentation. Every new transformation of the DeepLab architecture represents learning from the previous versions for addressing particular concerns.

### 4.2 DeepLabv2

DeepLabv2, in fact, can be considered an improvement over other architectures that are part of the DeepLab family of segmentation models, and is based on seminal ideas developed with the DeepLab model. DeepLabv2 was introduced by Chen et al. in 2016, which involved the addition of several new components for better semantic segmentation performance and reliability. The model embeds the main design of the DeepLab framework but assimilates important enhancements that overcome the weaknesses of the prior version, including the management of multiscale contexts as well as improving the segmentation outcomes.

### 4.2.1 ASPP

A central feature of DeepLabv2 was the introduction of ASPP. ASPP was designed to capture multiscale information by applying atrous (or dilated) convolutions at multiple rates. This technique allows the model to capture context from different scales without the need for extensive pooling operations, which can reduce spatial resolution. ASPP enhances the model's ability to handle varying object sizes and complex scene structures by aggregating features from different receptive fields. This innovation significantly improves the segmentation of objects with varying scales and complex backgrounds.

### 4.2.2 Advantages over previous models

DeepLabv2 offers several advantages over its predecessors, particularly in terms of accuracy and flexibility. The integration of ASPP allows for better contextual understanding and more precise segmentation boundaries. By avoiding excessive downsampling and preserving more spatial information, DeepLabv2 addresses one of the key limitations of earlier models, which often struggled with accurately delineating fine details and small objects. Additionally, DeepLabv2's architecture facilitates improved performance on challenging benchmarks, such as the PASCAL VOC and COCO datasets, demonstrating its effectiveness in real-world applications.

### 4.2.3 Implementation details and performance

In actual use, DeepLabv2 leverages a ResNet backbone for feature extraction, which improves training stability and feature learning, while also exploiting residual connections to boost speed. To preserve high resolution in the segmentation maps that are produced, the model uses dilated convolutions in both the ASPP and the final convolution.
