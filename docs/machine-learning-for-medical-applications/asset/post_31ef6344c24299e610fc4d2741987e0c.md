## Customization of medical implants using 3D printing

**Abstract:** Three-dimensional (3D) printing technology has dramatically changed the approaches to developing and designing implants and other medical devices. In composing this chapter, the author provides an understanding of 3D printing in medicine, being historically developed, currently applied, and potentially powerful in the future. The advancements in body models, custom implants and prostheses, enhanced planning of operations, and formation of 3D models based on the anatomy of different patients can be listed as the major achievements. The chapter also looks at the limitations and challenges of developing and implementing fast, accurate visualization and simulation of surgeries such as the integration of new technology into existing practices, legislation, and costs. The chapter focuses on how the renowned scientific advancements and studies in the area of 3D printing revolutionize surgical precision, patient care, and training. New possibilities of individualized treatment are opening before humanity by virtue of 3D medicine; therefore, its further research and development are most essential for a fast-growing segment.

## 2 Historical development of 3D printing technologies

### 2.1 Origins and evolution of additive manufacturing

Additive manufacturing, commonly known as 3D printing, was a field that was initiated in the early 1980s and was a revolutionary technology compared to what was known as subtractive manufacturing. While AM has grown as the opposite tactic to the conventional material eradication technique, the concept of additive manufacturing was the progressive installation of material to create 3D objects.

The history of additive manufacturing can be dated back to 1983 when Hull created SLA. When Hull used ultraviolet (UV) light to cure liquid photopolymer resins, he paved the way for a radical revolution in the manufacturing and conception of articles in the future. This type of additive manufacturing was the inception and paved the way for the development of other innovations of this technology used in the manufacturing of small shapes with high accuracy.

A few more types of additive manufacturing processes were developed subsequent to the appearance of SLA in the early 1990s. Of course, it should be noted that Scott Crump, in 1988, created fused deposition modeling, which made 3D printing affordable and much more accessible. Fused deposition modeling was a type of AM in which the model was constructed layer by layer, whereby thermoplastic material was extruded through a heated nozzle.

Due to its simplicity and versatility of usage, the technology was employed in 3D printing, which was among the most preferred applications.

At the beginning of the 2000s, SLS and direct metal laser sintering (DMLS) paved the way for growth in additive manufacturing. SLS was a technology that was developed in the late 1980s at the University of Texas by Carl Deckard and his colleagues. It bonds a variety of solid powder materials by using a laser beam. For making finer as well as complex and large components, which are used in areas of aerospace and in the medical field, this method was rather fruitful. DMLS was actually an extension of SLS that focuses on metal powders and was now needed to produce segments that possessed great strength and load-bearing capability.

The advancement of multimaterial printing and bioprinting also helped along the way in the progress of additive manufacturing. Utilizing living cells and biological materials, bioprinting, which emerged with the start of the twenty-first century, allows the development of tissue and organ replicas. The presented technique was a step forward in the field of tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. On the other hand, in multimaterial printing, the use of several materials was enabled, and this leads to the complexity and interest of printed products.

The commercialization phase of additive manufacturing witnessed in the 2010s saw significant advancements in the manufacturing technique. The availability of open-source printers, along with the drop in price for the printing material, made the technology widely available for researchers, engineers, and the hobbyist fraternity. Primarily due to the recent democratization of the 3D printing process, the technology’s applications have ranged from rapid prototyping to custom-built medical implants.

First, additive manufacturing has continued to advance today through materials science, process improvement, and, more importantly, integration with other technologies such as robotics and artificial intelligence. The historical development of additive manufacturing shows that it became a turning point in production and design and could eventually change some industries such as automobiles and airplanes, especially in the medical field.
