
Edge detection methods that denote the capability of detecting these abrupt rates of change are used to explain their performance.

Basically, edge detection algorithms work on changes in pixel intensity, and these changes give you the boundary of an image. Such methods usually involve using the differential of intensity of the image in the hope of magnifying drastically different areas. The primary aim is to identify and outline the shape of anatomical structures, which in turn are essential for further characterization.

Several edge detection operators are commonly used, each with its own strengths and limitations:

- **Sobel operator:** Sobel operators are used to operate on an image and measure the first derivative of image intensity in the horizontal and vertical directions. It applies convolution kernels to enhance edges and is found to be useful in direction-based edge detection. But it is sensitive to noise and not very effective on areas with weak edges.

- **Canny edge detector:** This is a multistage technique, which involves hysteresis for edge detection of the image in addition to the gradient computation, non-maximum suppression, and Gaussian smoothing. It has excellent edge detection accuracy and some level of noise reduction capability. Due to its high performance in any kind of imaging scenario, it is often used.

- **Prewitt operator:** Like the Sobel operator, the Prewitt operator also measures the first-order grey-level difference and intensity gradient. It allows the use of different kernels. It is successful in edge detection and can easily be modified for use in different environments. It is too noisy and needs some additional processing to get better edge detection results.

- **Roberts Cross operator:** The Roberts Cross operator aims at detecting edges; its gradient is calculated by the use of two convolution masks. It is very effective in images where the contrast between objects and their backgrounds is high, though it is not very good at determining edges where the contrast is low and images are noisy.

In medical image processing, edge detection methods are employed to locate sharp boundaries like organs’ structural boundaries. They are used as a standalone method in less complicated segmentation methods and together with other methods to improve its performance. This is especially applicable in imaging modalities such as X-ray, in which the high-contrast image can be processed well with edge detection.

Medical image segmentation poses several challenges to edge detection techniques. Noise and artifacts commonly present in medical images can significantly impact edge detection accuracy, increasing the likelihood of false positives and resulting in incomplete or fragmented edge mapping. These methods are particularly ineffective in scenarios where image contrast is low and edge transitions are smooth, a condition often encountered in specific pathological cases. However, these limitations have been addressed in more advanced methods that incorporate noise reduction and edge enhancement, leading to more reliable and accurate segmentation outcomes.

## 3.4 Clustering-based approaches

This approach to segmenting medical images has been widely used for many years as a powerful and general framework for partitioning images based on similarity criteria. These techniques operate on the principle that image pixels with similar features such as intensity or texture can be grouped into distinct clusters. ### 3.4.1 *k*-Means clustering

*k*-Means clustering is one of the most employed clustering methods for image segmentation. In this approach, an image is divided into *k* clusters through iterated assignment of each pixel to the closest cluster centroid; the centroids, on the other hand, update according to the mean of the assigned pixels. *k*-Means is useful and simple in terms of segmenting images that mainly had fairly equal means with clear partitions. Note that the algorithm is sensitive to the initialization and requires the number of clusters to be prespecified, thus limiting its applicability.

### 3.4.2 Gaussian mixture models (GMMs)

Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) are an extension of *k*-means that make the assumption that data were produced from a number of Gaussian distributions. Each cluster distribution was assumed to be a Gaussian density function, and the maximum likelihood estimates of the parameters of these densities were computed using the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm for Gaussian mixture distributions. GMM is especially useful for cases where the underlying image intensity distributions are overlapping and the shapes of these distributions are different. This method allows for what is known as soft clustering; instead of assigning each pixel to a specific cluster, it gives a probability of belonging to each cluster.

### 3.4.3 Fuzzy *c*-means clustering (FCM)

Fuzzy *c*-means (FCM) is an extension of the *k*-means algorithm in the direction of fuzzy logic. In FCM, each pixel can belong to as many clusters as the actual number of clusters and possesses membership values that differentiate the significant levels of classification. This approach was helpful for segmenting images where the boundaries between segments were quite difficult to determine and where segments overlap, allowing better representation of the pixels. A pixel intensity weighted by membership degrees enhances the cluster center update process and replaces the conventional *k*-means update process.

### 3.4.4 Mean shift clustering

Mean shift clustering searches for modes of data distribution to produce clusters. The algorithm iteratively moves data points toward the center of the window, which defines a density-based cluster. Mean shift is quite effective when the underlying image density and segmentation shapes are arbitrary. Since the number of clusters does not have to be forced beforehand, it can be used in images with numerous clusters. The results are significantly affected by the bandwidth parameter, through which the window size is determined.

### 3.4.5 Watershed algorithm

The watershed algorithm was derived from topographic watersheds and was applied to image segmentation under the notion of “water flow.” It deals with the image as a topological surface and detects the catchment basins and watershed lines by mimicking the water flow from local minima. The largest margin of success is recorded while using the algorithm to segment images that are clearly defined and highly structured. It is noisy, and if noise has to be attenuated, smoothing the image is often necessary to get the best solution.

The approaches proposed based on clustering formulate the problem in a flexible way with a variety of tools dedicated to medical image segmentation, each with its own merits and demerits. Although there are various clustering procedures available in the literature that provide superior solutions to the abovementioned requirements for different segmentation problems, these techniques in most cases need precise parameter tuning and data preprocessing to get better accuracy on medical images. Current advancements in clustering algorithms have also combined them with newer advanced methods like DL, and this continues to refine segmentation, making it efficient for clinical use.

## 3.5 Model-based methods

Model-based methods for medical image segmentation involve using mathematical and statistical models and aim to draw out structures and regions from images. These approaches provide a framework for image segmentation and take into consideration a priori knowledge and assumptions about the form and characteristics of the structures of a patient’s body.

### 3.5.1 Active contours and snakes

Active contour models, also called snakes, are a model-based technique that utilizes energy to detect edges of an object. The method adjusts an object contour using an energy function composed of internal and external forces. Internal forces keep the contour as smooth as possible, whereas external forces drive the contour toward the object edge. This method is especially effective in segmenting objects with clear edges but is not as good with objects that have blurred edges.
