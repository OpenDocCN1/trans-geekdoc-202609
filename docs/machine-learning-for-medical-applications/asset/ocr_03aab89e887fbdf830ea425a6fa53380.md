# 5.4 X-ray and other modalities

X-ray images are one of the main and most trusted imaging methods used in diagnosis of patients. This way, pictures of interior structures are made by passing X-rays through the body and then registering the extent to which they are picked up by a detector. Different tissues absorb X-rays differently, which results in the contrast shown in X-ray images: fatty tissues, for example, are darker than less fatty tissues, and materials like bones, which are very dense, appear white.

# 5.4.1 Characteristics and applications

Bone and joint imaging: The ideal imaging modality is usually X-rays, which are particularly useful in visualizing bony structures and are used often in the diagnosis of fracture, dislocations, and degenerative bone diseases. Given that fat has a high attenuation value and soft tissues have relatively low attenuation values compared to the bone, excellent depiction of the skeletal system is achievable.

Chest imaging: Thoracic conditions were diagnosed using X-ray imaging as the standard form of diagnostics in the corresponding pathological state. It is used for the diagnosis of lung disease, including pneumonia, tuberculosis, and pulmonary edema, and for observing the heart and large vessels.

Limitations: X-rays give useful diagnostic information about the tissues; however, they are unable to distinguish between soft tissues; further, they are not as useful in staging early diseases. Radiation exposure is critically important in X-ray imaging since it involves use of ionizing radiation and this is dangerous particularly in children and pregnant women.

# 5.4.2 Computed tomography

CT is a very advanced imaging method employing X-rays that create cross-sectional views of the body in thin slices. CT scanners accomplish this by reconstructing many X-ray pictures from preceding orientations and create a series of two-dimensional slices, which when combined, create three-dimensional views of interior structures.
