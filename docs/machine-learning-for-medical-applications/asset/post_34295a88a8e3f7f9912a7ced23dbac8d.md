## 2.5 Comparison with traditional fluorescent materials

The use of QDs has an important advantage over the use of traditional fluorescent materials in a variety of practices, especially medical imaging practices. This comparison is aimed at shedding light on the major differences in terms of properties and efficiency between QDs and conventional fluorescent dyes.

### 2.5.1 Size-tunable emission

The emission of light is another distinctive property of quantum dots (QDs), and it surpasses that of traditional fluorescent materials due to the tunability of their emissions based on particle size. This tunability provides QDs with a significant advantage in multiplexed imaging applications, enabling the simultaneous labeling of multiple biomarkers that would otherwise be challenging to distinguish.

### 2.5.2 Photostability and brightness

In addition to efficiency, QDs also demonstrate high photostability in contrast to the traditional fluorescence materials. Generally, organic dyes undergo photobleaching, which causes the intensity of the emitted fluorescence to diminish with time upon exposure to the exciting light source; not the case with QDs. This extraordinary photostability makes it possible to prolong the imaging sessions and obtain more stable data. Further, QDs also show enhanced brightness and fluorescence intensity than the traditional organic dyes, which increases the sensitivity and resolution levels of imaging modalities.

### 2.5.3 Quantum yield and efficiency

Quantum yield, which is a representation of the ratio between the amount of emitted fluorescence and the quantity of absorbed photons, is generally higher in QDs than in typical fluorescent dyes. QDs enjoy more efficient conversion of the absorbed photons into emitted fluorescence owing to the small size of nanometers and unique electronic characteristics. This higher quantum yield leads to stronger and sharper imaging signals making the overall performance of the imaging applications better.

### 2.5.4 Spectral range and overlap

QDs provide for wide spectral range and low spectral width compared to conventional fluorescent materials. The fact that narrow and size-dependent emission spectra can be generated enables separation of signals in multiplexed assays. Conventional fluorescent dyes depict wider spectra of emission and greater levels of spectral overlap between them and thus analyses involving multiple targets within one sample are somewhat challenging. Due to the sharp emission peaks of QDs, it is easier to distinguish between closely placed absorption wavelengths in the multiplexed imaging.

### 2.5.5 Chemical stability and functionalization

QDs typically demonstrate higher chemical stability than fluorescence dyes, for example, organic dyes. This stability reduces the frequency with which the reagent must be replenished and minimizes artifacts in imaging studies. In addition, the surface chemistry of QDs can be altered with different coatings and ligands that make it possible to control specificity with biomolecules. Both classic dyes can be functionalized, but the chemical stability and other opportunities of the QD surface modifications provide extra opportunities for the specific targeting and greater imaging ability.

## 3 Materials and synthesis methods

### 3.1 Semiconductor materials used (e.g., CdSe, CdTe, and Si)

QDs consist of semiconductor materials that determine their optical and electronic characteristics. The kind of semiconductor has direct implications on the emission wavelength, quantum yield, and overall performance of the QDs. Semiconductor substances: potential materials for QD application are CdSe, CdTe, and Si. Every material has its own benefits and properties to enforce different uses and applications.

### 3.1.1 Cadmium selenide (CdSe)

CdSe is also one of the most popular materials in the field of QDs among all types of semiconductors. CdSe QDs are distinguished by their highly efficient photoluminescence and wide absorption bands. CdSe QDs, which can be synthesized to emit light at a variety of wavelengths across the visible spectrum due to changes in particle size, allow for a great variety of imaging uses. Though the material, which is utilized here is cadmium which was a heavy metal and has environmental and health effects, it has led to more research being conducted to find other materials.

### 3.1.2 Cadmium telluride (CdTe)

The QD materials include CdTe, which is another broadly used semiconductor material. CdTe QDs are characterized by high photostability and the quantum yield, as well as the transition to the emission of light with different wavelengths is achieved with the help of the change in the size of the CdTe QDs. Of CdTe QDs, which can be used in productive applications, special attention is given to those that possess good absorption and emission characteristics for sensing. Like in CdSe, the utilization of cadmium in QDs, specifically on CdTe, adds to the environmental and safety concerns, and hence researchers are looking for environmentally friendly replacements.

### 3.1.3 Silicon (Si)

In fact, Silicon QDs are less hazardous compared to the cadmium-related commodities. Si QDs are safe since the substrate material, silicon, is nontoxic and readily available, the former characteristic being of great significance in biomedical image enhancement. Generally, Si QDs have comparatively lower quantum yield and lesser control on photoluminescence emission as compared to CdSe and CdTe QDs. Back then, QDs were difficult to process mainly due to their poor photoluminescence and stability, but progress in the surface passivation engineering of Si QDs has revolutionized the quality of these materials. Therefore, applications such as tissue imaging, cell imaging, and environment friendly applications are best enhanced by the use of Si QDs.

This work specifically deals with lead sulfide (PbS) and lead selenide (PbSe) materials.

PbS and PbSe are two important examples of semiconductor material that is appropriate for QDs because of the following optical characteristics. It was found that distinct PbS and PbSe QDs possess narrow emission band, which can be size-tuned up to the infrared regime. These materials demonstrate high efficiency of photoluminescence and photostability; toxicity of lead constituent indicates the need for proper control over application and disposal of such materials.

### 3.1.4 Zinc oxide (ZnO) and zinc sulfide (ZnS)

Zinc oxide and zinc sulfide are other semiconductor materials that are employed in the synthesis of QDs. ZnO QDs are characterized by large band-gap and high resistance to photobleaching, which makes them good prospects for use in optoelectronics and imaging. ZnS QDs are used in the structure of core-shell QDs, where they act like the shell to enhance stability and minimize surface defects. The ZnO and ZnS materials have been reported to have merits as regards the toxicity and environmental solutions as compared to the cadmium QDs.
