## 5 Reinforcement learning enhanced hybrid models

### 5.1 Reinforcement learning for adaptive anomaly detection

RL was a particularly useful approach to adaptive anomaly detection because it provides systems with an opportunity to develop the best strategies according to the discovered environment. Unlike supervised learning, RL agents do not depend upon fixed databases. Instead, they learn policies that maximize the reward signal, and this was why they were successfully applicable to the detection of anomalies in a changeable and uncertain world. In the context of anomaly detection, a reward function can be tailored to focus on the correct identification of anomalies while at the same time lowering the rate of false positives, to make the system agile enough to adapt to a different scenario with different data distributions.

![](img/18722240e44c225d7ff2f58a10eecb4a_307_0.png)

Figure 10: Reinforcement learning for adaptive anomaly detection.

The RL-based anomaly detection procedure in Figure 10 was predicated on the need for the detection job to be stated as a Markov decision process (MDP). The state of the system constitutes the current context and is calculated with the help of the features of the input data. Actions relate to assigning given data as normal, while the reward signal is the feedback for the correctness of the labels. In the long run of the interactions, the RL agent optimizes the policy for the cumulative reward and enhances the anomalous data detection capability. This feature of the formulation enables the system to understand that feedback from its operating environment is sometimes bizarre and hence employs different decision mechanisms in the case of feedback of such a nature.

Another characteristic feature of RL is the dual approach that involves exploration of the environment as well as exploiting the learned information. Exploration is very important to enable the model to search for new strategies that have not been identified in the data set, especially when searching for new and quite rare anomalies; while in exploitation, learned policies are used to gain the maximum detection rate of miscellaneous objects. In anomaly detection tasks, this balance is important because it makes the system capable of producing good results for new anomalous patterns without being impacted by previous patterns of anomalies. To keep exploration at an acceptable level, other methods like epsilon-greedy strategies are used, which also make RL-based systems more capable in more complex and nonstationary environments.

The approach of RL deepens anomaly detection across various application areas, among which are network security, industry monitoring, and healthcare diagnostics. For example, in cybersecurity, RL agents can learn different strategies for protection from new threats since the policies can be easily updated. Thus, RL-based anomaly detection in industrial systems can adapt to equipment deterioration and operating settings, which attempt to hint at failures before they worsen. Similarly, in the context of healthcare, RL can be used to detect signals of variation in patient data streams in real time and manage variability for each and every patient.

### 5.2 Exploring deep Q-learning in anomaly detection tasks

DQL, an improved form of Q-learning integrated with deep NNs, has been used in anomaly detection because of its ability to learn in high-dimensional data spaces. Original Q-learning approaches are based on the use of tabular representations of Q-values, which cannot be applied to large datasets. DQL resolves this issue by training deep NNs to approximate these Q-values to design anomaly detection in variants of environments, including time-series analysis, image sorting, and cybersecurity. With experience replay and TD learning, DQL is capable of finding good solutions to real-world anomaly detection problems.

![](img/18722240e44c225d7ff2f58a10eecb4a_308_0.png)

**Figure 11:** DQL framework for anomaly detection.

In frameworks of DQL for anomaly detection based on DQL, the environment is the source of data; states are therefore the features of the data, actions define the decision of whether the data was normal, and reward is given based on the rate of accurate detection. The idea is to find the best policy for the agent, which results in the maximum total reward, with well-detected anomalies and as few false alarms as possible. Strictly speaking, the deep Q-network is a mode of function approximation to transform state-action pairs to Q-values that can help make decisions. Figure 11 shows how experience replay and the target network are utilized to improve convergence and reduce instability throughout the training process.

In terms of using DQL for anomaly detection, another big advantage was the possibility to analyze high-dimensional input. This capability is most useful in situations like image-based anomaly detection, where each image translates into a high-dimensional feature space. DQL applies CNNs to identify innovative spatial characteristics from visual data, which minimizes dimensionality and targets the significant anomalous area. In time-series data, we use recurrent structures inside DQL that help in capturing temporal structure for improving anomaly detection in sequential data patterns. These strategies help DQL to detect various and sophisticated patterns in various datasets.

In DQL, the touchpoint of reward function design is very important for anomaly detection. The reward function aims at tuning between true-positive detections and false alarms while keeping the model locked on worthwhile anomalies. Sanctions on false positives and bonuses on correct anomaly detection build an optimal detection policy for the agent. In addition to simple reward functions that encourage the agent to avoid unknown states, more advanced reward functions that increase when anomalies are encountered, taking into account the number of critical anomalies and the rarity of their occurrence, also complement the DQL approach to work with imbalanced datasets. They make sure that the model does not let important kinds of failures pass through but is also not overly protective about less serious kinds of failures.

DQL has been implemented and used successfully in various domains that require an advanced form of anomaly detection functionality. In cybersecurity, DQL models find existing intrusions within networks through the behavior of incoming and outgoing data packets and self-learning. In the healthcare field, a number of DQL-based systems possess the ability to scan medical imaging data and find anomalies suggestive of diseases, which forms the basis for early diagnostics. In industrial automation, DQL observes sensor data to identify any failure in equipment in operation, reducing downtime. The above applications show the extensibility of DQL in meeting a multiplicity of anomaly detection needs.
