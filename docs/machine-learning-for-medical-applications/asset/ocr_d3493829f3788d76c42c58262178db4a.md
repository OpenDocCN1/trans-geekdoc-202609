Scaffold optimization has been exercised with the help of the most popular evolutionary algorithm known as the GA. The central scheme of GAs is to maintain a population of potential solutions, and then evolve them on the basis of selection, crossover, and mutation operators, and in this respect, GAs mimic the process of natural selection. In Figure 7, we have exemplified the use of evolutionary algorithms on scaffold structures and demonstrated how the algorithms loop through and improve the structures for maximum effectiveness. The “weaker” genes, in terms of fitness, are gradually replaced over many generations, while those that are “stronger”, measured in terms of the objective function, have a higher chance of being selected for reproduction, crossover, and high mutation rates. Due to this, a population of candidate solutions is repeatedly improved in each generation of GAs and eventually come closer to the optimal solution.

Scaffold optimization also uses another evolutionary approach, known as PSO. PSO simulates the societies of fish and birds, where each particle moves its position in the search space according to its neighboring particles, and by its own experience, the best solution is found. In the search space, every particle maintains its position and velocity and updates them based on the global best solution discovered by the swarm and the best solution found by the particle. PSO methods allow finding solutions in the search space and solve the problem by using the particles’ interaction and data exchange.
