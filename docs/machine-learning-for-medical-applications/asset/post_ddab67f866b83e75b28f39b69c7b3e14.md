Combining GANs and CNNs harnesses the strengths of both techniques, providing both realistic data generation and high accuracy in identifying outputs that deviate from normal patterns making them particularly well-suited for visual anomaly detection.

Based on NNs, GANs are two NNs – the generator and the discriminator in conflict. The generator follows the next objective – to generate fake data samples, which resemble the training data set, while the discriminator has the opposite task – to distinguish fake samples coming from the generator from the real ones. In this manner, GANs are capable of learning the complex data distribution, which makes them perfect candidates for anomaly detection. In the context of anomaly detection, GANs are trained on normal data, to approximate a distribution of samples that looks like a part of the input data as close as possible. When a sample outside this learned data distribution is used, the generator produces an unrealistic output because of an anomaly. This mismatch is quantifiable; the extent of difference between generated and real data can be computed, which allows the anomalies to be seen.

CNNs have been found to be very efficient when handling visual data because of the spatial hierarchical nature of the data. CNNs pass a number of filters over the input data; these filters search for features such as edges, textures and shapes. These features are then accumulated gradually in order to build top-level abstractions for the data. Although CNN is often mentioned in the context of feature extraction in the image, the extracted features can then be forwarded to a GAN for further use. The CNNs’ capturing of fine features reduces the complexity of the extracted features by the GANs and provides high-quality input features that are required for sample generation and detection of abnormal patterns.

The approach that utilizes GANs and CNNs for anomaly detection is performed in two steps. To recall, the initial component in the CNN actively learns to perform feature extraction from the inputted visual data. These are then passed as inputs to the generator of the GAN, which tries to generate new samples that can be confused with the normal data. In the GAN, the discriminator analyzes and approves sample images synthesized by the generator and the actual samples in the dataset. When an anomalous input is fed to the network, the generator fails to synthesize a sample that belongs to the normal data distribution. Such deviations of the produced data from the real one can be measured with the help of some distance measures, including the mean-squared error, and can be implemented for anomaly detection in terms of the specified perceptual loss function.

## 4.4 Recurrent neural networks for sequential anomaly detection in visual data

The detection of anomalies in sequential pictorial information such as a series of images is not easy due to the temporal relations and intricate patterns that are embedded in the sequential data. Data can then be sequential in which its structure changes over time indicating that the frames are in a developmental state. RNNs have recently emerged as leading solutions for working on sequential data as they can learn temporal relations and other contextual features. Because sequence information over time can be learned, such as learning the normal sequence of events, RNNs can identify events that are out of the ordinary, so together with LSTM it is excellent for activities like video surveillance, activity recognition, and anomaly detection in time-series images.

RNNs are supposed to deal with sequential inputs: take an additional hidden state, which contains the information about previous inputs and affects the current input prediction. This ability to pass on temporal information was the reason that RNNs have the capacity to comprehend long term relationships; something that cannot be done by feedforward NNs. In the case of anomaly detection, RNNs are used to train normal sequences in order to acquire normal temporal patterns of the data. With regards to normal data, the program learns the temporal pattern and whenever it is presented with an anomalous input, the program highlights the difference from the learnt pattern. The utility of the model is based on its ability to assess not only the frames themselves, but the relations between them in terms of temporal dynamics.

Gated recurrent units (GRUs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks’ outputs are two RNN types that were created to solve some of the issues with conventional RNNs, such as the vanishing gradient issue seen in Figure 9. LSTMs and GRUs employ additional control gates to enable the networks to capture long dependency information and discourage an otherwise known forget-algorithm in processing long sequence data. These architectures are especially useful in identifying abnormalities in the visual data if the temporal organization of the sequences is important. For instance, in video surveillance, LSTMs can learn the sequences of movements in the object over time and if something is irregular, including a motion in a way that is not normal, LSTMs detect them as abnormal.

The first benefit of utilizing RNNs in perception-based sequential anomaly detection is that temporal recurring characteristics can be learned among two consecutive frames in visual data. This temporal context becomes especially important in such cases as the detection of a subject’s odd behavior in security camera footage of equipment in some manufacturing lines. Here, what is considered normal is clear; in other words, normal means that there is an established sequence, and anything that altered the sequence is abnormal. For example, in video-based anomaly detection, a human walking pattern in specific direction can be learnt by an RNN as a sequence. In the case of an abnormal observation, namely, when a vehicle is not moving and/or changes its direction, the RNN recognizes those conditions involving analysis of the current frame and previous frames.

Since RNNs are used for visual anomaly detection, the visual data needs to be transformed into a suitable sequential form. A common method is to represent the video frames as feature vectors by using CNNs and extracting a feature map from each frame. These feature vectors containing basic information of the visual content are then passed into a model based on RNN to consider temporal relationships. The applicability of CNNs in the spatial feature extraction and RNNs in temporal modeling means that the system can attend to both the content of the frames and their temporal dependencies, providing the basis for detecting even small changes in dynamic scenes.

One of the input parameters of the RNN training for the discovery of sequenced anomalies is the loss function, which describes the ability of the model to discover the presence of anomalies. In most cases, the learned model forecasts the subsequent frame in the sequence that contain the frame based on the prior frames. There are two methods of finding the anomalies: prediction error and reconstruction error method on each frame. If the error signal goes beyond a certain value, then a frame is said to be anomalous. For instance, if motion trajectory of an object in a video was learnt using RNN, any drastic and sudden shift from the normal expected direction of an object leads to a high value of reconstruction error signifying an anomalous behavior.

As for the next two types of RNNs, namely LSTMs and GRUs, good at capturing temporal dependences, the types of RNNs described above have their own problems, especially when the sequence processing is very long with complex and variable visual information. Some of these difficulties are remediable using attention mechanisms, which help the model to hone in on some parts of a sequence while making predictions. Stacked and bidirectional RNNs enable attention mechanisms to guide the examination through some key frames within the sequence where there are changes in behavior and hence enhance the ability of the model to detect the anomalies. As a result, attention-based approaches can increase the model’s ability to identify time-step features and provide more accurate anomalous patterns in high dynamics.

## 4.5 Combining variational autoencoders and deep neural networks for robust anomaly detection

The integration of VAEs and DNNs is believed to present the best solution for anomalous data detection regarding high-dimensional data, especially in visual data sets. VAEs, which belong to the family of generative models, are adept in learning data-efficient probabilistic representations of data by mapping the input into a latent space and reconstructing it from the learned one. Such a reconstruction process enables VAEs to achieve an appreciable level of capturing the raw structure and inherent patterns in the data. On the other hand, DNNs, which incorporate depth in their architectures, are capable of inferring complex and nonlinear mapping of large data sets. Through integration of these two functional models, the system has a capacity of incorporating generative properties as well as discriminative learning properties that make the anomaly detection systems more accurate and robust.
