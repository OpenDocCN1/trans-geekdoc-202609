
## 2.5 Evolution of standards and protocols

Standard and protocol development in MIP have played a crucial role in the advancement of this field and in achieving synergy across different imaging systems. The increased use of standards has ensured standardization of data transfer, which, together with increased image quality and improved flow of clinical processes, is critical in diagnosis and management.

### 2.5.1 Beginning of DICOM standard

A major milestone in the evolution of medical imaging occurred in 1985, when the American College of Radiology (ACR) and the National Electrical Manufacturers Association (NEMA) introduced the DICOM standards. DICOM provided a unified framework for the digital archiving and transmission of medical images, ensuring compatibility across various imaging equipment and healthcare networks. As shown in Figure 3, the standard defines both image storage formats and communication protocols, significantly enhancing interoperability between systems from different manufacturers. With imaging data now commonly stored in DICOM format, integration into Electronic Health Records (EHRs) and Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) has improved the efficiency and consistency of medical imaging workflows.

### 2.5.2 The development of image quality assurance protocols

With the improvement of the medical imaging technologies, there were growing needs for developing sound image quality assurance. The aim of these guidelines was to ensure that imaging equipment consistently delivers excellent image quality, which is mandatory to aid diagnosis and treatment planning in surgeries performed at the hospital. Numerous guidelines and measures for picture quality have already been set and developed by various organizations such as RSNA and AAPM. Some of these are protocols for calibration and testing of imaging equipment's performance, as well as protocols for imaging equipment maintenance. These quality assurance measures that have been put into practice have helped make medical imaging more accurate and credible to enhance diagnostic processes.

### 2.5.3 Importation of regulatory guidelines for implementation of medical images

The FDA and EMA are two of the major regulatory authorities that set rules, regulations, and guidelines for management of medical imaging technologies. They include the rules regulating the access to novel imaging devices, requirements for the safety of such equipment, and data protection rules. This was due to the fact that the regulatory framework needed to reflect standard protocols across organizations to guarantee that the generated datasets are clean, accurate, and diverse – all valuable attributes that can help minimize the risk of algorithm overfitting.

### 3.1.2 Manipulating differences in image resolution and its formats

Different techniques may preserve image resolution and/or format, which define how finer details and accuracy of the information captured are affected. Images at higher resolution give better details about the scene but when it comes to image processing or analysis more computational power is needed. On the other hand, coarser images as a result of low resolution will prove difficult to analyze by virtue of containing less detail. To ensure that an algorithm can provide accurate results, it has to be designed to correct the image for different qualities, meaning that the methods of image processing must be scalable depending on the quality of the image; while different formats and resolutions might lead to distortion, it is necessary to make algorithms adapt to a number of imaging conditions.

The data heterogeneity of medical image analysis increases the need for defining and developing new methods and rules for handling issues arising from the variations in modalities, protocols, populations, and datasets. It is for these reasons that the following problems had to be resolved in order to make MIP systems helpful tools in supporting clinical decision-making within a wide range of healthcare applications. Thus they also present problems with regard to the validity, reliability, transferability, and dependability of the systems.

## 3.2 Scalability and data volume

Medical image analysis is a diagnostic procedure highly affected by the scalability and data volume management problems. Solving these problems is crucial to maintaining the quality and availability of medical imaging services as imaging equipment changes and the volume of information generated increases.

### 3.2.1 Managing a growing amount of imaging information

The continued advancement of imaging technologies has led to a substantial increase in the volume of data generated during each imaging session. Modalities such as MRI and CT scans produce extensive image datasets, making the processes of acquisition, storage, analysis, and management increasingly complex. As illustrated in Figure 4, the growing demand for higher-resolution and more informative images driven by the need for personalized and precise diagnostics further contributes to this data surge. Effectively managing this data volume is now critical, not only for maintaining operational efficiency but also for ensuring the timely and accurate delivery of diagnostic results.

### 3.2.2 Problems in data storage and management for big data

Due to the large cumulative size of imaging datasets, their storage and modalities of handling necessitate significantly large computing power and hardware. The current conventional storage systems face a problem in responding to the expanding data storage demands, resulting in problems such as data congestion and slow data access. Any large dataset thus requires scalable means of storage such as cloud storage systems and high-capacity storage arrays. Therefore, the data management practice must provide assurance on the quality, security, and retrievability of imaging data, which makes the data management process complex.

### 3.2.3 Challenges in designing image processing algorithms for huge high-dimensional images

The problem arises from the fact that many medical image data come in formats that are difficult to handle, say from imaging techniques that produce multiple slices or contain time-series analysis. The amount of computations that go into the analysis and processing of these voluminous and multifaceted data is considerable and requires high-level algorithms and computing power. Self-similar processing models, utilization of distributed computing and GPUs can be useful in overcoming these kinds of scalability problems, but also introduce problems of their own, especially in implementation and resource management.

### 3.2.4 Ways in which big data affects the amount of computation required

One of the main challenges, at least in the United States, concerns demands on computer properties like memory and storage as well as computational power due to high volumes of imaging data. Software and general organization of large amounts of data in image processing activities require efficient methods and high-performance computing systems. Large dataset processing can be time-consuming and hence associated with longer processing time and higher operating costs, which are not favorable in health practices and reduce efficiency in medical imaging. Algorithm optimization and resource allocation are two ways to minimize these difficulties in the clinical practice environment.

In today’s world, the need for efficient data compression techniques has long been felt, owing to increasing demand for storage space and shrinkage of storage costs.

Compression assists in reducing the bulk of qualification data with medical imaging known to be sensitive to compression. Compression has been defined as an essential process in medical imaging that can minimize the storage space needed to contain images and enhance the speed with which images can be transmitted across networks without any loss of visual quality. Different mechanisms of data compression terms – lossless compression technique and the lossy compression technique – help in trade-off of extra data and image quality. The refinement of highly compressed data leads to high-quality images and small data volume, which is under development and may have further influence to storage and analysis.

It is crucial to make changes regarding the object of analysis since medical images cannot be effectively analyzed at scale; they are simply too numerous to be handled manually. The strategies that have been put in place include the deployment of scalable storage platform, review of data processing strategies, and highly efficient compression techniques to curb the increasing issue of demand and to provide timely medical imaging services.
