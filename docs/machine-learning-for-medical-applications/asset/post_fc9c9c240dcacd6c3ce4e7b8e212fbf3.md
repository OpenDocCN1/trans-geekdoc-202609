## 1 Introduction

Scanned visual information was incorporated in essential applications like industrial automation, healthcare diagnostics, and security systems, and the demand for reliable and efficient approaches to countering anomalies has scaled up [1]. Abnormalities in the optical signals point to defects and irregularities, which require reliable schemes for detection in order to maintain system and decision-making reliability [2]. Some anomaly detection techniques were rule-based and statistical models, which are not so effective in the case of large, high-dimensional data [3]. These limitations are a result of the discrete methods utilized in the algorithms, where fixed thresholds and assumptions cannot capture to a great extent the variability of data sets in real-life scenarios [4].

Traditionally implemented techniques cannot handle these issues, for which machine learning (ML) models enjoy popularity due to their data pattern learning capability to handle them [5]. The regular approaches, enumerated as SVMs and PCA, have remained popular for conducting anomaly detection [6]. These approaches sometimes suffer from many issues, such as how to handle big data and feature extraction at higher dimensions of vision databases [7]. But deep learning has brought the next level of enhancement to this field, mainly in aspects of feature learning and enhanced detection capability [8]. The convolutional neural network (CNN) and autoencoder are decidedly remarkable for spatial patterns and tiny variances in intensified scanned images [9].

Modern trends in the sphere have pointed towards the efforts to complement the existing and novel ML approaches with context-specific requirements to increase the detection performance [10, 11]. To deal with the issues of data imbalance, real-time adaptability, and time complexities, different supervised and unsupervised learning methodologies, including reinforcement learning (RL) models, have been put forward [12]. The overlaying of explainable AI (XAI) approaches has become a prominent region of study for enhancing the explainability of anomaly detection frameworks, thus increasing trust and usefulness in applications that entail high levels of risk. Here we present advanced ML models for anomaly detection on scanned visual data that try to balance accuracy and complexity [13–15].

### 2.1 Semisupervised learning approaches for anomaly detection

In a variety of real-world applications, first and foremost was the issue of the lack of labeled data, especially when anomalies are the exception and often hard to characterize. Semisupervised learning appears to be one of the best solutions to the problem as it incorporates advantages of both approaches from the group of supervised and unsupervised learning. Semisupervised learning happens when the models are trained with a little annotated data with a greater amount of unmarked data. This approach was a good compromise since it uses the little available labeled data to supervise the process, while at the same time utilizing the large amount of available unlabeled data, thus making it very useful in anomaly detection where labeling is very expensive.

In this method of semisupervised learning, the algorithm focuses on learning the nature of the normal data and applying it on the unidentified portion to separate the anomalies. It was especially beneficial when labeled anomaly points are limited, and the model learns more from a big number of unlabeled data that was typical. Semisupervised methods are generally realized by a combination of clustering, classification methods, and density estimation in order to differentiate the normal data from the unusual cases.

The most popular method of applying semisupervised anomaly detection was self-training, where the first model was trained using a small labeled data set and was then iteratively refined with predictions of the same data from the unlabeled set. In the self-training process, the considered model labels the unlabeled data based on the current learned normal behavior, and such pseudo-labeled instances are incorporated into the training data set. In multiple passes through the set, the model was rendered refined to identify patterns of anomalies not contained in the selected set. This approach was advantageous in that it can use labeled as well as unlabeled data and thus improve generalization to unseen data.

The other pattern was semisupervised anomaly detection that uses a graph where nodes are the data points, with the edges forming the connections in between. Semisupervised graph-based anomaly detection methods center on the labeled nodes to spread the labels through the graph, so that strong similarity between a labeled node and an unlabeled node can be used in order to label the unlabeled node as an anomalous node. These methods are especially beneficial in identifying outliers in large, intricate systems and data spaces of high dimensions because relationships between the data points provide insights when the single attributes do not.

Similarly, autoencoders and variational autoencoders (VAEs) are being implemented in semisupervised anomaly detection. These models, which are trained to extract compressed representations of the input data, can be trained with a limited set of labeled data that are normalized. A trained autoencoder reconstructs the input data afterward, and the difference in the two data sets was considered as a measure of anomaly. Due to the fact that these models are normally trained on normal data, high reconstruction errors imply anomalous data. It also indicates that the model can be further fine-tuned with other unlabeled data in order to enhance the model's exquisite ability to identify further deeper and concealed anomalies.

An application of ensemble learning to the semisupervised learning approaches also enhances detection, since multiple models are trained on different splits of the labeled and unlabeled data sets and the joint result was used in detecting malicious patterns. For instance, bagging and boosting strategies are beneficial for semisupervised anomaly detection methods, since, due to the model decentralization, the influence of overfitting is reduced. Ensemble methods can combine various opinions on what should be considered as normal behavior and then use these opinions to assess deviant behaviors more skillfully.

Despite the seemingly high efficiency of the approaches of semisupervised learning, the latter is not without its drawbacks. One of the challenges was to prevent the pseudo-labeled data from introducing noise that arises from wrong labeling, which definitely affects the performance of the model in terms of detection. Also, the choice of model of semisupervised learning depends on the nature of the data set and the particular problem of anomaly detection. Nevertheless, the use of both labeled and unlabeled data in a semisupervised framework offers a practical solution to the critical issue of limited labeled data that was commonplace in anomaly detection applications.

Semisupervised learning in the context of anomaly detection also shows promise, and the current investigation focuses on increasing the stability and effectiveness of the models. Further evolutions concern the enhancement of the algorithms themselves used for pseudo-labeling, the domain adaptation of the learning process with additional domain knowledge, and the generalization of the concept of semisupervised learning to more high-powered, multidimensional features.
