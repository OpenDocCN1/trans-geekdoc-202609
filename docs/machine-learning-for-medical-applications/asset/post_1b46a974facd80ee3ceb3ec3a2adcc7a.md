## 3 Basic principles of 3D printing

### 3.1 Fundamentals of additive manufacturing processes

3D printing, as additive manufacturing, is a process of constructing a 3D object by depositing material successively. This methodology is in contrast to other, more conventional subtractive manufacturing processes that entail the removal of material from a block of material. Because of the need to produce complex forms and intricate geometries in additive manufacturing, full control of material placement is mandatory.

Additive manufacturing was unarguably constructed on a layer-by-layer concept. The first step that was completed required one to develop a computer-aided design (CAD) to begin the model. Subsequently, we subdivided this model into a number of thin, flat layers in the XY plane. These slices serve the machine as a blueprint for layer-by-layer deposition of the materials in order to create the object. Structures so complex and convoluted that it would be impossible to provisionally design and manufacture by other means could now be built due to this layer-by-layer approach.

There are several identifiable core technologies that are associated with the additive manufacturing process, where each process employs different techniques for laying down materials. The most common technologies include:

- Fused filament fabrication is a process of using a heated nozzle to extrude thermoplastic filaments. Sequences of solid material are formed because the substance is accumulated in layers and becomes solid when it cools. Due to the relatively low cost and multifunctionality, this technique is widely used; however, the practical application is generally limited by the mechanical properties of the final parts and the resolution of the extruded layer.

- SLA: In the case of SLA, the liquid photopolymer resin that was applied was cured with the help of UV light. To solidify the resin surface at matching cross sections of the digital model, a UV laser light source was used. SLA was famous for the high accuracy of the part's resolution and its ability to produce complex, polished components. In particular, it was efficient when accuracy and specific detail of a situation were required.

- SLS: powder metallurgy, commonly known as selective laser sintering, refers to a process where powdered materials, such as metal, are joined together into solid structures using a strong laser. Free from design constraints, the laser melts and solidifies the particles in the powder selectively. This technique is appreciated for the possibilities to achieve fabulous geometries with the incorporated supports and really

### 3.3.3 SLS

SLS employs a laser to fuse powder materials, usually metal, into a solid form. The build platform was coated with a thin layer of powder, and then selective exposure to the laser was used to melt the powder particles as per the prescribed design. As the construction platform descends, the second coat of powder is applied. SLS is valued for allowing the creation of even the most detailed patterns and, at the same time, producing useful elements equipped with a high level of mechanical density and durability, which is ideal for the production of medical implants that must bear the load.

### 3.3.4 DMLS

There exists a subcategory of SLS which focuses on metal materials, and it is referred to as DMLS. In DMLS, a high-intensity laser is utilized to selectively sinter metallic particles to produce thick and dense metallic parts. For uses that need high-strength parts, including orthopedic implants and dental prostheses, the above method is compulsory. Precisely due to the need to modify the mechanical properties and biocompatibility of the implant, DMLS allows for the formation of complex metal shapes as well as the integration of lattice structures.

### 3.3.5 Digital light processing and digital micromirror device (DMD)

SLA looks very similar to DLP, but instead of using a digital light projector to solidify the photopolymer resin, the projector can illuminate the entire layer of the design all at once, while the SLA works in a point-to-point method. This technique is also renowned for the benefit of shorter build time and the provision to produce high-quality prints. DLP is deemed most useful in the generation of intricate medical models and prototypes with precision of surface details and smoothness.

### 3.3.6 Inkjet printing

Material deposition of small droplets of the build material on the build platform was termed inkjet printing, also known as material jetting. Structured in the same manner as inkjet print technology, print heads spread layers of material such as wax over the build platform and solidify. That inkjet technology that allows printing using various materials and colors was remarkable because it provides complex and rather attractive medical models.

These popular 3DP methods each have their benefits and their application to various stages of medical implant creation. Consequently, recognizing their network functions and capabilities would allow for choosing the right methods for applications within the medical industry.

### 3.4 Materials used in 3D printing and their properties

It has been established that the success of 3D printing in medical applications and the efficiency of the technology depends significantly on the choice of materials. Each material used in additive manufacturing offers distinct properties that determine its suitability for specific medical implants and devices. This understanding has led to important questions regarding how best to enhance the functionality of these materials and assess their effectiveness for various therapeutic applications.

Polymers remain the most widely used materials in 3D printing due to their affordability, ease of processing, and versatility. Common materials include those used in fused deposition modeling (FDM), such as polylactic acid (PLA) and acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS). PLA has been particularly favored especially until approximately four years ago for its biocompatibility, biodegradability, and minimal thermal shrinkage, making it ideal for applications sensitive to thermal changes. ABS, on the other hand, offers superior mechanical strength and impact resistance, making it suitable for durable prototypes. In more advanced medical implantations, high-performance polymers like polyetheretherketone and polyamide are employed due to their enhanced mechanical properties, chemical resistance, and thermal stability.

Metals used in 3D printing include titanium, cobalt-chromium alloys, and stainless steel – primarily processed through direct metal laser sintering (DMLS) and selective laser sintering (SLS). Titanium is especially valued for its favorable strength-to-weight ratio, excellent corrosion resistance, and established biocompatibility, making it ideal for craniofacial reconstruction, dental prostheses, and orthopedic implants. Stainless steel is commonly used for surgical tools and temporary implants due to its high tensile strength and fatigue resistance. Meanwhile, cobalt-chromium alloys are selected for applications involving high mechanical loads, thanks to their superior wear resistance.
