## Deep learning for image segmentation

**Abstract:** Image segmentation, an essential part of computer vision, has perhaps undergone some of the most revolutionary changes through the application of deep learning structures. This work aims at presenting a systematic survey about deep learning methods for image segmentation, with a major attention on state-of-the-art architectures and some of the problems associated with them. Starting with the first heuristic convolutional neural network and the initial U-Net model, the discussion covers features of DeepLab family and the recent attempt to incorporate transformers into segmentation tasks. Problem areas, including computationally expensive methods, loss of small features, and transfer between various datasets, are considered. Future prospects are provided and suggestions for enhancing the efficiency of the mechanism, scalability, and flexibility in the future are also added. The purpose of this chapter was to provide the readers with a comprehensive knowledge and analysis on the state and direction of the current development and potential research topics of image segmentation.

**Keywords:** image segmentation, deep learning, convolutional neural networks, U-Net, DeepLab, transformer models

## 1 Introduction

In computer vision, segmenting an image into meaningful areas was a basic process, known as image segmentation [1]. Applications, including object identification, medical picture analysis, and autonomous driving, are made possible by this mechanism, which was essential for comprehending and evaluating visual input [2, 3]. Image segmentation has undergone a revolution in the last 10 years because of developments in deep learning, which have produced models with great accuracy and resilience in a variety of scenarios [4]. Traditional machine learning algorithms and handmade features were the mainstays of early systems, but they frequently had trouble with complicated pictures and a wide range of object sizes [3]. These restrictions have been overcome by the development of deep learning, which has produced models that can segment data with previously unheard of accuracy, and automatically extract characteristics from the data [5].

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) marked a significant breakthrough in image segmentation [6]. Pioneering work such as fully convolutional networks (FCNs) demonstrated the potential of CNNs for pixel-wise classification, enabling the segmentation of images into distinct classes [7, 8]. The introduction of architectures like U-Net further refined this approach, specifically tailoring it for biomedical image segmentation [9]. U-Net’s encoder-decoder structure, combined with skip connections, allowed for precise segmentation of complex anatomical structures by retaining spatial information through its layers [9]. These early models laid the foundation for more advanced techniques by addressing some of the fundamental challenges in segmentation, such as maintaining spatial resolution and handling variable object sizes [8, 10].

The DeepLab family of architectures represents a significant advancement in segmentation technology [10–12]. DeepLab introduced innovations such as atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP), which enhances the model’s ability to capture multiscale features and context [13, 14]. This capability was particularly important for segmenting objects at different scales and in varying contexts, which was a common challenge in complex environments [15]. DeepLab models have demonstrated superior performance on standard benchmarks, such as PASCAL VOC and COCO, by leveraging both local and global information effectively [10, 16, 17]. Despite their success, these models also face challenges related to computational complexity and the ability to preserve fine details in segmented images [18].

Transformer-based architectures have recently emerged as a powerful alternative to CNNs for image segmentation [19, 20]. Transformers, initially designed for natural language processing tasks, offer a new approach to handling visual data by capturing long-range dependencies and global context through self-attention mechanisms. This innovation addresses some of the limitations of CNNs, particularly in handling complex interactions and maintaining contextual information across large image areas. Recently, several models related to vision transformers (ViTs) and the swin transformer have demonstrated better segmentation results, especially with broad and complex datasets. However, they pose problems such as high computational complexity and the necessity to use a substantial volume of reference data.

Even with the advancements made possible by these sophisticated structures, there are still a number of obstacles in the way of picture segmentation. Model performance was nevertheless impacted by issues with computing efficiency, generalization across datasets, and preservation of fine features. A current field of study was the incorporation of novel methods like self-supervised learning and hybrid models that combine the advantages of transformers and CNNs. To overcome these obstacles, new techniques must be developed in order to improve the efficiency and accuracy of segmentation, as well as ongoing innovation and model improvement. This chapter offers a thorough analysis of the development of deep learning architectures for image segmentation, looking at the successes as well as current difficulties in this rapidly evolving subject.

## 2 Early developments in CNNs for image segmentation

### 2.1 Introduction to CNNs in computer vision

CNNs have significantly contributed to the development of computer vision by transforming the processing and analysis of visual input. CNNs are a distinct category of deep neural networks that are particularly engineered to manage the intricacies of image data and provide significant enhancements in the areas of feature extraction and pattern identification. Through a succession of convolutional layers, CNNs are able to automatically learn important characteristics directly from the input pictures, in contrast to typical machine learning methods that mostly depend on constructed features. This capacity to capture spatial hierarchies and relationships across different dimensions has made CNNs the backbone of several vision tasks, from object classification to picture segmentation.

The basic architecture of a CNN was built upon three core operations: convolution, pooling, and activation. A collection of filters was applied by convolutional layers. These then move over the input picture to create feature maps that emphasize significant patterns like edges, textures, and forms. Because the convolutional process maintains the spatial connections between pixels, CNNs are very useful for processing data that was arranged like a grid, like photographs. To lower the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, pooling procedures like max pooling and average pooling are used after each convolutional layer. By keeping important characteristics, this down-sampling aids in lowering the network’s computational complexity. CNNs are further enhanced in their ability to represent complicated visual patterns by the addition of nonlinear activation functions, such ReLU, which enable the network to capture complex, nonlinear correlations in the input.

One of the key advantages of CNNs was their ability to learn hierarchical representations of images. In the lower layers, CNNs typically capture low-level features such as edges and corners. As the network deepens, it begins to detect higher-level abstractions, including textures, shapes, and eventually entire objects.
