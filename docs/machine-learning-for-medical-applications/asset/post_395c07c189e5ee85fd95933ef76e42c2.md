## Size dependence

For the purpose of quantum confinement effects, the emission spectra of QDs are found to be size-dependent. When the QD size is smaller, then the energy levels are quantized and thus peak emission wavelength experiences a blue shift. On the other hand, large QDs show a red shift. This size-dependent emission can be utilized to fit the color of the emission anywhere between those two extremes for nearly any intended application.

## Core-shell structures

As illustrated in Figure 8, when a core material is encapsulated with a shell made of a different semiconductor, the resulting core–shell quantum dot (QD) exhibits a shift in its emission spectrum. This shift arises due to alterations in the electronic structure caused by the shell. The shell plays a critical role in enhancing desirable photoluminescence characteristics, such as increasing the quantum yield and improving the color purity of emitted light. By carefully selecting and engineering the core and shell materials, researchers can finely tune the emission wavelength, allowing for precise spectral control suited to specific imaging or sensing applications.

## Surface effects

The emission spectra are also affected by surface passivation in which the QD is covered by organic. Substrate surface can influence the electronic structure and minimize nonemissive recombination centers, hence increasing luminescent quantum yield and stability. Optimization of the surface passivation can also improve the quality of emission spectra as is evident.

### 5.1.1 Color tuning techniques

### Size control during synthesis

One of the methods of tuning emission color is through adjustment of QD size during synthesis. By carefully controlling the reaction conditions, for example, temperature of the reaction, concentration of precursors, and time of reaction, it is possible to obtain QDs with required size and in turn conjugate them with the specific emission wavelength. This method can be used independently for simple and direct means of obtaining color across the visible spectrum.

### Composition engineering

Local control can also be applied in QD material whereby modification of the composition of the material can change the emission spectra. For example, altering the proportions of some elements of core can move the emission wavelength. In this approach, the QDs are incorporated in differing compositions in order to attain a set of colloids and fine-tune their performance attributes.

### Post-synthesis modification

Surface passivation treatment is also used on the synthesized QD samples to alter the emission wavelength. For instance, incorporating specific ligands onto the surrounding surface can cause the emission wavelength to be tuned and improve color stability. Any of these changes can be applied to satisfy specific needs of certain uses of optical characteristics of QDs.

### 5.1.2 Performance metrics

### Quantum yield

Fluorescence efficiency: Fluorescence yield is an important performance parameter for quantifying the ratio of emitted photons to the number of photons absorbed by the fluorophore. High quantum yield means that the majority of the absorbed energy is turned to emitted light. High quantum yield QDs give out brighter and more intense emission, which is crucial in cases where sensitivity and resolution are required.

### Photostability

Photostability is measured in terms of the capability of QDs to exhibit the same performance in terms of emission intensity and lifetimes of the excited state for a large number of excitation cycles of the specific light. The high photostability of QDs means that they have a very low level of photobleaching and therefore make reliable and constant optimized imaging over long periods. This property is significant in applications where constant instances of light exposure are necessary.

### Full width at half maximum (FWHM)

FWHM in the emission spectra measures the width of the peak of emission at half of the maximum intensity. A low FWHM represents, therefore, a high color purity and very steep emission lines, which are beneficial in all applications where very high color selectivity and minimal crosstalk between adjacent wavebands are needed. Narrow FWHM of QDs is preferable for multiplexed imaging and high-resolution displays.

### Lifetime and decay characteristics

Another relevant characteristic is the fluorescence lifetime of QDs defined as average time, when a QD stays in the excited state, emitting a photon. This implies that longer fluorescence lifetimes improve the imaging contrast and allow for time-resolved measurements. Fluorescence decay behavior, which refers to the rate and shape of QD fluorescence, is a key feature in the photophysics of these semiconductor nanocrystals and a pointer to their possible uses.

## 5.2 Photostability and brightness

Photostability and brightness are two of the most important optical attributes of QDs in determining their utility in different applications such as bioimaging and bio-diagnostics. This section focuses on the importance of photostability and brightness of the QDs and their relation with its performance.

### 5.2.1 Photostability

### Definition and importance

Photostability describes the ability of QDs to maintain similar fluorescence emission over time under continuous excitation. Long observation periods are important for imaging scenarios, especially ones that require prolonged photostability of the fluorescent molecules. This property also proves superior to traditionally applied organic fluorophores, which typically undergo photobleaching that limits the imaging time and the possibility of multiple measurements.

### Factors influencing photostability

The photostability of QDs was therefore affected by parameters such as size of the QD, the composition of the QD, and the type of surface coating for the QD. Surface passivation is also a reason why QDs with a core-shell structure have higher photostability compared to other QDs. Properties such as surface coatings and functionalization are capable of controlling the rate of photobleaching of QDs by minimizing active contact with the surrounding medium.

### Comparative advantages

In general, photostability of QDs is higher in comparison with conventional fluorescent dyes. This advantage is especially valuable in live-cell imaging when continuous imaging sessions are needed. Due to the long-time stability of QDs, the data collected is more reliable and fewer samples are required to be replaced, so the efficiency of the experiments was improved.

### 5.2.2 Brightness

### Definition and importance

Brightness is one of the attributes associated with the fluorescence emission intensity of QDs and is dependent on the quantum yield and excitation efficiency. Since sensitivity and resolution are critical for most practical applications, high brightness is required to guarantee that the emitted signal is strong enough to be distinguished from background noise.

### Quantum yield and its role

Brightness of QDs is attributable to another parameter recognized as the quantum yield. Fluorescence intensity represents the ability to transform the number of photons absorbed to fluorophore emission. High quantum yield QDs are desirable for applications such as multiplexed imaging assays where the signal intensity is a factor.

### Size and composition effects

The standards of emitted light can also be controlled taking into account the size and composition of the QDs. Lower quantum yield values occur when the size of the QDs is increased because larger QDs show additional nonradiative recombination processes. Moreover, the semiconductor materials used and the core-shell structures have an effect on the overall brightness of the QDs. In order to obtain the necessary brightness for applications, exact synthesis parameters were crucial for the compounds.

### Comparative advantages

In most cases, QDs can be brighter than old fashioned fluorescent materials. The analytes are more sensitive and have better signal-to-noise ratios attributed to their capability to fluoresce at certain wavelengths. This increased brightness again shows that QDs offer considerable advantages in translational settings, including early detection of diseases and precise cell diagnostics.
