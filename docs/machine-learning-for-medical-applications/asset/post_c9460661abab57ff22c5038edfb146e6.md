It is possible to select QDs for practical use by determining excitation and emission wavelengths referring to the imaging tool and application necessity. The type of excitation source, for instance, lasers, LEDs, and the choice of the detection system such as filters and detectors should be appropriate for the QDs. Information on the photostability and quantum yield of QDs when used at different wavelengths such as excitation and emission can be considered in the functionality and efficiency of the imaging tasks.

## 5.5 Influence of size and surface modification on optical properties

QDs have tunable photoluminescence properties that depend greatly on the size and the nature of surface coating. Knowledge about these factors is of great importance for tuning QDs more appropriately to a particular task, especially in areas like biomedical imaging and diagnostics, and sensing.

## 5.5.1 Size-dependent optical properties

### Emission wavelengths:

The emission wavelength of quantum dots (QDs) is primarily governed by their size due to the quantum confinement effect shown in Figure 10. When the size of QDs decreases, the energy gap between the valence band and conduction band increases, resulting in emission shifting toward the shorter wavelengths (i.e., blue end) of the visible spectrum. Conversely, larger QDs exhibit a smaller bandgap, causing their emission to shift toward longer (red) wavelengths. This intrinsic, size-dependent tunability in emission makes QDs exceptionally valuable in multiplexed imaging systems, allowing researchers to design and utilize QDs that fluoresce in distinct colors from a single material platform.

### Quantum yield:

Fluorescence quantum yield defined as the ratio between the emitted photons and absorbed photons is QD size-dependent. There is evidence that smaller QDs have higher quantum yields, as it is less likely for them to undergo a nonradiative decay. The quantum yield is reduced sharply in very small QDs for increased surface effects and nonradiative recombination. Control of size to achieve high quantum yield together with other favorable characteristics is crucial to its application in imaging and sensing.

### Photostability:

This also has implications on the photostability of QDs, or, in other words, their ability to be photobleached. In general, larger QDs are more photostable than QDs of small size. This increased stability is believed to be linked to a relatively lower surface area-to-volume ratio, thereby negating the effects of surface anomalies such as oxidation on the materials. But, the size effect on photostability has to be correlated with other optical properties for service lifetime of the samples in practice.

## 5.5.2 Surface modification effects

### Surface coating and ligands:

The optical characteristics of QDs are mainly affected by surface coatings and ligands. Fluorescence intensity and emission spectra of the QDs can be impacted based on the type of surface coating used. The use of a coating material can influence one of these characteristics: fluorescence intensity; emission spectrum; photostability. Such organic ligands would improve solubility and cut back on nonradiative losses; silica in inorganic coatings improves the stability of QDs and prevents degradation. This indicates that varieties of coating types and thickness can impact the light incidence and absorption on the optical properties of QDs.

### Surface passivation:

It has been discovered that surface passivation is an important step in minimizing surface defects and improving the optical quality of QDs. By creating a suitable ligand, the surface nonradiative recombination of charge carriers can be suppressed, thus increasing the quantum yield and photostability. Passivation also plays a critical role in the determination of emission properties because it shields the core of the QD from undesirable environment factors that change its optical characteristics.

### Functionalization effects:

The conjugation of biomolecular groups onto the surface of QDs affects their emission properties due to new interactions and/or changes in the surrounding milieu. Besides the quantum confinement and size of the QD, the functional groups have consequences on the fluorescence intensity, emission spectrum, and stability of the material. For instance, the biomolecular interactions with targeting ligands affect the fluorescence of the QD depending on the alterations of the local environment with other molecules.

### Size and surface interactions:

Size and surface modification of materials can complicate the impact on optical properties. For example, ultra-small QDs with much surface functionalization show fluorescence properties that are quite different from those of large QDs with little surface functionalization. In particular, the structure provides information about the relationship between the dimensions of the nanoparticles and their surface roughness, thus suggesting the optimal conditions for size and surface modification to provide the necessary optical properties for specific applications, for example, in high-resolution imaging detectors.

## 5.5.3 Performance metrics for optimization

### Fluorescence intensity and resolution:

QD fluorescence intensity is the performance parameter that depends on size and surface modification, whereas concern for resolution and detection and higher intensity are preferred by most of the respondents. To increase the fluorescence resolution and to reduce the background signals, it is very important to control the size and surface chemistry of the microstructures.

### Spectral range and separation:

The greatest concern is the broad spectral range and high spectral separation that are important for multiplexed imaging. The emission range depends on the size of QDs and the surface modifications must be optimized to give suitable spectral characteristics of the fluorescent markers and the lack of overlap with other markers.

### Long-term stability and reproducibility:

Optical and electronic stability, size distribution, and dispersion are crucial factors influencing the stability and their performance in several applications. Large surfaces and changes to the surface that increase photostability also decrease batch-to-batch variability to yield consistent results.

## 6 Challenges in quantum dot development

## 6.1 Toxicity and environmental concerns

A major issue associated with the uses and advances in QDs consists of toxicity linked to the QDs. This is, of course, an important issue to consider since QDs have already been incorporated in various applications including medical imaging and diagnosis. Appreciating these challenges as well as efforts to overcome them are critical so that risks of problems related to application of QDs are eliminated.

## 6.1.1 Toxicity of quantum dots

### Chemical composition and potential risks:

QDs typically made from such materials as CdSe, PbS are hazardous if spread in the surroundings in a biological system. Cadmium and lead could be the most worrisome because they are toxic to human health and carry health risks in the long run. The emission of such materials from QDs by degradation is a big threat to human health and the environment.

### Cellular and systemic toxicity:

QDs used in biological applications can be toxic in size, coating, and concentration, and in other biological applications. Cellular damage results from the internalization of QDs by cells and it induces an oxidative stress, the formation of reactive oxygen species and disruption of cell functions. Another issue for the in vivo application of QDs is the systemic toxicity; QDs are thought to accumulate in organs and thus produce negative impacts. Mitigating and quantifying these toxic impacts are important for the future use of QDs in biomedical applications.

### Potential for accumulation and bioaccumulation:

The ability of QDs to accumulate and bioaccumulate in living beings are also other issues of interest. QDs are found to be stable in biological systems and this results in longer circulation and slow, continuous release of hazardous substances. Accumulation in the organs would lead to continued effects and health hazard after a long time. To overcome these risks, it is crucial to make certain that QDs are biocompatible and biodegradable in design.

## 6.1.2 Environmental impact

### Environmental release and persistence:

The discharge of QDs into the environment through industrial applications, misuse of QDs, and their degradation can cause environmental pollution. There is a concern of whether the QDs will not degrade in the environment in which they are placed, especially if they are not biodegradable. Here, it is stipulated that persistent discharge of QDs can build up on soils and in water and affect wildlife and the balance of ecosystems.
