### 2.3.1 Stereolithography of three-dimensional objects with the aid of light (1986)

Chuck Hull of the University of Utah invented the process known as stereolithography (SLA), which started the present-day 3D printing. SLA was originally conceived to use UV light to cure and solidify one layer of liquid resin at a time in order to create 3D objects. Finally, this discovery paved the way for further development of 3D printing technology and introduced a clear understanding of how the additive manufacturing process can be applied to the formation of complex and highly detailed structures.

### 2.3.2 Patent of the fused deposition modeling (1988)

A significant development in the advancement of technology was realized with Scott Crump’s creation of fused deposition modeling. To create its items, FDM utilizes thermoplastic material that is then laid down in a specific pattern by an integral heated carriage. This technology became popular in both industrial and consumer fields owing to the fact that it brought down the cost of production and development.

### 2.3.3 Selective laser sintering development (1989)

Another type of 3DP depends on SLS, invented by Carl Deckard, which fuses powder materials with a laser in a selective manner. SLS made it possible to create parts that are far more durable and useful than the previous parts that could be made using additive manufacturing technology. This development cleared the way for uses referring to accuracy, mechanical strength, and some other aspects.

### 2.3.4 Emerging technique: direct metal laser sintering (1995)

With DMLS, significant progress has been made in the process of printing metal parts with high-strength characteristics. DMLS involves using a laser beam to sinter metal powders, and the final geometry can be complex since it retains the excellent mechanical properties of the base metal. This material application has drastically transformed industries that rely on metal parts such as aerospace, car manufacturing, and implants.

### 2.3.5 Bioprinters outlined: challenges of bioprinting (2003)

Since bioprinting technologies involve the creation of biological tissues and structures, 3D printing has become one of the significant breakthroughs in the field. The first steps in bioprinting addressed this approach by creating scaffold structures infused with cells for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine purposes. As it enables the development of structures that are both biological and functional concurrently with tissues, this development revolutionizes the medical implant industry.

### 2.3.6 Multimaterial and color printing, multimaterial printing, advanced color printing (2009)

The versatility and functionality of AM technology were improved by integrating additional features into the color and multimaterial printing technologies of 3D printers. It has become possible to design and create components that are both multicolor and multifunctional due to the availability of new technologies for the combination of different colors and materials. From this development, the range of applications was increased to such areas as individual prosthetic devices and medical implants.

### 2.3.7 High-resolution 3D printing, which was released to the public in 2014

The precision and detail that are achievable with additive manufacturing have been enhanced by the new high-resolution 3D printing technologies. The fidelity of implants and prosthetics has increased because of the formation of intricate and realistic structures due to the enhancement of the printing accuracies. Achieving this mark has been crucial to meeting the high requirements for the applications of the technology in medicine.

These milestones are major steps forward in 3D printing technology: all of them contribute to the improvement of additive manufacturing and expand its applications. From the early inventiveness to convert abstract designs into tangible models, 3D printing has caused a revolutionary impact on numerous fields, especially in the field of biological customization, medicine, and implants.

## 2.4 Comparative analysis of early and modern 3D printing techniques

It was essential to understand that even the early techniques, which are in use today, have evolved since the inception of 3D printing technologies, modern practices offer finer ability and more applications. By comparing the first-generation techniques of 3D printing with the current generation techniques, it was evident that the technology has drastically advanced, especially in terms of accuracy, material selection, and functionality.

### 2.4.1 Early techniques: The present and future role of 3D printing

The earliest methods of 3D printing originated during the 1980s and the main goal of the first techniques was to develop the foundation of FDM. The first of these was the SLA, which Charles Hull introduced in 1986. SLA employs a laser to solidify photopolymer resin one layer at a time, allowing it to produce detailed models based on the digital model provided. It was seen that the first generation of SLA systems was often hampered by a small selection of materials that could be used and comparatively slow fabrication rates that did not lend themselves to many medical applications.

Scott Crump invented another method known as FDM in 1988. FDM behaves in a way that builds structures in layers by applying thermoplastic rods through a heated extruder. Whereas the resolution and mechanical strength of FDM technology were comparatively poor for more intricate uses until recently, it was cheaper and easier to use than SLA.

### 2.4.2 Modern techniques: advancements and innovations

Such was the state of affairs in the sphere of 3D printing technology, as present-day examples indicate significant advancements that eliminate the deficiencies of previous approaches. Thus, the options of materials and the functionality where 3D printing was applicable have expanded due to modern approaches like DMLS and SLS.

Having been invented in the 1990s, SLS, which stands for SLS, binds powder materials such as metals and polymers together to form solid objects using a laser. Improved mechanical properties, the ability to produce complex shapes, the ability to create functional, useful prototypes, and finished goods are benefits of this method. This made it possible to use SLS for a number of applications, including in the production of medical implants, as the technology improves on some of the resolution and material shortcomings typical of previous technologies.

SLS had a variation known as DMLS, which focused on metal particles and was specifically useful where demanding mechanical parts, such as dental and orthopedic implants, were required. Thanks to precise control of geometry and material parameters, DMLS provides dependable applications to serve load-bearing medical functions.

### 2.4.3 Comparative insights

The evolution of early technologies to modern 3D printing has some defined differences, as seen below. The first techniques included SLA and FDM as foundational but had some drawbacks in the choice of materials, layers’ thickness, and components’ manufacturing rate. On the other hand, techniques like SLS and DMLS are free from these limitations and have better properties, better resolutions, and are capable of producing functional parts.

## 2.5 Pioneering studies and breakthroughs in medical 3D printing

Specifically, the development of medical 3D printing has been influenced greatly by groundbreaking studies, which set the basis for modern uses in the literature. Several early case studies have been proof-of-concept studies that have established the possibility and versatility of additive manufacturing in the medical field.

Dr. Carl Deckard remains one of the most celebrated pioneers of medical 3D printing from the 1980s, when he invented the SLS technique. This breakthrough enabled people to form concrete structures from powdered materials that are both complex and robust compared to traditional processes. In 3D printing, Deckard created a foundation that would later be useful in the development of complex medical devices and implants produced through the use of this technology.

The second major new day was marked in the early nineties with the introduction of SLA technology by Dr. Chuck Hull. SLA, which was invented by Hull, enabled the right alignment of intersecting photopolymer resins to create complex 3D objects. In the medical field, this technique is quite helpful for creating accurate anatomical models and surgical tools.

At the beginning of the new millennium, researcher Dr. Anthony Atala and his team at the Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine hit the headlines. By integrating and optimizing 3D printing technology to generate vascularized tissue constructs that biomimicked natural tissues, they achieved an important developmental landmark. This study demonstrated that bioprinting holds great promise for generating tissue and organ models for research and therapy.

Other advancements made in the 2010s include the application and use of 3D printing in crafting customized medical implants. One of the most groundbreaking projects led by Dr. Vladimir P. Zharov in 2014 involved the development of 3D-printed cranial implants for reconstructive surgery of complex skull defects in patients. While advocating for the adoption of 3D printing in surgeries because of its effectiveness in creating custom implants that fit the patient's anatomy, this study showed how surgery positively impacted patient recovery rates.
