# 1.4 Cost and commercialization barriers

The development and commercialization of QDs face significant challenges related to cost and scalability [18]. The production of high-quality QDs often involves expensive raw materials and complex manufacturing processes, which contribute to high production costs [19], [20]. Scaling up from laboratory-scale synthesis to industrial-scale manufacturing presents difficulties, such as maintaining consistent quality and managing increased expenses [21]. Economic viability is another concern, as QDs must demonstrate clear advantages over existing technologies to justify their higher costs. Addressing these cost and commercialization barriers is essential for making QDs a practical and widely adopted technology in medical imaging and other fields [22].

# 1.5 Future directions and research opportunities

Nonetheless, due to the ever-present investigation on the advanced strategies aims toward achieving better results and cheaper costs of using QDs [23]. Future modifications in the nature of synthesis approaches, for instance, getting synthesis routes that are cheaper and reproducible in larger scales, advance the improvements in eco-nomic efficiency of techniques that apply QDs [24]. Functionalization in addition to surface coating was expected to enhance the biocompatibility and targeting of QDs as well as expanding their use in diagnosis and treatment [25]. Researchers will become interested in achieving these current shortfalls and explore other intended prospective applications making QDs an ever-relevant tool advancing in medical imaging as well.

QDs display tunable photophysical characteristics that depend on size, such as emission wavelength and quantum yield [15]. The ability of the size of the QDs to alter the electronic properties due to the quantum confinement effect is a key factor that determines the optical characteristics. It should be noted that smaller QDs emit shorter wavelengths of light while larger QDs emit longer wavelengths of light [16].
