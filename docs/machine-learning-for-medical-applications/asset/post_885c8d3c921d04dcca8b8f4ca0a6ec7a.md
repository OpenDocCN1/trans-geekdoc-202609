The random forests comprise one more ensemble method that accumulates the decision trees to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of the anomaly detection process. A random forest generally builds many decision trees on a random sample of the data sets and combines the individual forecasts through voting. Random forests are powerful for processing big data with lots of features and low risk of overfitting. They are very effective for use in anomaly detection scenarios when data has many features that cannot be easily modeled by a single construct. Random forests can be used to detect anomalies since the various trees capture different decision boundaries across the trees to boost the detection capability.

Stacking generalization was another online ensemble technique that was applied when different models have to be combined at several levels. The idea of this method was to produce several base models, which learn from the databases, and their predictions are combined to make the final prediction of a metamodel. This can be literally any ML algorithm; it could be a logistic regression and its task is to provide the best answer based on information provided by base models. Stacking is a very effective approach when used in the problem of anomaly detection as different models are trained on the different features of the data and yield their results through the stacking system in order to make the final prediction. In addition, it works best in applications in which we have to detect various types of anomalies that differ from each other and covers a broad spectrum of characteristics.

Another commonly used ensemble method for anomaly detection is called XGBoost; this approach is selected for its efficiency and ability to generate high predictive accuracy for large datasets. XGBoost, like boosting, constructs a sequence of weak learners, but it does this while including a measure of shrinkage, which is used to avoid overfitting and increase the model’s ability to generalize. Specifically, XGBoost’s tree-based method is more suitable for high-dimensional and sparse data to detect complex patterns of anomalous features while at the same time efficient in terms of computation. The great freedom that this approach offers is to adapt XGBoost algorithms in various settings of anomaly detection that can include missing observations.

Use of diversified ensemble methods for anomaly detection is advantageous over conventional single model methods in the following ways: first, increased detection rate since multiple views on source data are less likely to miss significant cases; second, improved resilience in that the influence of noise that led to the deterioration of single models can be reduced. For instance, one particular model that was erroneous in identifying the anomaly is corrected by other models in the group. Third, the same anomaly type can be detected by different models of different types, for example by tree-based models and NNs, which can substantially enhance their performance.

### 3.1 Random forests and gradient boosting for detecting visual anomalies

This becomes a problem because inconsistencies in visual data are difficult to uncover due to the inherent complexity and because images constitute a high-dimensional data set. Nevertheless, random forest and gradient boosting have posted significant results in handling this challenge. Such methods, which combine the result of multiple models, are good approaches to building reliable frameworks for the identification of visual anomalies in generic applications such as medical imaging, industrial defects inspection, and surveillance systems. As such, factors that normally do not appear important due to their low frequencies in the data are captured by these ensemble methods as the strengths of individual classifiers are added together, making it easier to track the often-subtle abnormalities that are missed by a single model.

Because Figure 5 uses photographs, which are high-dimensional data, another technique called random forests, which is similarly based on decision trees, is employed for visual anomaly identification. Random forest applies a number of decision trees trained on different samples of data and each tree in the forest gives its own prediction. Besides, the last prediction is derived from the outputs of each of the trees produced and involves simple voting. This approach minimizes overfitting and the model's overall reliability is higher when working with imagery with visually strange artifacts that are highly sporadic. Random forests are also learning algorithms that perform well when there is either noise or missing values, which are prevalent in real-life visual anomaly detection. The possibility to determine the locality of decisions within the single tree and across many trees makes random forests highly suitable for analyzing anomalies in visual data.

Random forests can be applied to numerous tasks in the context of visual anomaly detection including, but not limited to, the identification of defects in production lines, differentiating between abnormal tissue patterns in medical imaging, and identifying invasive and unusual events in surveillance videos. For instance, in the case of detecting manufacturing defects, random forests are applied for clustering images of produce that have pixel features about texture and color densities. From several decision trees built on different image segments, random forests can recognize features, which are barely discernible – cracks and scratches, for example. Again, random forests consume less computation time and therefore can work well with big image datasets.

Gradient boosting algorithms like XGBoost and LightGBM present another strong method to identify visually abnormal data. Unlike random forests that are a set of trees that are grown independently from the data, gradient boosting algorithms grow trees sequentially where the next tree grown aims at minimizing the error of the previously grown tree. This cyclic procedure enables the model of gradient boosting to concentrate on the intricate and the most hard-to-solve cases, making it suitable for the cases where existing anomalies are not only scanty but also intricate. The last decision in GB is a weighted sum of the outputs of all the trees, which in turn aids in the final adjustment of boundary and thereby improves the detection rate.

In general, gradient boosting methods are useful for models based on the VAD approach when the anomalies are nonlinear within pixel characteristics. For instance, in medical imaging, gradient boosting models have been used to identify such irregularities as tumor or lesion, which are barely visible in classical image processing. Therefore, multiple weak learners in gradient boosting models can model complex relationships in image data to yield an almost perfect decision on anomaly classification. These models also show good properties at handling a vast number of data points, so it is possible to use them in tasks involving high-resolution images.

Another benefit associated with the use of gradient boosting in the context of visual anomaly detection is that it self-adjusts to the data intricacy. One of the major advantages of gradient boosting over most other ML algorithms is that it can self-fine-tune the learning process across the entire training data to provide more importance to hard-to-learn samples. This dynamic control leads to better levels of detection, particularly when the given anomalies are not frequent in the training datasets. Furthermore, gradient boosting methods, including XGBoost, have integrated methods of handling feature space complexity and preventing overfitting, more important when working with a limited number of samples, which is often the case in visual anomaly detection.

Comparing random forests and gradient boosting algorithms, it is quite clear that both should be utilized for visual anomaly detection because each of them can be improved by the other one. Using the random forest and gradient boosting approaches, one can achieve the advantages of both methods; random forests deal with the noise and high-dimensional data effectively and gradient boosting provides iteration to pick the anomalies out more efficiently. An approach utilizing both such methods is likely to yield a more precise and efficient anomaly detection system, particularly when dealing with real-world visual information.
