A critical challenge in segmentation was maintaining high-resolution predictions throughout the network, especially after multiple downsampling operations (such as pooling and strided convolutions), which are typical in traditional CNN architectures. Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) addressed this issue through a process known as upsampling. By applying learned upsampling layers, FCNs could restore the spatial resolution of feature maps, enabling the network to produce high-resolution predictions. As shown in Figure 1, FCNs also utilized skip connections to fuse high-level semantic information from deeper layers with low-level spatial details from earlier layers. This integration helped refine segmentation boundaries and capture finer details key to tasks like edge detection and object delineation. The introduction of FCNs marked a significant advancement in the design of neural networks for segmentation, laying the foundation for more sophisticated encoder-decoder architectures such as
U-Net and SegNet. These architectures expanded on the FCN structure by enhancing the effectiveness and speed of segmentation tasks. Additionally, various FCN variants emerged, incorporating multiscale features to improve performance in complex scene segmentation. While newer models have since been developed, the FCN remains a foundational deep learning architecture and continues to serve as a benchmark for evaluating segmentation approaches.

## 2.3 SegNet

Existing deep learning architecture SegNet was another of the first designs introduced for semantic image segmentation to serve the purpose of pixel-wise classification in high-dimensional images. Designed with a flavor of computer efficiency and memory utilization, SegNet had proposed an encoder-decoder network with high-performance balance. Its design was driven from the problem of obtaining the high-level feature representation while keeping the spatial information needed for pixel-level segmentation. It was for this reason that the SegNet’s approach would have been ideal for use in applications such as compact and real-time image understanding in real-world scenes such as road scenes.

SegNet’s fundamental architecture was an encoder-decoder framework. The encoder mimics well-known CNN architectures, such as VGG-16, by employing pooling and convolutional layers to extract hierarchical features from the input image while gradually lowering its spatial resolution. In order for the model to identify intricate patterns in the image, the encoder’s job was to record abstract representations of the input at different scales. In contrast, the decoder upsamples the encoded features in an attempt to rebuild the high-resolution segmentation map.

A key feature of SegNet’s decoder was the use of max-pooling indices from the encoder stage during the upsampling process. Instead of learning new parameters for upsampling, SegNet reuses the indices from the max-pooling layers in the encoder to perform nonparametric upsampling. This strategy reduces the number of parameters in the decoder, making the network more computationally efficient while also helping to preserve the boundary information critical for segmentation tasks.

One of the distinguishing innovations of SegNet was the use of pooling indices for upsampling in the decoder. Traditional upsampling methods often rely on deconvolution (transposed convolution) layers, which require learning additional parameters, and increase the computational burden. In contrast, SegNet’s nonparametric approach uses the indices from the max-pooling layers to guide the upsampling process. By recording the location of the maximum activation during pooling, SegNet ensures that the upsampled feature maps retain important spatial information from the original input image, leading to more accurate segmentation, especially along object boundaries.

This approach not only improves the accuracy of the segmentation but also makes SegNet highly memory efficient. This was particularly important in real-time applications, such as autonomous driving imagery analysis, where memory and processing power are often limited.

In several applications and experimental studies, SegNet has shown promising results, especially in driving scenario understanding. By now, its capacity to separate multiple objects and complicated scenes is profitable for such applications as autonomous driving, for which precise segmentation of roads, automobiles, passengers, and others was critical. The architecture’s balance between accuracy and computational efficiency makes it suitable for tasks where real-time processing was required without sacrificing segmentation quality.

In benchmark tests such as the CamVid dataset for road scene segmentation, SegNet showed competitive performance against other early architectures like FCNs and DeepLab, particularly in terms of inference speed and memory usage. While more recent architectures have surpassed SegNet in terms of overall segmentation accuracy, SegNet remains influential due to its innovative use of pooling indices and its emphasis on computational efficiency.

Despite its contributions to efficient segmentation, SegNet has certain limitations. The reliance on pooling indices, while memory efficient, does not always capture fine-grained details as effectively as architectures that utilize learnable upsampling layers like transposed convolutions. This can result in less precise segmentation in areas with complex textures. Additionally, the absence of skip connections, which became standard in later architectures such as U-Net, means that SegNet struggles to fully preserve low-level spatial information throughout the network.

## 2.4 Advancements with conditional random fields (CRFs)

The integration of CRFs with CNNs has played a pivotal role in enhancing the precision of image segmentation, particularly in terms of refining boundaries and capturing detailed spatial relationships. CRFs, traditionally employed in computer vision for structured prediction tasks, are probabilistic graphical models that capture dependencies between adjacent pixels in an image. While CNNs excel at feature extraction and dense prediction, they often produce coarse segmentation outputs, especially around object boundaries. CRFs are introduced as post-processing layers to refine these predictions by enforcing spatial consistency and modeling the relationships between neighboring pixels, thereby leading to more precise segmentation results.

CRFs are typically used to refine CNN outputs by taking the initial coarse prediction and applying a probabilistic approach to adjust pixel-level labels based on both local and global context. One of the most prominent applications of this combination was in the DeepLab architecture, where CRFs were employed as a post-processing step to correct the boundaries that CNNs struggled to delineate accurately. By considering the likelihood
that neighboring pixels could share similar labels, CRFs help in producing more accurate and visually coherent segmentations. This refinement was crucial in real-world scenarios where pixel-level accuracy, particularly along object edges, was essential for tasks such as medical imaging, autonomous driving, and object recognition.

CRFs achieve this refinement by incorporating both appearance information (such as pixel intensity) and spatial relationships. They work by minimizing an energy function that penalizes discrepancies between pixel labels, based on the similarity of their visual features and their proximity in the image. The energy function assigns lower costs to configurations where neighboring pixels with similar intensities have the same label, thereby encouraging smooth transitions between segments while preserving sharp boundaries where significant changes in intensity occur. This ability to model long-range dependencies and enforce smoothness constraints makes CRFs an effective tool for correcting the limitations of CNNs, which typically rely on fixed-size receptive fields and overlook fine-grained spatial details.

Despite their effectiveness in improving segmentation quality, the combination of CRFs and CNNs presents certain computational challenges. In early implementations, CRFs were applied as a separate post-processing step, leading to increased inference times and additional complexity in the overall pipeline. Advances in differentiable CRFs have allowed for the seamless integration of CRFs directly into the neural network architecture. This innovation, exemplified by works such as DenseCRF and CRF-RNN, enables end-to-end training where the CRF parameters can be learned alongside the CNN weights, resulting in more efficient and powerful segmentation models.

CRFs are not without limitations. While highly effective in improving boundary localization, they struggle with complex scenes where global context was necessary to resolve ambiguities, such as differentiating between similar-looking objects in close proximity. CRFs rely heavily on the choice of hyperparameters and require fine-tuning to perform optimally across different datasets and tasks. Research has continued to explore more sophisticated alternatives to CRFs that combine the strengths of CRFs with the representational power of deep learning, such as graph-based methods and attention mechanisms, which offer more flexible ways to model context and spatial relationships in images.

## 2.5 Segmentation with patch-based CNNs

Patch-based CNNs represent an early approach in the evolution of convolutional neural networks for image segmentation. Unlike Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs), which process entire images in a single forward pass, patch-based CNNs divide the input image into smaller, manageable patches. Each patch is then processed independently by a CNN to generate segmentation predictions.
