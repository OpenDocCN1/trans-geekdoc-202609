## Machine learning approaches for optimizing the synthesis and functionalization of quantum dots for medical imaging

**Abstract:** Recent advances in biomedical engineering show that quantum dots (QDs) are powerful imaging agents that exhibit desirable characteristics like size-tunable emission and photostability. In this chapter we review the synthesis of QDs and their surface functionalization, with an emphasis on their employment in state-of-the-art imaging systems. Of course, there are discussion of the effects of QD size and the surface structure on optical characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of various synthesis methodologies, and the difficulties that QD production poses for cost reduction and manufacturability. In addition to providing an overview of the synthesis of QDs and functionalization of QDs for biomedical applications, the chapter also discusses recent developments in the area of surface coatings in QD that improve their biocompatibility and specificity in medical applications. This chapter seeks to map the existing and future agenda for QD technology by analyzing the possible developments and limitations as of today and suggest future research questions that enable the practical implementation of this technology. Major topics covered include synthesis of QDs and their surface chemistry, optical characteristics, and functionalization of the particles; and the issues of cost and practical bottlenecks to the commercial exploitation of the material.

**Keywords:** quantum dots, medical imaging, optical properties, surface modification, synthesis methods, commercialization challenges

## 1.4 Cost and commercialization barriers

The development and commercialization of QDs face significant challenges related to cost and scalability [18]. The production of high-quality QDs often involves expensive raw materials and complex manufacturing processes, which contribute to high production costs [19], [20]. Scaling up from laboratory-scale synthesis to industrial-scale manufacturing presents difficulties, such as maintaining consistent quality and managing increased expenses [21]. Economic viability is another concern, as QDs must demonstrate clear advantages over existing technologies to justify their higher costs. Addressing these cost and commercialization barriers is essential for making QDs a practical and widely adopted technology in medical imaging and other fields [22].

## 2 Definition and characteristics of quantum dots

### 2.1 Definition and basic concept

QDs display tunable photophysical characteristics that depend on size, such as emission wavelength and quantum yield [15]. The ability of the size of the QDs to alter the electronic properties due to the quantum confinement effect is a key factor that determines the optical characteristics. It should be noted that smaller QDs emit shorter wavelengths of light while larger QDs emit longer wavelengths of light [16]. The surface chemistry of QDs alters the quantum yield and photostability drastically. The quantum yield is important in achieving brightness and high contrast in the images while photostability is important to keep the QDs active for the intended image capture time. Knowing these properties and tailoring them by design and subsequent synthesis is important for medical imaging using QDs [17].

The major advantage of the quantum confinement effect lies in the fact that the dimensions of quantum dot (QD) devices are comparable to the de Broglie wavelength of electrons and holes. This confinement alters the energy states available to the charge carriers, making the performance of QDs significantly different from that of their bulk counterparts. As shown in Figure 1, this unique feature enables precise tuning of the emission wavelengths of QDs simply by modifying their size: smaller QDs emit light at shorter wavelengths (e.g., blue), while larger QDs emit at longer wavelengths (e.g., red).

### 2.4 Electronic properties and quantum confinement

QDs are semiconductor nanocrystals having size-quantized electronic nature due to quantum confinement. Quantum confinement is described as a condition where the electrical and/or optical characteristics of a material change due to miniaturization of the size to the nanoscale. This effect is a direct result of the confinement of electron and hole movement within the confinements of a volume called a QD.

For QD, its electronic properties are influenced by its size, shape, and the type of material used in its construction. As the size of the QD changes, the electronic states of the electrons and holes become quantized, leading to distinct energy levels that differ significantly from the continuous energy bands found in bulk materials. This quantization results in size-dependent optical transitions, meaning the emission wavelength of QD can be tuned simply by altering its size. Smaller QDs exhibit stronger quantum confinement, leading to higher energy transitions and emission that shifts toward the blue end of the spectrum. Conversely, larger QDs have lower energy transitions, with emission shifting toward the red end of the spectrum.

The nature of charge carrier confinement in QDs means that there are quantized energy levels within the material, quantum states. These quantum states are similar to the electronic energy levels of an atom hence the name QDs. The space between the quantized states depends on the size of the QD, where the size of the dot determines the energy difference between the two states. Devices based on quantum confinement exhibit the property where smaller QDs have a larger bandgap than the larger ones. This size-dependent band-gap facilitates the appropriate control over the emission wavelength of associated light and hence the QDs owe their high flexibility of use in a wide field including medical imaging.

The electronic properties of QDs depend significantly on the surface chemistry and the surrounding medium. By altering the surface charge and passivating sensitive surface sites, surface ligands and coatings can be used to modify the electronic structure of QDs. These surface modifications can influence key characteristics such as photostability and fluorescence efficiency. Quantum confinement makes the electrical behavior of QDs distinctly different from that of traditional electronic materials, enabling their use in a range of optical and electronic applications. These unique properties are particularly valuable in medical imaging, where QDs offer high-resolution images with tunable emission wavelengths suitable for the identification and visualization of biological structures. To maximize the performance of QDs in ultrahigh-resolution imaging systems, it was essential to understand and control their electronic properties through precise size control and surface engineering.
