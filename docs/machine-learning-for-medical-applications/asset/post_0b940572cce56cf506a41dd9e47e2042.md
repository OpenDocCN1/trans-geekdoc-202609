### 6.3 Cost and accessibility of 3D printing technologies

Cost and availability of 3D printing technologies were a major issue in the medical profession. Even though 3D printing was revolutionizing the possibility of providing tailored medical implants, prosthetics, and educational models, some factors affect the use of these technologies.

- High initial investment: 3D printing equipment, particularly sophisticated systems that can deliver intricate health care items, comes at an initially high cost. Therefore, such high expenses include not only the price of the printer itself but also other related technologies like imaging systems, slicing software, as well as value-adding facilities for quality control. This financial constraint can prevent healthcare developers and practitioners from acquiring 3D printing technology, especially minor players serving underprivileged regions.

- Material costs: Some materials used in 3D printing, such as metals, biocompatible resins, and specialty polymers, are expensive. These materials are often more costly than conventional inputs used in manufacturing processes, hence increasing the overall cost of developing implants and other medical equipment through 3D printing. The high cost of the material defines the capability of the 3D printing solutions and limits their application in the spheres of medicine.

- Operational and maintenance expenses: Apart from capital costs, the running and maintenance costs of 3D printing technologies can be expensive. Maintenance, calibration, and repairing of 3D printers are frequently necessary and demand some expertise and tools. Costs include material costs as well as costs related to software updates that keep the operation running in the context of 3D printing. All these operational costs can extend and limit the use of 3D printing in medical facilities.

- Limited availability of expertise: Appropriate application of 3D printing in medicine involves an adequate understanding of areas relating to CAD/CAM design, operation of the printer, and postprocessing methods. Qualified personnel were scarce at the time, and the quest for qualified staff necessary for the implementation and enhancement of 3D printing solutions became a hurdle. These discrepancies can limit the optimized use of 3D printing technology across value chains and its outcomes in medical fields.

- Primary medical imaging and diagnosis equipment. The data integration was governed by direct feed, where the data from imaging procedures like CT scans should be transferred effortlessly to the 3D printing software. Problems with data translation and transfer, as well as data processing, negatively impact the outcomes of the 3D printing applications and prevent the use of 3D prints in practice.

- Integration into surgical planning workflows: The introduction of surgical-planning tools in the form of 3D-printed models requires changes to the existing sequential planning processes. Skills in using 3D models are also changing as surgical and medical teams are forced to incorporate models as traditional means of operating are blended with technology. Such integration involves procedural changes to the flow, extra time for training, and adaptations to planning approaches that capture the value of 3D printing.

- Standardization and quality control: There was no standard regulation that could be used for 3D printing for medical purposes; these circumstances can result in having machines that yield varying quality of prints. Defining and maintaining stable requirements for the fidelity of models, physical characteristics of the material, and methods of fabrication was crucial to the dependable and replicable use of 3D printing for medical devices. Nonetheless, if there was no clear direction as to how this particular technique must be incorporated into practice, then the effectiveness of 3D printing would be disparate and unsafe.

- Regulatory and compliance issues: In the case of integrating 3D printing technology in medical practice, compliance with legal and regulatory policies is required. To meet the requirements of safety and efficiency, products such as medical tools and implants created by 3D printing should be highly regulated. The general acceptance of items that are 3D printed is hampered by the fact that accessing the regulatory environment and obtaining the relevant permits for the same is daunting and lengthy.

- Cost and resource management: The deployment of 3D printing technology in medical practice requires capital investment, which ranges from equipment purchase and materials to the acquisition of special software. The application of 3D printing leads to additional investment spending in training costs, infrastructure, and/or procedural adaptations. One of the factors that healthcare institutions need to consider when it comes to 3D printing is the costs and resources required to create the technologies against the advantages bestowed on the institutions once they adopt the technology.

- Interdisciplinary collaboration: It was thus clear that any attempts at a successful integration of 3D printing technology must involve multidisciplinary approaches from the medical field, engineering, to the IT field. The stakeholders mentioned above included manufacturers of 3D printers, software designers, medical practitioners and engineers, and patients who would benefit from 3D-printed biological replicas. To support the effective use of 3D printing in medical applications, it was vital to ensure efficient communication among the different stakeholders. Organizing different functions and teams and encouraging collaboration are not always easy but should be done in order to remove integration issues.

Overcoming these challenges and limitations was crucial for applying innovative 3D printing technology to the existing medical practice and infrastructures. With these challenges in mind, there was a wealth of opportunities through the optimization of 3D print applications among the healthcare workforce in the treatment and management of conditions, which should, in turn, improve patient outcomes as well as the medical field.

## 7 Conclusion

The use of 3D printing technology in healthcare signifies a shift with various consequences related to patient outcomes, surgery, and training. This chapter has highlighted the overall progress, use, and current issues regarding 3D printing, with a focus on its overall impact on the delivery of healthcare.

The advancement in the technologies of 3D printing from the time it was discovered up to now has made it possible to develop anatomical models that have numerous details that one patient has; these detailed models boost surgical planning as well as the results of surgery. This chapter aims to highlight how the application of 3D printing in the operating theater enables proper preoperative planning by providing enhanced accuracy in the visualization of a patient's anatomy and developing accurate surgical strategies to be used in the operating theater. This institutionalization of personalized surgery has resulted in decreased operative times, less risk, and faster patient healing, which now clearly shows the implications of 3D printing in the reinforcement of surgeries.

The chapter has also described various applications of 3D printing, including the designing and manufacturing of custom prostheses and implants, the production of tissue and organ models, and many others. These applications extend what was possible in medical technology, besides meeting the specific needs of the patient. It has revolutionized the concept of care since a manufacturer of medical equipment can now make detailed, tailored equipment to match the patient's needs and reproduce complex processes that previously could not be achieved with standard manufacturing technologies, reducing psychological trauma.

There exists some difficulty in incorporating 3D printing into the conventional medical field, though it has many benefits. There are concerns about how to integrate it with current imaging systems, what protocols are needed, and the most important issue of legal standards to meet, which should be addressed to get the most out of this technology. These challenges have to be addressed and solved with the cooperation of doctors and engineers, as well as numerous regulatory authorities, to make the integration of 3D printing into clinical practice possible.
