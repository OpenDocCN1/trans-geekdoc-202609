Because Figure 5 uses photographs, which are high-dimensional data, another technique called random forests, which is similarly based on decision trees, is employed for visual anomaly identification. Random forest applies a number of decision trees trained on different samples of data and each tree in the forest gives its own prediction. Besides, the last prediction is derived from the outputs of each of the trees produced and involves simple voting. The following approach minimizes overfitting and the model's overall reliability is higher when working with imagery with visually strange artifacts that are highly sporadic. Random forests are also learning algorithms that perform well when there is either noise or missing values, which are prevalent in real-life visual anomaly detection. The possibility to determine the locality of decisions within the single tree and across many trees makes random forests highly suitable for analyzing anomalies in visual data.

Random forests can be applied to numerous tasks in the context of visual anomaly detection including, but not limited to, the identification of defects in production lines, differentiating between abnormal tissue patterns in medical imaging, and identifying invasive and unusual events in surveillance videos. For instance, in the case of detecting manufacturing defects, random forests applied for clustering images of produce that have pixel features about texture and color densities.
