
Information on how effectively QDs exhibit the photoluminescence phenomenon of converting the absorbed light into emitted fluorescence, essential in applications such as imaging and sensing.

### 5.3.1 Quantum yield

Definition and measurement:

As illustrated in Figure 9, absolute photoluminescence quantum yield (PL Quantum Yield) is defined as the ratio of emitted photons to absorbed photons in a quantum dot (QD). This metric evaluates the efficiency of fluorescence emission and is typically expressed either as a percentage or on a scale from 0 to 1. A high quantum yield indicates that a substantial proportion of the absorbed photons are re-emitted as fluorescence, resulting in brighter signals. This increased brightness enhances detectability and is especially valuable in applications such as bioimaging, where strong signal clarity is critical.

Influencing factors:

The properties such as size, the type of material, and the chemistry at the surface of the QDs determine their quantum yield. The size of QDs is also an important parameter for determining electronic structure and optical properties – the lower size QDs give higher quantum yield because of the smaller number of nonradiative decay pathways. Material selection and synthesis procedure play a pivotal role in quantum yield since changes in these aspects are related to the fluorescence output. The general properties of surface coatings and functionalization depend on the quantum yield as a result of the modification of the environment of the QDs and decrease in nonradiative losses.

### 5.3.2 Photoluminescence efficiency

**Definition and measurement:**

Another quantity closely related to photoluminescence efficiency is quantum yield, which states the ratio of emitted to absorbed photons out of all the incident light occurring at the QD. It is measured in percent, calculated on a per-QD basis, and quantifies the efficiency of how well a QD converts the absorbed energy into emitted light, rather than heat. Large photoluminescence efficiency suggests the extent to which the energy absorbed by QDs can be used to produce luminescence.

**Comparison with quantum yield:**

Although the quantum yield is closer to the ratio of emitted and absorbed photons, the photoluminescence efficiency gives more general indication concerning the light conversion efficiency. While these two metrics are closely connected, they have their variables, which affect their outcome. Quantum yield is the common parameter used to determine the performance of QDs in a particular application, but overall photoluminescence efficacy is the best parameter that identifies the efficacy of the QDs in converting the absorbed photons into emitted fluorescence.

### 5.3.3 Nonradiative processes

**Types of nonradiative processes:**

Nonradiative processes are processes that cause absorbed energy to be dissipated without an accompanying output of fluorescence. These processes are internal conversion, vibrational relaxation, and surface trapping. Nonradiative losses greatly decrease the quantum yield and the photoluminescence efficiency of the QDs.

**Impact on performance:**

Reducing these unwanted effects is essential in order to achieve the high quantum yield and efficient photoluminescence of QDs. Methods for surface passivation include coating with a layer that is protective and functionalization with stabilizing ligands, so that energy loss through non-emissive mechanisms is minimized. Optimal passivation brings improvements to the general performance of QDs, achieving higher fluorescence intensity and better stability.

### 5.3.4 Measurement techniques

Spectroscopic methods:

Most times, quantum yield and photoluminescence efficiency of QDs are determined using spectroscopic methods. These methods include the use of a standard calibrated light source to excite the QDs and measuring the emitted fluorescence in order to deduce the ratio of emitted to absorbed photons. Among standard methods, steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy and time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy are widely used for the analysis of optical characteristics of QDs and their performance parameters.

Standardization and comparisons:

Quantum yields and photoluminescence efficiencies can only be measured with an acceptable degree of repeatability and reliability if the key parameters are fixed and defined and the results are expressed in terms of reference materials. When comparing different QD methods, it is important to do it in the same environment so that the results that are obtained are real and genuine. Standardization of measurement often assists in rating and comparing the performance of any QD for specific uses.

## 5.4 Excitation and emission wavelengths

QDs have tunable photoluminescence properties that lend themselves well to applications in imaging and sensing. Considering their properties and versatility, the excitation and emission wavelengths of QDs are useful parameters when determining their efficiency and functionality. This section explains the reasons for these wavelengths and their practical usage.

### 5.4.1 Wavelength dependence on size

Both excitation and emission wavelengths are size-dependent because of the quantum confinement effects characteristic of QDs. When the size of the QDs is reduced, the energy required for the transition from the valence band to the conduction band is higher in energy, thereby shifting the emission wavelength to the blue region of the spectrum. In contrast, larger QDs have more redshifted emission at longer wavelengths. This size-tunable property also enables the synthesis of QDs with emission across a broad range of colors needed in multiplexed imaging.

### 5.4.2 Excitation wavelengths

Due to the quantum confinement, QDs exhibit a distinct fluorescence emission when excited by particular wavelengths of light. Generally, the excitation wavelength is absorbed by the QD to move electrons to a high energy level. The selection of the excitation wavelength depends on the QD material implemented and the size of the QDs. For example, for CdSe QDs, UV to visible light is necessary for excitation, while for PbS QDs, near-infrared excitation is needed. The excitation spectrum of QDs is typically broad involving excitation at different wavelengths and is helpful in various imaging modes.

### 5.4.3 Emission wavelengths

When QDs are excited, they glow at certain wavelengths dependent on the energy difference in QDs. Emission wavelength depends on the size of the QD, its core materials, and any additional surface coatings. In general, QDs with a smaller size radiate blue to green light while those with larger size radiate red to near-IR light. This tunability allows targeting of QDs exhibiting specific emission characteristics for various imaging uses. Fluorescence emission spectra are normally sharp and distinct, which minimize spectral congestions and improve the dexterity of multi-hued imaging.

### 5.4.4 Spectral range and resolution

QDs have a broad spectral range with sharp emission peaks making it easier for imaging to be more sensitive and have high resolution. The ability to change the wavelength of emitted light in this system within a narrow range offers the possibility of distinguishing the channels in multiplexed assays. The reduced FWHM of the emission peaks lowers the noise level and enhances the reliability of signal measures. This property is useful in applications where fine detail is needed to be identified.

### 5.4.5 Influence of surface coatings

It is therefore possible to modify the surface of QDs and use surface coatings to alter the excitation and emission wavelengths. These surface changes alter the electronic structure of the QD, thus invoking changes in excitation and emission wavelengths. For instance, the binding of specific ligands modifies the energy states of QDs as well as their fluorescence optical properties. Some applications require ideal optical properties of the surface, which is why layering of the surface coatings is carefully performed.
