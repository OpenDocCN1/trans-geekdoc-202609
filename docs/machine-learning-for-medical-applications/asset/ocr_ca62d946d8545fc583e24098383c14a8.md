The technique discovered by Rontgen, which made it possible to examine bones and some internal organs indirectly, without any surgery, quickly found applause in the medical community. The later prospect of capturing pictures of the skeletal system through X-ray enhanced diagnostics became the order of the day in hospitals and clinics across the globe.

The second great advancement following the X-rays was the development of the computed axial tomography (CAT or CT scan) by Godfrey Hounsfield in early 1970s. Using CT scans cross-sectional views of the body were taken to give a detailed and improved picture of internal organs, tissues, and abnormalities. This innovation revolutionized medical diagnostics by making three-dimensional pictures using both computer algorithms and X-rays. Apart from helping in illness identification and diagnosis, this new technology of CT imaging opened a new level of additional imaging advancement.

During the second half of the century, MRI emerged as a revolutionary imaging technique that enabled the generation of highly detailed images of soft tissues using non-ionizing radiation. This advancement is credited to scientists such as Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield, who adapted the principles of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) to create internal body images. As shown in Figure 1, MRI has proven especially valuable in neurological, musculoskeletal, and cardiovascular imaging due to its ability to differentiate between various soft tissue types. The development of MRI represents a major milestone in noninvasive medical imaging, providing clinicians with powerful new diagnostic capabilities.
