# 1 Introduction

Literature has shown that with the help of the new deep learning (DL) technology, the field of medical picture segmentation has undergone a revolutionary change [1]. To differentiate between an organ and a cancerous region, the earlier methods, such as thresholding, region growing, and edge detection, require a significant number of handcrafted features and human intervention [2]. These techniques though helpful in some cases more often had issues of noise, poor and deteriorating picture quality, and the inherent difficulties of medical imaging [3–5]. Automatic and highly accurate segmentation methods thus shifted from other learning methods toward DL, and particularly toward convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [6]. For sophisticated structures and relations to be detected in medical pictures and generalization across different imaging modalities and clinical applications, large amounts of annotated data are required in DL models [7]. This transition has created possibilities for improving the diagnostic accuracy, development of detailed and accurate treatment plans, and consequently the overall results of the patients [8].

One of the prominent accomplishments of DL in medical picture segmentation was the development of the specialized architectures, in particular, U-Net and its derivatives [9, 10]. These models are designed to compress and decompress encoder-decoder structures so that the analysis takes into account both the local and global context in an attempt to more accurately pinpoint characteristics of varying scales [11]. It was highly useful in biomedical segmentation due to its ability to produce accurate dense pixel segmentation maps even with minimum input data [12]. This design and its variations have proven to be significantly superior to the traditional methods when applied in a broad range of medical imaging applications: tumor segmentation, organ delineation, lesion detection, among others [7]. Possibilities for the application of DL models while a lot of annotated datasets are not available have also been enhanced by developments in data augmentation and transfer learning, which was a major concern in medical imaging.

The necessity of the fusion of multimodal data was underlined especially by current developments in medical image segmentation [13]. The combination of data acquired through multiple imaging techniques was called multimodal technique; in combining the data the merged picture of the anatomy and pathology was better-defined especially through the use of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), computed tomography (CT), and positron emission tomography (PET) scans [14–16]. Both of these methods make use of the synergistic features of each modality in improving the reliability of the diagnosis and efficiency of clinical decision-making [17]. For instance, PET/CT combines PET and CT scan images to improve the ability to identify the location of the tumor and determine its nature to improve the treatment plan in cancer.
