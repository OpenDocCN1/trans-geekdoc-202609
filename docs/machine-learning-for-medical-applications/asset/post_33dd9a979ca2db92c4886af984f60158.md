
## 3.2 Boosting and bagging in anomaly detection systems

Boosting and bagging are two common types of ensemble learning techniques employed in detecting anomalous patterns because of their effectiveness in improving the outcomes and tolerance to noise of corresponding learners. Such techniques utilize more base models and produce a more reliable and stable overall prediction, which is particularly important in the case of outlier detection. Anomalies are by their very nature uncommon and do not conform to typical procedures in data, which make their identification difficult. Both boosting and bagging address this problem by summing up the outcome produced by various base learners in order to enhance the training functionality and reduce mistakes. Despite being categorized under ensemble learning techniques, each method has a different perspective when handling the problem and each has its strengths in anomaly detection scenarios.

Bagging is an ensemble technique that involves training multiple and unrelated models of the training data with different random samples of data. These subsets are generated by selecting a subset of data with replacement, then passing the new subset of data to the next model. Subsequent to training, the individual models perform prediction and the results are ensembled through averaging (of residuals/root mean square error for regression type problems). The use of bagging lowers the variance of the model as the results of the individual learners are averaged out so that overfitting is avoided, which is common with high-variance models like decision trees.

Bagging is helpful because it allows the detection system to identify the noise and presence of high variance that is present in real databases. Since anomalies are rare and have a high variability, bagging can stabilize the detection process using multiple base models, while introducing deviations from the input. For instance, in random forest model which belongs to a bagging-based method, the decision trees are trained on different samples of the data, and the outcome from all the trees is used to make a conclusion of whether a data point was an anomaly. Such a method is useful because even if an anomaly is missed by some of the individual trees at the time when built, with a large number of trees, the anomaly is likely to show up somewhere once all trees collectively make their prediction.

A common problem with anomaly detection tasks is class imbalance where majority of instances belong to the normal class. This problem is addressed in the bagging process because when each model has a different view of the data it provides the ensemble a better chance to identify those infrequent and obscured patterns. Overall, bagging minimizes the chances of false negatives, that is, it makes sure that outliers are detected regardless of how rarely they are encountered during training data.

Whereas, boosting is another kind of ensemble technique in which one model builds another model successively and the current model tries to minimize the mistakes of the previous model. Unlike bagging whose models are trained separately, boosting builds on the outputs of the previous weak learner models by focusing on the difficult sample points that previous models misclassify. The last prediction in boosting is arrived at from averaging the weighted prediction of all the single models. This iterative learning feature enhances the ability to work on the most difficult examples, a factor that is very helpful in anomaly detection cases as most of the anomaly samples are not easily identifiable from the normal dataset.

AdaBoost and gradient boosting are basically enhancing methods, which are superior in identifying intricate anomalies that cannot be easily pointed out individually by a single model. For instance, in medical imaging, boosting can be used to make out fine details in images that include disease anomalies though not well seen. The strength of boosting is that as the model's accuracy increases through an iterative redefinition of the decision boundaries it increases the emphasis on ambiguous cases. The above makes it very ideal for applications in fields that involve anomaly detection where anomalies are few and can be in complex structures of the feature space.

XGBoost and LightGBM are two widely used structures in anomaly detection because of the efficiency of gradient boosting algorithms. These compute efficient training schedules by reducing a particular loss function normally derived from the mistakes made by past models. Therefore, gradient boosting exhibits very fast convergence to the accurate solution if the learning is performed on noisy data. For anomaly detection, gradient boosting was revealed to be able to classify the selected data points as either normal or anomalous by identifying the rich and possibly nonlinear relationships within the feature dimensionality. The gradient boosting involves the fixing and refixing of the errors in the field where these models are applied, thereby making it ideal for the use of the detection process for time series, where gross interactions between the features are involved.

## 3.3 The role of diversity in ensemble-based anomaly detection

Ensemble learning models have become increasingly prominent in the field of anomaly detection due to their ability to enhance detection accuracy by combining the strengths of multiple models. As illustrated in Figure 6, ensemble-based approaches leverage the diversity among individual models each trained with different structures, features, or training sets to capture a wider range of anomaly patterns. This diversity is crucial, as it prevents the ensemble from merely replicating the same insights and instead integrates varied perspectives, leading to a more robust and generalized detection capability. In the context of anomaly detection where abnormal cases are rare and often subtle this variation among models significantly improves the system's sensitivity and reliability. Thus, model heterogeneity and prediction diversity are key drivers of ensemble learning's success in detecting outliers and unexpected behaviors.

There are several ways to create diversity among the models that are available in an ensemble that include the following; Different datasets used in training the model, different model structures used in creating the models, and different learning algorithms used in developing the models. When the models are conceptually different, then, they detect different types of anomaly because anomalies present many entity and feature patterns that an individual model find difficult to identify. For instance, in a situation where we have different type of anomalies present in a given data set, one model performs well when it comes to identifying certain kinds of anomaly, while another model performs well in identifying other kinds of anomaly. By fusing these models, the net outcome of the ensemble improves in its ability to detect a wider variety of anomalies in comparison to any one constituent model of the ensemble, and therefore yields a better global anomaly detection system.

Effective change of ensembles in the context of anomaly detection and its diversification was recognized and one of the methods is the use of different subsets of the data during the formation of each of the models included in the ensemble. This can be done by using techniques such as bagging where each model is developed using some data randomly chosen from the training data, or boosting where each model is developed using the difficult instances for the previous models to learn. By training models on different portions of the data the ensemble knows about different parts of the underlying data distribution. Such a spread of the training data also enables the ensemble to generalize better and become less sensitive to noise and outliers, both of which are typical for the anomaly detection problem.

Besides in the data that the system uses to train, different learning algorithms can be used within the ensemble to temporarily increase the level of diversity. For example, some models within the ensemble method would be decision trees while other could implement SVM. The reason for joining several learning algorithms is that each of the learning methods has its advantages and is equipped for different patterns and discovers various sorts of anomalies. Popular algorithms such as decision trees, for instance, can address categorical features as well as nonlinear decision margins while SVMs do well in high-dimensional feature spaces. Due to the synergy of several algorithms, the ensemble can be enhanced in such a way as to have increased ability to identify anomalous events in various data types.

Architectural difference is another crucial element that improves variety in ensemble-based anomaly identification. The performance does not remain the same even within the learning algorithm, as variations are possible within the classifier model’s architecture. For example, a deep structure that contains more than one hidden layer develops the capability of identifying patterns inherent to the data set, while a shallow model develops the capability of identifying straightforward characteristics in the data set. It is also noteworthy that by including models with different depth, the ensemble can enhance its capacity to recognize patterns that are largely anomalous. This diversity in the architecture helps build a reliable anomaly detection system because different aspects of the data are utilized by the different components of the pipeline.
