# 3.3.6 Inkjet printing

Material deposition of small droplets of the build material on the build platform was termed inkjet printing, also known as material jetting. Structured in the same manner as inkjet print technology, print heads spread layers of material such as wax over the build platform and solidify. That inkjet technology that allows printing using various materials and colors was remarkable because it provides complex and rather attractive medical models.

# 3.4 Materials used in 3D printing and their properties

It has been established that the success of 3D printing in medical applications and the efficiency of the technology depends significantly on the choice of materials. Each material used in additive manufacturing offers distinct properties that determine its suitability for specific medical implants and devices. This understanding has led to important questions regarding how best to enhance the functionality of these materials and assess their effectiveness for various therapeutic applications.

Polymers remain the most widely used materials in 3D printing due to their affordability, ease of processing, and versatility. Common materials include those used in fused deposition modeling (FDM), such as polylactic acid (PLA) and acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS). PLA has been particularly favored especially until approximately four years ago for its biocompatibility, biodegradability, and minimal thermal shrinkage, making it ideal for applications sensitive to thermal changes. ABS, on the other hand, offers superior mechanical strength and impact resistance, making it suitable for durable prototypes. In more advanced medical implantations, high-performance polymers like polyetheretherketone and polyamide are employed due to their enhanced mechanical properties, chemical resistance, and thermal stability.

Metals used in 3D printing include titanium, cobalt-chromium alloys, and stainless steel – primarily processed through direct metal laser sintering (DMLS) and selective laser sintering (SLS). Titanium is especially valued for its favorable strength-to-weight ratio, excellent corrosion resistance, and established biocompatibility, making it ideal for craniofacial reconstruction, dental prostheses, and orthopedic implants. Stainless steel is commonly used for surgical tools and temporary implants due to its high tensile strength and fatigue resistance. Meanwhile, cobalt-chromium alloys are selected for applications involving high mechanical loads, thanks to their superior wear resistance.
