## 3 Evolution of deep learning in computer vision

### 3.1 Early neural networks and their impact

As for the origin of neural networks in the domain of computer vision, it can be traced back to the 1980s, which was the start of the whole process of developing new methods in the sphere of image processing and, in particular, image recognition. Initial simple structures aimed at avoiding human thought and making computers recognize what we want, like perceptrons and multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), at the initiation stage depicted how to learn the representation from the imagery data. These models were helpful to illustrate that despite their shortcomings, neural networks were capable of providing good solutions for problems belonging to pattern recognition.

Among these models, the first one that was developed was known as the perceptron and was described in 1957 by an American researcher, Frank Rosenblatt. Although its influence persisted into the 1980s, the perceptron was one of the first attempts to build a model of a single neuron in the human brain. It was shown that it can differentiate linearly separable patterns, which formed the basis for further research in neural networks. The perceptron was not well-suited to more complex, non-linearly separable patterns, and further development of the algorithm continued.

The single-layer perceptron was further improved when it gave way to the MLP, which has more than one layer of neurons to match more extensive processes. The beliefs and design of neural networks’ basic architecture are discussed in detail in Figure 5 and show that early neural networks laid down the foundation for contemporary AI and machine learning. The authors found an issue with training the MLP model; despite having enhanced capacity, the MLP had problems when training, especially when facing the vanishing gradient problem, which makes it difficult to learn deep networks. This was so limiting until better training techniques were invented to emulate computer-generated methods.

The backpropagation learning algorithm in the 1980s was a major advance in developing techniques for training multilayered neural networks. Argued by Prof. Geoffrey Hinton and others, backpropagation came in handy in the computation of gradients, and the optimization exercise of weights across different layers made some of the early training challenges easier to overcome. This advance extended the opportunities for neural networks in computer vision and contributed to the development of strong connections.

The real surprise in comparing CNNs was unveiled in 2012 with the introduction of a deeper CNN structure called AlexNet, which outperformed all the conventional methods in ILSVRC. It was claimed that AlexNet’s actual architecture incorporated dropout regularization and ReLU activation functions; besides that, it possessed three fully connected layers and five convolutional layers. This achievement excited researchers and created an extensive level of awareness about CNNs in large-scale visual recognition work due to the high accuracy of deep learning.

Later innovations in the designs of CNNs took the field even further. Launched in 2014, VGGNet included a uniform structure of 16–19 layers, compact concurrently, and transmitted profound nets to emphasize their depth and regularity. This made it easier to improve the quality of image classification jobs and also boosted feature extraction capacity. VGGNet was followed by GoogLeNet (called Inception v1), which widely spread the inception modules that allowed the creation of deep and highly effective networks due to pooling operations and many powerful convolutional filters in a single layer.

By including residual connections, ResNet was created in 2015 to address some issues associated with training very deep networks. These connections reduced the vanishing gradient problem, which occurs at one level, thereby making it possible to train networks with hundreds of layers. The deeper networks converge faster and, at the same time, are resistant to vanishing gradients throughout the training process, as was highlighted in the architecture of ResNet.

There has been the development of other types of CNNs, some of which are known as EfficientNet, which improve the network capability through depth, width, and resolution scaling. All these innovations have shaped new improvements in CNNs, making them solve intricate computer vision jobs with higher accuracy and efficiency.

The use of computer vision with CNNs was marked as a great breakthrough in the history of applying deep learning in image recognition systems. Further development of CNN architectures and methods proves to be fluid and active in applied fields and further research in different fields.
