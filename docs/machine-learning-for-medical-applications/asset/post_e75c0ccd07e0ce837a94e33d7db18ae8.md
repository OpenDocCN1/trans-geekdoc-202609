## 3.4 Optimizing ensemble models for imbalanced anomaly detection datasets

There are always a large number of normal instances that overwhelm the small number of instances in an anomaly dataset and this brings a lot of challenges. In such datasets, traditional ML models, including ensemble methods often have a problem of classifying the minority class. This skewed distribution affects performance since models are inclined to the majority category and yield high false negatives for anomalies. Mitigating this challenge is needed in order to evaluate the replication of ensemble model optimization methodologies that can improve class imbalance yet retain effective anomaly detection on the financial data.

One of the main approaches that help fine-tune the ensemble models for imbalanced anomaly detection is the proper tuning of decision thresholds. In general, in ML models, the decision boundary is established for an instance to be classified as anomalous depending on the probability/score set a priori. Balancing the loads for unbalanced datasets presents a big problem in that the default threshold for training tends to overpredict the normal class. By reducing the decision threshold for flagging anomalies, the ensemble model is in a position to achieve higher sensitivity to the minority class. This adjustment is made to generalize more data as anomalous, thereby achieving higher recall of uncommon cases at the possible instance of lower precision. The question is about the need to achieve the best compromise between precision and recall in order to maintain high efficiency of the model.

Another technique using ensemble-based method for the treatment of class imbalance in anomaly detection is the resampling technique including oversampling the minority class and undersampling the majority class. Oversampling duplicates some specimens from the category of interest to make them more present in data sets, and undersampling does the reverse to normal samples. Such measures can assist in reducing the impact of imbalance of the majority class on the performance of the ensemble while at the same time enhancing the capability of the model to identify anomalies. To improve the model learning, other approaches such as SMOTE (synthetic minority oversampling technique) create new artificial instances of the minority class. But the selection of resampling technique has to be done judiciously because wrong application can result in overfitting and underfitting of the model.

Besides data-level methods, the methods specifically applied to the ensembles can be used for enhancing the performance on the imbalanced anomaly detection problem. For instance, in ensemble methods decision making, the weighted voting approach recognizes different weights based on performance of each model over the anomalous instances. The best models that are effective in distinguishing the minority class can be assigned with the higher weights in a way that influences final decision-making processes more than other models. This approach assists in defining a new method for obtaining more precise anomaly detection since it focuses on the models having a greater probability of identifying rare anomalies.

Another synthesis method includes ensemble reduction, which can be of two types: first, where weak classifiers are rejected from the ensemble in order to enhance efficiency and optimization of the models; second, where high-variance classifiers are reduced from the ensemble, so as to improve efficiency and optimize the models. Specifically, in samples with an uneven distribution of anomalies, ensemble pruning has the useful function of removing models that are inclined to favor the majority class and thus are noncontributory to the identification of anomalies. Some of the bad models can be pruned – those that are not able to contribute to the solution but only add extra noise to the solution by being part of the ensemble. This then increases the efficiency of the model in using the less represented class.

Furthermore, the ensemble models can be adapted with a cost-sensitive learning that assigns different costs to using an anomaly classifier and a normal instance classifier. A common problem with imbalanced datasets is that missed classifications of anomalies are costly due to the nature of the problem domain. Using the cost-sensitive approach, both true positive and false negatives are given higher cost in the ensemble framework; hence the models are forced to enhance the detection of anomalies. This adjustment makes the ensemble pay more attention to the minority class, making its capability of identifying imbalanced datasets better.

Other enhancement techniques, which can be used in fitting ensemble models, are also very efficient in cases of imbalanced data sets such as AdaBoost and gradient boosting. These techniques have the operationality of training models in an unbounded loop, then emphasizing those misclassified data of previous rounds. When misclassifying anomaly patterns, samples are trained with higher weights in order to improve on the performance of the ensemble on the particular minority class in anomaly detection. Due to the iterativeness of the boosting approach, the model is capable of learning from mistakes in order to accurately detect the few anomalous instances. Furthermore, boosting methods are immune from significant class imbalance compared to bagging-based ensemble, because boosting methods naturally pay more attention to the samples that are most difficult to classify.

## 4 Hybrid deep learning architectures for complex anomaly detection

### 4.1 CNN and RNN hybrid models for spatiotemporal anomaly detection

The analyzed patterns of objects’ states in production, video surveillance, and climate observation often occur at any point in time and space and are dependent on space and time. Being able to take these relations entails using models, which are capable of handling spatial and temporal data at the same time. CNNs are acclaimed for their capacity to capture spatial features in data, especially image-like data while recurrent NNs (RNNs) are known for their ability to capture sequential features in the data. The integration of these two architectures in a hybrid model has been proven to be an effective solution toward spatiotemporal anomaly detection model by developing benefits of CNN and RNN models toward improvement of detecting changes in anomalies that happen both in space and time.

CNNs are most commonly applied in extracting spatial features from vision data. In the context of anomaly detection, CNNs can indeed discover local pattern and texture of the data that serves as important departure for separating normal and abnormal regions. Figure 7 illustrates how CNNs are used to extract features from individual frames in video data that contain static spatial patterns that show irregularity. These fixed-size local patches allow CNNs to emphasize differences in pixel-level intensity, object contour, and spatial layout, all of which are also characteristic of the anomalies. Simple CNNs do not suffice to detect temporal changes because they do not model temporal dynamics by themselves.

LSTM models are intended to work with sequential data since they carry information on preceding inputs from the previous time through recurrent connections. In temporal data, especially for anomaly detection where the patterns of behavior change with time, these architectures are excellent in capturing long-range dependencies. For example, in video surveillance, it detects the motion of objects with time, for instance, appearance of people in some areas is a model that can detect that something is not normal. While CNNs are limited by their inability to supply the temporal context needed for reliable anomaly detection in a spatiotemporal domain, the inherent temporal structure of RNNs makes them capable of offering this context. As powerful as RNNs are, there is a problem that they encounter long-term dependency issues if the temporal information is variable.

To obtain benefits of both representations, CNNs and RNNs can be linked together in a way, wherein CNNs provide the spatial image input to the RNNs to model temporal sequence. This approach enables the model to exploit both CNNs’ strong capability in recognizing small and complex spatial relations, as well as RNNs’ capacity to learn temporal relations.
