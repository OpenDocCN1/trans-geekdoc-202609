
- Improved biocompatibility: The shell layer can be incorporated to enhance the compatibility of the QDs with the biological system. These functionalities can be modified on the surface to improve solubility, as well as decrease toxicity and facilitate targeting of biological molecules.

### 4.1.3 Synthesis methods

Several methods are employed to synthesize core-shell QDs, each with its advantages and limitations:

- Colloidal synthesis: The first method entails stepwise addition of the first core material and a shell-forming precursor in a well-defined system. In the beginning, the formation of the core is made and then the deposition of the shell material takes place. The core-shell structure, size, and composition can be controlled easily during colloidal synthesis, but the reaction conditions need to be optimized.
- CVD: In CVD techniques shell materials can be grown on presynthesized core QDs for the fabrication of the compound structures. High purity and uniformity are also benefits but this method is also not versatile and needs specialized equipment in comparison with colloidal synthesis.
- MBE: MBE is another technique employed to enhance the growth of core-shell QDs with a precise atomic scale. This makes it possible to control carefully the dimensions of the core and shell layers and thus it is possible to synthesize high-quality QDs. MBE requires sophisticated and expensive equipment and has been generally applied to specific uses only.

### 4.1.4 Applications

Core-shell QDs are widely used in various applications due to their enhanced properties:

- Medical imaging: Enhanced photostability as well as brightness of core-shell QDs enables their uses in enhanced imaging methods including fluorescence microscopy and in vivo imaging.
- Biological labelling: These multifunctional core-shell structures are anchored by specific ligands and thus can be used in bio targeting of biomolecules and cells. This makes it possible to track and analyze in detail in the biological research fields.
- Optoelectronic devices: The optoelectronic applications of these core-shell QDs include LEDs and solar cells in which the tunable optical properties of the dots are used to enhance the performance and efficiency.

## 4.3 Dot-in-rod and dot-in-matrix structures

Dot-in-rod (QD) structures and other related structures are essential in modifying the light-emitting and electric features of QDs for purposes of application. Of these structures, the dot-in-rod and dot-in-matrix structures have different uses and applications because of differences in their structures.

### 4.3.1 Dot-in-rod structures

Dot-in-rod QDs refer to a structure where a QD is coated within a semiconductor rod. This structure incorporates elements from the solid core and the rod to improve performance in a range of applications.

- Enhanced optical properties: Due to the presence of the rod-shaped matrix around the core QD, it is possible to have different absorption and emission characteristics. The nature of the rod structure can improve the emission intensity and photostability of the core QD.
- Anisotropic emission: The dot-in-rod structure can demonstrate anisotropic emission characteristics, that is, the direction of the emission changes according to the position of the rod. For applications where directionality of emitted light detection is desired, this can be an advantage.
- Improved quantum efficiency: The rod matrix can also work as a confinement layer, preventing nonradiative recombination processes, which will enhance the quantum efficiency of the core QD doped within the matrix.
- Complex synthesis: Synthesis of dot-in-rod structures requires extensive construction processes that are difficult to regulate with high accuracy. This makes the detail of its composition somewhat more complex and leads to problems with obtaining the necessary homogeneity and repeatability in mass production.
- Size and shape control: It is often difficult to obtain a fine control over the size and aspect ratio of the rod matrix and the core QD, which affects the optical properties.

### 4.3.2 Dot-in-matrix structures

Dot-in-matrix QDs are constructed by placing a QD in the middle, encased in a three-dimensional matrix of a different semiconductor material. This structure gives extra flexibility in tuning the optical and electronic features of the quantum dots.

## 4.5 Functionalized and surface-coated quantum dots

Surface-functionalized and surface-coated QDs are a breakthrough in nanotechnology, particularly in areas of application where tagged nanotechnological reagents require improved stability, specificity and performance. This section introduces functionalization and surface coating of QDs, the techniques used, and their importance in various applications.

In the present article, we will discuss the various surface functionalization techniques to improve their performance.

- Ligand exchange: Ligand exchange is a popular method of providing the synthetic change of the QDs' surface chemistry. This process entails the substitution of the initial stabilizing ligands by others that should improve solubility, biocompatibility, and targeting of substances. In this way, researchers can modify the surface ligands of the QDs to meet the requirements for their applications including selective cells in medical imaging.
- Covalent bonding: Covalent chemistries are used to functionalize the QD surface, whereby functional groups are grafted onto the core. This approach enables the formation of a robust and irreversible linkage of biomolecules including peptides and antibodies. Specificity and stability of QDs in the biological environment are enhanced using the covalent functionalization process that makes the QD relevant in imaging as well as diagnostics.
- Electrostatic and van der Waals interactions: Coulombic and non-Coulombic forces are employed for chemical tailoring of QDs as chemisorbed charges onto the QD surface. This method creates the possibility of connecting other biological molecules that can modify the action of the QD. This approach is less stable than covalent bonding, thus it forms a weaker interaction and finally loses functional groups easily.

### 4.5.2 Challenges and considerations

- Stability and aggregation: The problems that functionalized as well as surface-coated QDs can encounter include stability and aggregation. Reliability of the above methods of surface modification is determined by the environmental conditions and type of coating to be applied. It is critical to maintain QD performance, keeping in mind that functional groups must not degrade and the coatings must be hard.
- Toxicity and biocompatibility: Since modifications like functionalization of the polymer surface and coating of the material can improve biocompatibility, the selection of the material and the method of its application should be discussed thoroughly to exclude toxicity. It is important to test the toxicity of QDs and their properties after the surface modification for biological application.
- Cost and complexity: The functionalization of QDs and the coating process bring about some extra costs and difficulties in fabrication. Thus, the search for cost-effective and scalable techniques for producing the surface modifications still remains an essential question for QDs and their further commercial use.
