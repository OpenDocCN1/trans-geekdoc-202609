Using these architectures, visual data processing has reached a level that was impossible before and has improved the performance of models on tasks such as segmentation, object identification, and picture categorization. ## 1 Introduction

Literature has shown that with the help of the new deep learning (DL) technology, the field of medical picture segmentation has undergone a revolutionary change [1]. To differentiate between an organ and a cancerous region, the earlier methods, such as thresholding, region growing, and edge detection, require a significant number of handcrafted features and human intervention [2]. These techniques though helpful in some cases more often had issues of noise, poor and deteriorating picture quality, and the inherent difficulties of medical imaging [3–5]. Automatic and highly accurate segmentation methods thus shifted from other learning methods toward DL, and particularly toward convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [6]. For sophisticated structures and relations to be detected in medical pictures and generalization across different imaging modalities and clinical applications, large amounts of annotated data are required in DL models [7]. This transition has created possibilities for improving the diagnostic accuracy, development of detailed and accurate treatment plans, and consequently the overall results of the patients [8].

One of the prominent accomplishments of DL in medical picture segmentation was the development of the specialized architectures, in particular, U-Net and its derivatives [9, 10]. These models are designed to compress and decompress encoder-decoder structures so that the analysis takes into account both the local and global context in an attempt to more accurately pinpoint characteristics of varying scales [11]. It was highly useful in biomedical segmentation due to its ability to produce accurate dense pixel segmentation maps even with minimum input data [12]. This design and its variations have proven to be significantly superior to the traditional methods when applied in a broad range of medical imaging applications: tumor segmentation, organ delineation, lesion detection, among others [7]. Possibilities for the application of DL models while a lot of annotated datasets are not available have also been enhanced by developments in data augmentation and transfer learning, which was a major concern in medical imaging.

The necessity of the fusion of multimodal data was underlined especially by current developments in medical image segmentation [13]. The combination of data acquired through multiple imaging techniques was called multimodal technique; in combining the data the merged picture of the anatomy and pathology was better-defined especially through the use of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), computed tomography (CT), and positron emission tomography (PET) scans [14–16]. Both of these methods make use of the synergistic features of each modality in improving the reliability of the diagnosis and efficiency of clinical decision-making [17]. For instance, PET/CT combines PET and CT scan images to improve the ability to identify the location of the tumor and determine its nature to improve the treatment plan in cancer. The analysis of multimodal data was facilitated by stereotactic image registration as well as data fusion in which various sets of images were aligned and fused together into a unified data analysis system [18]. These are changes that are revolutionizing medical imaging since they generate more and detailed information on complex diseases.

With the use of DL-based segmentation algorithms in healthcare facilities, a number of problems and questions arise. One issue is determining how these models can be generalized and effective across a wide cross section of patients as well as across different types of imaging technology [7, 19, 20]. Despite that, when applied to different clinical scenarios, the models derived from specific datasets proved to exhibit lower accuracy, and need significant tweaking to adapt. In addition, since physicians need to trust and understand the decision-making of DL models, interpretability was essential for therapeutic use of these models. It was necessary to introduce techniques that would help explain the model in order to increase confidence and trust in such tools [21]. More research and work were needed to make DL-based segmentation tools more effective and fairer in clinical use as well as ensure the compliance with regulations and ethical principles such as data privacy and model bias [22, 23].

## 2 Definition and scope of medical image segmentation

### 2.1 What is medical image segmentation?

Medical image segmentation is the procedure to split a medical picture in parts with appropriate area and/or segments that are directly associated with an organ of the disease. This procedure involves identifying and outlining some aspects of the image like lesion or tumor of clinical interest. The main purpose of segmentation was to decentralize an image's representation so that it could be analyzed and explained for research and therapeutic purposes.

Segmentation can be done in a number of ways, ranging from fully manual to fully automated methods, as well as semi-automated techniques that balance between the two. Manual segmentation, though sometimes cost-efficient, introduces operator bias and may compromise performance consistency. In contrast, automated methods improve both precision and speed by mimicking human cognition through algorithmic logic. Segmentation in medical imaging is important for dealing with a myriad of issues. It allows for quantification of the size of body parts, determination of the stage of a disease, and reconstruction of a surgical operation plan.
