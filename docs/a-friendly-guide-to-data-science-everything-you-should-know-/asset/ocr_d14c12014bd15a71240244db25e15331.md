With the exception of R and Python, the products mentioned above are proprietary, so they are very expensive and not accessible to most individuals. The proprietary tools are falling out of favor as people switch to open source R or Python. A couple things that keep the proprietary tools around are that they include guaranteed security and compliance, critical in many industries, and companies that have code written in these languages or tools would have to rewrite everything in their chosen open source language. This is expensive, time-consuming, and not without risk (somebody could accidentally introduce a new bug), so it will be time before these tools are abandoned, if they ever are. An additional reason that organizations sometimes stick to proprietary software is because they are paying for it, they are entitled to customer support and can also influence future development of the software. Anyone who has stuck with some code that isn't doing what they expect can understand the value of just being able to pick up the phone and call someone for help rather than hitting up Stack Overflow, the Internet's best free spot for technical questions. However, it is worth noting that there is customer support available for open source tools through some private companies (for a fee, of course).
