The models first learned that most of the features weren't useful, and they disregarded certain characteristics that were common across all the resumes under that job function (like programming skills for software development engineers). So far, so good.

At that point, things started to go south. The models picked up and started favoring “strong verbs”—words like “executed” and “captured” appeared far more often on men’s resumes than women’s and because so many of the hired people were men. It also penalized those that were clearly associated with women’s resumes, like “women’s” (as in something like “women’s rugby”) and the names of certain women-only colleges.

What basically went wrong was that the team failed for technooptimism—believing that they automated it, it would be better than doing it manually. They wanted to use the Web to find the diamonds in the rough. But what they forgot was that all they were doing was training a system to do about more below.

There are a couple of other biases to play in here, of which we will talk about more below. One is selection bias—their training set consisted of people who had applied to Amazon. Amazon is known to break into, so anyone who doubts themselves will be less likely to apply, and imposter syndrome is in a lot more women than men. It’s known that while men tend to apply for jobs even if they don’t meet all the required skills, women generally don’t. The other is survivorship bias, which is when only those who made it a certain point are considered. In this case, few women were hired over the ten-year training period that information about them (and their quality) isn’t a consideration. They can determine if the algorithm is mostly leaving out candidates who will be successful through, but they have no idea if other, way better, candidates were rejected. They have no idea about the quality of those applicants.
