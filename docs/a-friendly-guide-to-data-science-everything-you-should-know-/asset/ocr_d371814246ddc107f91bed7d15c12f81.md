# Duplicate Rows

Duplicate rows often hide in plain sight if you forget to look for them. They can seriously bias models, especially if there’s a particular pattern to why they’re duplicated. Sometimes a duplicate row may be a literal duplicate of another row, with every column having the same value. These are the easiest to detect. Other times, only some of the rows need to be duplicated for the record to be considered a duplicate. If you had a table of people and you know one of rows is bad, but different places of birth, something would definitely be fishy and you know how to deal with duplicates depends on context and business knowledge. You always have to think about how a record got duplicated, which can inform what you do to fix it. Even in the scenario where every value is duplicated between two rows, there might be cases where they aren’t truly duplicate entries—they could both be valid. For instance, imagine a table storing people’s names, birth dates, and state of birth, but no other info. It’s unlikely but not impossible for two people with the same names to be born on the same day even in the same state.
