# # Sampling with Replacement

We often talk about sampling with or without replacement. This is really only relevant when you are sampling data to use from a larger, complete dataset. Sampling with replacement would allow some data points in the complete dataset to be selected more than once—basically, the data point is put back into the dataset after being “removed” in selection. Sampling without replacement is just the opposite, each time a data point is selected, it is effectively removed from the complete dataset. The most natural way to sample from the marble dumbbell would be to pull one marble out and set it aside, then reach in for another to set aside, and so on, which would be sampling without replacement. You could also toss the marble back in after selection and stir it a bit, which would be sampling with replacement. Having people fill out a survey would normally be sampling without replacement (you wouldn't ask someone to fill it out twice). If you want a truly sample of numbers, where each number has an equally likely chance of being selected, you'd want to sample with replacement (otherwise, the probability of each number goes up, and it's no longer random).

# # Sample Size

Like many things, what we are trying to accomplish should dictate the amount of data needed for a study. Most of us have a general sense that more data is better than less data. Often, the opposite is true. Especially in the era of big data, we often want to use as much of it as possible—maybe even the whole population. It's perfectly true that we're more likely to miss exceptional cases if we take a subset of a population.
