One of the biggest areas of improvement is in the analysis of medical images like MRIs, CT scans, and X-rays, something that needs to be done exclusively by specially trained people. We talked about one of these tools helping to diagnose hard-to-spot breast cancer in Chapter 15—ML-based image evaluation systems are especially good at detecting very mild anomalies that are usually missed by people. Additionally, relying on ML for this saves time from doctors—they still need to look at the images, especially where the ML detected nothing, but time is saved when it finds something. The main ethical risk here is relying too much on ML. These tools make mistakes, and humans still need to do their due diligence. One advantage of using these tools is that they minimize the risk of discrimination. When looking only at the inside of our bodies, things like ethnicity and socioeconomic status aren't visible.

There is also potential for ML to improve other methods of diagnosis as well as treatment plans. Tools that can recommend whether a patient should receive a particular medicine, or a particular treatment, are already in use in some hospitals. These can spot spots doctors miss because of the sheer number of factors, so they can be very powerful. Some of these tools can take a step further and actually predict outcomes for patients based on different combinations of treatments, helping doctors choose a strategy. One thing we are finding is that these can be biased because human biases are baked in. For instance, one system is known to be less likely to recommend a pain medication for Black patients because there are inaccurate beliefs that they have higher pain tolerance and are at higher risk for addiction, and these biases have captured in the model system uses. But the truth is, there is also great potential here to overcome doctors' biases, if fair ML tools can be created.
