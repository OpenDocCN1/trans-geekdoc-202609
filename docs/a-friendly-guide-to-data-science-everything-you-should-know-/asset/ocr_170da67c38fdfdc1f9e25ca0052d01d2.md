There are several biases that fall under *extension neglect*, which happens when experimenters don’t consider the sample size when making judgments about the outcome. The most important here is *base rate fallacy*, which crops up when people only focus on information relating to a specific case and disregard more important general information. The term “base rate” basically means the general case, and it is sometimes disregarded in favor of something more interesting. An example particularly relevant to data scientists is in situations where we have a classifier’s testing for a relatively rare thing and has low precision (a lot of false positives); we have to not fall into the trap of believing all the positives. This is called the *false positive paradox* and is a specific type of base rate fallacy.

ophenia is how we tend to see connections and relationships between things that are unrelated. This is why the warning “correlation is not causation” is so important—it’s a fundamentally human thing to identify connections in our world. We have to be careful. If you see a connection, you need to confirm it’s real. Don’t jump to conclusions just because it feels like something interesting. This is one reason that statistical testing is used. Just because one number is higher than another doesn’t mean it’s a true reflection of reality and not just a result of randomness.

There’s a famous (but apocryphal) story that illustrates another bias, *survivorship bias*, which is when we look at the results of something and only think about the things that made it through (“survived”) a filter of some sort, disregarding the things that didn’t.
