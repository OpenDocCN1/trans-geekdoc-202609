We don't know if they looked at the results by race, but if they did, they would have seen the significant differences between Black and white people's scores.

If race was not in the model, did it perform so differently on Black and white people? Even though the questionnaire didn't ask about race, did it contain many questions that social scientists know are proxies for race. A sample questionnaire is shared online.2 Most of the questions seem reasonable, asking about criminal history—types of crimes, number of arrests, and whether the defendant was impaired by drugs or alcohol at the time of the current crime. But then there are potentially damaging and unfair questions. One asks the defendant when their parents separated (if they did). Another asks how many of the defendant's friends or acquaintances have ever been arrested, and one more asks how often they see their family. There's one asking how many times the defendant has moved in the last year. It's hard not to notice that some of the questions address issues would not be admissible in court cases because they are not relevant to the case at hand. And yet, this information is used to influence decisions about others' futures.
