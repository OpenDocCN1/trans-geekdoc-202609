
## Categorical or Qualitative

### Nominal Data

One thing your doctor tracks is your gender. This falls under the simplest type of categorical data—nominal (sometimes nominative), a word that basically means “name,” because this data type represents things that have no mathematical properties and can't ever be ordered meaningfully. Other nominal types would be religion, name, and favorite color. These are obviously not numeric, and there is no natural order to them.

It might surprise you that a number can also be nominal, such as a US zip code, a five-digit number. There is a slight pattern to them, as they increase as you move east to west across the United States. They are not numbers in the mathematical sense—you can't multiply two zip codes together to get something meaningful, and taking an average of them makes no sense. Also, the pattern isn't consistent—it's not true that subsequent zip code is always more westerly than a smaller zip code (they are jumbled especially in metropolitan areas, and the order isn't perfect because of the distance north and south that has to be covered). There's no way to know just by looking at the number. It's important to remember that just because it looks like a number doesn't mean you can treat it as a number.

One nominal value that is a number is the Social Security number in the United States. It seems different from a zip code because it is unique—each one is a unique value, and it can also be used uniquely identify an individual. But it still has no real mathematical properties. It's important to always consider the nature of any number you see in data.

One special case of nominal data worth mentioning is the binary (or Boolean) type, because it is used very frequently in data science. The old approach to gender could have fallen under this type, but now there are usually other options. The binary type is more common with simple yes-or-no scenarios. For instance, perhaps your doctor wants to track whether you play any kind of sport or not. The answer would be yes, you play a sport or no, you do not play a sport. Often, the number 1 is used for yes and 0 for no, but some systems can also store the values True and False. The important thing to remember is that it has exactly two possible values. ### Ordinal Data

Ordinal data has a lot in common with nominal data, which is why they are both called qualitative or categorical. In the case of ordinal, you can think of it as nominal with one special trait: it has a natural order, where each value comes before or after each other value. We intuitively understand T-shirt size has a natural order that is clear from the value. These would be values like S, M, L, XL, and so on. So there is an order, but there is nothing numeric about these values, and there is no such a zero value in the ordering. There is also no mathematical relationship between the sizes or their relative order—that is, XL is not twice as large as L or even S. Shoe size and grade level in school are additional ordinals. Again, they are numbers, but they aren't related by any mathematical rules.

## Numeric or Quantitative

### Ratio Data

The first numeric data type is ratio, where the data is numbers and there is a mathematical relationship between the different values, as well as a natural zero point. Going back to the height and weight your doctor measured, these are both ratio. You can see the mathematical relationship: someone 78 inches tall is twice as tall as someone who is 39 inches tall. Although a real person would never have a height or weight of zero, we can what a zero height would mean, so we can say there is a natural zero. Other common ratio data is age, numeric grades on a test, and number of course credits completed.

### Interval Data

Interval data is the second numeric data type and has a lot in common with ratio data, with some limitations. Interval data doesn’t have a natural zero point, and the mathematical operations you can do on it are significantly limited because of this. For instance, temperature seems like it would be ratio because we have numbers and we can have a temperature of 0. But if you think about what on Fahrenheit and Celsius scales, those two values mean entirely different things, as a temperature of 0 doesn't mean there is zero heat, so you can't count it as a true zero. Because of this, you can't say 60 is twice as hot as 30, and summing temperatures would never make sense. It's interesting to note that unlike Fahrenheit and Celsius, temperatures on the Kelvin scale would work as ratio data because Kelvin means no heat, and the relationships between the different values follow mathematical rules.

IQ is another example of interval data, though at first glance it might seem like ratio data. There are different IQ scales that are arbitrary, unlike T-shirt sizes, which cannot be quantified. However, there is no meaningful 0, which means mathematical operations break down.
