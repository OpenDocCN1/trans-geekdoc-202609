Fig. 15a reveals that the high-rise buildings near the street formed a strong canyon effect [75], and the wind speed within the street was greatly increased. The AES velocity also significantly increased compared to the control group A and B. However, the increase in velocity in these two cases did not lead to an accelerated dispersion of pollutants into the block (Fig. 15b). This is due to the fact that the high-rise buildings facing the street formed a flow on the windward side just opposite to the dominant wind direction and stopped pollutants from entering the block further with the fluid. The comparative analysis of S4 and M2 shows that the AES area of case S4 was polluted to a greater extent than M2. Still, the static wind area within the block of M2 was relatively more significant, which might lead to uncomfortable pedestrian activities. The reason may be that row E on the street side of M2 was all occupied by buildings, making it difficult for clean air to flow through the building gap. Previous studies also illustrated that BHstd might significantly enhance the drag force produced by taller buildings and increase the momentum flux while reducing pollutant dilution capacity by mean flows but enhancing turbulence characteristics within urban areas [30,76]. Since both mean flows and turbulent diffusion contribute to pollutant removal in urban areas, thus BHstd may reduce or strengthen the ventilation capacity.

As can be seen in Fig. 16a, when BHstd = 0, pollutants in the street could go over the building roofs and tumble into the block (Control group A) or go through the first row of building gaps on the windward side of the block and enter the block (Control group B). When BHstd > 34 m and the windward building is a row of high-rises, it is difficult for pollutants to enter the block due to the enormous vortex formed on the upwind side of the buildings. Compared with the control groups A and B, pollutants in the two optimized cases did not accumulate within the street canyon but spread to both sides of the target block with the lateral 3D vortex. The results of the optimized cases show that pollutants entered the block through the gap between buildings, E–2 and E–3. Since the E–3 buildings are relatively low, pollutants can tumble into the AES from the roof. When the single-objective optimization focuses on Cped,Block, the block construction protects AES from pollutant accumulation and ignores the further diffusion of pollutants into the block's interior. Row E of M2 occupied more buildings compared to S4. It can be speculated that this is due to a more comprehensive focus on global and local indicators in this multi-objective optimization process.

The discussion of BHstd in this paper was still based on the wind direction perpendicular to the street canyon. However, some literature suggests that ambient wind directions of large angles (for example, 30 and 45) increase the drag of buildings and transfer more kinetic energy into turbulent kinetic energy than small angles. Therefore, large angles usually reduce the capacity of pollutant dispersion in 3D urban-like geometries [77]. Block forms that mitigate the impact of pollutants on the AES when the dominant wind direction of the angle with the street canyon is worth exploring in future work. Meanwhile, adjusting the size of the data set and tuning parameters to better couple with the optimization algorithm can further improve the prediction accuracy of ML models for pollutant concentrations. Other factors in urban design can also be added to the optimization objectives for discussion in further work, such as lighting, and thermal comfort, to conclude that a neighborhood form is more conducive to resident activity.

## 6. Conclusion

In this paper, a large number of block configuration cases with different BHstd and layouts were sampled and CFD simulated to obtain a dataset related to block form and traffic air pollutant dispersion. Based on the ML approach, this dataset generated surrogate models for pollutant concentration prediction. Through the conjugation of the ML model and optimization algorithm, various morphology strategies for reducing pollutant concentrations in AES and block were summarized in this study. - The standard deviation of building height (BHstd) in the block is above 33 m can effectively reduce the concentration of pollutants in AES. Optimization results show that taller buildings placed in areas adjacent to pollution sources and lower buildings set back is a reasonable strategy to reduce the diffusion of pollutants into the block. This strategy can reduce at least 48% of pollutant concentrations in the AES area and 86% of pollutants in the block compared to a homogenized building height layout.

- Building heights of 40-60 m on the street can prevent pollutants from entering the block. The taller the building in the downwind inside the block, the more cause pollutants accumulate. Therefore, building heights should be controlled below 60 m. Building height impacts the average pollutant concentration in the AES area more than in the block. When focusing on pollutant concentration levels in the AES area, it is still necessary to examine the building configuration of the entire block.

- Whether the optimization objective focuses on a global or local index can lead to different optimization strategies. The variation of the area of concern in the study of pollutant dispersion problems in the block can lead to distinctions in conclusions.

- ML can speed up the CFD simulation by at least 800 times. Using the workflow of CFD, ML, and optimization algorithms in this study, the multi-factor and multivariate decision problems in urban design can be solved efficiently, which can provide some references for urban planners and architects.
