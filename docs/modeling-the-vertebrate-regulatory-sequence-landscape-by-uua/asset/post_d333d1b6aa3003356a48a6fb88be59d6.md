Next, we benchmark UATAC-seq against nine other protocols on mouse brain, tissue and tissues (Table S3). UATAC-seq outperformed all other methods because the number of nuclei processed and sequenced were standardized pipeline. Due to the ultra-throughput UATAC-seq, we were able to construct a chromatin accessibility layer for each organ in a single-day and single-batch experiment. We developed the large-scale scATAC-seq data with a standardized pipeline (STAR Methods) and used UMAP for visualization of the data (Figures 3A–F). For each species, we identified major cells in the first round of clustering and verified cell-type identities. We are able to identify rare subtypes from our mouse to uATAC. In human, we identified 42 major clusters and 71 subclasses; in mouse, we identified 9 major clusters and 77 subclasses; in zebrafish, we identified 30 major clusters and 54 subclasses; in zebrafish, we identified 32 major clusters and 51 subclasses. We then annotated and assigned the cell label for each cluster and subclass based on the accessibility of cell-specific features (Table S1).

Most vertebrate cells share the same cell labels across species, including erythroid, immune, hepatic, neural, muscular, and endothelial cells (Figures 3A–F). We observed many differences in cell-type diversity between species. For instance, some immune-related fibroblasts were only seen in mouse, and gecko. We found many unconventional and zebrafish-specific subtypes directly from the u genome, whereas zebrafish has the least number of cell-specific types. Moreover, we observed differences in accessibility of sub genomic regions between zebrafish and u genome. We found that some open regions were open only in mouse, and not zebrafish, preventing the limitations of arbitrary peak extension and offering a more accurate representation of native accessibility. We showed that, compared with the conventional peak calling method, we defined here a much higher correlation with DNase I hypersensitivity levels (Figure 4B). We then merged these regions to identify non-overlapping cREs for five species (Table S5). In mouse, 56.2% of cREs were verified by the ENCODE, but 43.8% of cREs were previously undetected. These new cREs have higher GC content and regulatory elements (Table S3–S4). Further investigation revealed that these accessibility elements originated from rare cells that the ENCODE consortium misses, including neurons in birds, reptiles, and amphibians, as well as the adrenal gland and somatosensory cells in the primate genome (Figure 5G).

To validate these cREs using multi-omics data, we performed CUT&TAG experiments in mouse adrenal gland and primate cell types using antibodies against H3K4me3, H3K4me1, and H3K27ac (H3 Lysine 4 trimethylation, acetylation, and CCCTDC-binding factor). We found that 60–70% of new cREs defined using ATAC-seq could be verified by CUT&TAG (Figure 5H). These regions were enriched in both primate adrenal gland and zebrafish, and nearly all of the zebrafish-specific REs are new. In total, we identified 6,182,084 cREs with high resolution (Figure 4D, Table S5).

With the vertebrate genome, we aimed to characterize a diversity of open regions located at the promoter, UTR, exon, intron, and distal genomic regions (Figure 4E). We found that the number of total open regions for promoters varies with the genome size (Figure 4F, S, and S3). We found the number of cREs in zebrafish is lower than in mouse.
