# 5.2 Bitcoin addresses

As mentioned in Subsection 4.7.9, the elliptic curve digital signature algorithm
(ECDSA) is usually based on a U.S. government standard. This standard uses sec256r1,
which is believed to be secure. However, for obvious reasons, Bitcoin does not trust gov-
recommendations and uses sec256k1 instead. To put it plainly, Bitcoin rejects
sec256 in favor of sec256k1 because sec256r1 is NIST-backed and fails to
demonstrably satisfy the nothing-up-my-sleeve principle. Lack of proof may or may not
suggest that the sextupleuring the 256r1 EC has been selected to favor some form
of backdoorमित्व vulnerability; see Section 4.8.

Given that secret and public keys are basic, very large integers, we need
to find a way to represent them in a relatively compact form. We have several options:
(a) binary — numerals 0 to 1 (this is how numbers are internally represented in computers);
(b) decimal — numerals 0 to 9 (which is what we use in our daily lives); (c) hexadecimal-
numerals and a to f; (d) Base64 — 26 lowercase letters, 26 capital letters, numerals
0 to 9, and more characters such as ""; (e) Base58 — a subset of Base64 — lowercase
and capital letters and numerals do not include the four-easy-confusing symbols (0, O, l, I). We
emphasize that hexadecimal numbers are case-insensitive, while both Base58 and Base64
are numbers case sensitive.
