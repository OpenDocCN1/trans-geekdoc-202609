For the Student's $t$ distribution, $x \sim ST(\mu, \nu, \sigma)$, with $f_{ST}(x)$ and $\phi_{ST}(\chi)$ of the following form:

```
$f_{ST}(x) = \frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+1}{2})}{\sigma\sqrt{\nu\pi}\Gamma(\frac{\nu}{2})} (1 + \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{\nu\sigma^2})^{-(-(\nu+1)/2)} \quad (10.8)$
```

```
$$\phi_{ST}(\chi) = \frac{\sigma(\nu\sigma|\chi|)^{\nu/2}\exp(i\mu\chi)K_{\nu/2}(\nu\sigma|\chi|)}{2^{\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu)}$$
```

see Gosset (1908); Abramowitz and Stegun (1994); Johnson et al. (1995). The meaning of the parameters is as follows: $\mu$ — location, $\nu$ — degrees of freedom, and $\sigma$ — volatility. The t-distribution, being bell-shaped and symmetric regarding its mean, is similar to the normal distribution. An important distinction is that the t-distribution has heavy tails and produces more values from the mean than the normal. Hence it is suitable candidate for fitting BTC returns.

For the normal-inverse Gaussian distribution, $x \sim N\{IG}(\alpha, \beta, \delta, \mu)$, $f_{NIG}(x)$, $f_{NIG}(\chi)$ are as follows:

```
$f_{NIG}(x) = \frac{(y/\delta)^{-1/2}e^{\alpha\beta(x-\mu)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}y^{-1/2}} y^{-1}K_{-1}(\alpha) \quad (10.9)$
```

```
$$\alpha\delta e^{\gamma\delta+\beta x}/\pi y^{-1}K_{-1}(\alpha)$$
```

```
$$\phi_{NIG}(\chi) = \exp(\gamma\delta+i\mu\chi-\delta\nu)$$
```

where

```
$$\gamma = \sqrt{\alpha^2-\beta^2}, y = \sqrt{\delta^2+(x-\mu)^2}, \nu = \sqrt{|\delta^2+(i\chi)^2}, \quad (10.10)$$
```

see Aas and Hobaek Haff (2005); Barndorff-Nielsen (1997). Here K is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. The meaning of the corresponding parameters is as follows: $\alpha$ — tail heaviness, $\beta$ — asymmetry, $\delta$ — scale, and $\mu$ — location.

For the generalized hyperbolic distribution, $x \sim GH(\alpha, \beta, \delta, \lambda, \mu)$, the corresponding $f_{GH}(x)$ and $f_{GH}(\chi)$ can be written as follows:

$f_{GH}(x) = \frac{(\gamma/y)^{\alpha}e^{\beta(x-\mu)}y^{\lambda-1/2}K_{\lambda-1/2}(\gamma)}{\sqrt{2\pi}K_{\lambda}(\gamma)} \quad (10.11)$
```

```
$$\phi_{GH}(\chi) = \frac{\gamma^{\alpha}\exp(i\mu\chi)K_{\lambda}(\delta\nu)}{K_{\lambda}(\delta\nu)v^{\lambda}}$$

see Barndorff-Nielsen (1977). In addition to $\alpha, \beta, \delta, \mu$, we add the parameter $\lambda$, which determines the shape of the distribution. The generalized hyperbolic distributions are used when large deviations from the mean are essential, for example, to model financial instruments. It is the most general distribution we include in our analysis. The Student's $t$ and the normal-inverse Gaussian distributions belong to the class of generalized hyperbolic (GH) distributions. Unfortunately, the class of generalized hyperbolic distributions is not closed under a linear combination of its members, making it very difficult to aggregate daily returns to monthly or annual, see next section.

The hyperbolic distribution, which is a special case of $\mathcal{GH}$ with $\lambda = 1:$

```
$H(\alpha, \beta, \delta, \mu) = \mathcal{GH}(\alpha, \beta, \delta, 1, \mu),$
```

is used as a candidate for fitting BTC returns; see Barndorff-Nielsen (1977). However, our experience suggests that the corresponding fit is generally not good, so for brevity, we omit the corresponding calculations.

Straightforward comparison of the corresponding characteristic function confirms the well-known fact that the Normal and Cauchy distributions are special cases of the special distribution:

```
$\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) = S\left(2, 0, \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2}}, \mu\right),$
```

```
$C(\delta, \mu) = S(1, 0, \delta, \mu).$
```

The Student's $t$, and normal-inverse Gaussian are the special (or limit) cases of the generalized hyperbolic distribution:

```
$\mathcal{ST}(\mu, v, \sigma) = \mathcal{GH}(0, 0, \sqrt{v}\sigma, -\frac{v}{2}, \mu).$
```

```
$\mathcal{NIG}(\alpha, \beta, \delta, \mu) = \mathcal{GH}(\alpha, \beta, \delta, -\frac{1}{2}, \mu).$
```

The normal and Cauchy distributions can be obtained in the limit as well, although neither of these belong to the $\mathcal{GH}$ class. Each distribution is fitted by the classical method of maximum likelihood; e.g., Hamilton (2020) among numerous others. We introduce the penalty in $L(\Theta)$ as follows:

```
$ln L(\Theta) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} ln(x_n),$
```

maximize it with respect to $\Theta$, and choose the corresponding optimal value $\hat{\Theta}$. The confidence intervals for $\hat{\Theta}$ are calculated by approximating the covariance matrix by the inverse of the information matrix. Thus,

```
$cov(\hat{\Theta}) = (H(\hat{\Theta}))^{-1}$
```

where $H(\hat{\Theta})$ is the corresponding Hessian,

```
$H(\hat{\Theta}) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_1^2} & \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_1\partial\theta_2} & \dots & \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_1\partial\theta_k} \\ \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_2\partial\theta_1} & \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_2^2} & \dots & \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_2\partial\theta_k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_k\partial\theta_1} & \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_k\partial\theta_2} & \dots & \frac{\partial^2 L(\Theta)}{\partial\theta_k^2} \end{pmatrix} \quad (10.17)$
```

To account for the difference in the number of parameters in our choice of fitting candidates, we use several criteria to pick the winner:

- (1) The Akaike information criterion (AIC), as Akaike (1974):
$AIC = 2K - 2\ln L(\hat{\Theta}) \quad (10.18)$

The Akaike information criterion estimates the out-of-sample prediction error of different statistical models for a given set. One can increase the likelihood by adding additional parameters. For instance, the normal distribution has two parameters, $\mu, \sigma$, while the generalized hyperbolic distribution has five, $\alpha, \beta, \delta, \lambda$. However, introducing extra parameters increases the risk of overfitting the data.

- (2) The Hannan–Quinn information criterion (HQIC), as Hannan and Quinn (1979):
$HQIC = 2K\ln(N) - 2\ln L(\hat{\Theta}) \quad (10.19)$

- (3) The Schwarz information criterion (SIC), also known as Bayesian information criterion (BIC), as Schwarz (1978):
$SIC = K\ln N - 2\ln L(\hat{\Theta}) \quad (10.20)$

BIC and AIC are strictly close, however, the penalty term is larger in BIC than in both AIC and HQIC. For each of the above, the objective is to choose the fitting distribution producing the lowest value.

- (1) The Anderson–Darling test, as Anderson and Darling (1954):
$ADT = -N - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} (2n-1)\ln(\hat{F}(y_n)) + \ln(1-\hat{F}(y_{N+1-n})) \quad (10.21)$

where $y_1 \le \dots \le y_n \le \dots \le y_N$ are the observed returns arranged in increasing order. The Anderson–Darling test (AD test) estimates goodness of fit for a given probability distribution, with the parameters calculated via the maximum likelihood method.

(2) The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, see Kolmogorov (1933); Smirnov (1948):
$KST = \sup_{x} |F_N(x) - \hat{F}(x)| \quad (10.22)$

Where $F_N(x)$ is the empirical distribution, and $\hat{F}(x)$ is the maximum likelihood distribution,

```
$F_N(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \delta_{[-infty,x]}(x_n) \quad (10.23)$
```

$\delta_{[-infty,x]}()$ is the indicator function, equal to 1 if $x_n \le x$ and 0 otherwise. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (KS test) is a nonparametric test for comparing a data sample with a given probability distribution. As for the AD test, the parameters of this distribution are calculated via the maximum likelihood method.

The objective of optimization is to choose the fitting distribution, which minimizes metrics. Tables 10.2 and 10.3 summarize the calibration and maximization results for BTC returns.

The generalized hyperbolic distribution is a clearer winner with regards to all the criteria and tests. We show the empirical distribution fitting with parametric distributions in Figure 10.5.

This figure clearly shows that the normal distribution provides the least adequate fit to the empirical data. In contrast, the generalized hyperbolic distribution fits the data well, particularly as heavy tails are considered. Large deviations from the mean are more frequent for the actual returns than would have happened if they were normal. Figures 10.6 (a), b show the QQ plots for the fitted normal and generalized hyperbolic distribution over the kernel distribution.

An ideal fit should result in a straight line. Comparison of Figures 10.6 (a) and (b) shows that the generalized hyperbolic distribution produces an almost perfect QQ plot, while the normal distribution exhibits significant deviations. Assuming the fitted generalized-hyperbolic distribution adequately describes the price dynamics, we can calculate the Value-at-Risk (VAR) for both gains and losses, shown in Figure 10.7. While there is a discernible difference between the empirical and $GH$ VAR, it is relatively small. Moreover, VAR computations with $GH$ provide more conservative estimates for both long and short positions.
