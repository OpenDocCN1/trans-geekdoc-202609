## 8.6 Summary

Among potentially useful applications of distributed ledgers in finance, CBDCs and stablecoins are very promising avenue. If physical cash disappears, it is possible to imagine a future where everyone has access to central bank, albeit indirectly. In this scenario, retail banks may bifurcate into NBs and investment pools. Asset-backed cryptocurrencies can serve as a much-needed counterpart for fiat currencies and a very potent geopolitical tool.

The time comes for central banks and other regulators to open competition between slow-moving incumbent financial institutions and new newcomers, especially once fully exploiting new technologies, including distributed ledgers.

## 9.1 Introduction

There was a time when robbing assets necessarily involved physical force, gunpowder, and death. The Hollywood industry became expert at depicting all sorts of historical or fictional narratives around the clash of noble defenders of property rights and faithless criminals looking to acquire assets by any coercive means. From highwaymen being heroes of violence to impressive bank hold-ups, these depictions have the merit of emphasizing one painful truth about security: that of its imperfection.

One of the first learnings in the field of security is that a security framework should never pretend to be absolute. Rather, it is defined as a set of counter-measures against specific threat vectors. A well-designed framework models attacks that it aims to control but also classes of risks that it knowingly does address. Security is also a dynamic field: it is an evolving arms race between attackers, who will use ingenuity to come up with new ways of breaking defense mechanisms, and security engineers reinforcing the defense and widening the protection surface.

A simple look at the history of bank robberies provides an interesting material illustration to this principle. Bank vaults, which were designed to secure money, valuables, and documents against theft and destruction, have been notoriously fragile to all sorts of attacks. After thousands of years perfecting locking mechanisms — from the ancient Egypt lock to the Chinese combination lock one could have expected vaults to finally have reached an ultimate level of protection. However ever-practice revealed that the preferred attack of bank robbers during the 19th century Gold Rush was not to break the lock but to bypass it.

The Digital Revolution initiated in the late 1950s and amplified with global connectivity brought an entirely new playground for criminals: the human element. It became obvious that security models often had a weak point: the human risk. Although the Bitcoin network and other cryptocurrencies have generally been resilient to protocol-level attacks, there have been many extraordinary thefts since 2009, including some of the most prominent exchanges such as Mt Gox, Bitstamp, Bitfinex, or Binance (see Table 9.1).

The unavoidable trend strongly suggests that persistence and size of negative cyber shocks will further grow. The increasing diversity of perpetrators, from state and non-state actors, cyberterrorists, and hackivists, also shows that financial gain is no longer the only motivation. Intuitively, the financial sector is an obvious high risk industry. Information security management defines risk as a combination of consequences and likelihood (ISO, 2011), such that

> Risk = (Threat, Vulnerability, Consequence).

For a financial institution, threat level is high due to cybercrime, hacktivism, proxy organisation, surveillance of communication; dependence on highly interconnected networks implies high vulnerabilities; and the dematerialized activity means that cyber lead to big consequences; Kopp et al. (2017). Distributed ledger technologies (DLTs) and cryptocurrency service providers arguably are even more critical targets. The aggregate value of the cryptocurrency market being in the hundreds of billions at the time of writing, and its being fully available publicly, it is one of the biggest hot pots of the Internet. Its security foundations should be solid enough to withstand constant attacks on its infrastructure, its accounting ledger, and its users — attacks with the specific intent to steal funds, disrupt the service availability, or generate panic as the market manipulation technique or as an end-goal in itself.
