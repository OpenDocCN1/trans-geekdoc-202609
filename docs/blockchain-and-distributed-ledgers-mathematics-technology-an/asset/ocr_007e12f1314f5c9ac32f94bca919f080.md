The frustration of the general public with the status quo is palpable. This frustration manifests itself in various aspects of social and economic life.

This book argues that the seemingly unscathed opportunities to reshape the financial system are not all lost. More, we show that, if used deliberately, new technologies, including blockchains and distributed ledgers, can create new business models. New technologies will put pressure on the incumbents. More importantly, they will allow newly formed fintech companies to enter the market in earnest, providing considerable benefits to the general public.

One can expect a great deal of innovation from DLT. As newcomers understand banking and its role in society, and regulators allow competition between business models, we hope to see highly contested races. Specifically, we anticipate contests between central reserve banks versus narrow banks, digital cash vs physical cash, currencies versus asset-backed cryptocurrencies, and most importantly, centralized payment systems versus distributed payment systems. The outcome of these races is to reshape the entire financial ecosystem. In a few years, it might change recognition. DLT can have geometrical implications and a significant weapon in the inevitable struggle for supremacy between different central digital banks (CBDCs).

Besides, DLT will reshape other, less financially oriented aspects of the economy, such as accounting, biomedical research, supply management, and many others.

# 1.2 Distributed ledgers in a nutshell

Simply put, blockchain is a shared, distributed ledger designed to record transactions in a business network and track resulting changes in assets ownership; see, e.g., Gupta (2017). The corresponding assets are tangible (money, shares, real estate) and intangible (intellectual property, including patents, trademarks, copyrights, and brand recognition). In theory, ownership of anything value can be tracked and traded on a blockchain reducing risks by increasing business interoperability, and cutting costs for all involved by removing intermediaries. Besides, it might be possible to use blockchain to establish individuals’ and businesses’ digital identities and radically reorganize business modus operandi.

As shown in Figure 1.1, we can think about three complementary ways of organizing information: (a) centralized, (b) decentralized, (c) distributed. At present, a centralized system, also known as the hub and spoke model, is underpinning most industries, for example, banking in a single country, with the hub being the central, and spokes being individual commercial banks. A decentralized system with hubs and spokes is typical for multi-country arrangements, such as cross-border banking. Finally, a distributed system characterizes direct or peer-to-peer (P2P) business organization (a rare instance) and can potentially be viewed as the most robust of the three.
