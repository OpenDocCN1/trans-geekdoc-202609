## 3.5.2 Illustration in a decentralized setup

Let us now consider a similar situation in a decentralized setup such as Bitcoin. The account now holds 1 BTC on the Bitcoin ledger and he would like to buy both a red bike and a blue bike, each worth 1 BTC. Intuition suggests that the owner can only purchase either the red bike, or the blue bike, as the value of both goods is 2 BTC, which is larger than his total wealth. The owner aims to claim the secret and creates two transactions at the same time: the first transaction sending 1 BTC to pay for the red bike and the second transaction sending 1 BTC to pay for the blue bike. Details are shown in Figure 3.6.

The peer-to-peer broadcast of both transaction requests starts, and each network node notified of each transaction immediately broadcasts it to its own set of neighbor nodes. Following a cascade effect, one of the, or generally both transactions quickly become known to all participating nodes on the Internet. We emphasize here that the process is stochastic: the propagation path depends on the topology of the peer-to-peer network, which itself is dynamic and anarchic in nature, as well as network latencies between peers — which depend on the geographical distance, the quality, the quantity, and the throughput of the Internet transport relays — also the simple willingness of nodes to play their relay nodes indiscriminately. Because there is no deterministic way to guarantee that all nodes see the red bike transaction first, or alternatively the blue bike transaction first, the nodes cannot jointly agree about which transaction to consider valid and which one. In fact, the original sender may well attempt to cheat the system by sending the red bike transaction to a specific partition of the network while sending the blue bike transaction to another partition of the network, such that different groups of nodes perceive the red and blue transactions in a different order.

As there is no natural ordering of transactions, there is no network-wide agreement on which of the two transactions should be considered valid and which one should be rejected. One cannot trust a timestamp that the sender would attach to its transactions given there would be no way to enforce the validity of the timestamp. The sender may as well send two transactions with the same timestamp, or create multiple copies of the same transaction with different timestamps and broadcast them at different moments, trying to revert previous transactions. The consequence of this incapacity to decide which conflicting transaction to keep and which transactions to block is commonly called the double spending. The reason is that if two bike merchants receive a Bitcoin transaction, one for the red, one for the blue, they would both think that they have received the funds it may very well be that another transaction clashes with theirs — and possibly that the balance of the sender account is not sufficient to cover both purchases.

## 3.6 Network consensus

It is obvious that a distributed ledger needs a robust solution against double spending, or in other words, it must embed a consensus algorithm allowing each network participant to reach high certainty that their view on which transaction keep is shared by the rest of the network. A consensus algorithm is precisely about agreeing on a decision when there is more than one node involved, and making sure that the agreement is robust to kinds of failures or attacks. In the case of double spending, the consensus aims to promote a decision about which transaction to keep and to disregard. The consensus does not guarantee that the earliest transaction be kept; it merely drives the general agreement around one that the "network" agrees. Many different consensus algorithms may be considered for the problem at hand, although the most will depend on the topology of the network (e.g., how many nodes) and the degree of trust between participants. ## 3.6.1 Sybil attacks

Consider a simple model where a majority of network participants are required to approve a transaction. Under this model, one waits that a majority is reached concluding that a transaction is final. A careful analysis quickly reveals that it would not hold. In a permissionless setup, one cannot enforce a fair allocation of voting rights. Can the system guarantee that a network participant only once cannot artificially inflate its presence at will? This is called Sybil attack: a network participant attacks the system by creating an arbitrary number of pseudonymous identities and uses them to increase his influence on the outcome, as demonstrated in Figure 3.7. Related to this, one also concludes that nothing prevents a voter from voting in favor of incompatible outcomes. Why would voters only vote on one single transaction in a single scenario? Again, because there is no cost to voting, the assumption should always be that some malevolent network participants will exploit the weakness and create distrust in the system.

An alternative model would be to elect a leader of the network participants and grant him the power to decide unilaterally on the transactions to approve or reject. In a distributed setup, becoming the leader should be achievable by any participant and should not be a permanent position in order to avoid centralization of the decision-making, and therefore the whole system. This approach only moves the needle to another challenge, that of the leader-election process. Selecting a leader is no easier than voting in favor of a specific transaction: one is still subject to Sybil attacks. Consensus algorithm tackles the above challenge precisely. It establishes a new consensus for permissionless distributed ledgers, which makes Sybil attacks expensive and collaborative behaviors rewarding in order to set up a proper incentive system where network participants have a lot to gain by behaving properly, and a lot to lose by behaving improperly. This is the so-called Proof-of-Work (PoW) algorithm.

## 3.6.2 Digital work with proof-of-work

In a context where every network participant can inflate his presence at will — for instance multiplying his Internet connections — and where participants have no reason to trust each other, how can one set up a process under which an network-wide agreement may be reached? Should there be a leader taking decisions, and if so, who the participants agree upon whom this leader should be? Be a leaderless democracy model and if so, how to ensure that every participant only votes once?

It seems that Sybil attacks prevent any form of democratic model because of the inherent distrust between anonymous participants and their capacity to abuse a voting scheme at low cost and no penalty. To vote, Bitcoin must limit the ability of participants to inflate their power arbitrarily. Bitcoin must set up a framework where each and any participant may contribute, but voting is a costly enterprise. It must create an incentive system encouraging users to behave well and penalizing dissidents for misbehaving. Bitcoin must create an identity-less democratic system that does not allow fraud.

Satoshi Nakamoto sets the Bitcoin protocol so that the validation of a transaction block is conditioned on the preliminary resolution of a computationally expensive work. The protocol guarantees that only blocks with a valid proof of resolution to the problem — or proof-of-work — are accepted as valid blocks; blocks without a valid proof-of-work are rejected by the network nodes. Satoshi properly designs the problem such that finding a solution is a computationally expensive, random challenge, while verifying the estimated amount of work invested by the solver of the problem. This approach still allows any participant to submit a new block of transactions to the network as long as this block contains a valid proof-of-work and therefore demonstrates to any observer that the work was achieved by the submitter — work which directly translates into an investment in time and money. The specifics of this work are left to Chapter 5.
