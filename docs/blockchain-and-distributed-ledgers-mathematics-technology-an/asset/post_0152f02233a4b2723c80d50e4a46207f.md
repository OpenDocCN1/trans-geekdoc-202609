Transfers contain the following information:

- (1) nonce — for anti-replay;
- (2) sender’s address (implicit);
- (3) recipient’s address;
- (4) signature of the sender;
- (5) amount of ETH to transfer;
- (6) STARTGAS value, which determines the maximum number of computational steps the originator of the transaction is willing to pay for;
- (7) GASPRICE value representing the actual fee per computational step the sender is willing/able to pay;
- (8) data field to specify an optimal smart contract method to call and its parameters.

The last two steps are crucial anti-denial of service measures and represent a unique feature of the Ethereum protocol. Gas pays for miners. Simply, gas is a unit measuring the computational effort required to perform operations and data store. Every successfully executed operation in Ethereum, be an elementary ETH transfer or a sophisticated SC, requires gas. Miners are paid in ETH equal to the total amount of gas needed for executing a request times the gas agreed upon by the transaction’s initiator. A typical Ethereum transaction affecting only Alice’s and Bob’s addresses is shown in Table 6.3.

The evolution of the Ethereum system caused by such a transaction is shown in Figure 6.2.
The amount of gas needed to support a simple fund is 21,000 gas. If Alice indicates her willingness to pay STARTGAS ≥21,000 gas; miners will process her transaction, of course, Alice has the requisite funds. If she only agrees to pay STARTGAS <21,000 gas for the transaction, the gas will be spent, but the transaction will not be completed and will not change the state of the Ethereum ecosystem. On the other hand, gas equal to the difference between STARTGAS and actual gas consumption will return to Alice. Therefore, there is a built-incentive to share a relatively high value of STARTGAS. However, if it becomes too high, the miners will not process Alice’s transaction to avoid clogging their blocks.

The price of gas, GASPRICE, is a very different story. If Alice indicates her price per unit of gas lower than the market, the miners may not process her transaction. If on the other hand, she may pay the price higher than the market, the miners will process her transaction, consuming the needed amount of gas (such as 21,000 for the standard transaction) at Alice's (rather than market) price.

In summary, one can include a relatively high STARTGAS number and expect the excess back, indicating a low GASPRICE will result in not being executed at all, causing necessarily irreversible losses. however, like everything else in the Ethereum protocol, the situation is highly nuanced — showing a very high STARTGAS will make the transaction unattractive to miners since the blocks they form have a total gas limit, which currently stands at 10 million gas. Thus, a standard transaction showing 100,000 STARTGAS will prevent other transactions (and their associated fees) from being included in the block. Driven by their inherent self-interest, miners will reject it. A simple analogy is in place: Putting sufficient fuel in an airplane is must for it to reach the intended destination. However, putting too much will make the plane so heavy that it will not take off in the first place.

## 6.7 Consensus

As in Bitcoin, Ethereum miners, supporting consensus in the Ethereum protocol, tend to coalesce in mining pools. These pools are colossal so that Ethereum's mining is not sufficiently decentralized as claimed; see Figure 6.6.

Given that Ethereum's block generation rate is approximately 40 times higher than in Bitcoin (a block every 15 seconds vs. 10 minutes), the former protocol routinely creates more orphan blocks than the latter one. Recall that an orphan block is a valid block, not included in the blockchain, in favor of a different block mined the same time. While the Bitcoin protocol discards orphan blocks so that miners' efforts are wasted and viewed as the cost of doing business, see Chapter 5, the Ethereum protocol cannot afford such extravagance.

Ethereum elevates orphan blocks to the status of uncles and rewards them with partial mining rewards. The rationale behind this approach is: First, frequent emergence of uncle blocks is a natural outcome of shorter block times comparable with the network lag, hence the protocol needs to adapt and find a meaningful remedy.
