## 9.7 Key isolation and transaction approval

### 9.7.1 Security integrands

Overall, the security of a wallet heavily depends on two factors:

- (1) the protection of its keys;
- (2) the protection of its transaction signature process.

Even when the keys are well protected, the process under which the system may be authorized to use the secret key to sign a transaction can become the weak point of a system. An HSM, for instance, provides strong guarantees over the confidentiality of keys; however, HSMs often expose an interface to which external service connects to ask for a signature. Even assuming that the key is well protected in the HSM, the interface becomes an attack vector: the attacker may attempt to access this interface and ask the HSM to sign an arbitrary transaction. Therefore, such an interface must be well guarded, and must enforce good controls and process to alleviate the risk of a single point of failure.

### 9.7.2 Hot and cold storage

We define two broad classes of wallets often called hot wallets and cold wallets.

- (1) Hot wallet: a hot wallet is a wallet whose secret keys are manipulated on a device that is, or may be, connected to a network (generally the Internet). Hot wallets are typically implemented for applications where access to the funds must be fast, and inexpensive. Because the keys are exposed to attackers, the attack surface is large and such wallets are generally used when high availability or automation is required.

- (2) Cold wallet: a wallet is a wallet whose secret keys are under no circumstance stored or managed on a device that may be connected to a network. The keys are physically isolated, they are immune against remote attacks and may be attacked with physical access to the infrastructure. Cold wallets are typically implemented for applications where security is critical (e.g., when assets under management have significant value) and where the access time and cost to the funds is a serious concern.

Naturally, specific implementations of hot wallets may embed robust protocols mitigating the large attack surface. Equivalently, inconsiderate implementations of cold wallets may reveal critical weaknesses trumping their apparent resilience. A solid wallet implementation should model specific thread scenarios to maximize security under specific availability requirements of the wallet use case.

In practice, most cryptocurrency exchanges store 90% or more of their assets in cold storage; the rest of the assets are distributed in hot wallets to facilitate the processing of client withdrawal requests. The Binance hack has only affected hot wallets at the exchange; assets stored in cold storage were unaffected.

### 9.7.3 Four-eyes principle

In order to mitigate the risk of centralized trust, one often defines a transaction approval workflow where multiple independent parties operate the operation. At minimum, a four-eyes principle is enforced: a submitted operation must gather the validation of two users before its execution. By extension, more schemes may be defined, such as a so-called m-by-n multi-approval scheme where m parties must approve the operation out of a total of n potential approvers. This scheme generalizes the four-eyes principle to an m-eyes principle, and also defines the total number of users authorized to participate in the quorum.

In addition, the approval workflow is often conditioned to the risk of the transaction to be approved: the transaction amount, the destination of the transaction, its fees, the time of the day, etc may be factors impacting the workflow as defined by the custodian.

Approval workflows may be implemented on-chain, that is, directly on a distributed ledger, or off-chain, that is, in a traditional secret lying on top of a distributed ledger. We discuss the options below.

### 9.7.5 On-chain validation

Most ledger protocols allow the definition of accounts controlled by more than one party, as illustrated in Figure 9.12. One can implement transaction approval workflows requiring on-chain validation of multiple signatures, as an m-by-n scheme. The approach has been common in the Bitcoin protocol, which natively supports multi-signature scripts; it can also be trivially implemented in Ethereum via smart contracts.

On the flip side, on-chain validation suffers from drawbacks:

- (1) it makes the approval process visible to the blockchain observers
- (2) it increases transaction fees depending on the complexity of the workflow
- (3) it is not guaranteed by all ledger protocols

### 9.7.6 Multi-party computation

Multi-party computation (MPC) algorithms (see, e.g., Lindell and Pinkas (2007); Lindell et al. (2015); Archer et al. (2018)) provide a powerful tool for validation off-chain.

Multi-party computation is capable of maintaining key shards in different locations that jointly compute a signature without ever reconstructing the key. From the distributed ledger point of view, a single key is used; from the user point of view, a m-by-n scheme may be implemented such that less than m fragments have recovered cannot be reconstructed.

Although elegant, this model imposes more communication between the signing nodes and can be computationally expensive. Promoters of MPC technology in this industry argue that the distribution of shards removes the need for hardware environments, although the argument is debated.
