## 3.7. Initial coin offerings

The Initial Coin Offering (ICO) craze started in 2016. The ICO terminology is coined after the more traditional Initial Public Offering (IPO) related to the listing of a company on an exchange. IPOs have historically been limited to large companies deciding to move away from a private shareholder to a public listing where their shares are traded at a market-defined value. Due to the complexity of existing exchange platforms, and the various regulatory and reporting requirements, IPOs have notoriously been inaccessible to earlier stage companies looking to raise capital or to improve the liquidity of their stocks—whether for cost reasons, or for lack of profitability. ICOs aimed to resolve that and a crowdfunding platform for early stage and later stage ventures also allowing the early listing of shares, or tokens.

A series of early-stage companies prepared white papers and business plans to support the promotion of crowdfunding based on ICO. In practice, the issuing company distributes new tokens in exchange for established cryptocurrencies. The tokens may be denominated in a new cryptocurrency packaged with its own distributed ledger, or may leverage a token protocol on an existing ledger, such as ERC-20 contracts on Ethereum. This is not unlike the foundation event of a traditional company where initial investors get a share of the capital depending on the liquidity they bring into the company. However as compared to company shares, ICOs are generally open to any investor irrespective of their geographical location and the size of their investment; also, once the tokens are freely exchangeable peer-to-peer and can often be immediately traded on secondary markets (through centralized exchanges or decentralized trading). As compared to publicly traded companies, tokens can be issued and held with no intermediary and are highly interoperable.

After the fast-growing success of ICOs in 2016 and 2017 and their many failures — Ponzi schemes, exit cams, and other schemes defrauding their investors — regulators in most jurisdictions banned ICOs or at least the regulatory processes over their implementation. Regulations being antithetical to the even principle of decentralized finance, the attractiveness of ICOs as they were implemented in 2016 and 2017 massively decreased. In January 2018, the total market capitalization of cryptocurrencies and tokens reached its all-time high of US$831 billion before collapsing by more than 85%.

### 3.7.7 Decentralized finance and autonomous organizations

In parallel to the growth of cryptocurrency and tokens, the end of the decay was marked by the increasing importance of a broader ambition to decentralize finance and commerce as a whole. Rather than relying on centrally-regulated, "real-world" companies, a new notion of decentralized autonomous organization, DAO, gained traction. DAOs are the decentralized equivalent to companies or organizations; they rely on smart contracts and are tokenized on a distributed ledger such that "shareholders" are anonymous token holders. Rather than relying on articles of association, shareholders' agreements, the local law, or other complex legal documentation, the governance of DAOs, as well as its profits, can be arbitrarily defined within the contract with no relation to the laws and habits of regulated companies. Because of transparency and immutability, DAOs are inherently robust to many forms of risks (e.g., legal, jurisdiction) and by removing intermediaries as commercial registers or banks, they provide a highly efficient vehicle for international, self-enforced, anonymous organizations.

DeFi, for decentralized finance, has been the hottest in 2020 with a complete financial system being replicated by little in a fully decentralized way such that it does not depend on central banks, commercial banks, payment processors, governments, or regulators. From stablecoins providing a stable token indexed to some currency or basket of national currencies (see Chapter 8), to peer-to-peer exchanges, decentralized products, insurances, prediction markets and gambling contracts, decentralized orders, lending tools, DeFi is growing massively and over the perspective of a fully disintermediated, alternative financial system where established financial institutions lose their dominant position. Whether DeFi is yet another bubble ready to pop, whether it is the future of finance or a short-term experiment, one thing is sure: blockchain technology is far from reaching its full potential and we are prone to discover exciting cases that today are still invisible.
