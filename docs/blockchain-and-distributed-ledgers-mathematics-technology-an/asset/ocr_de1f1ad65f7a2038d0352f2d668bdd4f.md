The Digital Revolution initiated in the late 1950s and amplified with the global connectivity by the Internet brought an entirely new playground for criminals and redefined security best practices as well as the universe of threat vectors and their defense mechanisms. It became obvious to criminals that risking their life to be favored exchanged for the seemingly free risk, yet often rewarding, extraction or tampering of digital data. Cyber criminals did not disappear: in 2017, more than half of chief executive officers (CEOs) experienced cybersecurity and data breaches to threaten stakeholder trust in their industries over the last five years. The average per-record-compromised of data breaches reaches all-time highs every year, costing US$4 million in 2020 with a 60% increase over the last decade; Poonemon (2017).

The unavoidable trend strongly suggests that persistence and size of negative cyber shocks will further grow in the future. The increasing diversity of perpetrators, from state and non-state actors, cyberterrorists, and hackivists, also shows that financial gain is no longer the only motivation. Intuitively, the financial sector is an obvious high risk industry. Information security management defines risk as a combination of consequences and likelihood (ISO, 2011), such that

> Risk = (Threat, Vulnerability, Consequence).

For a financial institution, threat level is high due to cybercrime, hacktivism, proxy organisation, surveillance of communication; dependence on highly interconnected networks implies high vulnerabilities; and the dematerialized activity means that cyber lead to big consequences; Kopp et al. (2017). Distributed ledger technologies (DLTs) and cryptocurrency service providers arguably are even more critical targets. The aggregate value of the cryptocurrency market being in the hundreds of billions at the time of writing, and its being fully available publicly, it is one of the biggest hot pots of the Internet. Its security foundations should be solid enough to withstand constant attacks on its infrastructure, its accounting ledger, and its users — attacks with the specific intent to steal funds, disrupt the service availability, or generate panic as the market manipulation technique or as an end-goal in itself. Although the Bitcoin network and the other cryptocurrencies have generally been resilient to protocol-level attacks, there have been many extraordinary thefts since 2009, including some of the most prominent exchanges such as Mt Gox, Bitstamp, Bitfinex, or Binance (see Table 9.1).
