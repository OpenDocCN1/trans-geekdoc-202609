# 4.5.4 RSA encryption-decryption algorithm

To start with, we show how the encryption-decryption works in the RSA framework. Denote the message and the ciphertext as $m$ and $c$, respectively. As was mentioned earlier, we assume that $m, c \in \mathbb{F}_n$. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

+   (1) Bob chooses secret primes $p$ and $q$, and sets $n = pq$;n(2) Bob computes $c$ with $gcd(e, \phi(n)) = 1$;n(3) Bob computes $d$ so that $de = 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}$;
(4) Bob makes $n$ and public keeps $p, q,$ and $d$ secret;
(5) Alice encrypts $m$ as $c = m^e \pmod n$;n(6) Alice sends $c$ to Bob;
(7) Bob computes $m = c^d$ and recovers Alice's message $m$.

For RSA work, we choose secret numbers $p$ and $q$ to be prime. How to test a given number $n$ for being prime? Use Fermat's theorem. Take $A \neq 0, \pm 1 \pmod n$. If $n$ is composite; otherwise, it is prime with high probability (but not for sure!). Repeat for many $A$ to increase the likelihood of $p, q$ being prime.

$A^{n-1} \neq 1 \pmod n, \quad (4.47)$

Somewhat ironically, RSA is occasionally used to transmit symmetric keys for AES or DES encryption.

# 4.5.5 RSA digital signature algorithm

We summarize the digital signature algorithm, along the lines of the original RSA paper:

+   (1) Alice chooses secret primes $p$ and $q$, and sets $n = pq$;n(2) Alice chooses $e$ with $gcd(e, \phi(n)) = 1$;n(3) Alice computes $d$ so that $de = 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}$;
(4) Alice makes $n$ and public keeps $p, q,$ and $d$ secret;
(5) Alice signs $m$ as $s = m^d \pmod n$;n(6) Alice sends $s$ and $m \pmod n$;n(7) Bob computes $m' = s^e$ and compares it with $m$;

If $m' = m$, then Alice has signed the message properly.
