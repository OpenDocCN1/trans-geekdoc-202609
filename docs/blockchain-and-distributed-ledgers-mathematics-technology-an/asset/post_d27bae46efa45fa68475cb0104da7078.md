## 8.3 Central banks as issuers of CBDC

### 8.3.1 Central banks as issuers of CBDC

Experience shows that central banks routinely open accounts for licensed banks and selected non-banking financial firms, such as central clearing counterparties. However, they do not allow a broader range of corporate or individual participants (particularly if they wish to be anonymous) to have an account with them. There are several reasons for that. For instance, central banks are too small to solve the Know Your Customer (KYC) and Anti-Money Laundering (AML) problem independently; they don’t want to face potential political complications in agreeing or denying an account.

### 8.3.2 Narrow banks as issuers of CBDC

Keeping a one-to-one parity with the fiat currency and digital tokens is possible only if the equal amount of fiat currency is held in escrow; however, the collateral in a fractional-reserve bank cannot be safe at all times. Since bank depositors are junior creditors of a bank, their deposits will be in danger in case of default. Even if the recovery of a portion of these deposits is possible, the money will not be available until resolving the bankruptcy issues, which is a very lengthy process.

Thus, an NB is a natural emitter of CBDC. A properly constructed NB issuing its digital currency offers a much more palatable and practical solution than CBDC by allowing access to full collateral at any time and providing necessary KYC and AML checks. This approach suggests that digital currencies issued by NBs could become competitive with fiat currencies for everyday use. Such alternative currencies have a long history of smoothing bumps in the economic environment. If backed by an NB, they could avoid the bankruptcy problems that have traditionally plagued alternative currencies.

### 8.3.3 Technical considerations

One can think about CBDC in various ways. One possibility is to follow Nakamoto's idea and use it on a blockchain. However, due to various limitations inherent in the blockchain itself, it is not suitable for the large scale heavy-duty applications. The old Chaum's blind signature discovery, described in Section 4.5.6, might be more useful and come to the rescue. Rather than using proof-of-work (PoW) or other mechanisms for achieving consensus, one can avoid double spending by using a single-unique number per unit of currency issued by the central bank itself. Recall that paper banknotes have unique numbers already; in a decentralized fashion by a set of validators (or notaries), each knowing only the overall number. In this way, validators can secure multi-party computations to check the numbers' database and address the double spend problem in a Byzantine fault-tolerant way.

Major technical specifications for CBDC have to amalgamate the original Nakamoto and Ohta ideas and some additional considerations articulated by the Bank of England; see Okamoto and Ohta (1991); Bank of England (2020). We summarize these requirements in Table 8.1.

## 8.4 Stablecoins

### 8.4.1 Background

Since none of the existing blockchain ecosystems have banks worthy of the name, they have to rely on algorithmic monetary policies for cryptocurrency creation. Regardless of their creators' statements, they resurrect, in one way or the other, old ideas of central planning, without fully recognizing the consequences. More important and counterintuitive is that emerging DLT-based crypto suffer from the same drawbacks as the current banking system because they continue commingling payments and monetary creation (both primitive and ill-advised).

Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Ripple have tried to play the role of money but have ended up as a new speculative asset class. Despite their impressive technical achievements, existing crypto cannot replace money for at least four reasons. First, they are not stable enough, even for short transaction times, which prevents their use for commerce. Recent evidence shows that all decentralized crypto coins exhibit extreme volatility and thus are unsuitable for use as a de facto medium of exchange. Second, cryptocurrencies are operationally inconvenient for an average economic agent, which is a prerequisite for their wide adoption as a means of payment. Realistically, for such an agent, the only way to use cryptocurrencies is to buy them on a centralized exchange, which is both costly, inconvenient, and adds rather than removes an extra intermediary. Third, as already mentioned, most crypto protocols' built-in monetary policies are simple and inflexible. Some observers suggest this as an advantage, see Chapter 3. However, historical experience suggests and shows that having a fixed supply of money results in numerous economic distortions, of which are hard to anticipate in advance. Fourth, as explained in Chapters 5–7, cryptocurrencies are not truly decentralized while pretending to be so.

Not surprisingly, the status quo in the cryptocurrency land is less than satisfactory. As a result, developers and entrepreneurs directed much attention to designing stable crypto coins, which can play the role of tokenized cash and be viewed as private versions of CBDCs. Stablecoins come in several flavors. We give a brief discussion of the most visible types.

An adequately designed stablecoin can serve as the cornerstone of a fully digital financial ecosystem, facilitating fast payments on a commercial scale with minimal reliance on the existing banking system, yet doing so in a regulatory-compliant fashion. According to Sila (2018) and Hadorno et al. (2019a), such a coin addresses the gap left in the open access Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and the Internet Protocol (IP), also known as TCP/IP, namely the lack of specifications for money.

It is easy to see why stablecoins are necessary within the cryptocurrency universe. The vast majority of transactions occur on exchanges not fully integrated with the existing banking system. Accordingly, having a stable token to quickly and cheaply get in and out cryptocurrencies is necessary. Thus, entrepreneurs created tokens to link the crypto universe and the existing banking system.

¹An aphorism by Viktor Chernomyrdin (1938–2010), a former Prime Minister of Russia, jumps to mind. "We wanted it as good as possible, but it turned out as always."

We argue rather than designing monetary policy independently, which is a challenging task in both practice and theory, efficient applications of DLT should tokenize stable financial instruments. These instruments could be denominated in fiat currencies, for instance, the US dollars. Later on, if these efforts are successful, one should tokenize more diverse asset pools. Such pools can counterbalance fiat currencies and be very useful for cross-border transfers.

In our estimation, and contrary to numerous unfunded claims, it is not currently possible to build a genuinely decentralized blockchain. This observation is partly because the determination of a crypto's price in terms of a fiat currency requires an oracle and automatically breaks decentralization. Such oracles are prone to manipulation, and more importantly, at odds with the coin's claimed nature. Ideally, one should be able to directly observe this price from the blockchain in a distributed fashion. Unfortunately, at present, it is not quite possible for both conceptual and technical reasons. Hence, any potentially successful stablecoin combine centralized and decentralized features. The degree of centralization vs. decentralization can vary, as explained below.

### 8.4.2 Stablecoins fully collateralized with fiat

Nicolas Copernicus, who, in addition to his mathematical and astronomical studies, consulted the Prussian Diet on monetary issues, articulated his requirements for sound money as follows; Taylor (1995): 1. It must not be changed in value after right deliberation 2. One single coin must be chosen for the minting of the money, which must be in the name of the currency and not in the name of a single city.... 3. When a new currency is issued, the old currency must be demonetized and withdrawn from circulation.... 4. It is essential to have an inviolable and unchange rule to mint only 20 marks and no more from a pool of silver, deduct the quantity of silver necessary to cover the expenses of coinage.... 5. Too great a quantity of money must not be issued.... 6. All the kinds of coins should be issued at the same time....

It is truly astonishing to realise what extent of Copernicus's laws are valid today. For instance, the designers of the proposed cryptocurrency Libra miss the third postulate entirely. They do not understand and refuse to accept that Libra can create enormous inflationary pressures, especially in the emerging markets that it is supposed to help in the first place. However, such cavalier approach to creating new currencies is quite typical for most technologically innovative but financially naive.

Custodial stablecoins are relatively centralized. A single party performs the creation and destruction of such coins. However, once a coin is created, it can freely move on the corresponding blockchain before being eventually destroyed. This semi-centralized design, custodial coins are particularly prone to rogue influences, and, therefore, must be regulatory to survive. Several coins of this nature, including Tether (USDT), TrueUSD (TUSD), USD Coin (USDC), and Sila, already exist; several others are currently on the drawing board.
