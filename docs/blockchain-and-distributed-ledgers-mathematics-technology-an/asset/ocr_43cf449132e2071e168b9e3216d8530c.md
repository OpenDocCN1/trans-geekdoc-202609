Generally more, a soft fork is a retro-compatible change in the protocol. Segwit, which brought new capabilities, for instance, reinterpreted the meaning of some script operators, such that for updated nodes, they would have a new meaning described in the spec of Segwit, but also in a way that, nodes would not update, will still be able to process Segwit transactions even without knowing “what they do.” The trick is often of the form of making a change that looks like:

+   (1) if interpreted on the updated nodes, it'll have the expected behavior of the update;
(2) if interpreted on the old nodes, it'll be perceived as a trivially valid operation not worth investigating further.

For instance, a Segwit transaction may be a “trivially approved script” for all nodes but a “not trivially approved at all” for new nodes. Thus, old nodes don’t complain that these transactions exist (but they also cannot validate their security).

Figure 5.32 (b) illustrates hard forks, which are much more critical than the protocol as a whole. This figure happens when miners and full nodes cannot agree among themselves and decide to part while maintain their distinct versions of the blockchain, even though both versions share the same history until the time of split. For instance, some miners wish to increase the block’s size to improve the protocol’s throughput capabilities, others oppose the increase because they worry about its decentralization. We emphasize that hard forks are not just miners. They are full nodes. If substantial number of full nodes decline, a hard fork occurs.

The Bitcoin protocol underwent hard forks, including Bitcoin SV (Satoshi Vision), Bitcoin Cash, Bitcoin Core, Bitcoin Gold, to mention the most important ones. Thus, Bitcoin aficionados’ mantra — “There will only be 21 million Bitcoins,” does not hold in practice.

At a given moment $T_M$, we can think about the Bitcoin blockchain as being in a certain state $S_M$, which is characterized by a set of $N_M$ addresses $Addr_{M,1},...,Addr_{M,N_M}$ which contain UTXO $UTXO_{M,1},UTXO_{M,N_M}$. The most recent state is due to the addition of the winning block causes all the relevant changes in the state of the blockchain. As discussed above, a block remains unknown until it is buried underneath new blocks (typically, six).
