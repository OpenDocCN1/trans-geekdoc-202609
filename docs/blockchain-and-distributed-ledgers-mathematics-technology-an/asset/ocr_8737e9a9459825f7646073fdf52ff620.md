# 1.3 A retrospective view

The idea of transferring valuable objects and property rights via chains using some form of consensus is nothing new. For example, genealogical trees of royal and aristocratic families can be thought of as (block)chains, since they transfer rights according to a more or less strict set of rules from one to the next; see Lipton (2018). Figure 1.7 shows two representative family trees for the houses of Capet-Valois and Hapsburg.

One needs to have an excellent command of genealogy and heraldry to correctly interpret the unbelievably detailed information encoded in these family trees. However, one feature is beyond dispute — like Bitcoin transactions (see below) — the ownership is transferred from one owner to the next according to the predetermined rules. These transfers have be accepted by a group of peers who enforce the rules and validated by distributed parties, including the country's authorities, where the transfer takes place, and other royal courts.

What happens when things go wrong? If parties cannot agree upon a transaction, a hard fork might occur, much like it does in blockchains, when interested parties agree on what to do (a group). The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) is a case in point. It was a conflict between the House of Plantagenet, rulers of the Kingdom of England, and the House of Valois, rulers of the Kingdom of France. The prize was contested legitimacy. a typical example of the hard fork — in France, the House of Valois was legitimate, but in England (and subsequently Britain) — the House of Plantagenet and its offspring. Another example is the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748) — a conflict that involved most of Europe's powers to settle the question of Pragmatic Sanction and decide Maria Theresa's succession to the realms of the House of Hapsburg. Before us, the consensus was achieved by the force of arms.

The transition of power in a democracy is another example of a chain-like arrangement, with consensus achieved by tallying the votes. Unfortunately, it is not always smooth; failure to reach consensus in social upheavals.

To move from politics to economics, we present another example of blockchains — land title deeds in England, which have been in continuous use since the Middle Ages. According to the UK government: “Title deeds are paper documents showing the chain of ownership for land and property. They can include: conveyances, contracts for sale, wills, mortgages and leases."

In many instances, these chains are long very, since they can be traced to medieval times. It is clear that titles are blockchains; instead of miners, succession is verified by notaries; and held in the central repository. Titles are meaningful candidates for being treated on a distributed ledger; we discuss further in Chapter 1.
