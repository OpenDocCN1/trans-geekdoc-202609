Assume Charlie knows Alice is prepared to sign a document $d'$ and claim Alice signed it. For instance, instead of "Pay to the order of Bob", he signed the document to read "Pay to the order of Charlie". merely changing Bob's name to Charlie's would not do the trick because the hash of the corresponding documents would be completely different from the hash function collision resistance. However, Charlie can proceed as follows:

Charlie takes the genuine contract $d$ and produces small variations by adding spaces at the end, changing the wording slightly, or making other innocuous changes. Charlie takes the fraudulent contract $d'$ and produces small changes in it. $K$ will vary with $2^K$ different documents, $d_1, \dots, d_K$, and similarly, $2^K$ different documents $d'_1, \dots, d'_K$. He now attempts to find a match for the hash of $h(d_k)$ and $h(d'_l)$, for any arbitrary $(k, l)$, $1 \le k, l \le 2^K$. If there is a match $h(d_k)=h(d'_l)$, the signature will be valid for two contracts. Charlie presents the acceptable version, $d_k$, to Alice for signing. After Alice signed, Charlie takes the signature, attaches it to the altered contract $d'$, and claims Alice signed it. If the hash values are shorter than 2K, then a match's probability is very high due to the birthday paradox.

# 4.9.5 The random oracle hash

If it gets the same input, it will reproduce the same output. A ROHF is an ideal collision-resistant hash function.
