In general, developed economies, the proportion of cash versus bank deposits is relatively small. However, when large-denomination notices are available, they are occasionally used instead of bank accounts. Despite all the verbal acrobatics, depositors nolens volens become unsecured creditors of banks and therefore have the lowest priority in the event of a bank liquidity; see Devanaynes (1816). If a bank were to default, it would generally cause partial destruction of deposits. For small deposits, the bank's default risk is alleviated by insurance provided by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC); however, this is not an option for large deposits. For instance, when Indy Banc filed for bankruptcy in the summer of 2008, its depositors lost a significant portion of their money. Hence, banks are required to keep sufficient cushions, as well as liquidity.

At present, banks do not have sufficiently attractive opportunities to lend money. Hence they pile up reserves with the central bank; see Figure 2.7.

As private banks are unable or unwilling to find suitable borrowers, since the inception of the GFC and, significantly, the onset of the COVID-19-induced recession, central banks massively increase their lending operations, and as a result, their balance sheets; see Figure 2.8.
