We emphasize here that the process is stochastic: the propagation path depends on the topology of the peer-to-peer network, which itself is dynamic and anarchic in nature, as well as network latencies between peers — which depend on the geographical distance, the quality, the quantity, and the throughput of the Internet transport relays — also the simple willingness of nodes to play their relay nodes indiscriminately. Because there is no deterministic way to guarantee that all nodes see the red bike transaction first, or alternatively the blue bike transaction first, the nodes cannot jointly agree about which transaction to consider valid and which one. In fact, the original sender may well attempt to cheat the system by sending the red bike transaction to a specific partition of the network while sending the blue bike transaction to another partition of the network, such that different groups of nodes perceive the red and blue transactions in a different order.

As there is no natural ordering of transactions, there is no network-wide agreement on which of the two transactions should be considered valid and which one should be rejected. One cannot trust a timestamp that the sender would attach to its transactions given there would be no way to enforce the validity of the timestamp. The sender may as well send two transactions with the same timestamp, or create multiple copies of the same transaction with different timestamps and broadcast them at different moments, trying to revert previous transactions. The consequence of this incapacity to decide which conflicting transaction to keep and which transactions to block is commonly called the double spending. The reason is that if two bike merchants receive a Bitcoin transaction, one for the red, one for the blue, they would both think that they have received the funds it may very well be that another transaction clashes with theirs — and possibly that the balance of the sender account is not sufficient to cover both purchases.

# 3.6 Network consensus

It is obvious that a distributed ledger needs a robust solution against double spending, or in other words, it must embed a consensus algorithm allowing each network participant to reach high certainty that their view on which transaction keep is shared by the rest of the network. A consensus algorithm is precisely about agreeing on a decision when there is more than one node involved, and making sure that the agreement is robust to kinds of failures or attacks. In the case of double spending, the consensus aims to promote a decision about which transaction to keep and to disregard. The consensus does not guarantee that the earliest transaction be kept; it merely drives the general agreement around one that the "network" agrees with.

Many different consensus algorithms may be considered for the problem at hand, although the most algorithm will depend on the topology of the network (e.g., how many nodes) and the degree of trust between participants.
