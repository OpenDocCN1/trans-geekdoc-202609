# Blockchain and Distributed Ledgers: Mathematics, Technology, and Economics

Due the loose comparison to the mining of gold, where extracting gold nuggets requires infrastructure, physical effort, and time, block production was metaphorically named mining — and users performing this called miners. Some participants of the Bitcoin network would decide to become active maintainers, miners, and would start tracking the network to gather transactions from others, group them into blocks, start solving the proof-of-work, and on its resolution, broadcast the block to receive the reward. The reward would need to cover running costs (e.g., electricity, infrastructure management, depreciation) to achieve a sustainable business model.

The Bitcoin protocol includes two components as part of the reward.

+   (1) **reward**: freshly minted BTC up to the total number of BTC in the network. Importantly, the block reward schedule — monetary policy — is part of the consensus protocol of Bitcoin, which guarantees the immutability of the scheme against influential participants of the network. Satoshi Nakamoto set the block reward such that the inflation rate is originally high and predictably converges to zero. One can loosely compare this model to gold mining, which was relatively easier to extract in the 19th century given the abundance of unexploited mines, and is getting rarer and more expensive to extract as mines get emptier.

+   (2) **transaction fees**: the sum of all transaction fees of transactions included in the block, as paid by the respective senders. Fees, others, may be paid by the transaction sender to incentivize inclusion in a block. By setting a higher fee, the sender makes his transaction more attractive to the miner, who may process it with higher priority and therefore include it in an earlier block.

The sum of both constitutes the total BTC reward paid to the miner of a block.

# 3.6.5 Computational power rate

The definition of the difficulty of the proof-of-work problem remains. We start with what it should not be: a problem of constant complexity. Let us assume that at the inception of the Bitcoin network, only Satoshi Nakamoto himself is mining the network, and he is doing this with his personal computer. Setting the mining complexity can be calibrated such that solving the problem takes an arbitrary amount of time, for instance, 10 minutes, in average. Satoshi mines block and confirms that it takes 10 minutes to solve the problem and produce a new contemporaneous block. Assume now that a new network participant, Hal Finney, starts mining. The processing allocated to mining is now twice as large as Satoshi operated. Consequently, the block production rate is now 5 minutes. Half of the time, Satoshi finds the solution; half of the time Hal finds it. Hal invests in eight equivalent computers and gets them running side by side. As a result, the block production rate is now 10 times as fast as originally; also, Hal produces a block 90% of the time while Satoshi solves the problem first 10% of the time; he therefore gets 90% of BTC rewards but also has significantly higher operational costs.
