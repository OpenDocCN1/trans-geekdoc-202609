Miners provide proof-of-work (PoW) by finding nonces, which solve the requisite hash puzzles. The difficulty to find two nonces a single one is too short for satisfying the difficulty requirements, as described in Subsection 5.5.3. Usually, in distributed systems and computer security, the assumption is that a percentage of nodes are honest (robust), and the rest are malicious (faulty). The objective of the system's designer is to show that it will operate independent provided this assumption holds. For the Bitcoin protocol, we define the percentage of robust and faulty nodes by the amount of hash power they control. As we mentioned earlier, this is not dissimilar to how shareholders of people make their decisions.

However, an alternative way of thinking about this problem is to assume that nodes are neither honest nor malicious, and design a system that promotes honest behavior. We don't know if the honest conduct of nodes is a Nash equilibrium for Bitcoin. In, PoW consensus is robust, provided that more than 50% of the hashing power is controlled by “honest” miners, or to be more precise, using malicious miners can launch the 51% attack and rewrite the history of blockchain transactions, also described Subsection 3.6.8. In the context of the Bitcoin protocol, an attack is highly unlikely unless it will result in a precipitous drop in the BTC price. This drop would go against miners' self-interest.

An interesting example of PoW is the celebrated Suleman the Magnificent Tra's Tughra (or seal) shown in Figure 5.16. So much effort went into the production of a Tughra that its forgery was practically impossible. The fact that a forger, if uncovered, would immediately meet a horrible death, helps to preserve the integrity of communications well.

Bitcoin security is sequential. Once a block containing a particular transaction is buried deep in the blockchain, many (no less than six, preferably a few more) are built on top of it, the transactions become immutable. It is illustrated in Figure 5.17.

Bitcoin transactions have physical records, which are hard to duplicate, while electronic records' duplication is trivial. Therefore Alice can try to double spend transactions: transaction (B), which sends coins from Alice to Bob and transaction (C), which sends the same coins from Alice to Charlie the remainder to Alice. Only one of Bob and Charlie, for that matter, any other provider of goods and services, has to wait and see which transaction is included in a blockchain with certain confirms (no less than six). Only after that, one of them will send their merchandise to Alice. A double attack and a double defense are shown in Figure 5.18, where the arrows are pointers from a block to its predecessor.
