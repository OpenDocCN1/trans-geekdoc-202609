## 12.2.4 Regulations

Bitcoin came into existence as a spontaneous force, unregulated, uncontrolled, and untamed. Arguably, that was the only way to break the boundaries and succeed in the beginning. Bitcoin became a lodestar among those who lost faith in governments and central banks, initially only more of a cult-like trend than a rationale for a new asset class. However, as the movement gained recognition, the universe of cryptocurrencies and currencies has significantly expanded beyond Bitcoin. Thus, it became that without a proper regulatory framework, the crypto universe cannot achieve its full potential of gaining mainstream. This section discusses critical regulatory problems, which must be solved as crypto enters our lives. Most of them are inherent to the financial system as a whole, including legacy and crypto, while others are specific to the crypto universe. This area of research and development constitutes RegTech, although, for different people, this might offer a slightly different meaning.

The Greek philosopher Aristotle articulated legal aspects of money, emphasizing that money and government are joined at the hip: “Money has been introduced by convention as a kind of substitute for need or demand, and this is why we call money (νομύσμα) because its value is derived, not from nature, but from the law (νομο), and can be altered or abolished at will” (Crisp, 2014).

When we talk about the banking system in general and financial transactions in particular, it is hard to overestimate the importance of the KYC and AML requirements for regulatory compliance. KYC measures deal with identity verification and assuring suitability and risks for maintaining a business relationship with a customer, whether individual or business. In the current setting, the onboarding of a new customer can take up to days, hurting customer experience. AML admins the detection of money laundering and other illegal activities and behavioral concerns. The KYC/AML procedures are equally applicable to traditional financial transactions and those with cryptoassets, such as issuing new tokens and redeeming existing ones.

Davies (2010) looked at the situation pragmatically: “Because of the anonymity and widespread acceptance of money, financial fraud is always likely to be among the most attractive and direct of criminal’s temptations, from forgery to money-laundering, but it must be emphasized that the most calamitous of financial failures began with the cleverest of people operating with the best of intentions. Incompetents and Nobel Laureates themselves in the same basket.”

KYC measures for cryptoassets are further simplified by exchanging fiat currencies into stable tokens and vice versa using legacy bank accounts, whose owners have passed the standard KYC procedures performed by their banks. Such an ability to take advantage of existing bank accounts reduces KYC costs to less than a dollar per person. Digital Identity discussed in Section 12.3.1 can be used for the purposes of KYC.

AML policies can also be significantly simplified with the intelligent use of DLT. Given stable tokens circulate on a blockchain — currently on Ethereum, on others — which keeps an immutable record of all transactions, AML can be performed indirectly by analyzing the corresponding transaction social graph. In this regard, transactions with stable tokens are more transparent than conventional physical cash and are on par with traditional banking transactions. Initially, criminals about cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin and Ethereum, for illegal actions. However, with time, they realized that the immutable record of transactions opens new avenues for law enforcement agencies to monitor and prosecute their activities. They are reverting to using cryptocurrencies with built-in privacy.

Moreover, for finding suspicious activities or fraudulent transactions, DLT opens a new domain to further analyze the record at the level of an individual transaction. It enables chain analysis, including Know Your Transaction (KYT), to discover and follow all the chain links that lead to questionable ownership or illicit acts. KYT is offered as a service by several companies, such as Chainalysis, for constantly monitoring transactions for fraud detection in real-time. For additional insights, see Chain-Verduyn (2019) and references therein.

While the use of stable coins can significantly facilitate all steps in KYC and AML procedures, it also bears a mark of a surveillance hazard used by a repressive state. When high volumes of cash transactions are completed on a blockchain, the government can track a considerable portion of citizens' payments and exert control over their lives. Therefore, at least cash-like financial transactions must be anonymized, except when the anonymity is lifted for case of criminal investigations or in similar situations. When there is a social imperative to override anonymity, it should rely on an adequately qualified judiciary system. Thus, developing a suitable and competent infrastructure is paramount for successfully using stable tokens as a medium of exchange.

## 12.2.5 Trade execution, clearing and settlement

The holy trinity of the stock market is trade execution, clearing, and settlement. The initial public offering of stock is an essential rite of passage for a new company. Its secondary trading is a mechanism for allocating ownership and control in a (somewhat) optimal fashion. In addition to stocks, many other products, such as equity derivatives, interest rate swaps, commodities, etc., are traded on public exchanges. Moving many over-the-counter (OTC) products to exchanges is an essential regulatory imperative; see Lipton (2018); Subramanian (2017), among many others.

Unfortunately, this system suffers from numerous shortcomings and outright failures. Let us briefly discuss how Trading, Clearing, and Settlement function at present. Figure 12.5 shows the corresponding steps with black and grey indicating the information requests and securities transfers, respectively see Lipton (2018); Pinna and Ruttenberg (2016).

Here are the necessary steps:

- Seller (S) instructs her Broker (SB) to sell a security.
- At the same time, Buyer (B) instructs her Broker (BB) to buy this security.
- SGCM asks SB to deliver the corresponding security.
- SB sends this to Seller's Clearing Agent (SCA).
- SCA forwards to Seller's Custodian (SC).
- SC's request is sent to Central Securities Depository (CSD), where a series of transfers of the security ownership takes place:
    - (a) from SC to SCA (1->2)
    - (b) from SCA to SGCM (2->3)
    - (c) from SGCM to CCP (3->4)
    - (d) from CCP to BGCM (4->5)
    - (e) from BGCM to Buyer's Clearing Agent (BCA) (5->6)
    - (f) from BC to Buyer's Custodian (BC) (6->7)

As a result, the security in question originally held by SC is transferred to BC. Step 1 from the above sequence represents trading, step 2 — clearing, steps 3–6 — settlement. Figure 12.5 does not show the flow of money, which naturally occurs in the opposite direction, i.e., from buyer to seller.
