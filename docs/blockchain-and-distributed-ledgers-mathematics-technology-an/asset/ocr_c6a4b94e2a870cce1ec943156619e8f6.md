# 11.2 Intra- and interoperability

# 11.2.1 Background

A well-designed blockchain, viewed in isolation, provides intrinsic decentralization. However, for the DLT to be truly successful, it is essential to ensure that decentralization occurs across different blockchains. Such decentralization offers an application developer a single view regardless of the number of distinct blockchain systems involved. Thus, true decentralization should allow functions to spread across separate blockchains using different consensus mechanisms and other ledger data structures, with nodes implementing different software stacks; as Hardjono et al. (2019a); Lipton and Hardjono (2021). The TCP/IP Internet is architected along lines. This design explains why the Internet has grown in size and traffic capacity to serve end-users globally; rather than being a single IP network, the Internet is a collection of interconnected Autonomous Systems (ASs); as Clark (1988). Having a well-defined physical boundary, each AS is operated by an Internet Service Provider (ISP).

In this section, we discuss approaches to blockchain intra- and interoperability. We define blockchain intraability as an ability for automatically exchanging different assets defined on the same blockchain, supporting smart contracts, for instance, Ethereum. We define interoperability as a capability to move assets between different blockchains.

Currently, it is not known how to fully achieve either intraability or interoperability. We argue that using automated market makers (AMMs) discussed in Section 10.11, for interoperability the gateway-gateway protocols can be utilized. The reader can find additional information in Abadi and Brunnemer (2018); Buterin (2016); Hardjono et al. (2019a); Hope-Baillie and Thomas (2016); Koens and Pohl (2019); Lima (2018); Lipton and Hardjono (2021); Pillai et al. (2019); Thomas and Schwartz (2015), and references therein.

# 11.2.2 Interoperability

Lipton and Hardjono (2021) show that intraability is achievable using AMMs, which currently operate as dark pools. Recall that dark pools of liquidity (also dark liquidity) operate as consensus networks for large buy and sell orders providing liquidity not displayed on exchanges. By using dark pools, traders can buy or sell large numbers of shares without revealing their positions or impacting market prices because the trading company’s identity is displayed. Dark pools track prices established at public exchanges; they can be viewed as price takers. AMMs operate along similar coins' prices established on centralized exchanges. Thus, despite their name, AMMs cannot operate autonomously. To achieve interoperability, one needs to reach a state when AMMs from price takers, become price makers, i.e., their composition defines the relative price of constituent tokens and not the other way around. Interoperability requires AMMs to become market players, dominating centralized exchanges. While appearing in theory, time will tell if such dominance is achievable in practice.
