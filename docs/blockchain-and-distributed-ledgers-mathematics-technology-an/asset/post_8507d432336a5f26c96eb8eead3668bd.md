To make chain analysis more difficult (but not impossible), one can contemplate the amount of information revealed by a particular transaction by keeping the sender's and recipient's public keys private. In addition, the transaction size can be kept private, too. However, the idea of making the global state of the blockchain completely private is self-defeating. If the total number of coins in circulation were to be shielded, users could not be sure that there is no surprising issuance of additional coins.

In order to enforce user privacy, several methodologies have been developed. Below, we describe three of them, payment channels, coin mixing, and cryptographic obfuscation. The reader can find discussion and additional information in a recent survey of blockchain privacy; see Peng et al. (2020).

## 11.3.2 Payment channels

A straightforward way for a group of two or more agents, who wish to transact anonymously with each other, is to establish a payment channel among themselves. The simplest possibility — a payment channel between Alice and Bob. Figure 11.5 shows the process of a payment channel. To open the channel, both deposit 10 BTC to a particular address. Then, they transact with each other off-blockchain until such time that one of them (or both) wish to close the channel or the pre-funded buffer gets exhausted. The final settlement occurs on the blockchain. In the example shown in Figure 11.5, after a series of transactions, which are kept private, Alice ends up with 2 BTC, Bob with 8 BTC. In Section 11.4, we show that, in addition to enhancing privacy, payment channels improve the underlying protocol’s scalability. Strictly speaking, such transactions cannot be qualified as blockchain-based and are prone to various risks.

## 11.3.3 Coin mixing

Coin mixing is an alternative to establishing payment channels. The simplest mixer is shown in Figure 11.6. This figure shows four agents with Bitcoin addresses A, B, C, D, each sending 1 BTC to the mixing service. In turn, the mixer’s operator sends their BTC to new addresses A’, B’, D’ (minus a small fee). Assuming that the operator is honest and does not steal from clients, agents end up with BTC that are not directly linked to their addresses. Given that all participants send the same amounts, their anonymity is further. External observer may see which owner of unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs) belongs to the set {A, B, C, D}. Without merits, this approach is centralized and relies on honesty and discretion of the operator. Centralized mixing services (CMS) rely on a trusted or semi-trusted third party to mix multiple users’ input and output them to new addresses, breaking the link between inputs and outputs. A centralized mixer is easy to use, but it is expensive not to mention that its operator is a single point of failure. Hence, researchers proposed to use decentralized mixers instead. Decentralized mixers are cheap or free; besides, they don't rely on a third party and don't have a single point of failure. A decentralized mixer is shown in Figure 11.7.

One of the first decentralized mixing services, like CoinJoin, was proposed by Greg Maxwell in 2013; see Maxwell (2013). Instead of relying on a third party, CoinJoin users mix coins themselves. Mixing starts with negotiations allowing a group of players to agree on the set of input and output addresses they wish to use. Then, a transaction containing all input/output pairs is generated and checked by users for accuracy. Finally users jointly sign the transaction and publish it to the Bitcoin blockchain. Compared to decentralized mixers, CoinJoin significantly reduces the risk of information leakage and eliminates the possibility of coin theft. However, it is far from perfect; the negotiation process can be abused to discover its participants' information. Besides, the CoinJoin process is vulnerable to the Denial of Service (DoS) attack since a single user's inability to sign the final transaction results in the failure of the whole process. Subsequently several other protocols to other users; as Shuffle and CoinParty were designed; see Peng et al. (2020) and references therein.

The Monero protocol automatically mixes a sender's coin with randomly decoy coins belonging to others; it uses the so-called ring signature produced by the actual sender via a one-time spend key, corresponding to the output from the sender's wallet. Properties of ring signatures are such that it is possible to prove that someone within a group of addresses has signed a transaction without revealing a specific signatory. Mixing is particularly relevant for stablecoins since they are used as means of payment and have to preserve the transacting parties' privacy, like cash.
