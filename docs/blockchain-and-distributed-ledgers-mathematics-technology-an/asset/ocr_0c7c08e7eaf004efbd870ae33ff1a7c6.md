# ## 1.1 Introduction

The Global Financial Crisis (GFC) has demonstrated that the existing banking and payment system, while still working, is outdated and struggling to support the continually changing requirements of the modern world: Lipton et al. (2019). It would be underestimate to say that the GFC turned into a wasted opportunity to reorganize the world financial ecosystem. Too-big-to-fail banks have increased (rather than decreased!) in size, disproportionately amplifying their banking business size, while the number of banking institutions has significantly reduced. For instance, the size of JPMorgan's balance sheet is nearly twice as large as it was at the end of 2006, on the onset of the crisis; similarly, the balance sheets of China's four systematically important banks have more than tripled over the same period. The clearing of many over-counter derivatives was mandates transferred to central clearing counterparties (CCPs), which become potential points of failure for the system as a whole. This situation is further exacerbated by the high level of interconnectedness of CCPs, due to numerous clearing members having in common.
Central banks, nolens, in the massive Quantitative Easing (QE) efforts and embraced the fractional reserve modus operandi. At the same time, commercial banks reassessed their priorities and are switching to the Narrow Bank (NB) model, partly by choice (to satisfy their risk preferences), and partly by necessity (not being able to lend enough to the real economy). As central banks employed short-term interest rates tools for achieving their macroeconomic goals, the short-term interest rates moved to zero for much of the past decade, reflecting the effects of QE, well headline inflation, and low productivity growth; e.g., Rudbusch (2018).
