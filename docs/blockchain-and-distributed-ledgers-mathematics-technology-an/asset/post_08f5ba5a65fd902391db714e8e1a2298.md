In Chapters 5 and 6, we have discussed the Proof-of-Work (PoW) algorithm, which implies that any participant on the network can become a miner competing in the block generation. A miner must be the first to solve a specific computational problem to confirm a new block and receive a reward. As we have shown in Chapters 5 and 6, economic considerations force miners to coalesce in highly centralized mining pools.

Due to the inability of the original PoW to scale and its prodigious use of electricity, it is natural to entertain other consensus algorithms. We mention several popular approaches:

- (1) Proof-of-Stake (PoS)
- (2) Proof-of-Capacity (PoC)
- (3) Byzantine Fault Tolerance (BFT)
- (4) Direct Acyclic Graph (DAG).

For additional insights, Barreiro-Gomez and Tembine (2019); Houy (2014); Mazieres (2015); Nguyen et al. (2019); Zhou et al. (2020) among many others. Let us briefly discuss the corresponding algorithms.

PoS is based on the convivial capitalism that only owners of coins or smart contracts can decide whether transactions are legitimate. Stakeholders are randomly to become validators for a new block, with the probability of being selected increasing with the holdings size. Besides the straightforward PoS, Delegated Proof-of-Stake (DPoS) and Leased Proof-of-Stake (LPoS) have been tried. Some of the popular PoS-based protocols are Tezos, Ethereum, and Ethereum 2.0 upgrade. Several researchers pointed out that PoS has serious drawbacks as a distributed consensus protocol. The “nothing-at-stake” issue is tough to resolve; see Poestra (2014). In the PoW framework, block generators can vote for multiple blockchains without incurring significant costs. If they decide to do so, the consensus could not be.

PoC works similarly to PoW; however, than solving the PoW puzzle, participants need to allocate a large amount of memory or disk space to solve a computational problem specified by the protocol. Some popular protocols using this are Angel, File, and Solana.

BFT-based consensus is an inspiration for numerous blockchain protocols, thus we describe it here. BFT-based consensus tolerates dishonest or faulty validators, provided that their number is less than one-third of all validators. BFT plays a pivotal role in computer science, as a key tool for dealing with hardware failures; see Lamport et al. (1982).

The BFT consensus is based on the classical Byzantine Generals Problem (BGP), which works as follows. Generals attacking a city from different directions. Some generals are loyal; others are not. It is not known in advance who is faithful and is treacherous. The generals communicate with each other via messengers. A commanding general must send an order to his $n-1$ lieutenant generals such that IC1. All loyal lieutenants obey the same order.

IC2. If the commanding general is loyal, then loyal lieutenants obey the order he sends.

Conditions IC1 and IC2 are called the interactive consistency conditions. Note that if the commander is loyal, then IC1 follows from IC2. However, the commander need not be loyal.

When there is a commanding general, solving the BGP problem and achieving BFT is possible if the number of traitors is less than one-third of total.
