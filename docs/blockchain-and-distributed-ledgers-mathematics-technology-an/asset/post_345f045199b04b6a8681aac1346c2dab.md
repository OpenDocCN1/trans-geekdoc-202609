## 3.7 Altcoins

Beyond criminal cases, Bitcoin demonstrated a new capability that could have implications far beyond a peer-to-peer currency. The combination of sets up by Satoshi Nakamoto attracted crowds of passionate software engineers, researchers, and entrepreneurs that saw an opportunity to extract the good ideas of Bitcoin and incrementally augment its protocol. Bitcoin being an open-source initiative, any user can browse its inner workings, reparameterize it or change its behavior in order to launch an accept or modified copy of it. Once deployed, the replica bootstraps its own blockchain with a new genesis block and applies the consensus rules of the new network. In essence, the replica only shares design principles with Bitcoin, but it is not Bitcoin—the way twin brothers share a similar DNA but are different instances of it.

In 2011, Litecoin was launched by Charlie Lee, an ex-employee of Google. Litecoin is similar to Bitcoin in many ways it changes three aspects of the protocol. First, it replaces the mining algorithm with an alternative scheme to be more resilient to decentralization risks associated with Bitcoin’s primitives. Second, it decreases the block time to 2 minutes, as compare to Bitcoin’s 10 minutes. Third, Litecoin changes the inflation rate while maintaining the ultimately capped monetary supply. Although, many changes were sufficient to attract the interest of investors, miners, and engineers. Litecoin saw its market value growth, as well as its reach of its ecosystem. In 2020, the market capitalization of LTC reached US$3 billion.

Following the commercial success of Litecoin and its success, hundreds of arbitrary copies of Bitcoin quickly appeared. Startups struggled in the deployment of reparameterized it everycoin allowing their players to deploy a new Bitcoin-like, with for instance, a different inflation schedule, a custom block time, within minutes. Naturally, most of these alternative coins, also altcoins, quickly disappeared the absence of differentiation, use cases, and ecosystem. Interestingly, some of did manage to survive: among dogecoin, a meme-cryptocurrency whose only true innovation was to attach some funny dog picture to the coin (see Figure 3.12), managed to get enough media attention to bootstrap its ecosystem and grow in market capitalization and in support. ## 3.7.4 Ethereum and smart contracts

It is in 2013 Vitalik Buterin, a cryptocurrency researcher, programmer, and young entrepreneur first announced his project to create a new platform: Ethereum. Rather than being a copycat of Bitcoin, Ethereum aimed to reconsider multiple technical foundations of Bitcoin: Ethereum was faster, more scalable, and more capable. For detailed discussion of Ethereum, see Chapter 6.

But more than anything, Ethereum aimed at introducing the notion of stateful and Turing complete smart contracts. Invented by Nick Szabo in 1993, a smart contract is a piece of software designed to automatically coordinate and enforce the execution of terms. A smart contract helps disintermediate the contractual relationship between parties and decrease the risks of errors, frauds, and subjective interpretation, with the ultimate goal to decrease costs and uncertainties. The blockchain provided a powerful platform to consider the execution of smart contracts: with its strong guarantees of immutability and integrity, it could theoretically execute programmable software in a fully decentralized, yet trusted way.
