Bitcoin's design is such that anyone with money and access to the Internet everywhere in the world can buy BTCCs in a decentralized fashion. Of course, in reality, nothing can be verified from the truth — Bitcoin is being sold on centralized exchanges undermining its very premise of being decentralized! Analysis of its prices is very important for BTC trading on exchanges and BTC futures cleared by the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) and other firms.

Part of the problem is why it is so hard to discern, although only a few commentators manage to do so or admit it in public. Bitcoin has no value, and hence it can have any price! Besides, to make things even harder to understand, Bitcoin ownership is characterized by extreme, truly fueled, inequality; see Table 10.1.

More than often not, whales, i.e., owners of large amounts of BTC, are “hoders,” keeping their BTC out of circulation, regardless of the price volatility. A small group of BTC owners are probably engaged in price manipulation.

Currently, there are three schools of thought regarding the long-term price of bitcoin — (a) BTC at US50; (b) BTC at US$10,000,000 or more; (c) BTC at market price, whether it might be. It would be an understatement to say that adherents to both the first and the second viewpoints have no idea of what they are talking about and, in many cases, have strong financial incentives to promote their opinions publicly. The third viewpoint is of course, accurate but uninformative.³

For decades, researchers have found that financial assets' daily returns, such as foreign exchange rates and bond prices, are not Gaussian or normal. The corresponding empirical distributions are more peaked around the mean, exhibit heavier tails than the normal distribution, and often skewed towards the left tail, implying a higher probability of negative returns.
