Let us implement a forward contract as an SC, restricting ourselves to the Ethereum's native token, ETH. As before, a forward contract requires two parties, Alice and Bob. Assume that both of them have some ETH hold opposite views of its future price. Alice thinks ETH price will go down and wishes to accumulate others before they rally. They want to structure a contract so that Alice makes ETH if price drops in the short run and (naturally) loses money otherwise. Since a forward contract is a zero-sum game, Bob has a mirror image payoff. Alice and Bob can design the corresponding contract along the following lines:

+   (1) Alice deposits 100 ETHs.

+   (2) Bob deposits 100 ETHs.

+   (3) At the contract's inception, the USD value of 100 ETHs is X USD.

+   (4) At the contract's maturity, in one month, 100 ETHs are worth Y USD.

+   (5) The SC sends X dollars of worth to Alice and the rest (if anything is left) to Bob.

At the inception of the contract and its maturity, the price of ETH is calculated by having an agreed-upon feed contract step-by-step.

Let us discuss what happens step-by-step. Relative price change by Z = Y/X. If the ETH price in 30 days goes down, Y < X, Z < 1, Alice gains more ETHs compared to the buy-and-hold strategy, and if it goes up, Y > X, Z > 1, she loses ETHs. However, she cannot gain more than 100 ETHs in addition to her initial 100 ETHs, reflecting the total amount of collateral and derelating to Z ≤ 1/2. For Z = 1, Alice gets back 100 ETHs, regaining her original position. Moreover, ETH continues to rally and Z > 1, she receives less and less ETH that are worth X dollars. In the limit, she can lose her entire stake of 100 ETHs. Alice's and Bob's payouts are in Figure 6.10.

The beauty of the situation is that the counterpart risk is absent in the SC, since the contract is funded in advance. Of course, such funding is neither possible not entirely desirable in real life, so that the perceived advantage is illusory. Moreover, the whole premise of financial transactions is to reduce collateral and increase leverage to magnify a potential gain. Besides, if the price of ETH drops by a factor of two or more, the entire collateral of 200 can be less than X dollars. It is essential to understand that this contract requires an oracle intrinsic to Ethereum in the form of a centralized, or, at least, semi-centralized, feed. Thus, this contract is not entirely decentralized. Recently, ChainLink made some progress solving the above-mentioned critical issue; Section 6.8.4.

# 6.8.3 Advantages and disadvantages of smart contracts

SCs have several desirable properties. They are autonomous and can be entered between two parties directly without using the services of intermediaries. SCs are trustworthy, as long as the corresponding code is trustworthy and free of bugs, the agreed terms are enforced automatically. SCs are reliable since their execution is guaranteed by the entirety of miners supporting the Ethereum protocol's integrity at large. Besides, SCs are reasonably efficient.
