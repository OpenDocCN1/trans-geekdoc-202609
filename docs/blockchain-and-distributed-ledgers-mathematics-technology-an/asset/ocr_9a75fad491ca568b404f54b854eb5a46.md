The attacker is in control of the longest chain and can prevent any other miner from contributing to the consensus chain by forking them, or may create an alternative fork starting in the past and catching up with the consensus chain to replace it entirely reverting accounting. For the consensus algorithm to be trusted, the risk of such an event happening must be low enough to be ignored. For this reason, the security of the Bitcoin network is loosely defined by its total mining power (or its difficulty): the higher the difficulty, the more expensive and unlikely it is for an attacker to surpass 50% and take ownership of the block production.

Interestingly, Satoshi Nakamoto's consensus algorithm does not assume that miners are honest whatsoever. As long as no colluding of miners — honest or reaches a large enough control of the total mining power, the system is safe against most forms of attacks. We note however that the above explanation forgets many exogenous factors which may derail the natural incentives. In instance, a wealthy attacker may attempt to harm the system with no immediate need (e.g., governments); or may attempt to manipulate the BTC market by hurting the trust in the system and profiting from independent trading strategies, e.g short selling; or may implemented behaviors shifting the incentives and game theoretic equilibrium. In practice, despite many sorts of theoretical attacks, Bitcoin has remained resilient anti-fragile and is still perceived as the most secure, permissionless distributed ledger.
