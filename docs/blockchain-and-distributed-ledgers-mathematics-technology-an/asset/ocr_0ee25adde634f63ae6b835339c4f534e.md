# 4.7.2 Historical aside

A brief historical prelude explaining why elliptic curves were not confused with ellipses in order; see Brown (2000); Rice and Brown (2012) for further details.

Elliptic curves have ancient origins in antiquity. One was Leonardo da Pisa, also known as Fibonacci (c. 1175–1250), who encountered the following problem: “Find a rational number r such that $r^2-5$ and $r^2+5$ are rational squares.”

Fibonacci showed that $r=41/6$ is a number since $(41/6)^2-5=(31/6)^2$ and $(41/6)^2+5=(49/6)^2$. In his 1225 celebrated book, which is appropriately called Liber Quadratorum (The Book of Squares), Fibonacci introduced the concept of a congruent number. A positive integer $n$ is a congruent number provided that there exists a rational number $r$, such that $r^2-n$, $r^2+n$ are nonzero squares for some rational numbers $r_, r_+$, $r^2-n=r_-^2, r^2+n=r_+^2$. Thus, 5 is a congruent number, according to Fibonacci.

How are congruent numbers related to elliptic curves? The product $r^2r_-^2r_+^2$ is a square of a rational number $s:

```
$s^2=(r^2-n)(r^2+n)=r^4-n^2r^2=(r^2)^3-n^2(r^2).$
```

Thus, the point $(r, y)$ is a point on the elliptic curve,

```
$y^2=x^3-n^2x.$
```

In Fibonacci’s case, we have $x=(41/6)$, $y=6229/216$. An important observation is that $r$ has an even denominator, which is no coincidence. The following fundamental lemma reads:

+   Lemma 4.1 An $n$ is a congruent number if and only if there exists a rational point $(x,y)$ on the elliptic curve $y^2=x^3-n^2x$ such that $x=r^2$, where $r$ is a rational number with even denominator.

The name elliptic curve is used for historical reasons from the fact that such curves play an important role in calculating the arc length of ellipses. Recall that an ellipse curve given by the following equation in the (x,y) plane:

```
$rac{x^2}{p^2}+rac{y^2}{q^2}=1,$
```

where $p>0,q>0$; without loss of generality, we can assume $p \le q$. When $p=q$, the corresponding ellipse becomes a circle or radius $p$.

While calculating the circumference of a circle, which we denote by $C(p,p)$, is straightforward, $C(p,p)=2\pi p$, calculating the circumference of an ellipse, which we denote by $C(p,q)$ is never easy. Several mathematicians, Isaac Newton (1669), Leonhard Euler (1733), and Colin Maclaurin (1742), expressed the arc length of an ellipse as an infinite series. Subsequently, Adrien-Marie Legendre, Niels Henrik Abel, Carl Gustav Jacobi, and Karl Weierstrass studied the integrals. We notice that in contrast with evaluating $C(p,q)$, the area $A(p,q)$ of an ellipse is straightforward:

```
$A(p,q)=\pi pq.$
```
