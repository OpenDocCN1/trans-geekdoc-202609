### 6.9.6 Decentralized autonomous organizations

Given the nature of Ethereum as a CaaS provider, it is natural to explore some nontrivial possibilities for using this outside consensus for building new business models. One case is to create a decentralized autonomous organization (DAO), occasionally called a decentralized autonomous corporation (DAC). A DAO is governed by rules encoded in smart contracts, controlled by its members in peer-to-peer collaboration, and does not have a centralized government. All activities such as financial transactions are recorded on the Ethereum blockchain, or for that, any other blockchain that maintains the smart contract framework. This way, the costs of preserving consensus are offset by the fact that a DAO does not have central management.

The idea of a DAO is audacious. Surprisingly, there are numerous needs to address before it reaches the mainstream. The main one is conceptual—real-life organizations or corporations are too complex to be described by simple set of rules. This statement is evident because smart contracts are notoriously fickle and not powerful enough, give gas limits and other issues. ### 6.10 Summary

This chapter covers the most relevant aspects of the Ethereum protocol. In many respects, it is an extension of the seminal Bitcoin protocol discussed in Chapters 3 and 5, augmented with some striking capabilities. Because of that, Ethereum is more potent than its predecessor, but at the same time, due to its overarching ambitions, it has many weaknesses.

On the other side, Ethereum can do things best, moving the native token — ETH — from one address to the next. Moreover, because Ethereum is a transaction counting once concept, there is no need to spend the entire amount stored at a particular address every owner wants to pay in. As was mentioned earlier, one can view Ethereum as a CaaS provider. The actual consensus provision relies on smart contracts. Thus, Ethereum is well-suited for building tokens on top of its underlying blockchain.

On the con side, Ethereum is frequently expensive to use, on occasion unreliable. Given that smart contracts' verification requires a lot of effort from the miners, Ethereum must rely on an elaborate framework allowing users to pay for miners' services. The price of gas constantly fluctuates and reacts how the network is at any given moment each operation they perform. This fee structure is Ethereum's Achilles heel — it is unfortunately rigid and very often prohibitively expensive. The network capacity is relatively small, so the Ethereum blockchain is frequently slow and unreliable. The idea of the Ethereum blockchain being a Turing complete world computer is not valid in practice. Surprisingly, developers of practical real-life applications move their activities to the Polkadot protocol and some others, which are powerful and easier to use.

> Two conclusions, related to this saga, jump to mind: (a) strictly speaking, it is not possible to hack a smart contract because of the code-is-law principle; (b) the Ethereum blockchain is not an immutable source of truth and can be manipulated as necessary.

In a recent interview with Camila Russo from the Defiant Podcast, in summary, Ethereum is a bold attempt at improving Bitcoin and bringing it to the next level. However, in its present form, it is either difficult and expensive to use. Time will tell if switching to the proof-of-stake consensus can be a game-changer.

Additional details can be found in Antonopoulos and Wood (2018); Bistarelli et al. (2019); Buterin (2013); Wood (2015).
