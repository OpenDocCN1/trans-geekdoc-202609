Tether is one of the earliest and best known custodial coins, issued on the Bitcoin blockchain through the Omni Layer Protocol; Tether (2016). As its name suggests, is supposed to be linked (tethered) to the underlying fiat currency, as USD, since every tether in circulation is collateralized with a dollar in a dedicated bank account. While simple in practice, the situation is occasionally opaque; nobody knows the corresponding collateral exists, where it is, and who administers it. Given the above, Tether is not regulatory compliant. As per Tether's legal disclaimer: "Beginning on January 1, 2018, Tether Tokens will no longer be issued to the U.S. Persons." Surprisingly, all of the above issues resulted in USDT recently breaking the peg to the USD. Tether is geared toward crypto exchanges rather than for regular commerce.

Despite its shortcomings, Tether's imposing emanates from its very nature as an essential instrument for speculators. Namely, USDT has an exceptionally high velocity. Its capitalization is 60 times smaller than Bitcoin, the dollar value of BTC and USDT traded per day is broadly comparable. For instance, coinmarketcap.com, on September 26, 2020, BTC capitalization and daily volume were US$198 billion and US$552.5 billion, respectively, for USDT, they were US$15.3 billion and US$37.0 billion. The velocity of USDT is much higher than that of either BTC or USD. Many used to manipulate Bitcoin prices; see, e.g.; Griffin and Shams (2019); Leising et al. (2018).

The Utility Settlement Coin (USC), deployed by a consortium of banks called Clarity is a promising candidate for use in the interbank market. Initially, USC can be an internal token for a consortium of participating banks. These coins have to be fully collateralized by these banks' electronic cash balances by the Central Bank; see Lipton et al. (2018a). Eventually, these coins can circulate among a larger group of participants. However, in this case, the instance of USCs are to be outsourced to AN.

By construction, all stablecoins are centralized to a greater or lesser degree, so their issuers represent a single point of failure and an irresistible attraction for both regulators and hackers (for different reasons, obviously). Operationally, for some coins, there is no transparency regarding the underlying reserves, making it difficult for users to trust them. Typically, collateralized coins rely on third parties, most notably banks, to keep the corresponding collateral and execute transactions on their behalf, making their actual costs excessive and forcing them to impose high transaction fees on users. Ironically, on existing legacy banks brings to the forefront the instance of stability of the banking system itself, an alternative crypto ecosystem is supposed to reject in the first place.

Unless very structured, fiat-backed coins suffer from the high costs of carrying collateral, which leads to low profitability, which is a significant danger in its right. For example, creators of custodial blockchains on bank balance sheets to hold reserves have to pay a high price to effectively rent those balance sheets (and their capital buffers). This vastly reduces custodial coins' cost-effectiveness and requires a much broader customer to reach a profitable scale.

An NB is a natural candidate for issuing a stable fiat-backed cryptocurrency, which can be viewed as a private digital version of cash. A client can deliver fiat currency into an NB either from their existing account with the NB itself or via wire transfer from another bank. In the former case, the bank knows the client and does not need to do diligence. In the latter case, the NB performs the necessary KYC tests. In return, the client receives digital tokens, while the NB deposits funds with the central bank. The client can use their tokens to transfer value on a distributed ledger; see Figure 8.2.

The tokens exist on a distributed ledger. The consensus is maintained by PoW, proof-of-stake or by third-party notices. When the current owner decides to convert back into fiat currency, the NB returns the token to an NB, which either credits the account or wires it to a different bank. In the former case, the bank knows the client. In the latter case, the NB performs the necessary KYC tests.

Following the flow diagrams of Figures 8.2 (a), (b), (ially), the NB issues a digital token to Client, in exchange for currency. The currency comes from the Client at the NB. Next, Client passes this token to User, in exchange for goods and services. The act of ownership transfer is recorded on a distributed ledger maintained by miners or notices. In the case of digital business and transact, the NB provides AML checks. Eventually, User₁ passes the token to Participant, in exchange for goods and services. At time T₄, User₂ passes the token to Client; in exchange for goods and services. Finally, Client₂ passes the token to the NB for fiat currency, which is deposited in the Client account at the NB.

The tokens retain their value in a narrow band around arbitrage. When the token price falls significantly below par, its owner can immediately give it back to the bank and receive the full value. When the token price increases considerably above par, the pent-up demand causes an NB to issue more tokens into the ledger by attracting or attracting outside investors, reducing the price. Issuing stablecoins by an NB is the only way of satisfying the titular postulate of Copernicus.

## 8.4.3 Coins partially collateralized with fiat

Saga is a relatively rare example of cryptocurrencies having a time-varying degree of collateralization. By design, it starts its life as fully collateralized and eventually becoming free-flowing; see Saga (2018).

### 8.4.4 Coins (over)collateralized with crypto

An alternative to creating stablecoins is to use unstable native crypto, specifically ETH, as collateral. By providing ETH as collateral to a smart contract, an agent can get stablecoins in return. These coins, representing a sliver of the total value, are massively overcollateralized. We emphasize that over-collateralization is the opposite of conventional financial practice, such as trading on margin, based on the idea of under-collateralization. Thus, to get one dollar of stablecoins, a user needs to have one-and-half or two dollars worth of ETH. This is the situation of conventional margin trading, where a user can have an exposure of ten dollars of equity by one dollar of collateral.

Because of the above, crypto-collateralized coins have severe and apparent drawbacks. Since the price evolution of the underlying crypto, such as ETH, is driven by a jump process, it can breach the floor in the event of a black swan, or realistically, less of confidence by the users. Thus, although coins are over-collateralized, the peg is not guaranteed. Besides, over-collateralized coins suffer from the negative feedback loop, which arises because forced selling of collateral causes its price to decrease faster than it would otherwise.
