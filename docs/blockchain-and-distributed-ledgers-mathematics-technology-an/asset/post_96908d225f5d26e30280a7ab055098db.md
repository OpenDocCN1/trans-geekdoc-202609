### 3.6.3 Building the chain

The construction of the blockchain works according to the following process.

- (1) Download the existing chain of blocks up to the contemporaneous block from other peers on the network.
- (2) Assess the validity of the chain, of its accounting, of its transactions, and its proofs of work in respective blocks.
- (3) Select a set of pending transactions waiting to be included in the blockchain. These transactions, which have been exchanged between the peers of the network after the broadcast of respective senders, are stored in a memory pool of respective network nodes, waiting to be included in a new block.
- (4) Build a block with the selected transactions, set the parent hash within the block, and start the proof of work.
- (5) If and when a valid proof is identified, include it within the block and broadcast the block to the other nodes.
- (6) Let other nodes verify the integrity of the block, including its proof-of-work, and decide accordingly on whether to include it to their local copy of the blockchain.

Interestingly, other network participants do not participate in the same exercise to submit their opinion on the next block. Many questions remain however. Given the cost associated with block production, why would anybody willingly engage into this activity? How should the complexity of the work be defined, by whom, and should it be time varying? Which transactions would be selected for inclusion within a block? Which block to choose in cases where multiple conflicting, yet valid, blocks are broadcast to the network with the same parent?

### 3.6.4 Block rewards and miners

The production of blocks, which is conditioned on obtaining a valid proof-of-work, is an expensive enterprise. Satoshi Nakamoto knew well that network users would not engage into this activity without a proper incentivization scheme. He set up as part of the protocol reward, paid in BTC, that the creator of a block receives when successfully getting his block approved by the rest of the network.

Due the loose comparison to the mining of gold, where extracting gold nuggets requires infrastructure, physical effort, and time, block production was metaphorically named mining — and users performing this called miners. Some participants of the Bitcoin network would decide to become active maintainers, miners, and would start tracking the network to gather transactions from others, group them into blocks, start solving the proof-of-work, and on its resolution, broadcast the block to receive the reward. The reward would need to cover running costs (e.g., electricity, infrastructure management, depreciation) to achieve a sustainable business model.

The Bitcoin protocol includes two components as part of the reward.

- (1) **reward**: freshly minted BTC up to the total number of BTC in the network. Importantly, the block reward schedule — monetary policy — is part of the consensus protocol of Bitcoin, which guarantees the immutability of the scheme against influential participants of the network. Satoshi Nakamoto set the block reward such that the inflation rate is originally high and predictably converges to zero. One can loosely compare this model to gold mining, which was relatively easier to extract in the 19th century given the abundance of unexploited mines, and is getting rarer and more expensive to extract as mines get emptier.

- (2) **transaction fees**: the sum of all transaction fees of transactions included in the block, as paid by the respective senders. Fees, others, may be paid by the transaction sender to incentivize inclusion in a block. By setting a higher fee, the sender makes his transaction more attractive to the miner, who may process it with higher priority and therefore include it in an earlier block.

The sum of both constitutes the total BTC reward paid to the miner of a block.

### 3.6.5 Computational power rate

The definition of the difficulty of the proof-of-work problem remains. We start with what it should not be: a constant complexity. Let us assume that at the inception of the Bitcoin network, only Satoshi Nakamoto himself is mining the network, and he is doing this with his personal computer. Setting the difficulty can be calibrated such that solving the problem takes an arbitrary amount of time, for instance 10 minutes, in average. Satoshi mines block and confirms that it takes 10 minutes to produce a new block. Assume now that a new participant, Hal Finney, starts mining. The processing allocated to mining is now twice as large as Satoshi. Half of the time, Satoshi finds the solution; half of the time Hal finds it. Hal invests in equivalent computers and gets them by side. As a result, the block production rate is now 5 minutes. Considering the openness, or permissionlessness, of the network, allowing any user to connect powerful computers and compete for block rewards, one perceives the obvious outcome: of computational power leading to a growing block rate.

### 3.6.6 Difficulty under competition

The difficulty target defines the hardness of the proof-of-work problem. Difficulty is endously defined and time-varying such that the expected production rate is stable, or equivalently, that the time between blocks is constant on average. This approach ensures that two valid blocks should rarely be submitted at the same time by miners, and fast enough that waiting for blockchain update is still tolerable; Bitcoin sets it at 10 minutes. As the computational power of miners increases, the difficulty target rises; alternatively, as the computational power of miners decreases, they see their revenues decrease as their share of the total capacity drops. They must either accept it, or invest into further mining equipment and energy to remain competitive. This rate finds a local equilibrium when the expected mining gains balance the running costs, which highly depends on the price of electricity, the exchange rate of BTC, and the computational efficiency of the hardware. A higher exchange rate for BTC — equivalently means a higher value of BTC secured by the blockchain — will increase the potential gain for miners, attracting even more miners and leading to a higher difficulty; a lower exchange rate will see some miners go bankrupt or reduce their activity due to a lack of profitability and will readjust the difficulty to lower levels.

Ultimately, this scheme sees an increasing pool of miners and a rising difficulty as the value of BTC increases. With a higher difficulty, it becomes increasingly difficult for individual miners to control a large stake of the block production: whereas the above example with Satoshi and Hal allowed to control 90% of the block production, a more competitive network with higher difficulty requires a massive investment by Hal in components and electricity to maintain 90% control; an investment which, passed some reasonable, is so high that may be assumed to be virtually impossible. For further discussion, see Chapter 5.

It is through this Satoshi Nakamoto able to create a decentralized consensus model that regulates the block production. Any attempt to build a block and solve the proof-of-work requires a significant investment and faces the competition of other miners. By making block production a competitive market, Satoshi is able to attract thousands of participants that naturally compete to win blocks, and reward them a good enough level of decentralization.

Is this enough? Not quite. The above scheme reduces the pace of block production and defines a fairly fair, decentralized block production algorithm. However, we have not described the behavior of the network when multiple blocks may clash; in other words, when the blockchain forks.

### 3.6.7 Forks and consensus chain

A fork happens when two or more valid blocks refer to the same parent. In case of an ambiguity exists over which block to consider, and which block to build on for further blocks. These forks may have with no intent, for instance when multiple miners happen to solve the proof-of-work almost at the same time, broadcast their freshly produced block, and create confusion with the other network participants who cannot decide which to follow, as illustrated in Figure 3.9. A fork may also be the result of an attack on the system where miners aim to revert or modify the accounting by creating an alternative view of the blockchain where transactions may differ.
