# 12.6 Tokenization of real assets

Tokenization of art, intellectual property, music, and real estate are areas where DLT can be beneficially deployed; see Pentland et al. (2020) and therein.

For brevity, we will restrict ourselves to real estate. The world's real estate (RE) is the most valuable asset class in the world. According to Savills Impact, at the end of 2017, its value reached US$280 trillion, with residential, commercial, and agricultural real estate (RRE, CRE, and ARE for brevity) worth US$220.6 trillion, US$33.3 trillion, and US$27.1 trillion, respectively. When combined, global RE value dwarfs the value of financial instruments, oil reserves, gold, and cryptocurrencies.

Yet RE transactions tend to be lumps, expensive, and notoriously hard to execute. A complication is that, in many countries, establishing a transparent chain of ownership is difficult, if not impossible. Given the nature of RE transactions, DLT can help address some of the aforementioned pain-points. As discussed earlier, revolutionary changes in the way RE is transacted, financed, insured, and held are unlikely. However, even some of the seemingly radical innovations can have profound and significant consequences in reshaping the inefficiencies and asymmetries in RE transactions.

Here is a practical example illustrating the above point succinctly. According to Davies et al. (2020), on March 6, 2019, it took 22 weeks for a residential house in Gillingham, UK, to change hands rather than initially estimated six weeks. It is important to note that there were no additional complications involving uncertainties to house ownership. To check whether DLT can fasten the process, a public/private consortium of Her Majesty's Land Registry's (HMLR) Digital Street, Mishon de Reya, 3, and other parties produced a DLT-based conveyance process. The consortium built a prototype enabling a digital transfer of the property, resulting in the Register's automatic updates. Their prototype distributed ledger was successful beyond expectations. The transaction mentioned above, end to end, in less than 10 minutes, instead of 22 weeks!

There is a lot to learn from this experience from a methodological standpoint. First, the interested parties created a consortium. Second, they created a flow chart of the conveying process. Third, the consortium produced a distributed application built on the Corda DLT (also known as CordaDep), mapping a primary end-to-end contract conveyance. Finally, they used the Corda to replicate the property transaction of interest. As a result, a prototype for executing a fast, trustworthy, and transparent conveyancing process was successfully designed and implemented.

Of course, there are several applications of DLT in RE that go above and beyond the conveyancing process. These applications span the entire spectrum of RE-related activities, stretching from land acquisition and construction of new properties to their sale and subsequent maintenance.
