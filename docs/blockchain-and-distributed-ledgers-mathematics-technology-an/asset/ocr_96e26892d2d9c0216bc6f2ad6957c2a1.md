Bitcoin proposed building so-called colored protocols with the aim to augment the native capabilities of Bitcoin to support the issuance and management of traceable tokens. In part due to the plurality of emerging colored coins, the technology did not succeed at gaining enough momentum to become widespread. The lack of flexibility of the Bitcoin protocol also made it difficult to operate such protocols in practice or to build new functionality on top of tokens. The increasing success of Ethereum and its flexible smart contract structures quickly attracted most tokenization projects.

With its generic smart contracts, Ethereum provided all the tools to create powerful token management contracts. Such contracts would need to maintain the balance of each token holder, to allow transfers, and depending on the contract, the introduction of more advanced features to the lifecycle of the token, such as corporate actions for equity tokens, coupon payments for bond tokens, minting and burning for variable-supply tokens. In 2015, a smart contract interface holding the name of Ethereum Request for Comments number 20 (ERC-20) is proposed to standardize the format of contracts managing fungible tokens and to implement. This is not mandatory but it aims to standardize the interaction with token smart contracts such that the ecosystem can develop new services to interact with tokens under standardization, helped by the relative simplicity of the ERC-20 protocol, quickly becomes a massive success with hundreds of tokens building on this default interface. Colored coins on the Bitcoin ledger lost momentum and get eclipsed by the adoption of the new Ethereum standard.

# 3.7. Initial coin offerings

The Initial Coin Offering (ICO) craze started in 2016. The ICO terminology is coined after the more traditional Initial Public Offering (IPO) related to the listing of a company on an exchange. IPOs have historically been limited to large companies deciding to move away from a private shareholder to a public listing where their shares are traded at a market-defined value. Due to the complexity of existing exchange platforms, and the various regulatory and reporting requirements, IPOs have notoriously been inaccessible to earlier stage companies looking to raise capital or to improve the liquidity of their stocks—whether for cost reasons, or for lack of profitability. ICOs aimed to resolve that and a crowdfunding platform for early stage and later stage ventures also allowing the early listing of shares, or tokens.

A series of early-stage companies prepared white papers and business plans to support the promotion of crowdfunding based on ICO. In practice, the issuing company distributes new tokens in exchange for established cryptocurrencies. The tokens may be denominated in a new cryptocurrency packaged with its own distributed ledger, or may leverage a token protocol on an existing ledger, such as ERC-20 contracts on Ethereum.
