
# 5.5 Mining

## 5.5.1 Consensus

As mentioned in Chapter 3, a distributed ledger, such as Bitcoin, is worth its salt only if it can maintain its integrity via a proper consensus algorithm. In other words, all participants should be able to agree on the validity of all the transactions for the most recent ones. Of course, it is a difficult task in a distributed system, which was viewed as practically impossible for many years. However, by its very existence, the Bitcoin protocol has shown that such a consensus is indeed possible, albeit challenging and expensive to achieve; see Pass et al. (2017).

One can build a simplified consensus algorithm based on the assumption of a random, but fair, node selection. Such selection cannot give every node equal chance because it will make the whole system vulnerable to the so-called Sybil attacks, since acquiring a node, represented by an address, is practically costly, see Subsection 3.6. Thus, the consensus by the majority of nodes does not guarantee the system's integrity. Accordingly, opportunity to be selected as a validator should be proportional to the node's resources spent participating in the game. Of course, miners are not selfless. Their objective is to collect rewards by the system for validating transactions and all transaction fees they get.

As shown in Figure 5.12, participants broadcast new transactions to the entire ecosystem. Every miner collects some of the recent transactions and, for other reasons blocks are not identical. More importantly, if a particular transaction does not pay an attractive fee, it can be left dangling in the memory pool for a long time, or be dropped altogether, which is a feasible aspect of the protocol.

Achieving consensus by mining is done along the following lines, see Subsection 3.6. Once the previous block is included in the blockchain and new transactions announced, miners assemble them into blocks (2MB per block), hash them, and form a new block header. They need to solve a simple but computationally involved puzzle, similar to the one described in Eq. (4.1). Other nodes (tracking nodes) quickly check all the attributes of the block (i.e., inputs, outputs, signatures are valid, etc.), and the block's header (a proper nonce). Although it requires approximately 10 minutes of effort to solve, checking its correctness takes milliseconds. The block ticks but does not necessarily have to be accepted by miners.

Miners express acceptance by including the hash of the accepted block as part of the blocks they are working on. It is essential to recognize that for 60 minutes after a block is mined, there are several branches, all of which could be eligible to the main chain.

> This is not dissimilar to democracy in public companies. While, in theory, every shareholder can speak at the general meeting, their votes are counted proportionally to the number of shares they possess, which loosely correlates with the amount of money they have spent on buying their shares.

As time lapses, one of the chains grows and the longest-chain-wins rule, mentioned in Subsection 5.4.2, preserves miners' collective self-interest. Scenarios when this rule does not hold and leads to forks, are described in Subsection 5.5.7 below.

Miners provide proof-of-work (PoW) by finding nonces which solve the requisite hash puzzles. The difficulty to find nonces: a single one is too short for satisfying the difficulty requirements, as described in Subsection 5.5.3. Usually, in distributed systems and computer security, the assumption is that a percentage of nodes are honest (robust), and the rest are malicious (faulty). The objective of the system's designer is to show that it will operate independent this assumption holds. For the Bitcoin protocol, we define the percentage of robust and faulty nodes by the amount of hash power they control. As we mentioned earlier, this is not dissimilar to how people make their decisions.

However, an alternative way of thinking about this problem is to assume that nodes are neither honest nor malicious, and design a system that promotes honest behavior. We don't know if the honest conduct of nodes is a Nash equilibrium for Bitcoin. In PoW, consensus is robust provided that more than 50% of the hashing power is controlled by “honest” miners; to be more precise, using malicious miners can launch a 51% attack and rewrite the history of blockchain transactions, also described in Subsection 3.6.8. In the context of the Bitcoin protocol, an attack is highly unlikely unless it results in a precipitous drop in the BTC price. This drop would go against miners' self-interest.

An interesting example of PoW is the celebrated Suleiman the Magnificent's Tughra (or seal) shown in Figure 5.16. So much effort went into the production of a Tughra that its forgery was practically impossible. The fact that a forger, if uncovered, would immediately meet a horrible death, helps to preserve the integrity of communications well.

Bitcoin security is sequential. Once a block containing a particular transaction is buried deep in the blockchain, many (no less than six, preferably a few more) blocks built on top of it, the transactions become immutable. It is illustrated in Figure 5.17.

Bitcoin transactions have physical records which are hard to duplicate, while electronic records' duplication is trivial. Therefore Alice can try to double spend transactions: transaction (B), which sends coins from Alice to Bob and transaction (C), which sends the same coins from Alice to Charlie the remainder to Alice. Only one of Bob and Charlie, for that matter, any other provider of goods and services, has to wait and see which transaction is included in a blockchain with certain confirms (no less than six). Only after that, one of them will send their merchandise to Alice. A double attack and a double defense are shown in Figure 5.18, where the arrows are pointers from a block to its predecessor.

> *Some authors argue that a successful attack can be launched by a group of miners who control over 33% of the hashing power; see Eyal and Sirer (2014). We feel that their claims are greatly exaggerated — the very fact that Bitcoin has not collapsed in more than 10 years is the counterexample to their arguments.*
