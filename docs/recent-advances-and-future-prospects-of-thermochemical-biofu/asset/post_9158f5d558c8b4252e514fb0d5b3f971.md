## 3.1. Fully data-driven ML models

### 3.1.1. Yield and composition of products under dry conditions

Generally, the condition of biomass used in thermochemical biofuel conversion processes can be divided into dry and wet conditions, depending on whether external water is added to the conversion processes. The moisture content of the biomass used in those processes was neglected for the classification of the processes. The yield and composition of biomass thermochemical conversion products depend on feedstock characteristics (e.g., proximate and ultimate compositions) and operating conditions, such as temperature, residence time, and heating rate under dry condition [24]. They are also the main input variables used in existing ML-based models. ML-based models with ultimate composition data showed better performance than that with proximate composition data when the same dataset was used in both cases [50]. However, the performance of ML-based models was significantly improved when both composition data were included as input variables [50]. It should be noted that the chemical and structural composition of biomass resources depends on the types of feedstocks [51]. In the case of algal feedstock, the major components are carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins, whereas those of lignocellulose feedstock are hemicellulose, lignin, and cellulose [51–53]. Thus, some studies have also included the content of the major components as input variables for ML model development. The particle size of the biomass was also used as an input variable [27]. In the case of the operating conditions, the temperature, heating rate, retention time, and gas velocity were utilized as input variables.

The yield and composition of products and byproducts are typical output variables for ML-based models. The yield can be calculated as the ratio of the target product mass to the feedstock mass. When various combinations of input variables were tested, a R² value was reached to 0.862 [54–56]. Onsree et al. developed a model incorporating operating environments (N₂, O₂, and CO₂) and showed high performance for predicting biochar yield (R² > 0.915) using datasets with 10 types of feedstocks [57]. The performance of models for predicting the elemental composition and carbon content of biochar ranged from R² = 0.66 [54] to R² = 0.8480 [56]. A higher heating value (HHV) is the calorific value of the fuel associated with quality [58]. For the prediction of the HHV of biochar, a R² value of 0.9052 was achieved by Onsree et al. [57].

For predicting the yield of bio-oil per biomass, a R² value of 0.95 was achieved by the ML model of Ullah et al. [59]. The yield of water, a liquid-phase byproduct produced during thermochemical conversion, was also predicted by ML models [60,61]. The ML prediction of the composition of bio-oil produced under dry conditions is yet to be reported, which might be attributed to the fact that there are insufficient datasets in dry conditions.

H₂, CO, CO₂, and CH₄ are major gas species that evolve from thermochemical conversion and their yields have been commonly considered as the outputs of ML models [62,63]. Yucel et al. used various datasets with different feedstock types to develop ML models for predicting the total gas yield and gas composition of biomass gasification [63]. The performance for the total gas yield and for the gas compositions of H₂, CO, CO₂, and CH₄ were indicated by R² = 0.86, 0.85, respectively. Tang et al. developed a ML model to predict the yields of H₂, CO, CO₂, and CH₄ with an R² value of 0.999 [62]. The model was developed using a relatively large amount of data (3831 data points), but only two feedstocks were used, implying the necessity for further validation of other feedstocks. Ascher et al. developed a ML model to predict the composition of versatile gas species, including H₂, CO, CO₂, CH₄, N₂, and C₂H₄ [50]. The total gas yield, cold gas efficiency, and carbon conversion efficiency were also predicted in this study. ML models have also been developed to predict other output variables such as the total gas yield [55,60,64], HHV [63], lower heating value (LHV) [50,64], H₂ composition [42], CO/CO₂ composition [60], and the maximum yields of H₂, CO, and CH₄ [27]. Additionally, the tar concentration in syngas and total tar yield (per biomass mass) were also predicted based on the development of ML models [65]. The LHV of byproducts produced in dry thermochemical conversion was predicted well, with R² = 0.988 [64].

A few studies have included different gas environments under dry conditions as input variables for ML model development. Zhao et al. included the O₂ composition in gas as an input variable (predictor), and the yields of biochar, biogas, tar, and water were simultaneously predicted [60]. Onsree et al. included the composition of N₂, CO₂, and O₂ in a gaseous environment as input variables, and the yield and HHV of the solid product were predicted [57]. As mentioned above, the inclusion of both proximate and ultimate compositions as input variables usually led to good ML modelling performance. However, different forms of composition-based input variables were utilized. For example, some studies consider ultimate composition in as ratio form, such as the hydrogen/carbon ratio (H/C) and oxygen/carbon ratio (O/C). When various forms of input variables were tested during model development, the performance in the ratio form was better than that of the composition itself [54]. The O/C ratio is reported to be a good measure of carbohydrate, lipid, and protein levels in algae [72]. The yield of bio-oil mainly depends on the fraction of carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins, with lipids as the most significant factor, followed by proteins and carbohydrates [72,73]. A low H/C value in biomass could indicate a large fraction of lignin and a small fraction of hemicellulose [57]. In addition, the O/C and N/C ratios in biochar could be indicators of the polarity of biochar, as well as its surface properties [74]. In the case of lignocellulose biomass, high O/C and H/C ratios imply high fractions of hemicellulose and cellulose and low lignin fractions [51]. These studies have shown that the ratio of elements can better describe specific component (e.g., lignin or hemicellulose), which determines the mechanism of thermochemical conversion of biomass. This implies that choosing the elemental ratios as input variables can be beneficial for ML development.

Hence, it is recommended that the component ratio form that describes the determining factor of thermochemical mechanism is applied for ML development. If such mechanisms are not well known, a systematic search for a ratio form with better performance might shed light on the thermochemical conversion mechanism of biomass.

### 3.1.2. Conversion kinetics and kinetic parameters under dry conditions

Artificial neural networks and random forest have been widely applied to predict the kinetics or kinetic parameters such as the activation energy and pre-exponential factor of the thermochemical conversion of biomass. In addition, thermal gravimetric mass loss of biomass upon heating has been widely predicted using ML models.

Biomass composition was used as an input variable to predict the activation energy of pyrolysis under dry conditions, which was calculated based on various estimation methods such as Kissinger-Akahira-Sunose, Ozawa-Flynn-Wall, and Friedman methods [51]. Based on the data of thermogravimetric analysis, the fractions of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin of various biomass have been used to predict the reaction kinetic parameters, such as the pre-exponential factor (log k0), activation energy (log Ea), and reaction order (log n) [70]. Similar results were obtained for the heating rate used to predict the activation energy of pyrolysis [71]. The time series of mass loss upon algal pyrolysis in an argon environment was predicted using the heating rate, initial temperature, and target temperature of the system [69].

Wei et al. developed a ML model to predict the thermal gravimetric mass-loss curve for the co-combustion of biomass and coal [72]. The input variables included the composition of biomass and coal and their blending ratio and operating parameters, such as heating rate and temperature.

The importance of the input variables was as follows: temperature > blending ratio of biomass and coal > composition of biomass and coal > heating rate. ### 3.1.3. Yield and composition of products under wet conditions

Similar to dry conditions, the prediction of the yield, composition, and properties of solid products (called biochar) produced under wet conditions has been widely studied using various ML models. Under wet conditions, the reactant or water is the supplementary factor for the conversion process. A few studies have included reactant information, such as reactant-to-biomass ratio (or biomass-to-reactant ratio), as input variables [73–76].

Although the biomass-to-water ratio was included as an input variable in these studies [73,74,77], its importance to yield was estimated to be low. The composition of biochar was predicted with diverse output variables, such as carbon content (or carbon recovery) [44,78,79], nitrogen content [77], inorganic phosphorus content [79], atomic ratios [73,78], and ash content of the solid product [73]. Wei et al. predicted the H/C and O/C ratios of solid products before and after the reaction to estimate the product quality [80]. This was based on the fact that energy loss during combustion was lower for the biochar with lower H/C and O/C ratios [81]. The energy recovery and HHV of biochar were predicted using ML models [44,73,78]. In these studies, temperature and retention time were commonly included as input variables; however, different combinations of biomass characteristics were used. In the case of liquid products, higher efforts than under dry conditions are involved in developing ML models for the prediction of yield and composition of bio-oil [44,74,75,82] and byproducts (aqueous co-product) [44,74]. The elemental composition of the bio-oil (C, H, O, N, and S) was predicted [74], and the same was true for the specific composition of C, N, and O [44,83], energy recovery [44] and HHV [74,75] of the bio-oil. When a dataset with various types of reactants and catalysts was used to develop ML models, the types of reactants and catalysts were designated as categorical input variables [75].

Product gases with various compositions and yields produced under wet conditions were predicted using various ML models. The output variables of the developed models included the yield of total produced gas [38,44,74,83], the yield of H₂, CH₄, CO₂, CO, C₂H₄, and C₂H₆ [76,84], and the pollution index (CO₂ + CO) [75]. Notably, most ML models include biomass-to-water or biomass-to-reactant ratios as predictors. The distributions of biochar, syngas, and tar were well predicted by the models with good performance (R² > 0.91) [76]. It was shown that the effect of biomass characteristics was more pronounced for predicting biochar production, whereas temperature was more pronounced for predicting syngas and tar production. In addition, the reactant-to-biomass ratio is an important input variable for the CO₂ fraction in syngas, but not for other gases [76]. Functional groups containing oxygen and hydrogen in biomass are detached from the structure through decarboxylation and dehydration during the thermochemical conversion of biomass under wet conditions [91]. Thus, the changes in the O/C and H/C ratios in biochar can represent the extent of dehydration and decarboxylation during thermochemical conversion, respectively. The smaller the value of the indices, the stronger the decarboxylation and dehydration reactions that occur during thermochemical conversion, and the higher quality of solid fuel [73].
