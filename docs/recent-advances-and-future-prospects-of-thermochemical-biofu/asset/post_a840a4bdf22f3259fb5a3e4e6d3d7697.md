### 4.1.2. Gaseous biofuel

Masala et al. [107] installed a small-scale anaerobic digester with a capacity of 2477 t/year on an isolated island, and then analyzed and optimized three scenarios: (i) gas upgrading for the residential sector, (ii) upgrading for the transport sector, and (iii) electricity production in a combined heat and power plant. Akbulut et al. [108] performed TEA on biogas production in a small dairy farm in Turkey. Based on the operation data, net present value and energetic payback time were calculated. Additionally, the annual amounts of electricity, thermal energy, and CO2 were compared. Vo et al. [109] presented three biogas-upgrading scenarios. The digester and amine scrubber (scenario 1), methanation of CO2 and H2 discharged from the scrubber (scenario 2), and bio-methanation process that reacted biogas and hydrogen without an amine scrubber (scenario 3) were modeled. The process was simulated, and TEA was subsequently performed. The CH4 costs for Scenarios 1, 2, and 3 were 0.76, 1.50, and 1.43/m3, respectively. Rajendran and Murthy [110] reviewed the use of TEA and LCA for anaerobic digestion. The TEAs and LCAs for biogas upgrading were calculated based on simulated processes using operational data. Pääkkönen et al. [111] performed TEA for power to biogas based on the case in which the electricity price fluctuated. Renewable energy (excess electricity) is used to produce hydrogen, which then forms CH4 by reacting with the CO2 in biogas. Using this process, up to 40% of electricity can be stored as biomethane. It was confirmed that profitability can be secured if the electrolyzer investment cost is reduced by 60–72% or the price of CH4 produced is increased by 20–76%. Ferella et al. [112] performed TEA for upgrading biogas in the developed market. Maintaining the existing biogas plant and increasing the production of biomethane by

introducing the pressure swing adsorption process were compared using TEA indicators such as internal rate of return, net present value, and discounted cash flow. Lee et al. [113] performed a TEA for biodiesel production based on biochar, and when the discount rate was 10%, the breakeven price was $1.55/kg, which is competitive with the existing process considering the carbon penalty and tax exemption.

## 4.2. ML-assisted TEA

ML techniques are being applied for TEA. Byun et al. [114] studied the technical, environmental, and economic feasibility of a methanol-steam reforming process based on several techno-economic parameters for H2 production using an ML-based prediction model, as shown in Fig. 5. TEA was performed in three stages as follows. (i) Aspen Plus®-based process simulation including thermodynamic properties and environmental features; (ii) MATLAB-based numerical models for technical and environmental performance; and (iii) ML-based prediction model trained with 12,000 datasets with feasibility outputs of H2 production rate, H2 production cost, and CO2 emissions.

Cheng and Luo [115] introduced ML techniques to perform an economic evaluation of algae-derived biofuels from hydrothermal liquefaction. The ML model developed to predict biocrude energy yield incorporated (i) a biomass productivity model to show the correlation between lipid content and biomass productivity, (ii) a biomass production cost conclusion model to show the correlation between biomass productivity and minimum selling price of biomass, and (iii) biochemical composition. Olafasakin et al. [116] applied ML techniques to TEA for biomass pyrolysis. However, unlike the above two studies, a ROM was utilized, where the chemical reaction mechanism was identified by training with the results of the biomass pyrolysis experiment (see Fig. 6). Technoeconomic analysis was performed based on the obtained ROM that resulted in biofuel production ranging from 65 to 130 gallons per ton of dry biomass, minimum biofuel selling price of $2.62-$5.43 per gallon, and global warming potential of −13.62 to 145 kg of CO₂ per MJ. These results suggest that ROM can facilitate rapid feedstock screening in biorefinery systems.

Vondra et al. [117] developed a descriptive economic model based on ML to evaluate the economics of evaporation systems in biogas plants and provided a heuristic method for decision makers. Using a new techno-economic model, the economic aspects of implementing the latest vacuum evaporation system using the waste heat from the combined heat and power device of a biogas plant were studied. To consider the real-world probability and robustness of the analysis, Monte Carlo simulation techniques were used to generate >20,000 different possibilities for implementing an evaporation system. Subsequently, an advantageous decision path was selected through a new methodology that utilized an ANN and a hyper-optimized decision tree classifier.

## 5. Challenges and prospects

Although applications of the ML models for thermochemical biofuel conversion prediction have been extensively explored, these studies are still incapable of extending towards actual implementation and plant-scale optimization. Various reviews have emphasized the importance of datasets and theory-integrated ML models in improving the ML models for practical applications. In the case of datasets, the importance of training with small datasets, multi-view data, uncertainty quantification, and data quality has been emphasized [118,119]. Although multi-view data include more comprehensive information for ML modelling [118], it could deteriorate the training procedure owing to the increased complexity of model structure. However, this can be overcome by using the compact architecture of ML model. It was shown that the compact form of neural network provided the best performance without increasing the number of neurons or complexity [120]. In addition, most studies used the composition of biomass that was summed up to 100%. Because there is an intercorrelation between compositions (or input variables), balance-related issues and the usage of independently measured compositions should be explored further [55]. The important aspects of data collection will be discussed in the following section. Theory-integrated ML models are growing in diverse fields because of their greater extrapolability and interpretability [121]. However, their application in biomass thermochemical conversion is still limited. In the next section, the possible integration of theory-based and ML models for predicting thermochemical conversion of biomass will be discussed.

### 5.1. Comprehensive data set

More comprehensive datasets should be created covering a wide range of feedstocks and operating conditions for developing models that are more generative to thermochemical conversion process of biomass. This can be achieved by creating a dataset following a standardized protocol. First, the characterization of biomass and operating conditions must be standardized. The composition of biomass is commonly analyzed using ultimate and proximate analyses. Some existing studies only provided one of these analyses, resulting in an incomplete dataset. Details of gas environment (air, CO₂, or inert gases (Ar or N₂)) should also be reported because of its significant influences towards the thermochemical conversion of biomass. A few studies have failed to provide necessary information on the gas environment.

Second, it is recommended to avoid using categorical input variables in the dataset. In some studies, the reactor type, catalyst type, and feedstock were included as categorical input variables for convenience. Such categorical variables can help ML models distinguish between different reactors, but they do not provide information about the extent to which the reactors differ. An ideal ML model should be able to predict all the output variables, differentiating reactor types and catalyst types to great accuracy and granularity. Defining categorical input variables using numerical variables can contribute to the improvement of the extrapolation of ML models. Fluidized and fixed beds are widely used for biomass thermochemical conversion and these reactors are commonly characterized by gas velocity and biomass density. If these reactors can be defined with common variables in a dataset, it will improve the performance of ML model by increasing the amount of available data [122]. Similarly, reactants (e.g., acetone or ethanol) with different effects on thermochemical conversion have also used as categorical input variables for ML modelling [74]. The dielectric constant of the reactants can be a candidate input variable and significantly influences the reaction kinetics in the thermochemical conversion of biomass. Because of the difficulty in generalizing their structures, both the catalyst and feedstock were normally represented as categorical input variables. The graph neural network (GNN) is based on graph-structure data, and it has become popular because it can predict various molecular properties by generalizing molecular representation [123].

A molecule can be

represented as a topological graph where atoms and chemical bonds are considered nodes and edges, respectively [124]. Thus, atoms and bonds of catalysts and feedstocks can be generalized in graph-structure data, and GNN based on them can serve as a promising method for mitigating the adverse impact of categorical variables on ML modelling [124]. The GNN has been used to predict biodiesel combustion performance [124].

### 5.2. Integration of ML models with theory-based models

Combining ML models with a theory-based model can leverage their respective advantages [121]. The objective of this combination is usually to improve the predictive capability. Biomass thermochemical conversion processes are complicated and not fully understood. The proper integration of ML and theory-based models could be beneficial for solving chemical and biological complexity. Aykol et al. summarized possible integration methods for other complicated systems [98]. Among the integration method suggested by Aykol et al. [98], parameter learning can be the one easily implementable. In such integration, complex behavior, such as reaction kinetics, can be modeled by a data-driven model, whereas relatively well-known transport phenomena are described by a theory-based model. For biomass conversion, not all reaction kinetics are understood, but the reaction kinetic parameters that explain the experimental data can be predicted by ML models. The utilization of the obtained kinetic parameters with a theory-based model or process simulation can improve the interpretability of the final result. In addition, GNNs have received increasing attention owing to their ability to capture interatomic potential or the relationship between molecular structure and properties. The application of GNN to account for biomass structures would contribute to the generalization of various feedstocks. For example, the reaction kinetic parameters of pyrolysis could be modeled with GNN, and the kinetic parameters can be then utilized for process simulation. With such a GNN model, faster design of the thermochemical conversion process of new biomass can be achieved given the structure of the new feedstock can be rapidly characterized.

Another type of integrated modelling is theory-constrained ML, which is also known as physics-guided ML modelling. In general, the loss function (or error function) of a ML model is the summation (or mean) of the squared error between the target and prediction values. When a physics-guided loss term is added to the loss function, the ML model attempts to find the optimal weights and biases by reducing the physics-guided loss term [125]. Such physics-guided loss terms can encompass physics laws such as mass or energy conservation. In the case of syngas production, when physics-guided loss originating from the carbon balance before and after the conversion process is implemented, the biomass conversion process can be modelled with more rigorous mass balance.

The third is ML-accelerated theory-based modeling, in which nonlinear partial differential equations are rapidly solved by ML. This is a rapidly growing area, and there have been applications in diverse fields such as fluid mechanics and Schrodinger's equation [126]. The strength of the ML-accelerated theory-based modeling is based on the fact that a neural network is a universal approximator and includes automatic differentiation [98,127]. Thus, the neural network model can solve the underlying nonlinear partial differential equation, simultaneously satisfying the boundary conditions and physics laws. Solving partial differential equations in fluid dynamics is also important for the biomass thermochemical conversion process, in which a fluidized bed is widely utilized. In addition, thermochemical conversion via catalysis involves complex reaction kinetics. Thus, solving the governing equations of transport phenomena and reaction kinetics could contribute to the development of models for more sophisticated biomass thermochemical conversion processes in the future.
