It can be classified into conventional and hydrothermal types depending on the type of gasifying agent, reaction conditions, and quality of the produced syngas [2,28]. Unlike other thermochemical technologies that normally favor dry biomass, hydrothermal treatment can transform wet biomass into gases, solid fuels, and other constituents at reasonable energy efficiencies. Hydrothermal treatment involves the utilization of hot pressurized water as a reactant to produce biofuels [12]. It can be classified as either hydrothermal carbonation or hydrothermal liquefaction, based on the temperature, pressure, and proportions of the converted intermediate products [29]. The design of these processes presents various challenges, mostly owing to their multiparametric and complex characteristics. In addition, serious consideration should be given to catalyst [30], in terms of basic properties [31] that increased the yield of oil and gas while that of char is suppressed [32].

Typical thermochemical conversion research often requires rigorous experiments to determine the optimal design and operation [5,12]. Over the years, novel modeling techniques, including computational fluid dynamics (CFD), kinetic and thermodynamic models, and process models, have been employed to accelerate process optimization while reducing operation time and cost [33–37]. However, modeling using these conventional techniques is sometimes unrealistic and difficult [38,39]. This is because these models are based on a variety of assumptions, either elaborated or oversimplified, affecting the quality of the model predictions and/or leading to great difficulty in implementation [40]. In addition, these models are difficult to incorporate under different operating conditions [35,41]. For example, CFD is time-consuming and computationally expensive because it requires a significant amount of processing power and predefined engine-specific parameters [36,37]. Therefore, developing an accurate, non-time-consuming, non-computationally expensive, and robust modeling approach to model the processes is of utmost importance.

Machine Learning (ML) techniques have proven to be reliable alternatives to the traditional modeling techniques for studying and comprehending complex conversion processes. They can model complex problems by leveraging a series of interconnections between the input and output parameters. Recently, the application of ML approaches to model thermochemical biofuel conversion processes have increased significantly. The applications include the prediction of the yield and composition of products, kinetic parameters, and calorific values of the products. The ML models developed have also been used to determine the optimal conditions for desired production. The preprocessing or normalization of input variables with distinctive magnitudes is a hyperparameter that contributes to improve the performance of machine learning-based models. However, some did not state the measures adopted to prevent overfitting, which is a crucial challenge against the model improvement [37,42–44].

Previous reviews summarized the ML application of biomass conversion processes, where specific datasets (e.g., not diverse type of feedstock and operating conditions) were utilized. The developed ML models showed good performance, but extrapolation or further improvement of the ML model was limited by the variability in the conversion process such as types of reactors, feedstocks, and operating conditions. In real applications, the development of a universal ML model is necessary to produce biofuel stably regardless of the condition of biomass feedstock. As a first step to develop such universal model, the variability in the dimensions of the dataset, as well as the number of data points, were compared in this study. Preprocessing and the possibility of overfitting in the developed ML are also discussed. Then, this review summarized the results of estimating the importance of input variables in individual ML models in order to discuss which input variables are important. Finally, relevant challenges in the development of universal ML model are summarized and potential solutions and future research directions are suggested.

# 2. General overview of machine learning

Machine learning methods can be classified as supervised, semi-supervised, unsupervised, or reinforcement learning. In supervised learning methods, labeled input and output datasets are used for the training and validation of the model. The model then learns to map the input data to its targeted output [45]. Supervised learning methods can be categorized into regression and classification methods. Classification methods seek to identify behavioral patterns across datasets, whereas regression methods aim to establish the best match between the input and output parameters [46].

In contrast to supervised learning, unsupervised machine learning, also known as self-organization, works best with unlabeled data and is excellent at detecting any possible hidden patterns. As input data are fed into an unsupervised ML model, the model seeks and classifies the similarities and differences between the data accordingly. Examples of unsupervised ML include principal component analysis, dimensionality reduction, and clustering. Semi-supervised learning combines the advantages of supervised and unsupervised learning. It aids in the resolution of supervised learning challenges and can handle systems with both unlabeled and labeled inputs [45]. It is worth noting that labeling data for supervised learning is often difficult and time-consuming. Semi-supervised learning can overcome this drawback by incorporating a portion of the total unlabeled input into the supervised learning process [45,47].

The last class of ML algorithms, reinforcement learning, involves a learning system called an agent, which understands its environment through a series of actions that could give it a reward or penalty. A positive action yields rewards and negative action results in a penalty. The agent allows a better understanding of its environment without human intervention, based on maximizing profit and reducing penalties [39,48]. Several ML algorithms have been utilized for different aspects of biomass thermochemical conversion processes. The most widely explored algorithms are artificial neural networks (ANN). Other less popular algorithms include decision trees, random forests, support vector machines, deep neural networks, and hybrid intelligent models. # 3. Machine learning (ML) applications in thermochemical biofuel conversion processes

Thermochemical conversion processes convert biomass resources into various biofuels such as hydrogen, methane, biogas, bio-oil and biochar. To maximize the selectivity or yield of biofuel and the conversion efficiency, various ML approaches have been applied to the thermochemical biofuel conversion processes. Before applying ML methods, the performance of a trained model needs to be assessed using the test dataset acquired from thermochemical biofuel conversion processes. Most studies describe the performance of models using a correlation coefficient (R²) or root mean square error, which is sufficient for a single-output model. However, in the case of a multioutput model, the performance of model should consider the different magnitudes of each output, and the role of R² should be limited to evaluating the correlation of each output. Thus, for multioutput models, relative measures, such as the average percentage error or root relative squared error, are recommended to compare the averaged performance of different models.

It is important to know how the prediction of ML models could deviate from experimental values, but few studies provided the respective yield values in ML studies. Instead, some studies provided the averaged percentage error between experimental value and prediction.
