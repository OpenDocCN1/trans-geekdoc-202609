

# The Other Side of Security Assessment: Penetration Testing

The other side of security assessment vulnerability analysis is **penetration testing**, or **pen testing**. Penetration testing is simply attacking a network in an effort to find security weaknesses. This essentially involves attempting to break into your own (or your client's) system. Penetration testing exists so that an organization can use resources (external or internal) to attempt to break into its network or systems and possibly attack them in order to gain information about their network vulnerabilities so that they can assess their risk level.

Penetration testing not only looks for vulnerabilities but also searches **exploit** databases (computer programs designed to take advantage of a specific vulnerability). These exploits may be used to deliver a payload to a target system, thereby granting a hacker access to that system.

Penetration testing is a booming business for outside security firms. This service can be a valuable tool to help an organization find its network vulnerabilities. However, there are also many open-source penetration testing tools available for individuals or organizations to use. Certain Linux distributions not only contain penetration testing utilities but also **vulnerability emulators** that can be deployed as targets for these tests.

## The Penetration Testing Process

There are multiple standard frameworks and methodologies for conducting penetration testing. The following are some of the most famous penetration testing frameworks:

*   Open Source Security Testing Methodology Manual (OSSTMM, `www.isecom.org/research.html#content5-9d`)
*   Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES, `www.pentest-standard.org`)
*   NIST SP 800-115 (`https://csrc.nist.gov/publications/detail/sp/800-115/final`)
*   Information Systems Security Assessment Framework (ISSAF, `www.oissg.org/issaf`)
*   Open Web Application Security Project Testing Guide (OWASP, `http://owasp.org/www-project-web-security-testing-guide`)
*   Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain (`www.lockheedmartin.com/en-us/capabilities/cyber/cyber-kill-chain.html`)

Each of these penetration testing methodologies presents its own approach to the penetration testing process. However, all methods share three general phases of the penetration testing process:

*   **Negotiation**: Define and agree upon the scope of the test
    *   Agree on personnel involved.
    *   Commit to rules of engagement to be adopted.
*   **Penetration Testing**: Conduct the attack
    *   Assess risk.
    *   Conduct discovery.
    *   Develop a test plan.
    *   Conduct technical security testing.
*   **Documentation**: Generate reports and documentation
    *   Analyze test results.
    *   Publish and deliver test results.

### Negotiating the Penetration Test

The skills used by a penetration tester to research and attack a network are no different from those used by a **black hat hacker** to gain access to an organization's network. However, the primary difference is that a legitimate penetration tester is a **white hat hacker** who will not use their skills to steal valuable sensitive information or destroy organizational property. Instead, they will generate and provide a report to the organization, called a **briefing**. This briefing will highlight the weak points in the organization's security policies and practices found during the test, including items such as how a hacker could bypass network perimeter defenses to gain access.

In cases where a company hires an outside penetration testing organization or firm to conduct the test, both parties will determine who plays what role in the test. There are usually three groups:

*   The **red team**, which conducts the attack
*   The **blue team**, usually consisting of the victims (i.e., network administrators)
*   The **white team**, which monitors the test

One might initially think that a penetration testing firm would simply come in and execute a full suite of attacks to ensure the network is 100% tested for vulnerabilities. However, this is usually not the case. Before testing begins, the penetration testers and the client must negotiate and clearly establish the exact parameters of the test.

For example, many penetration testing firms feel that since hackers have no restrictions on what they can or cannot attack, penetration testers should not have restrictions either. This idea is very sound but makes some companies uncomfortable, which is why a great deal of time is spent defining what can be attacked.

It is also important for the company to set a reasonable window of time for the attack. In this case, the company knows that if a breach occurs outside that window, it is not the penetration testers but a genuine malicious attack.

Key items that must be defined in this phase include:

*   **What is to be accomplished?** Perhaps the company has a problem with phishing attacks where users inadvertently give away passwords. In this case, the goal would be to reduce the number of successful phishing attempts.
*   **What systems (people, or otherwise) can be attacked, and for how long?** For example, if I am conducting a phishing attack, the penetration tester is not necessarily interested in attacking things outside the scope of the target. Remember that depending on how the company operates, this can be a very gray area and is often one of the largest areas the white team administrators struggle to define.
*   **Who in the blue team will know about the attack?** Suppose you are conducting a social engineering attack with the goal of infiltrating the company to gain access to the server room. What happens when you are walking down the hall looking very suspicious and someone challenges you? What if they ask for ID and you fail to get a visitor badge from the front desk? Perhaps they will just escort you out of the building. However, if this happens at night when the building is closed, the outcome could be very different, and you might end up in handcuffs. Having the company contact on speed dial, or having a letter of authorization from the Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) or Chief Security Officer (CSO) would be very handy. In other words, it is important that someone in the company knows, but they are not involved in the test itself. That way, if something unexpected happens, you can quickly contact that person and explain the situation.
*   **Legal issues**: There are many laws regarding ethical and non-ethical hacking. The company needs to know what they are getting into, and the penetration testing firm needs to know what is legal and illegal at the local, state, and federal levels. The company needs to ensure the penetration testers have signed all necessary confidentiality and non-disclosure agreements required by federal law.

### Types of Penetration Tests

Once the details of the penetration test have been negotiated and agreed upon, the next phase involves executing the various attacks that have been agreed upon. Basically, penetration testing can be conducted in three scenarios:

*   **Black Box Testing**: Black box penetration testing is a method of penetration testing in which the ethical hacker has no prior knowledge of the system or network infrastructure being attacked, and the client company provides no such critical details.
*   **White Box Testing**: White box penetration testing is a method of penetration testing in which the ethical hacker has full and complete knowledge of the system or network infrastructure being attacked.
*   **Gray Box Testing**: An ethical hacker conducting gray box penetration testing will have partial knowledge of the target system or infrastructure they are attempting to attack.
