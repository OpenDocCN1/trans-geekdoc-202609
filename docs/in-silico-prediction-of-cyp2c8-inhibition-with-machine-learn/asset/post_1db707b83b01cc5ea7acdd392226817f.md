# In Silico Prediction of CYP2C8 Inhibition with Machine-Learning Methods

Xiaoxiao Zhang, Piaopiao Zhao, Zhiyuan Wang, Xuan Xu, Guixia Liu, Yun Tang, and Weihua Li*

Cite This: Chem. Res. Toxicol. 2021, 34, 1850−1859

Read Online

ACCESS | Metrics & More | Article Recommendations | Supporting Information

ABSTRACT: Cytochrome P450 2C8 (CYP2C8) is a major drug-metabolizing enzyme in humans and is responsible for the metabolism of ~5% drugs in clinical use. Thus, inhibition of CYP2C8, which causes potential adverse drug events, cannot be neglected. The in vitro drug interaction studies guidelines for industry issued by the FDA also point out that it needs to be determined whether investigated drugs are CYP2C8 inhibitors before clinical trials. However, current studies mainly focus on predicting the inhibitors of other major P450 enzymes, and the importance of CYP2C8 inhibition has been overlooked. Therefore, there is a need to develop models for identifying potential CYP2C8 inhibition. In this study, in silico classification models for predicting CYP2C8 inhibition were built by five machine-learning methods combined with nine molecular fingerprints. The performance of the models built was evaluated by test and external validation sets. The best model had AUC values of 0.85 and 0.90 for the test and external validation sets, respectively. The applicability domain was analyzed based on the molecular similarity and exhibited an impact on the improvement of prediction accuracy. Furthermore, several representative privileged substructures such as 1H-benzo[d]imidazole, 1-phenyl-1H-pyrazole, and quinoline were identified by information gain and substructure frequency analysis. Overall, our results would be helpful for the prediction of CYP2C8 inhibition.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Inhibition of cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYPs) is one of the most important reasons for adverse drug reactions and drug delisting.[1] Therefore, it needs to be determined whether drug candidates are inhibitors of major drug-metabolizing P450s before clinical trials. The in vitro drug interaction studies guidance for industry issued by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) stipulates that drug developers need to evaluate whether drugs inhibit the major P450 metabolic enzymes, including CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4.[2]

Experimental identification of P450 inhibition is generally time consuming, costly, and labor intensive. Thus, many in silico models have been developed to identify P450 inhibition.[3–8] At present, the in silico prediction for P450 inhibition reported in the literature is mainly based on machine-learning methods, which are increasingly used in data analysis, classification problems, and other fields.[9–11] Shao et al. used the rule-based C5.0 decision tree algorithm to build classification models for the inhibitors of five major P450s. Rydberg et al. exploited a support vector machine (SVM) to classify inhibitors and noninhibitors for the five main P450 enzymes and developed the WhichCyp Web server for predicting the P450 inhibitors. Our group[12] employed an artificial neural network to develop a combined classifier, which is used to predict the inhibitors of five major P450 metabolic enzymes. Recently, Li et al.[13] developed a multitask model based on an autoencoder deep neural network to discriminate inhibitors and noninhibitors of the five major P450 isoforms. Wu et al.[14] used two ensemble-learning and two deep-learning methods to build classification models to predict inhibitors for the five major P450 enzymes. Although the reported models demonstrated relatively high accuracy, these studies mainly focused on the five major P450 isoforms including CYP3A4, 2D6, 2C9, 2C19, and 1A2. Other major drug-metabolizing P450s have not been involved in the public reports.

CYP2C8 is a member of the CYP2C subfamily, accounting for about 6−7% of the total P450 enzymes in the liver.[12] CYP2C8 is responsible for the metabolism of ~5% marketed drugs,[13] including amodiaquine, ceveratinone, and esziltaxeme.[14] In the past decade, the importance of CYP2C8 in drug response has been recognized due to adverse reactions resulting from drug−drug interactions and genetic polymorphism.[14] For example, the combination of the inhibitor gemfibrozil and the substrate civezine of CYP2C8 has caused serious adverse drug reactions.[15] Therefore, rapid identification of CYP2C8 inhibition is of great significance for guiding the design and development of new drugs. At present, however, no prediction models have been reported for CYP2C8 inhibition.

In this study, we developed in silico models for classifying CYP2C8 inhibitors and noninhibitors by multiple machine-learning methods. The robustness of the built models was evaluated by the test and external validation sets. On the basis of the Euclidean distance method, the applicability domain was defined to exclude some extreme compounds. In addition, the differences in structural characteristics between inhibitors and noninhibitors were detected by information gain and substructure frequency analysis to derive privileged substructures accounting for the CYP2C8 inhibition.

## 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

### 2.1. Data Collection and Preparation

In this study, we collected the data sets from the PubChem bioassay database[16] and two publications.[14,17] Compared to other literature reports, the data provided by these two publications is more comprehensive, basically covering the currently known CYP2C8 inhibitors.

The data sets used for developing classification models were manually collected from the PubChem bioassay database[16] and the literature published in 2005.[17] For the data from PubChem, compounds with activity ≤ 50 μM or explicitly reported as active by ChemBL are flagged as active in the CYP2C8 bioassay representations. The active type used for each compound is one of IC50, INH, and Ki. From the literature from 2005,[17] 209 drugs at 30 μM were examined for the inhibition of CYP2C8. Among them, 48 compounds exhibited greater than 50% inhibition and were further evaluated for measurement of IC50. The results showed that the IC50 values of 6 drugs are less than 1 μM, 17 drugs are between 1 and 10 μM, and 25 drugs are in the range from 10 to 40 μM. The compound activity criteria in the literature and in PubChem are similar. Therefore, the data from PubChem and the literature, we regarded the compounds labeled "active" as CYP2C8 inhibitors and "inactive" as noninhibitors. The chemicals without compound values or literature support were deleted. The 48 compounds collected from the literature[17] were categorized into inhibitors and noninhibitors. All molecules were converted into simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) strings. In order to get high-quality data, the two data sets were carefully preprocessed by the following steps:
- organic substances were retained,
- salts were converted to the corresponding bases or acids, and
- duplicate chemical structures were removed.

Then, the CYP2C8 inhibitors were marked as "1" and noninhibitors as "0". Finally, the pretreated data sets were randomly divided into a training set and a test set with a ratio of 8:2 by an in-house python script.

To assess the generalization ability of the built models, an external validation set was constructed from different sources, including the DrugBank database,[18] the literature by Backman et al.,[15] and the Indiana University School of Medicine ( https://drug-interactions. medicine.iu.edu/Main/Table.aspx ). Similar to the data processing for PubChem, the drugs obtained from the DrugBank without a literature source were excluded. The remaining CYP2C8 inhibitors were considered as the positive data. In addition, six CYP2C8 inhibitors collected from the Indiana University School of Medicine data set were added to the positive data set. The compounds with IC50 > 100 μM reported in the literature were regarded as the negative data. For the external validation set, the same data preprocessing procedure as in the training and test sets was applied and the duplicate data in the training or test sets were excluded.

The diversity and quality of the data set are crucial for building a highly robust model.[6,13] In our work, we exploited the chemical space distribution and Tanimoto average similarity index[20] to evaluate the chemical diversity of the data sets. The chemical space distribution is defined as a two-dimensional space distribution map of the molecular weight (MW) and the lip/water partition coefficient (ALogP), which were calculated by the Pipeline Pilot 2017 R2 software.[19] The Tanimoto similarity index, having a value between 0 and 1, was calculated using the python script. The smaller the value, the less similar the structures of two compounds.

### 2.2. Molecular Representation

Molecular fingerprints were employed to represent the molecules in the above data sets. A molecule can be translated into a binary string of structural keys and an index for input to machine-learning models by fingerprints. Nine types of molecular fingerprints were calculated by the PADEL-Descriptor software tool,[21] including the Atom Pairs 2D fingerprint (AP2D, 780 bits), Estate fingerprint (Est, 79 bits), Extended fingerprint (Ext, 512 bits), Fingerprint-FP (FP, 1024 bits), Graph only fingerprint (Gra, 1024 bits), Klekota-Roth fingerprint (KR, 4860 bits), MACCS fingerprint (Mac, 166 bits), Pubchem fingerprint (Pub, 881 bits), and Substructure fingerprint (Sub, 307 bits). The detailed descriptions of these fingerprints can be found in the original literature.[1,2,5]

### 2.3. Construction of the Models

In this study, five widely used machine-learning algorithms were employed to build the predictive models of CYP2C8 inhibitors and noninhibitors, including support vector machine (SVM),[23] random forest (RF),[24] k-nearest neighbor (KNN),[25] neural network (NN),[26] and logistic regression (LR).[27] All algorithms were implemented using the scikit-learn package version 0.21.4.

Since several machine-learning algorithms were heavily affected by the parameter settings, k-fold cross validation was exploited to traverse all of the data in a training set. First, the training set was divided into k subsets, k − 1 subsets were first used to build the models, and the remaining subset was applied for model validation. This process was repeated k times. Then a grid search algorithm was chosen to select an optimal parameter for the machine-learning method. In this study, the value of k was set to 10. ### 2.4. Performance Evaluation of Models

In this study, the 10-fold cross validation of the training set was employed to examine the robustness of the built models. The test set was applied to further evaluate the predictive power, and the external validation set from different sources was used to assess the models generalization ability. Several indicators were calculated to evaluate the quality of the models intuitively. In binary classification models, data sets are generally divided into two categories, positive and negative. True positive (TP) means that CYP2C8 inhibitors are correctly predicted to be positive. True negative (TN) indicates that CYP2C8 noninhibitors are accurately predicted to be negative. False positive (FP) means that CYP2C8 noninhibitors are incorrectly predicted as inhibitors, while false negative (FN) infers that CYP2C8 inhibitors are incorrectly predicted as noninhibitors.[28] On the basis of these definitions, sensitivity (SE), specificity (SP), Mathews' correlation coefficient (MCC), and classification accuracy (CA) were calculated using the following formula

$$ SE = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \quad (1) $$

$$ SP = \frac{TN}{TN + FP} \quad (2) $$

$$ CA = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} \quad (3) $$

$$ MCC = \frac{(TP \times TN - FP \times FN)}{\sqrt{(TP + FP)(TP + FN)(TN + FP)(TN + FN)}} \quad (4) $$
