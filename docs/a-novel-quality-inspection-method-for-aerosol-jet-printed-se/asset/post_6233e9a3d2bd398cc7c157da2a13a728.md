## ABSTRACT

The quality of printed electronic sensors by aerosol jet printing (AJP) process is hard to guarantee due to an insufficient reproducibility of the AJP process. This paper proposes a novel quality inspection method to identify defects on the printed sensor by AJP process using infrared imaging and machine learning. Potentially defective regions with high temperature distributions on printed lines are estimated from the infrared imaging when the current is applied. Demanded regions for the repair are identified by a machine learning algorithm. Finally, the applicability of the proposed method has been demonstrated by repair experiments.

## 1. Introduction

Aerosol jet printing (AJP) process, also known as aerosol jet deposition process, is a non-contact additive manufacturing technique [1]. The AJP process utilizes a fine mist of suspended particles in a gas, called an aerosol, to deposit precise and uniform layers of material onto a substrate [1]. This process can produce high resolution, three-dimensional (3D) structures with feature sizes as small as 10 µm [2]. AJP has a wide range of applications, including the production of microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), circuit boards, solar cells, as well as for the creation of intricate patterns and structures in a variety of materials, including organic semiconductors, ceramics, and carbon nanotubes [3–7]. It has also been used for the repair of PCB traces and flat panel displays [8].

Despite the advantages of AJP, adoption of the technology is hindered by process drift. The process drift is a phenomenon where the printed line morphology can vary over time, despite no change in the process parameters. In this case, it is caused by a gradual shift in operating conditions of the printing process. Such a shift in conditions can be attributed to several factors. For example, ink particles collide with the walls while being carried by the carrier gas during printing. These particles adhere to the walls and do not get deposited, affecting the deposition rate. Buildup of such particles can lead to clogging of the nozzle, causing further deviation of the deposition rate [9]. Process drift affects the repeatability of the aerosol jet printing process. Thus, despite using the same materials and process parameters, the thickness of a printed electronic sensor can vary stochastically along its geometry, affecting its electrical properties.

Machine learning (ML) approaches are currently being used to assess quality in different AM processes. A vision-based online monitoring system, for example, can be used to examine surface quality and detect

internal defects in selective laser melting (SLM) and directed energy deposition (DED) [10–13]. The ML-powered automated inspection system eliminates the need for time-consuming human inspections while also paving the way for closed-loop quality assurance. However, because of the limited process information that can be captured in the AJP system, implementing an automated quality inspection system in AJP is challenging. However, the research on ML-assisted automated quality inspection system for the printed sensor by AJP process has hardly been performed yet.

This research investigates the quality of printed sensors using thermal properties and a current. The objective of this research is to propose a novel quality inspection method for identifying defects on the printed sensor by AJP process using infrared imaging and a machine learning approach. Potentially defective regions with high temperature distributions on printed lines are estimated from the infrared imaging when the current is applied. Demanded regions for the repair are identified by a machine learning algorithm. Finally, the applicability of the proposed method has been demonstrated by repair experiments.

## 2. Methodology and features

A whole procedure of the proposed quality inspection and repair method for aerosol jet printed sensor is shown in Figs. 1 and 2. The sensor is printed by the AJ5X aerosol jet printer from Optomec, as illustrated by the schematic diagram in Fig. 1(a). The ink is maintained at 25 °C by the water bath and a cooler. The ultrasonic transducer atomizes the ink particles into an aerosol cloud. The carrier gas stream, which is nitrogen gas, entrains the aerosolized ink and carries it to the virtual impactor, where excess carrier gas is diverted away, concentrating the ink particles. The aerosol stream then travels to the print head. There, the sheath gas enwraps the ink, forming a collimated stream of ink particles. The focused ink aerosol would impact the substrate and deposit the ink particles. The substrate is a polyimide film and the nozzle used has a 200 µm diameter orifice. The adjustable parameters of the AJP process includes sheath gas flow rate, carrier gas flow rate, atomizer current, print speed (which is the platform moving speed, as the print head is stationary). The printed line features are also influenced by factors such as ink viscosity, substrate properties, and print head configuration.

A VarioCAM HD Head 600 thermographic camera from InfraTec was used as the thermographic camera to capture the thermal data during the heating process caused by the current passing through the sensor. The current passing through the printed sensor can generate heat, as governed by the following equation:

```
P = I²R
```

where P, I and R are the power generated in the printed sensor, the current passing through the printed sensor and the localized resistance of the sensor, respectively.

With increased power, additional thermal energy is produced, and the temperature rises. The defective areas that demonstrate increased resistance will be heated up more quickly than the normal lines. Therefore, it can be observed by infrared thermographic imaging. Fig. 1(c) depicts the proposed thermographic pictures recorded by the infrared camera, which display potential defective spots and lines. To further evaluate the quality of the printed sensor, it was then put under a confocal microscope to examine the morphology of the printed lines. Key quality indicators can be retrieved from the microscope pictures, such as line width, height, and cross-sectional area. It was then connected to the temperature value at relevant regions from the thermographic images using machine learning (ML).

Meanwhile, the confocal microscope is used to inspect the real quality of the printed lines. The thermographic images are sent to a supervised machine learning model to predict the defective spot's locations, which is later confirmed by the microscope quality inspection results. Predicted defective locations will be sent back to the AJP machine for defect correction as shown in Fig. 1(d). The whole process continues until the part is finished.

In this research, a support vector machine (SVM) algorithm is used to differentiate defective thermal image to non-defective images, as shown in Fig. 3. The SVM algorithm is a supervised machine learning algorithm that can be used for classification and regression tasks. The main idea behind SVM is to find the best boundary, called a hyperplane, that separates the different classes in the feature space. To achieve this, SVM maps the input data into a higher dimensional space, called the kernel space, where a linear boundary can be found. The mapping is done using a mathematical function called a kernel, which can be linear, polynomial, or radial basis functions (RBF).

In the context of predicting aerosol jet printed line height using temperature values, SVM is used as a classifier. The process involves training the model on a dataset containing temperature values and corresponding line heights, and then using the trained model to predict the line height of new data points based on their temperature values. The accuracy of the predictions can be evaluated using metrics such as precision, recall, and F1-score. An expected outcome of using SVM for this task would be high accuracy and robustness to noise and high dimensional data. Additionally, SVM is a powerful tool to handle non-linearly separable data, which is common in many real-world datasets. The selection of the appropriate kernel function, such as RBF, can improve the results of the model by capturing the complex non-linear relationships between the features. Therefore, SVM is a suitable choice for predicting aerosol jet printed line quality using temperature values as it can provide accurate and robust predictions. Its ability to handle non-linearly separable data and its robustness to noise make it well suited for this task. Additionally, the use of appropriate kernel function can improve the model performance.
