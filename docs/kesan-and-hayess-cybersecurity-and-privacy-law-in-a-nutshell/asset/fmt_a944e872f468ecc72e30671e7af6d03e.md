
# GDPR Data Subject Rights and Lawful Processing

## Data Subject Rights (Article 12, Chapter 3)

Article 12 of the GDPR requires controllers to inform data subjects of their rights under the GDPR. These rights are enumerated in the remainder of Chapter 3 of the GDPR:

- **Right to be informed** about the processing of their personal data (Articles 13–15)
- **Right to correct** their personal data (Article 16)
- **Right to restrict or object** to the processing of their personal data (Articles 18 and 21)
- **Right to data portability** — obtain their personal data in a machine readable format (Article 20)
- **Right to be forgotten** (Article 17) — requires controllers to have the ability to delete all of a data subject’s personal data
- **Notification obligation** (Article 19) — controllers must notify any recipients of changes to personal data made pursuant to a subject’s request
- **Right to human review** (Article 22) — data subjects have the right to have their personal data processed by a human instead of an automated system if the processing could potentially have “legal effects” on the data subject or otherwise significantly affect them

The controller has one month to respond to requests from customers asserting their rights under GDPR, and cannot charge the customer for this response except in limited circumstances.

## Lawful Basis for Processing (Article 6)

There are several ways for processing to be lawful under Article 6 of the GDPR:

### Consent
First, processing is lawful if the data subject has given their consent. The GDPR holds consent up to a higher standard than many Internet users are accustomed to:

- **Article 3**: consent must be “freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous.”
- **Article 7**: sets out several conditions for consent. The data controller must be able to show that the data subject gave consent.
- **Right to withdraw**: Data subjects have the right to withdraw their consent at any time, and withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving consent.
- **Opt-in model**: The GDPR uses an opt-in model for consent where an active choice must be made to consent to processing.
- **Conditional consent**: If consent was a condition of using the service, the GDPR suggests that dynamic may sway a court against finding that consent was freely given.
- **Article 8**: requires controllers to obtain parental consent prior to processing the personal data of data subjects under 16 years old. A member state may choose to set a lower age, but in any case a data subject’s consent will not be valid if the data subject is younger than 13 years old.

### Other Lawful Bases
Processing without adequate consent may potentially still be lawful under the GDPR when:

- Necessary for the performance of a contract
- To comply with a legal obligation
- To protect the vital interests of others
- The task involved is in the public interest
- **Legitimate interests** — broader provision making processing lawful, but the GDPR warns that a data subject’s rights might trump those legitimate interests

## International Data Transfers

Personal data subject to the GDPR can be transmitted to a country outside of the EU, provided conditions are met:

- **Article 45**: If the Commission has already decided that the receiving jurisdiction ensures an adequate level of protection for personal data, the transfer will not need special authorization.
- **Article 46**: If there has been no such adequacy decision, the controller or processor must provide appropriate safeguards for the transfer.

### Historical Context
International data transfers have been an important consideration ever since the passage of Data Protection Directive 95/46/EC:

- **2000–2015**: Data transfers between the United States and Europe were made possible by the **Safe Harbor Framework**.
- **October 2015**: The European Court of Justice threw out Safe Harbor.
- **Since 2016**: Transfers have been governed by the **EU-US Privacy Shield**.
- Presumably, the Privacy Shield will continue to be used as a tool to determine if the company’s safeguards were sufficient for compliance with the GDPR.

## Compliance Requirements

To comply with the GDPR, companies that do business with European customers are often advised to:

- Conduct thorough audits of their systems to identify the paths that information takes and where it all ends up
- Implement security measures
- Appoint a **data protection officer**

### Data Breach Notification
- Controllers must notify the supervisory authority (generally a government agency) within **72 hours** of discovering the breach.
- Data subjects affected by the breach must be notified “without undue delay.”

## Enforcement and Penalties

- **Article 79**: Data subjects have a right to sue controllers or processors for violations of their rights under the GDPR.
- **Article 83**: Sets out conditions for administrative fines.
- **Maximum fine**: 20 million EUR or 4% of the controller’s global annual revenue.
- **Failure to report a personal data breach**: Fine of up to 10 million EUR or 2% of the controller’s global annual revenue.

# II. Privacy and Social Media

Though the federal government has several sector-specific privacy laws, the information technology industry remains largely unregulated in the United States. This may change, as popular opinion toward privacy on social media services seems to be shifting away from a permissive attitude towards data collection. Instead, possible privacy violations on social media websites are often judged based on whether and to what extent these violations go against the terms of use for the website.

## Facebook Emotional Contagion Study (2012)

If you had a Facebook account in January of 2012, Facebook may have altered what you saw in your newsfeed to see if it affected your mood.

In 2014, a team of researchers published a controversial study titled **“Experimental Evidence of Massive-Scale Emotional Contagion Through Social Networks.”** One of the three authors was a **Data Research Scientist at Facebook**, which is how the researchers were able to have almost **700,000 unwitting test subjects**.

### Methodology
- Most Facebook users keep updated about their social network using Facebook’s **Newsfeed** tool.
- Researchers manipulated the **Newsfeeds** of test subjects to either show them more positive or negative posts from their Facebook friends.
- Researchers then monitored subjects’ posts to see if the emotional content of the manipulated Newsfeed influenced the emotional content of the subjects’ posts.

### Ethical Concerns
On the Internet, it is common for companies to experiment with their user interface to see whether a new design would be better at keeping the interest of its users — this is called **A/B testing**. Many technology professionals have defended Facebook’s experiment as being similar to A/B testing. However:

- Facebook wasn’t trying to determine which interface was better for business.
- Facebook was deliberately manipulating what users saw in order to experiment on its users’ emotions.

#### Informed Consent Issues
In the published article, the authors explicitly state that because Facebook conducted the study, the participants were already subject to Facebook’s **Data Use Policy**, and because they had agreed to that policy, they provided informed consent.

**However**, according to archived websites, Facebook’s data use policy did not say that Facebook could use your data for research until **May of 2012 at the earliest**. This means that when Facebook conducted this experiment in **January of 2012**, even if accepting a privacy policy is the same thing as informed consent, those **689,003 users** had not consented to this research when the data was collected.
