# 2.6.2 What Is a Good Explanation?

This section further condenses Miller’s summary on “good” explanations and adds concrete implications for interpretable machine learning.

Explanations are contrastive (Lipton 1990). Humans do not ask why a prediction was made, but why this prediction was *instead of another prediction*. We tend to think in counterfactual cases, i.e. “How would the prediction have been if X had different?” For a house price prediction, the house owner might be interested in why the predicted price contrast to the lower price they expected. If my loan application is rejected, I do not care about all the factors that generally speak for against a rejection. I am interested in the factors in my application that would want to change or I want to know between the application and the would-accepted version of my application. The recognition that contrasting explanations matters is an important finding for explainable machine learning. From most interpretable models, you can extract an explanation that matches the prediction of an instance with an artificial data source or an average of instances. Physicians might ask: “Why did the drug not work for my patient?” And they want an explanation that contrasts the patient with whom the drug worked and is similar to the non-responding patient. Contrastive explanations are easier to understand than complete explanations. A complete explanation of the physician’s reason why the drug does not work might include: The patient has had the disease for 10 years, 11 genes are over-expressed, the body is very quick in breaking the drug down into ineffective chemicals,. A contrastive explanation might be much simpler: In contrast to the responding patient, the non-responding patient has a certain combination of genes that makes the drug less effective. The best explanation is one that highlights the greatest difference between the object of interest and the reference object.

**What it means for interpretable machine learning:** Humans do not want a complete explanation for a prediction, but want to compare what the were to another instance’s prediction (can be an artificial one). Creating contrastive explanations is application-dependent because it requires a point of reference for comparison. This thus may depend on the data point to be explained, but also on the user receiving the explanation. A user of a house price website might want to have an explanation of a house price contrastive to their own house or may want to have another house on the website or maybe to an average house in the neighborhood. The solution for the automated creation of contrastive explanations might also involve finding prototypes or archetypes in the data.
