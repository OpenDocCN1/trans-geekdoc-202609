### 4.1.3.2 Effect Plot

The weights of the linear regression model can be more meaningfully analyzed when they are multiplied by the actual feature values. The weights depend on the scale of the features and will be different if you have a feature that measures e.g. a person's height and you switch from meter to centimeter. The weight will change, but the actual effects in your data not. It is also important to know the distribution of your feature in the data, if you have a very low variance, it means that all instances have similar contribution from your feature. The effect plot can help you understand how much combination of weight and feature contributes to the predictions in your data. By calculating the effects, which is the weight per feature times the value of an instance:

> $$effect_j^{(i)} = w_j x_j^{(i)}$$

The effects can be visualized with boxplots. A boxplot contains the effect range of half of your data (25% to 75% quantiles). The vertical line in the box is the median effect, i.e. 50% of the instances have a lower and the other half higher effect on the prediction. The horizontal lines extend to $\pm1.5IQR/\sqrt{n}$, with IQR being the inter quantile range (75% quantile minus 25%). The dots are outliers. The categorical feature effects can be summarized in a single boxplot, compared to the weight, where each category has its own row.

The largest contributions to the expected number of rented bicycles comes from the temperature feature and that captures the trend of bike rentals over time. The temperature has a broad range of how it contributes to the prediction. The day feature goes from zero to large positive contributions, because the first day in the dataset (01.01.2011) has a very small trend effect and the estimated weight for this feature is positive (4.93). This means that the effect increases with each day and is highest for the last day in the dataset (31.12.2012). Note that for effects with a negative weight, the instances with a positive effect are those that have a negative feature value. For example, days with a high negative effect of windspeed are the ones with high wind speeds.

### 4.1.5 Encoding of Categorical Features

There are several ways to encode a categorical feature, and the choice influences the interpretation of the weights. The standard in linear regression models is treatment coding, which is sufficient in most cases. Using different encodings boils down to creating different (design) matrices from a single column with the categorical feature. This section presents different encodings, but there are many more. The example used has six instances and a categorical feature with three categories. For the first two, the feature takes category A; for instances three and four, category B; and for the last two instances, category C.

#### Treatment coding

In treatment coding, the weight per category is the estimated difference in the prediction between the corresponding category and the reference category. The intercept of the linear model is the mean of the reference category (when all other features are). The first column of the design matrix is the intercept, which is always one. Column two indicates whether instance is in category B, column three indicates whether it is category C. There is no need for a column for category A, because the linear equation would be overpecified and no unique solution for the weights can be. It is sufficient to know that an instance is neither in category B or C.

#### Effect coding

In effect coding, the weight per category is the estimated y-difference from the corresponding category to the overall mean (given all other features are zero or the reference category). The first column is used to estimate the intercept. The weight $\betaeta_0$ associated with the intercept represents the overall mean and $\betaeta_1$, for the column two, is the difference between the overall mean and category B. The total effect of category B is $\betaeta_0 + \betaeta_1$. The interpretation for category C is equivalent. For the reference category A, $-(\betaeta_1 + \betaeta_2)$ is the difference to the overall mean and $\betaeta_0 - (\betaeta_1 + \betaeta_2)$ the overall effect.
