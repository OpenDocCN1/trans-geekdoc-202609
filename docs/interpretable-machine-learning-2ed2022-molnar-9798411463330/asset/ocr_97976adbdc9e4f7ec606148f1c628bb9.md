But in other cases, knowing the 'why' can help you learn more about the problem, and the reason why a model might fail. Some models may not require explanations because they are in a low-risk environment, meaning a mistake will not have serious consequences (e.g. a movie recommender system) or the method has already been extensively studied and evaluated (e.g. character recognition). The need for interpretability arises from an incompleteness in problem formalization (Doshi-Velez and Kim 2017), which may mean that for certain problems or tasks it is not to get the prediction (what). The model must also explain how to the prediction (why), because a correct prediction only partially solves your original problem. The following reasons drive the demand for interpretability and explanations (Doshi-Velez and Kim 2017 and Miller 2017).

Human curiosity and learning: Humans have a mental model of their environment that is updated when something unexpected. This is performed by finding an explanation for the unexpected. For example, a human unexpectedly and asks, “Why do I feel sick?” He learns that he gets sick every time he sees those red berries. He updates his mental model and decides that the berries caused the sickness and should be avoided. When opaque machine learning models are used in research, scientific findings remain completely hidden if the model gives predictions without explanations. To facilitate learning and satisfy curiosity as why certain predictions or behaviors are created by machines, interpretability and explanations are crucial. Or course, humans do not need explanations for everything that happens. For most people it is okay that they do not understand how a computer works. Unexpected events make us curious. For example: Why is my computer shutting down unexpectedly?

Closely related to learning is the desire to find meaning in the world. We want to harmonize contradictions or inconsistencies between elements of our knowledge structures. “Why did my dog bite me even though it never done before?” a human might ask. There is a contradiction between the knowledge of the dog's past behavior and the newly made, unpleasant experience of the bite. The vet's explanation reconciles the dog owner's contradiction: “The dog was under stress and bit.” The more a machine's decision affects a person's life, the more important it is for the machine to explain its behavior. If a machine learning model rejects a loan, this may be unexpected for the applicants. They can only reconcile this inconsistency between expectation and reality with some kind of explanation. The explanations do not actually have to fully explain the situation, but should address a maine. Another example is algorithmic product recommendation.
