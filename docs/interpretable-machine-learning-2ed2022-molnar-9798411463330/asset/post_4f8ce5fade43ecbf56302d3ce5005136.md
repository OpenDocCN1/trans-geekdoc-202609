# Introduction

This book explains how to make (supervised) machine learning models interpretable. The chapters contain some mathematical formulas, but you should be able to understand the ideas behind the methods even without the formulas. This book is not for trying to learn machine learning from scratch. If you are new to learning machine learning, there are a lot of books and other resources to learn the basics. I recommend the book “Elements of Statistical Learning” by Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman (2009) ^1 and Andrew Ng’s “Machine Learning” on the online learning platform coursera.com to start with machine learning. Both the book and the course are available free of charge!

New methods for the interpretation of machine learning models are published at breakneck speed. To keep up with everything that is published would be madness and simply impossible. That is why you will not find the most novel and fancy methods in this book, but established basics and concepts of machine learning interpretability for making machine learning models interpretable. Internalizing the basic concepts also empowers you to better understand and evaluate any new power on interpretability published on arxiv.org ^2 in the last 5 minutes since you began reading this book (I might be exaggerating the publication rate).

This book starts with some (dystopian) short stories that are not needed to understand the book, but hopefully entertain and make you think. Then the book explores the concepts of machine learning interpretability. We will discuss when interpretability is important and what different types of explanations there are. Terms used throughout the book can be looked up in the Terminology chapter. Most of the models and methods are explained using real data examples which are described in the Data chapter. One way to make machine learning interpretable is to use model-agnostic interpretation tools such as linear models or decision trees. The other option is the use of model-agnostic interpretation tools and local methods that describe the average behavior of the model and explain individual predictions. The Model-Agnostic Methods chapter deals with methods such as partial dependence plots and feature importance. Model-agnostic methods work by changing the input of the machine learning model and measuring changes in the output. The book ends with an outlook on what the future of interpretable machine learning might look like. You can read from the book beginning directly to the methods that interest you.

“It’s definitely not the worst way to die!” Tom summarised, trying to find something positive in the tragedy. He removed the pump from the intravenous pole.

“Just for the wrong reasons,” Lena added.

“And certainly with the wrong pump! Just creating more work for us!” Tom complained about unscrewing the back plate of the pump. After removing all screws, he lifted the pump and put it aside. He plugged a cable into the diagnostic port.

“You didn’t just complain about having a job, did you?” Lena gave him a mocking smile.

“Of course not!” he exclaimed with a sarcastic undertone. He booted the computer.

“All right, diagnostics are running,” she announced her tablet. “It certainly shot John Doe into Nirvana. That high concentration of morphine stuff, Man. I mean… that’s first, right? Normally a broken pump gives too little of the stuff or nothing at all. But never, you know, like that crazy thing,” Tom explained.

“I know. You don’t have to convince me… Hey, look at that.” Lena held up her tablet. “Do you see this peak here? That’s the potency of the painkillers mix. Look! This line shows the reference level.”

The poor guy had a mixture of painkillers in his blood system that could kill him 7 times over. Injected by our pump. And here…” she swiped, “here you can see the moment of the patient’s demise.”

“So, any idea what happened, boss?” Tom asked the supervisor.

“Hm… The sensors like Heart rate, oxygen levels, glucose… were collected as expected. Some missing values in the blood oxygen, but that’s not unusual. Look here. The sensors also detected the patient’s slowing heart rate and extremely low cortisol levels caused by the morphine derivative and other pain blocking agents.” She continued to swipe through the diagnostics report. Tom stared at the screen. It was his first investigation of a real device failure.

“Ok, here is first piece of the puzzle. The system failed to send a warning to the hospital’s communication channel. The warning was triggered, but rejected at protocol level. It could be our fault, but it could also be the fault of the hospital. Please send the logs to the IT team.” Lena told Tom.

Tom nodded with his eyes fixed on the screen.

Lena: “It’s odd. The warning should also have caused the pump to shut down. But obviously failed to do so. That must be a bug. Something the quality team missed. Really bad luck. Maybe it’s related to the protocol issue.”

“So, the emergency system of the pump somehow broke down, but the pump did go bananas and injected so many painkillers into John Doe?” Tom wondered.

“Good question. You are right. Protocol failure aside, the pump shouldn’t have administered that amount of medication at all. The algorithm should have stopped on its own, the low level of cortisol and other warning signs.” Lena explained. Maybe some bad luck, like one in a million thing, being hit by lightning?” Tom asked.

“No, Tom. If you had read the document I sent you, you would have known that the pump was trained in experiments, then later on humans, to learn to inject the perfect amount of painkillers by the sensory input. The algorithm of the pump might be opaque and complex, but it’s not random. Means that the same pump would behave exactly the same way again. Our patient died again. A combination or undesired direction of the sensory inputs must have triggered the erroneous behavior of the pump. That is why we have to dig deeper and find what happened here,” Lena explained.

“I see…” Tom replied, in thought. “Was’t the patient going to die anyway? Because of cancer or something?”

Lena nodded when she read the analysis report. He looked out, his eyes fixed on a point in the distance. “Maybe the machine did him a favor, you know, freeing him from the pain. No more suffering. Maybe it just did the right thing. Like a lightning, you know, a good one. I mean like the lottery, but not random. But for a reason. If I were the pump, I would have done the same.”

She finally lifted her head and looked at him.

He kept looking at outside.
