
$\\frac{\\mathcal{l}\_{1} \\vee \\cdots \\vee \\mathcal{l}\_{k},\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, m\_{1} \\vee \\cdots \\vee m\_{n}}{\\text{SUBST(}\\theta\\text{,}\\,\\mathcal{l}\_{1} \\vee \\cdots \\vee \\mathcal{l}\_{i - 1} \\vee \\mathcal{l}\_{i + 1} \\vee \\cdots \\vee \\mathcal{l}\_{k} \\vee m\_{1} \\vee \\cdots \\vee m\_{j - 1} \\vee m\_{j + 1} \\vee \\cdots \\vee m\_{n}\\text{)}}$

where `UNIFY`(𝓁[*i*], ¬*m*[*j*]) = *θ*. For example, we can resolve the two clauses

\[*A**n**i**m**a**l*(*F*(*x*)) ∨ *L**o**v**e**s*(*G*(*x*), *x*)\]       and       \[¬*L**o**v**e**s*(*u*, *v*) ∨ ¬*K**i**l**l**s*(*u*, *v*)\].

by eliminating the complementary literals Loves(G(x),x) and ¬Loves(u,v), with the unifier `θ = {u/G(x), v/x}`, to produce the resolvent clause

\[*A**n**i**m**a**l*(*F*(*x*)) ∨ ¬*K**i**l**l**s*(*G*(*x*), *x*)\] .

This rule is called the binary resolution rule because it resolves exactly two literals. The binary resolution rule by itself does not yield a complete inference procedure. The full resolution rule resolves subsets of literals in each clause that are unifiable. An alternative approach is to extend factoring—the removal of redundant literals—to the first-order case. Propositional factoring reduces two literals to one if they are identical; first-order factoring reduces two literals to one if they are unifiable. The unifier must be applied to the entire clause. The combination of binary resolution and factoring is complete.

## 9.5.3 Example proofs

Resolution proves that `KB ⊨ a` by proving that `KB ∧ ¬α` unsatisfiable—that is, by deriving the empty clause. The algorithmic approach is identical to the propositional case, described in [Figure 7.13](#Chapter07-05.xhtml#fig7-13), so we need not repeat it here. Instead, we give two example proofs. The first is the crime example from [Section 9.3](#Chapter09-03.xhtml#sec9-3). The sentences in `CNF` are

$\\begin{array}{l} \\begin{array}{l} {\\neg American(x) \\vee \\neg Weapon(y) \\vee \\neg Sells(x,y,\\ z) \\vee \\neg Hostile(z) \\vee Criminal(x)} \\\\ {\\neg Missile(x) \\vee \\neg Owns(Nono,x) \\vee Sells(West,x,Nono)} \\\\ {\\neg Enemy(x,America) \\vee Hostile(x)} \\\\ {\\neg Missile(x) \\vee Weapon(x)} \\\\ \\end{array} \\\\ {\\begin{array}{l} {Owns(Nono,M\_{1})} \\\\ {American(West)} \\\\ \\end{array}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\begin{array}{l} {Missile(M\_{1})} \\\\ {Enemy(Nono,America).} \\\\ \\end{array}} \\\\ \\end{array}$

We also include the negated goal `¬Criminal(West)`. The resolution proof is shown in [Figure 9.10](#Chapter09-05.xhtml#fig9-10). Notice the structure: single “spine” beginning with the goal clause, resolving against clauses from the knowledge base until the empty clause is generated. This is characteristic of resolution on Horn clause knowledge bases. In fact, the clauses along the main spine correspond exactly to the consecutive values of the goals variable in the backward-chaining algorithm of [Figure 9.6](#Chapter09-04.xhtml#fig9-6). This is because we always choose to resolve with a clause whose positive literal unifies with the leftmost literal of the “current” clause on the spine; this is exactly what happens in backward chaining. Thus, backward chaining is just a special case of resolution with a particular control strategy to decide which resolution to perform next.

Our second example makes use of `Skolemization` and involves clauses that are not definite clauses. This results in a somewhat more complex proof structure. In English:

Everyone who loves all animals is loved by someone.

Anyone who kills an animal is loved by no one.

Jack loves all animals.

Either Jack or Curiosity killed the cat, who is named Tuna.

Did Curiosity kill the cat?

First, we express the original sentences, some background knowledge, and the negated goal G in first-order logic:

$\\begin{array}{r} {\\text{A}.} \\\\ {\\text{B}.} \\\\ {\\text{C}.} \\\\ {\\text{D}.} \\\\ {\\text{E}.} \\\\ {\\text{F}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\neg\\text{G}\\text{.}} \\\\ \\end{array}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\begin{array}{l} \\left. \\forall x\\,\\lbrack\\forall y\\, Animal(y)\\Rightarrow Loves(x,y)\\rbrack\\Rightarrow\\lbrack\\exists y\\, Loves(y,x)\\rbrack \\right. \\\\ \\left. \\forall x\\,\\lbrack\\exists z\\, Animal(z) \\land Kills(x,\\ z)\\rbrack\\Rightarrow\\lbrack\\forall y\\,\\neg Loves\\text{(}y,x\\text{)}\\rbrack \\right. \\\\ \\left. \\forall x\\, Animal(x)\\ \\Rightarrow\\ Loves(Jack,x\\text{)} \\right. \\\\ {Kills(Jack,Tuna) \\vee Kills(Curiosity,Tuna)} \\\\ {Cat(Tuna)} \\\\ \\left. \\forall x\\, Cat(x)\\Rightarrow Animal(x) \\right. \\\\ {\\neg Kills(Curiosity,Tuna)} \\\\ \\end{array}$

Now we apply the conversion procedure to convert each sentence to `CNF`:

$\\begin{array}{r} {\\text{A1}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\text{A2}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\text{B}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\text{C}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\text{D}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\text{E}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\text{F}\\text{.}} \\\\ {\\neg\\text{G}\\text{.}} \\\\ \\end{array}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\begin{array}{l} {Animal(F(x)) \\vee Loves(G(x),x)} \\\\ {\\neg Loves(x,F(x)) \\vee Loves(G(x),x)} \\\\ {\\neg Loves(y,x) \\vee \\neg Animal(z) \\vee \\neg Kills(x,\\ z)} \\\\ {\\neg Animal(x) \\vee Loves(Jack,x)} \\\\ {Kills(Jack,Tuna) \\vee Kills(Curiosity,Tuna)} \\\\ {Cat(Tuna)} \\\\ {\\neg Cat(x) \\vee Animal(x)} \\\\ {\\neg Kills(Curiosity,Tuna)} \\\\ \\end{array}$

The resolution proof that Curiosity killed the cat is given in [Figure 9.11](#Chapter09-05.xhtml#fig9-11). In English, the proof could be paraphrased as follows:

Suppose Curiosity did not kill Tuna. We know that either Jack or Curiosity did; thus Jack must have. Now, Tuna is a cat and cats are animals, so Tuna is an animal. Because anyone who kills an animal is loved by no one, we know that no one loves Jack. On the other hand, Jack loves all animals, so someone loves him; so we have a contradiction. Therefore, Curiosity killed the cat.

The proof answers the question “Did Curiosity kill the cat?” but often we want to pose more general questions, such as “Who killed the cat?” Resolution can do this, but it takes a little more work to obtain the answer. The goal is `∃w Kills(w, Tuna)`, which, when negated, becomes `¬Kills(w, Tuna)` in `CNF`. Repeating the proof in [Figure 9.11](#Chapter09-05.xhtml#fig9-11) with the new negated goal, we obtain a similar proof tree, but with the substitution `{w/Curiosity}` in one of the steps. So, in this case, finding out who killed the cat is just a matter of keeping track of the bindings for the query variables in the proof. Unfortunately, resolution can sometimes produce nonconstructive proofs for existential goals, where we know a query is true, but there isn’t a unique binding for the variable.
