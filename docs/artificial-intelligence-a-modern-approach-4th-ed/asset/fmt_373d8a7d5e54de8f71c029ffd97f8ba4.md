The utility function `U(s)` allows the agent to select actions by using the principle of maximum expected utility from [Chapter 15](#Chapter15-00.xhtml)—that is, choose the action that maximizes the reward for the next step plus the expected discounted utility of the subsequent state:

$\\begin{matrix} {\\pi^{\*}(s) = \\underset{a \\in A(s)}{\\text{argmax}}{\\sum\\limits\_{s'}\\left. P\\text{(}s' \\middle| s,a\\text{)\[}R\\text{(}s,a,s\\text{')} \\right.} + \\gamma\\, U(s')\\rbrack.} & {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,(16.4)} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$

We have defined the utility of a state, `U(s)`, as the expected sum of discounted rewards from that point onwards. From this, it follows that there is a direct relationship between the utility of a state and the utility of its neighbors: the utility of a state is the expected reward for the next transition plus the discounted utility of the next state, assuming that the agent chooses the optimal action. That is, the utility of a state is given by

$\\begin{matrix} {U(s) = \\underset{a \\in A(s)}{\\text{max}}{\\sum\\limits\_{s'}\\left. P\\text{(}s' \\middle| s,a\\text{)\[}R\\text{(}s,a,s\\text{')} \\right.} + \\gamma\\, U(s')\\rbrack.} & {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,(16.5)} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$

This is called the Bellman equation, after Richard Bellman (1957). The utilities of the states—defined by [Equation (16.2)](#Chapter16-01.xhtml#eq16-2) as the expected utility of subsequent state sequences—are solutions of the set of Bellman equations. In fact, they are the unique solutions, as we show in [Section 16.2.1](#Chapter16-02.xhtml#sec16-2-1).

where the four expressions correspond to Up, Left, Down and Right moves. When we plug in the numbers from [Figure 16.3](#Chapter16-01.xhtml#fig16-3), with `γ = 1`, we find that Up is the best action.

Another important quantity is the action–utility function, or Q–function: `Q(s, a)` is the expected utility of taking a given action in a given state. The Q–function is related to utilities in the obvious way:

<p class="sidebar">Q&#x2013;function</p>

$\\begin{matrix} {U\\text{(}s\\text{)~=}\\max\\limits\_{a}Q(s,a).} & {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\text{(16}\\text{.6)}} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$

Furthermore, the optimal policy can be extracted from the Q–function as follows:

$\\begin{matrix} {\\pi^{\*}\\text{(}s\\text{)~=}\\underset{a}{\\text{argmax}}Q(s,a).} & {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\text{(16}\\text{.7)}} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$

We can also develop a Bellman equation for Q-functions, noting that the expected total reward for taking an action is its immediate reward plus the discounted utility of the outcome state, which in turn can be expressed in terms of the Q-function:

$\\begin{array}{l} {Q(s,a) = {\\sum\\limits\_{s'}{P\\text{(}s'\\text{~|~}s,a\\text{)\[}R\\text{(}s,a,s\\text{')} + \\gamma\\, U\\text{(}s\\text{')\]}}}} \\\\ {\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\, = \\,{\\sum\\limits\_{s'}{P\\text{(}s'\\text{|~}s,a\\text{)\[}R\\text{(}s,a,s\\text{')} + \\gamma\\,\\max\\limits\_{a'}Q\\text{(}s\\text{',}\\, a\\text{')\]}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\text{(16}\\text{.8)}}}} \\\\ \\end{array}$

Solving the Bellman equations for `U` (or for `Q`) gives us what we need to find an optimal policy. The Q-function shows up again and again in algorithms for solving MDPs, so we shall use the following definition:

$\\begin{matrix} {\\mathbf{function}\\text{~Q-VALUE(}mdp\\text{,~}s,\\ a,U\\text{)~}\\mathbf{returns}\\text{~a~utility~value}} \\\\ {\\mathbf{return}{\\sum\\limits\_{s'}{P\\text{(}s'\\text{|~}s,a\\text{)\[}R\\text{(}s,a,s\\text{')} + \\gamma\\, U\\text{\[}s\\text{'\]\]}}}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$

## 16.1.3 Reward scales

[Chapter 15](#Chapter15-00.xhtml) noted that the scale of utilities is arbitrary: an affine transformation leaves the optimal decision unchanged. We can replace `U(s)` by `U'(s) = m U(s) + b` where `m` and `b` are any constants such that `m > 0`. It is easy to see, from the definition of utilities as discounted sums of rewards, that a similar transformation of rewards will leave the optimal policy unchanged in an MDP:

`R′(s, a, s′) = m R(s, a, s′) + b`.

It turns out, however, that the additive reward decomposition of utilities leads to significantly more freedom in defining rewards. Let `Φ(s)` be any function of the state `s`. Then, according to the shaping theorem, the following transformation leaves the optimal policy unchanged:

<p class="sidebar">Shaping theorem</p>

`R′(s, a, s′) = R(s, a, s′) + γ Φ(s′) − Φ(s)`. (16.9)

To show that this is true, we need to prove that two MDPs, `M` and `M'`, have identical optimal policies as long as they differ only in their reward functions as specified in [Equation (16.9)](#Chapter16-01.xhtml#eq16-9). We start from the Bellman equation for Q, the Q-function for MDP `M`:

$Q(s,a\\text{)} = {\\sum\\limits\_{s'}\\left. P\\text{(}s' \\middle| s,a\\text{)\[}R(s,a,s') + \\gamma\\,\\max\\limits\_{a'}Q(s',a')\\rbrack \\right.}.$

Now let `Q′(s, a) = Q(s, a) − Φ(s)` and plug it into this equation; we get

$Q'(s,a\\text{)}\\,\\, + \\,\\,\\Phi(s) = {\\sum\\limits\_{s'}\\left. P\\text{(}s' \\middle| s,a\\text{)\[}R(s,a,s') + \\gamma\\,\\underset{a'}{\\max\\,(}Q'\\,(s',a')\\, + \\,\\Phi\\,(s'))\\rbrack \\right.}.$
