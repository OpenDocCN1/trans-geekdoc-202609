# Substack Week 5: Let your ROI drive a fact-based decision-making process

**Published on February 26, 2021**

Use cases abound for machine learning in the enterprise. A lot of the steps and processes are definable and repeatable based on the sheer volume of use cases that have been utilized across many different enterprises [1]. You have to start getting your bearings around how those use cases can be operationalized. During that process is the right time to let ROI guide you to fact-based decision-making. Getting into that groove is about enabling defined and repeatable processes to deploy machine learning in ways that meet the needs of the organization’s machine learning strategy statement. One strategy statement could be as simple as “Always provide a machine learning driven recommendation for alternate purchases before the payment part of the workflow.” You can begin to work both forward and backward from that strategy. It would be pretty easy to line up a budget item toward making that strategy reality. It is also a well-known use case for providing alternatives based on fairly standardized models at this point for recommendation engines.

Getting fact based is about letting the data drive your decision-making process. You can get a list of common use cases for machine learning that exist in the wild right now and are commonly used. Based on those common use cases, you should be able to quickly correlate them to your workflows and begin to engage in some ROI analysis on what it would take to apply machine learning to your workflows. Generally speaking, the start-up time to use a machine learning API from `GCP`, `Azure`, or `AWS` is very low [2]. Building out your own infrastructure and getting set up to attach it to your workflow with a pipelined machine learning setup to serve trained machine learning models would take a bit longer. You also don’t get the advantage of the scale, tuning, and investment those other companies are making with their API setups. However, the general API does not have any customization for your use case if you require something divergent from the nature use case the model they are serving was designed to solve. Some really good models designed to evaluate machine learning cost already exist [3]. Those models are being used in the wild of production, with more than half of respondents in one survey saying they could demonstrate actual ROI from what they were doing [4].

# Substack Week 6: Understand the ongoing cost and success criteria as part of your machine learning strategy

**Published on March 5, 2021**

Start working on your machine learning inventory. You need to know where and why the organization has applied machine learning. Chances are machine learning is built into some things that have been deployed along the way. Seriously, you need to get that list built out. That is where it all starts to come together as a strategy. A recent survey of 1,870 organizations found that machine learning strategies in the wild have a long way to go, according to 80% of respondents [1]. You can read the press release about that survey study and download it if you fill out a form [2].

To understand the ongoing cost and success criteria of your machine learning strategy, you need a pulse of what is going on and what will be going on in the future. We all have limited time and energy to put into figuring things out. This is something you want to make sure you get right from the start. Your machine learning and artificial intelligence strategy has so much potential upside that getting it wrong could reverberate for years. Sometimes being early to the party makes doing the hard things nearly impossible. Right now machine learning in the enterprise is moving beyond the early party stage into the rationalized and empirically considered phase of planning and deployment.

Some of the actual training costs that people have advertised online are very large [3]. Anybody using a cloud system for training would probably have a reaction to a cost of several hundred thousand dollars. My general training costs have always been tagged to either electricity from my personal computer system or within other environments where those costs did not come directly back to my credit card. I started reading a post series from Brad Cordova on Medium about machine learning surprises [4]. It even had a reference to a paper titled “Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems,” which brought me great joy on this cold Sunday morning [5].
