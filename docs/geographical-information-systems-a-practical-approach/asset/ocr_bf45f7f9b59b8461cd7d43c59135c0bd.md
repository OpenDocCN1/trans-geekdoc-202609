The area of the SDEs represents the concentration (or distribution density) of tornadoes. The area varied across the seasons and was, in general, greater in summer and autumn than in winter and spring (Figure 6.9m–p). The mean (median) area in winter was 1.2E+12m² (1.1E+12 m²); in spring was 1.7E+12 m² (1.7E+12 m²); in summer was 2.6E+12 m² (2.5E+12 m²) and in autumn was 2.2E+12 m² (2.1E+12 m²). These differences in the area of the SDEs show that the dispersion of tornadoes changed across the seasons, with the greatest dispersion seen in summer and autumn and the greatest concentration seen in winter and spring. The area of the SDEs also significantly decreased in summer and autumn at rates of –1.13E+10 m² per year and –1.63E+10 m² per year, respectively (Figure 6.9o, p). The mean (median) area of the SDEs in summer and autumn were 2.81E+12 m² (2.79E+12 m²) and 2.50E+12 m² (2.41E+12 m²), respectively, in the earliest 25 years of the record. These values decreased by 4.89E+11 m² (4.70E+11 m²) and 6.59E+11 m² (6.80E+11 m²) to 2.32E+12 m² (2.32E+12 m²) (2.32E+12 m²) and 1.84E+12 m² (1.73E+12 m²) in the last 25 years. The area covered by the SDEs in spring also significantly declined (at a rate of –6.34E+09 m² per year), but the decline occurred only after the late-1980s (Figure 6.9n). These decreases indicate that tornadoes had become less dispersed (more concentrated) over time.

The results of the study also suggested that the number of tornadoes falling within the SDEs varied with the seasons, but there is no long-term trend. The absence of long-term trends in the counts and percentages of tornadoes within the SDEs showed that the decrease in the area of the SDEs is not related to a decrease in the number of tornadoes falling within them. Although not all were statistically significant, there was actually a weak negative correlation between the area of the SDEs and the number of tornadoes falling within them. It was shown that (E)F1+ tornadoes tended to be less dispersed in seasons with more 20+ tornado days, and that the decrease in dispersion over time was co-occurring with an increase in the frequency of these tornado-active days. A decrease in spatial dispersion suggests that the risk of tornadoes was focused over a smaller geographical area. That is, the density of tornado clusters (tornadoes are closer geographically) and the strength of tornadoes were all increasing. Combining this with the increasing proportion of tornadoes occurring in the South-East and Midwest further suggests that the risk was becoming more focused in these regions.
