Another method for choosing the optimal bandwidth is to minimise the corrected Akaike information criteria (AICc) (Hurvich et al. 1998). Its mathematical form for GWR is complicated, so we focus on its interpretation. The AICc is basically a model selection criterion. It provides a measure of the relative quality of a model, here a regression equation, for a given set of data. The model with the smaller AICc value is the better one – that is, it provides a better fit with the observed data. Here, the bandwidth with the lowest AICc is the optimal value.

Kernels other than those mentioned earlier can be used in GWR and have been implemented in various software packages. Once a kernel or weighting function has been selected and the optimal bandwidth is obtained, all the observations are weighted relative to location i, and the weighted observations are then used to estimate a linear regression at the location based on the least-squares principle used in conventional regression. It is clear that the regression coefficient estimates could vary by locations and reflect the spatial heterogeneity. For further details, see Fotheringham et al. (2002). ## 6.7 Space-Time Analysis

While location is an important aspect of spatial data analysis, we often need to consider both location and time when determining spatial patterns and relationships in order to better understand geographical events and phenomena. **Space-time analysis** seeks to incorporate temporal properties of geographical phenomena in spatial data analysis and to answer questions of when and where geographical events and phenomena occur (An et al. 2015). It involves the representation, analysis and modelling of data that relate to both space and time. Such analyses can aid in the discovery of new spatial patterns compared with analysis relying solely on locations and can also be used to identify trends and changes in spatial patterns over time.

### Box 6.2 Exploring Relationships of Immature Mosquitoes and Human Densities with the Incidence of Dengue in Taiwan

Dengue is a viral disease spread by mosquitoes (particularly *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus*) in tropical and subtropical regions. It was once confined to South-East Asia but has now spread to Southern China and countries in the Pacific Ocean and the Americas. Scientists have long been studying the relationships between dengue incidence and mosquito abundance and dengue incidence and human density. However, they are still not well understood. Most studies on these relationships have presented a global view in which the relationships were assumed to be spatially constant across a region. Lin and Wen (2011) argued that this assumption may not be true, as these relationships could be positively correlated in some areas but negatively or not correlated at all in other areas. They explored these relationships in the cities of Kaohsiung and Fengshan in Taiwan using GWR.

Dengue has been observed in southern Taiwan since the late nineteenth century, initially occurring as intermittent epidemics at intervals of up to 40 years but becoming an annual phenomenon in the two cities since the beginning of the twenty-first century. Figure 6.17a shows the spatial distribution of dengue incidence (IR) in 2002 in the two cities. In order to understand the spatial variations in the relationships between dengue incidence and mosquito abundance and dengue incidence and human density, Lin and Wen (2011) built a GWR model using the disease incidence (measured as cases per 100,000 people) as the dependent variable, and the monthly maximum Breteau index (BImax) and population density (POPDen) as independent variables. The Breteau index represents the density of immature *Aedes* mosquitos measured as the number of containers with *Aedes* mosquito larvae/pupae per 100 houses. The population density is measured as the number of people per km2. Figures 6.17b and 6.17c show the spatial distributions of BImax and POPDen in 2002, respectively. **Tracking data**, also called movement data, represent individual objects (such as people, wildlife animals and vehicles) and their movement in geographical space over time. They are usually georeferenced point feature data with timestamps. Generally, a tracking dataset, despite the forms of data collection (for example, travel diaries, GPS tracks and Wi-Fi probe requests), is composed of a sequence of track points: {$(x_i, y_i, t_i) | i = 1, 2, ..., n\}$, where $(x_i, y_i)$ are the geographical coordinates of the track point $i$, and $t_i$ is the timestamp of the track point $i$. This sequence of track points represents a movement trajectory, which can be visualised in a 3D geographical space, with the $X$ and $Y$ axes being the location and the $Z$ axis being the time (Figure 6.20). The movement trajectory is also known as the **space-time path** in time geography (Hägerstrand 1970). The track point with $i = 1$ represents the start point of the movement trajectory, and that with $i = n$ is the end point. The space-time path describes the location of the track point as well as the travel or stay between track points during the period of observation.
