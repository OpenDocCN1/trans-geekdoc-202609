Another method for choosing the optimal bandwidth is to minimise the corrected Akaike information criteria (AICc) (Hurvich et al. 1998). Its mathematic form for GWR is complicated, so we focus on its interpretation. The AICc is basically a model selection criterion. It provides a measure of the relative quality of a model, here a regression equation, for a given set of data. The model with the smaller AICc value is the better one – that is, it provides a better fit with the observed data. Here, the bandwidth with the lowest AICc is the optimal value.

Kernels other than those mentioned earlier can be used in GWR and have been implemented in various software packages. Once a kernel or weighting function has been selected and the optimal bandwidth is obtained, all the observations are weighted relative to location i, and the weighted observations are then used to estimate a linear regression at the location based on the least-squares principle used in conventional regression. It is clear that the regression coefficient estimates could vary by locations and reflect the spatial heterogeneity. For further details, see Fotheringham et al. (2002). # 6.7 Space-Time Analysis

While location is an important aspect of spatial data analysis, we often need to consider both location and time when determining spatial patterns and relationships in order to better understand geographical events and phenomena. **Space-time analysis** seeks to incorporate temporal properties of geographical phenomena in spatial data analysis and to answer questions of when and where geographical events and phenomena occur (An et al. 2015). It involves the representation, analysis and modelling of data that relate to both space and time. Such analyses can aid in the discovery of new spatial patterns compared with analysis relying solely on locations and can also be used to identify trends and changes in spatial patterns over time.
