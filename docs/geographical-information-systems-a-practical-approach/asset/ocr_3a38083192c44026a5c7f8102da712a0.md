If the _p_-value is equal to or less than the significance level \(\alpha\), _H<sub>o</sub>_ is rejected and the regression equation is significant overall. Thus, the _p_-value associated with the _F_ ratio is usually used as a measure of how well the regression equation fits the sample data.

The significance of the regression coefficients is tested using the _t_ test statistic. The null hypothesis _H<sub>o</sub>_ is that the true value of the regression coefficient is zero. So if the _t_ test for _b_ fails to reject _H<sub>o</sub>_, then the independent variable _x_ has no significant influence on the dependent variable _y_, and thus _x_ is not important in understanding _y_. If the _t_ test for _a_ fails to reject _H<sub>o</sub>_, we could conclude that _a_ generated from the sample data is the result of chance or sampling error; therefore, the predicted _y_ value by the regression equation is not reliable. The standard errors of _a_ and _b_ are used to carry out a _t_ test. The mathematical formulae for calculating the _t_ test statistics for _a_ and _b_ can be found in Rogerson (2015). We can use _p_-values associated with the _t_ test scores to make a decision on whether or not to reject _H<sub>o</sub>_.

# ## 6.6.2 Multiple Regression

Many geographical problems are of a multivariate nature. For example, the concentration of fungal toxin in nuts at growing sites is dependent on rainfall, temperature and wind speed; the intensity of rainfall is related to sea surface temperature, sea level pressure, wind speed and other environmental factors. **Multiple regression** offers an extension to simple regression for addressing multivariate problems. It

establishes a linear relationship between a dependent variable _y_ and two or more independent variables (_x_<sub>_1_</sub>, _x_<sub>_2_</sub>, _x_<sub>_3_</sub>, ..., _x_<sub>_k_</sub>). The general form of a multiple regression equation is:

```
\(\hat{y} = a + b_1x_1 + b_2x_2 + ... + b_kx_k\) (6.39)
```

where _k_ is the number of independent variables, \(\hat{y}\) is the predicted value of the dependent variable _y_ and _a_ and {_b<sub>i</sub>_ | _i_ = 1, 2, ..., _k_} are the regression coefficients estimated from sample data. As with simple regression, the regression coefficients are found by minimising the sum of the squared residuals. How well the multiple regression fits the sample data is measured by the **multiple coefficient of determination**, _R_<sup>2</sup>, which is the ratio of the explained variation and total variation in _y_, conceptually identical to _r_<sup>2</sup> in simple regression. However, _R_<sup>2</sup> increases as more variables are included, whereas the best multiple regression equation does not necessarily use all the available variables. To address this flaw, _R_<sup>2</sup> is adjusted according to the number of independent variables and the sample size as follows:

```
\(adjusted\ R^2 = 1 - \frac{n-1}{n-k-1}(1-R^2)\) (6.40)
```

where _n_ is the sample size. _R_<sup>2</sup> and adjusted _R_<sup>2</sup> are interpreted in the same way as _r_<sup>2</sup>. _F_ and _t_ tests are also used to assess the statistical significance of the multiple regression equation and individual regression coefficients. The _p_-value is used to determine the statistical significance according to the significance level \(\alpha\).
