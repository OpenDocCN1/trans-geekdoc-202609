Training uses the magnitude of the error signal to determine how much the connection weights need to be adjusted so that the overall error is reduced. The training process is driven by a learning algorithm, such as back-propagation (Rumelhart et al. 1986) and scaled conjugate gradient algorithms (Hagan et al. 1996). Once trained with representative training data, the multilayer perceptron network gains sufficient generalisation ability and can be applied to new data.

**Radial basis function neural networks** (RBFNN) are feed-forward neural networks consisting of an input layer, a hidden layer and an output layer, but hidden nodes implement a set of radial basis functions instead of activation functions, and the output nodes implement linear summation functions as in a multilayer perceptron network. A radial basis function for a data point **x** is a function whose value depends only on the distance from some other data point **c**, called a centre, so that

$$\mathcal{G}(x, c) = \mathcal{G}(x - c)$$ (9.31)

where $\| \cdot \|$ denotes the Euclidean norm, usually Euclidean distance. The radial basis function $\mathcal{G}$ is univariate and usually continuous. The name 'radial' indicates that all data points equidistant from **c** produce the same value. Different types of radial basis functions could be used, but the most common one is the Gaussian function, expressed as:

$$\mathcal{G}(x, c) = e^{-\frac{x-c^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$ (9.32)

where $\sigma$ is the radius around the centre (also called spread) to which each neuron responds. Each neuron has a different centre and may have a different radius.

Let $X$ be the $m$-dimensional input vector and $Y$ be the $n$-dimensional output vector. $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m]$ and $Y = [y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n]$, where $m$ is the number of input nodes, and $n$ is the number of output nodes. Set the number of hidden nodes as $k$. The output value of the $j$th output node produced by an RBFNN is given by

$$y_j = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_{ij} e^{\frac{||X - c_i||^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$ (9.33)

where $\lambda_{ij}$ is the weight on the connection between the $i$th hidden node to the $j$th output node (Figure 9.13a). Here, $X$ can be seen as a data point with $m$ attributes or variables. It feeds the values to each of the neurons in the hidden layer. Each hidden node or neuron consists of a radial basis function centred on a point with as many dimensions as the input vector. When presented with the $X$ vector of input values from the input layer, a hidden node computes the Euclidean distance of the test case from the node's centre and then applies the radial basis function to this distance using the radius. The resulting value is then multiplied by a weight assigned to the node and passed to the summation layer, which adds up the weighted values and presents this sum as the output values of the network. The optimal number of hidden nodes, the centres and spreads of each radial basis function are determined by the training process. RBFNNs are able to model complex mappings, while perceptron networks can only model by means of multiple intermediary layers.

As an extension to RBFNNs, **radial basis functional link networks** (RBFLNs) learn more quickly and are more accurate in operation. An RBFNN is a nonlinear model, while an RBFLN incorporates an additional linear model (Looney 2002). As shown in Figure 9.13b, an RBFNN adds links that directly connect the input nodes to the output nodes, and these links have another set of weights \(\{w_j\}\). With the new links and weights, equation 9.33 is modified as:

\( y_j = \sum_{i=1}^k \hat{\lambda}_i e^{\frac{||X - c_i||^2}{2\sigma_i^2}} + \sum_{i=1}^m w_y x_i, (9.34) \)

When \(\{w_j\}\) are set to zero, an RBFLN becomes an RBFNN. Therefore, the RBFLN is a generalisation of the RBFNN, a more complete model of a general nonlinear mapping.

ANNs are applicable when a relationship between the independent variables (outputs) and dependent variables (inputs) exists. They are able to learn the relationship from a given dataset without any assumptions about the statistical distribution of the data. In addition, ANNs perform a nonlinear transformation of input data to approximate output data, learning from training data and exhibiting the ability of generalisation beyond training data. The capability of learning from data and modelling nonlinear relationships makes ANN particularly suitable for pattern classification (that is, classifying data into the pre-determined discrete classes) and predictive modelling. Zhu (2014) provided a review of ANN applications in Earth and environmental sciences.

## Box 9.3 Assessment of Landslide Susceptibility in the Langat River Basin, Malaysia, Using an ANN

A landslide is the movement of a mass of rock, debris or earth down a sloped section of land under the direct influence of gravity. It is a serious geological hazard that can endanger human life, destroy infrastructure and cause severe environmental damages, such as loss of forests, destruction of wildlife habitats and removal of productive soils. It can also block rivers and increase the risk of floods. Landslides have multiple causes. They can be initiated in slopes already on the verge of movement by rainfall, snowmelt, changes in water level, stream erosion, seismic activities, disturbance by human activities or any combination of these factors.

Landslides are a frequent occurrence in Malaysia. They are mainly linked to the construction of new developments, such as roads, highways, dams and housing complexes in steep slopes. Heavy rain, particularly during the monsoon season, induced by the south-west monsoon from May to September and the north-east monsoon from November to March, can cause landslides in Malaysia. It is important to understand the risk of landslide hazards and how local governments can plan for land use, engineering of new construction and infrastructure which will reduce the risk. It is also important to understand their causes and where they are likely to occur. A research team from Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia and Durham University in UK developed an ANN model for landslide susceptibility assessment in order to support more effective and efficient landslide hazard mitigation and management in the Langat River Basin, Selangor, Malaysia.

Landslide susceptibility assessment is to identify areas which are subject to landslides, by considering where the landslides occur (the extent of problem), what triggers them (for example, elevation, slope, soil type and the impact of water flow in an area) and when they occurred (document archived data sources and recent events). The Langat River Basin is located in the southern half of Selangor and the northern part of Negeri Sembilan, which is the most urbanised river basin in Malaysia. It is characterised by hilly terrain in the north-east part and progressive sloping in the south-west as it extends to the Straits of Malacca. Its elevation ranges from 1,448 m to 79 m. The main lithologies are clay, silt, sand, peat, minor gravel and acid invasion. Agriculture is the dominant land use. There were about 70 landslide incidents in this region from 2000 to 2021, and they tended to be concentrated in urban and agricultural areas, with 48.57 per cent of landslides occurring in built-up areas (Selamat et al. 2023). Landslides are governed by several conditioning and triggering factors that are complexly interrelated. The interrelationships between these factors and landslides are nonlinear in nature. Therefore, the research team applied ANN to assess the landslide susceptibility in the area.

In this study, a multi-layer perceptron network was built to predict the presence and absence of a landslide occurrence based on nine variables: elevation, slope, aspect, curvature, topographic wetness index, distance to river, distance to road, lithology and rainfall. Therefore, the neural network consists of an input layer with nine input nodes (representing the nine variables) and an output layer with two output nodes (representing two classes: presence and absence). The number of hidden nodes was determined through training with a back-propagation algorithm. A hyperbolic tangent function was used as the activation function for the hidden layer. The training points were selected from a landslide inventory compiled from the field surveys, historical landslide information and satellite imagery between the years 2000 and 2020. All inputs are in the raster format. The model was implemented in the statistical package SPSS, and the results were mapped in ArcGIS. Figure 9.14 is the landslide susceptibility map produced from the model. The map shows the landslide susceptibility classified into five categories: very low, low, moderate, high and very high. It was found that the highly and very highly susceptible areas were located in the north-east of the basin. They are among the most unstable and fragile areas and have the highest annual average rainfall (ranging from 180 mm to 296 mm) in the basin, and they have also recorded a large number of landslides in the past. Furthermore, human activities in these areas, such as road development, contributed to landslide occurrence. Most of the past landslide events were found close to roads. The areas highly or very highly susceptible to landslides require more effective land use planning and management and targeted strategies for managing the identified landslide risks. By considering the information generated from the study, relevant government and non-government organisations can work together to develop risk management priorities and determine what landslide risk management strategies need to be implemented in each of the priority areas.
