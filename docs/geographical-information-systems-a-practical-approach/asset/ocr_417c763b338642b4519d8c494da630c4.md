# Box 6.2 Exploring Relationships of Immature Mosquitoes and Human Densities with the Incidence of Dengue in Taiwan

Dengue is a viral disease spread by mosquitoes (particularly *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictu*) in tropical and subtropical regions. It was once confined to South-East Asia but has now spread to Southern China and countries in the Pacific Ocean and the Americas. Scientists have long been studying the relationships between dengue incidence and mosquito abundance and dengue incidence and human density. However, they are still not well understood. Most studies on these relationships have presented a global view in which the relationships were assumed to be spatially constant across a region. Lin and Wen (2011) argued that this assumption may not be true, as these relationships could be positively correlated in some areas but negatively or not correlated at all in other areas. They explored these relationships in the cities of Kaohsiung and Fengshan in Taiwan using GWR.

Dengue has been observed in southern Taiwan since the late nineteenth century, initially occurring as intermittent epidemics at intervals of up to 40 years but becoming an annual phenomenon in the two cities since the beginning of the twenty-first century. Figure 6.17a shows the spatial distribution of dengue incidence (IR) in 2002 in the two cities. In order to understand the spatial variations in the relationships between dengue incidence and mosquito abundance and dengue incidence and human density, Lin and Wen (2011) built a GWR model using the disease incidence (measured as cases per 100,000 people) as the dependent variable, and the monthly maximum Breteau index (BImax) and population density (POPDen) as independent variables. The Breteau index represents the density of immature *Aedes* mosquitos measured as the number of containers with *Aedes* mosquito larvae/pupae per 100 houses. The population density is measured as the number of people per km2. Figures 6.17b and 6.17c show the spatial distributions of BImax and POPDen in 2002, respectively. The GWR model has an adjusted R2 of 0.59, suggesting that 59 per cent of the total variation could be explained by the model. Figure 6.18 shows the spatial variations in the locally weighted R2 between the observed and predicted values.

It is evident from Figure 6.18 that the value of local R2 varies across the study area. The GWR model fits best in districts 1, 5, 10 and 11 (see the key to Figure 6.18) but does not fit well in district 12. This means that other variables would influence the dengue incidence in district 12.
