Things have gathered the ability to sense their environments and create information about what is happening. Things have become media, able to both generate and communicate information. IoT’s vast amount of real-time sensor data come from connected devices like smartphones, soil moisture monitoring feeds, acoustic and temperature sensors and smart traffic lights. Most devices combine their physical locations from a GNSS receiver, giving real-time sensor information and positioning. GIS has been used to ingest, process, analyse and visualise IoT data from connected sensors. For example, the GreenIoT project in Sweden used IoT and location data to map air pollution level in the city centre of Uppsala in real time (Kaivonen and Ngai 2020). It deployed mobile sensors on city buses, which took air quality measurements at different locations, and delivered data, via the mobile network with GNSS location tracking, to a cloud service where the sensor data were stored, processed and mapped on Google Maps.

In summary, GIS has evolved from the mainframe computers (in the 1960s and 1970s) to workstation GIS (in the 1980s), desktop GIS (in the 1990s), web GIS (in the 2000s), and cloud GIS (in the 2010s). The performance and functionality of GIS have been improved significantly with the rapid advancement in computing power, network capabilities and big data analytics. With its shift to web and cloud computing and integration with real-time IoT data, GIS is becoming a platform relevant to almost every human endeavour.

# 1.3 Roles of GIS in Research, Management and Planning

The value of GIS for problem-solving and decision-making increases as the scale and complexity of the problem increase. Challenges currently faced by society, such as global warming, resource shortages and loss of biodiversity, require the integration of a wide range of data and information, addressing problems from multiple perspectives and evaluating alternative solutions in a holistic, comprehensive, systematic, analytic and visual manner. GIS provides computerised tools for organising and integrating data, recognising spatial patterns and visualising spatial patterns and processes that enable decision-makers to address these challenges and make effective judgements. It allows various types of data relating to the problems and their geographical contexts to be considered together, thus enabling the analysis of the problems to have a greater depth and wider scope.

Essentially, GIS deals with environmental, social and economic processes and patterns in a spatially distributed manner. In other words, GIS uses spatial data to characterise, explore, analyse and model spatial variations, processes and spatial relationships of environmental and socio-economic phenomena at various times and at various spatial scales, ranging from local (such as a field and a forested area) to regional (such as a catchment) and global scales.
