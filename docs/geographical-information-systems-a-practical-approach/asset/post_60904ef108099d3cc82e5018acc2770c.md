Cloud GIS systems allow spatial data, information and knowledge to be accessed, shared, analysed and used anywhere and at any time.

The availability of low-cost devices plus reduced computing and memory costs, the innovation in network technologies that enable communication among devices and direct device connectivity, the emergence of cloud computing and the advancement in big data analytics led to the rapid and widespread adoption of the internet of things (IoT) from the early 2010s onward. IoT refers to the uses and processes that entail assigning a network address to a thing (from a smartphone to a vehicle, from a smart energy meter to an air conditioner in a house) and fitting it with sensors (Green-gard 2015). Things have gathered the ability to sense their environments and create information about what is happening. Things have become media, able to both generate and communicate information. IoT's vast amount of real-time sensor data come from connected devices like smartphones, soil moisture monitoring feeds, acoustic and temperature sensors and smart traffic lights. Most devices combine their physical locations from a GNSS receiver, giving real-time sensor information and positioning. GIS has been used to ingest, process, analyse and visualise IoT data from connected sensors. For example, the GreenIoT project in Sweden used IoT and location data to map air pollution level in the city centre of Uppsala in real time (Kaivonen and Ngai 2020). It deployed mobile sensors on city buses, which took air quality measurements at different locations, and delivered data, via the mobile network with GNSS location tracking, to a cloud service where the sensor data were stored, processed and mapped on Google Maps.

In summary, GIS has evolved from the mainframe computers (in the 1960s and 1970s) to workstation GIS (in the 1980s), desktop GIS (in the 1990s), web GIS (in the 2000s), and cloud GIS (in the 2010s). The performance and functionality of GIS have been improved significantly with the rapid advancement in computing power, network capabilities and big data analytics. ## 1.3 Roles of GIS in Research, Management and Planning

The value of GIS for problem-solving and decision-making increases as the scale and complexity of the problem increase. Challenges currently faced by society, such as global warming, resource shortages and loss of biodiversity, require the integration of a wide range of data and information, addressing problems from multiple perspectives and evaluating alternative solutions in a holistic, comprehensive, systematic, analytic and visual manner. GIS provides computerised tools for organising and integrating data, recognising spatial patterns and visualising spatial patterns and processes that enable decision-makers to address these challenges and make effective judgements. It allows various types of data relating to the problems and their geographical contexts to be considered together, thus enabling the analysis of the problems to have a greater depth and wider scope.

Essentially, GIS deals with environmental, social and economic processes and patterns in a spatially distributed manner. In other words, GIS uses spatial data to characterise, explore, analyse and model spatial variations, processes and spatial relationships of environmental and socio-economic phenomena at various times and at various spatial scales, ranging from local (such as a field and a forested area) to regional (such as a catchment) and global scales. GIS has a very diverse range of applications, depending on the particular environmental sphere (atmosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere or lithosphere) and the specific environmental or socio-economic issue being investigated. However, the role of GIS in research, management and planning can be generalised into three categories: inventory and monitoring, analysis and modelling and visualisation and communication.

### 1.3.1 Inventory and Monitoring

It is a well-accepted principle that spatial data and information at an appropriate spatial scale are required to underpin the complex decision-making in environmental and resource management and planning. A compilation of spatial data pertinent to a specific geographical area is called **spatial inventory**. Examples include land resource, land account, wetland, wildlife, pollutant and landslide inventories. A spatial inventory typically comprises data that represent both the thematic and locational attributes of environmental and socio-economic phenomena or resources. For example, an air emissions inventory is a database containing detailed information about pollutants discharged into the atmosphere by each source type during a given time period and at a specific location. A forest inventory contains forest attribute data for each forest stand in a given area, including stand attributes of a unique identifier, stand age, stand species, stand size, stocking, soil type, the level of reconnaissance (the method used to inventory the stand) and other forest attributes or variables, depending on the purpose of the inventory. Maps, imagery and related tabular data are the centrepiece of most spatial inventories. Traditionally, spatial inventories were stored in paper maps, orthophotographs and tally sheets and updated by aerial photography, field sampling methods and manual drafting. GIS has been used to store, organise, document, retrieve, integrate, summarise, update and map inventory data since the 1960s, when early GIS systems were mainly developed for land and natural resource inventory. Modern GIS not only provides spatial database management tools for compiling, managing and maintaining spatial inventories but also allows the use of alternative source data, such as high-resolution digital satellite imagery and LiDAR data, to automatically capture inventory data over a large and/or inaccessible area, which would be infeasible to collect using traditional field inventory and mapping methods.

GIS-based spatial inventory ensures accurate reporting with improved data collection and supports monitoring key environmental and socio-economic variables that are essential for resource and environmental management. Spatial inventory is often a foundation for environmental and socio-economic analysis and modelling.

### 1.3.2 Analysis and Modelling

Analysis and modelling with GIS are associated with a process of applying particular GIS analytical tools to spatial data. GIS provides a large range of spatial data analysis capabilities, as discussed in Section 1.1. They can be used for descriptive analysis, predictive modelling and decision analysis. **Descriptive analysis** attempts to examine spatial data for patterns, trends, correlations or other relationships. GIS is a flexible tool for descriptive analysis. For example, we may retrieve soil data in a given area from a GIS database and display them on a map, from which the spatial pattern of soils in the area can be visually examined. We can use a GIS to quickly generate a map of rainfall based on data measured at a set of rain gauge stations at a specific time. If a series of rainfall maps for different times is produced, they can be overlaid on each other, which allows us to analyse the temporal trend of rainfall in the area over a certain period of time. We may also use a GIS to overlay a soil map, an elevation map and a rainfall map of the same area to visually analyse the relationships between soil, elevation and rainfall distributions. Descriptive analysis with GIS seeks to describe, measure and explain what is where or where is what, rather than what should be where or where might be what. Water quality assessment, land evaluation and demographic movement are examples of descriptive analysis. Descriptive analysis can give a researcher intimate knowledge of the spatial data and a good understanding of environmental and/or socio-economic conditions before embarking upon further investigation.

**Predictive modelling** with GIS is used to predict where particular types of environmental or socio-economic phenomena or events could or should occur under certain environmental or socio-economic conditions, or what will happen at different locations, given projected changes in environmental or socio-economic conditions. The former type of predictive modelling involves establishing statistically valid, causal or covariable relationships between environmental or socio-economic conditions, such as land use/cover, elevation, slope, aspect, soils, vegetation, water availability, geomorphology and population density, along with the observed presence of the environmental or socio-economic features, then using that information to predict where the features might be or are likely to occur. The species distribution modelling described in Box 1.1 is an example of this type of predictive modelling. Other examples include forest fire modelling, landslide risk assessment and wildlife habitat modelling.

The second type of predictive modelling is mainly used for **scenario analysis**, or ‘what if’ analysis. It involves the use of models to produce the outcomes of potential future alternatives (scenarios) or to analyse possible future events by considering alternative possible outcomes or states. It is particularly useful for generating predictive scenarios for environmental and social impact studies. For example, GIS has been used to estimate the surface runoff and sediment yield of a river catchment under different scenarios of future urban development in order to evaluate the impacts of urban growth patterns on surface water hydrology (Kepner et al. 2004); to assess the ecological effects of alternative water management strategies by integrating GIS with water allocation, landscape dynamics and habitat suitability models (Schluter and Ruger 2007); to simulate crop responses to climate change scenarios to assess the impacts of climate change in agricultural systems (Hodson and White 2010) and to generate socio-economic scenarios of land cover change (Swetnam et al. 2011).
