The correlation between the two time series is valued at 1. The degree of dissimilarity between two time series is calculated by subtracting the correlation from 1. Thus, time series that are perfectly positively correlated (correlation = 1) have a dissimilarity value of 0, time series that are uncorrelated (correlation = 0) have a dissimilarity value of 1 and time series that are perfectly negatively correlated (correlation = -1) have a dissimilarity value of 2. Dissimilarity values range from 0 to 2. The larger the value, the higher the dissimilarity.

To cluster a set of time series into groups with high similarity in shape, a dissimilarity matrix needs to be created, whose elements are pair-wise dissimilarity values between two time series. Figure 6.25 provides an example of a dissimilarity matrix for four time series T1, T2, T3 and T4. In this example, T2 and T3 have the smallest dissimilarity value 0.45, indicating they are most similar in shape, and T1 and T2 are most different as they have the largest dissimilarity value of 1.78. A dissimilarity matrix is square and symmetric. Dissimilarity between two time series does not depend on their ordering, that is, the dissimilarity between time series A and time series B is the same as the dissimilarity between B and A. The diagonal members are defined as zero, meaning that zero is the measure of dissimilarity between an element and itself. The dissimilarity matrix is then clustered using a k-means or k-medoids algorithm. ### 6.7.3.1.3 Similarity in Periodic Pattern

Many geographical phenomena have a periodic pattern across space and time. For example, weather and animal migration often show inherent seasonality. Inside monthly temperature data, you can expect summer months to be always higher and winter months to be always lower. Temperature also has a daily cycle: colder at night and warmer during the day. Periodic patterns reveal repeated values or behaviours that occur at regular time intervals in specific places. They are often a very important aspect of time series. Time series are similar if they have about similar seasonality patterns. These patterns can be extracted through the Fourier transformation.

Time series data are a function depending on time. The Fourier transform is to decompose a function depending on time into a function depending on frequency. Here, a frequency can be hourly, daily, monthly, yearly or other time intervals. For an hourly temperature dataset, for example, the Fourier transform can detect the presence of day/night variations and summer/winter variations and suggest that those two seasonalities (frequencies) are present in the data. As time series data are generally measured at discrete intervals, they are not a continuous function. Therefore, the discrete Fourier transform is often applied.

Let us assume that a time series is a finite sequence of data, \(\{x(n) \mid n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N - 1\}\), where \(n\) is time-step and \(N\) is the number of time-steps. The discrete Fourier transform is to transform the series to a new series, \(\{X(k) \mid k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N - 1\}\), using the following equations:

```
\(\begin{aligned} X\left(k\right) &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x\left(n\right) e^{\frac{-j 2\pi}{N}kn} 0 \le k \le N - 1 \end{aligned}\) (6.49)
```

```
\(\begin{aligned} e^{\frac{-j 2\pi}{N}kn} &= \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}kn\right) - j\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}kn\right) \end{aligned}\) (6.50)
```

where \(j = \sqrt{-1}\), an imaginary unit. Equation 6.50 is a basis function, consisting of sinusoidal waves (sines and cosines) that oscillate up and down at a constant interval (frequency). For a given \(k\), the frequency of the set of sinusoids is \(\frac{2\pi}{N} \times k\). \(X(k)\) is the strength or amplitude of a specific frequency. If the amplitude of this frequency is high, this seasonality is important in the time series. The \(X(k)\) series captures periodic patterns of a time series with different frequencies and extents of fluctuation. The inverse transform of equation (6.49) is:

```
\(\begin{aligned} x\left(n\right) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X\left(k\right) e^{\frac{j 2\pi}{N}kn} 0 \le n \le N - 1 \end{aligned}\) (6.51)
```

Complex exponentials, \(e^{\frac{j 2\pi}{N}kn}\), are basis functions, which can be written as:

```
\(\begin{aligned} e^{\frac{j 2\pi}{N}kn} &= \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}kn\right) + j\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}kn\right) \end{aligned}\) (6.52)
```

Equation (6.51) approximates the time series of periodic phenomena as a weighted sum of those basis functions. X(k) is the weight for the basis function e^(j 2πkn/N). For detailed discussions on the discrete Fourier transform, refer to Briggs and Henson (1995).

Discrete Fourier transforms reveal periodicities in time series data as well as the relative strengths of any periodic components. The dissimilarity between two time series is calculated by summing the squared differences between the weights of the associated basis functions of each time series. This means that two time series that have similar dominant oscillating signals will be considered similar. Time series with similarity in periodic pattern can be clustered using k-means based on the weights X(k) of each basis function. Figure 6.27 shows the result of clustering the 2010 global monthly total precipitation time series data using the time series clustering tool with the Profile (Fourier) option in ArcGIS Pro based on the space-time cube shown in Figure 6.23. Two clusters are identified: the peak season of precipitation in Cluster 1 (in blue colour) is around July and August, while the peak season in Cluster 2 (in red colour) is around December and January. The chart in Figure 6.27 clearly shows the opposite oscillating seasonal patterns of precipitation in the two clusters.

### 6.7.3.2 Local Outlier Analysis

Local outlier analysis is to identify significant clusters and outliers in space-time data. Basically, it compares the value of each location (bin in a space-time cube) with the values of its neighbouring locations to search for locations in the study area that are significantly different from their neighbours in both space and time. For example, it can be used to determine whether there was a time period with unexpectedly high rates of disease outbreak in a region, or to detect any locations in a region with significant increases in the number of insurance claims filed over a certain period of time.

Local outlier analysis applies the local Moran's I statistic introduced in Section 6.5.2 in the space-time context, in which a neighbourhood is defined in terms of distance (for spatial neighbours) and time-step interval (for temporal neighbours). In other words, the local Moran's I statistic is calculated for each bin within the context of its neighbouring bins in a 3D space-time cube. A positive local Moran's I statistic value indicates that a bin has neighbouring bins with similarly high or low attribute values, and this bin is part of a cluster. A negative local Moran's I statistic value suggests that a bin has neighbouring bins with dissimilar values; thus, it is an outlier. The results of local outlier analysis distinguish six types of locations:

- 1 A location that has never been part of a statistically significant cluster or outlier.
- 2 A location that has been part of a High-High cluster (of high values) throughout time.
- 3 A location that has been part of a Low-Low cluster (of low values) throughout time.
- 4 A location that has been a High-Low outlier (a high value surrounded mainly by low values) throughout time.
