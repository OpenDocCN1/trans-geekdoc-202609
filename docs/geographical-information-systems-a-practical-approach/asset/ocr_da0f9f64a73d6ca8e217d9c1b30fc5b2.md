Multimedia mapping was attempted before the internet mapping started in 1993, when the first interactive map appeared on the World Wide Web, or the web. **Multimedia mapping** involves interactive integration of sound, animations, text, images and video clips with the map. It links multimedia information to the geographical features at different locations represented on the map. A multimedia map provides a digital multimedia environment, which allows users to explore interactively the mapped environmental or socio-economic phenomena and show dynamically the environmental or socio-economic processes. **Internet mapping** can easily integrate multimedia in and with web maps and deliver up-to-date or real-time spatial information accessible to anyone around the world via the internet.

By the late 1990s, GNSS and remote sensing imagery had been fully integrated with GIS. The launch of IKONOS satellite in 1999 started a new generation of very high-resolution satellite remote sensing, which provides remote sensing data with a spatial resolution of 1 m or below. In the meantime, the concept and design of miniaturised satellites for low Earth orbit space research and applications were conceived. CubeSats are a type of such miniature satellites. A CubeSat is typically made up of one or more 10 × 10 × 10 cm³ units, and each unit has a mass of no more than 1.33 kg (Selva and Krejci 2012). CubeSats were made possible by the ongoing miniaturisation of sensors, digital processors, power supplies, and other components. The first six CubeSats were launched in June 2003 from Russia's Plesetsk launch site. In the following decade, most CubeSats that were

launched came from university or research applications. It was in 2013 that the commercial sector began to launch CubeSats. Since then, a large number of CubeSats with Earth observation capability have been developed and deployed. Planet (formerly Planet Labs), an Earth observation company founded in 2010, has the world's largest private fleet of CubeSats – over 150 of them, for Earth observation. It currently has two constellations of CubeSats in operation: Doves and SkySats. Doves provide 3.7 m resolution, 8-band images of the entire Earth daily, while SkySats can be tasked to image any point on Earth in a resolution of 50 cm and at sub-daily frequency, and they can also capture stereo imagery. These CubeSat images have been used in many applications, from disaster response to climate monitoring.

In addition to satellite remote sensing, in the last few years, we have seen the development of drones, also commonly referred to as unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV), unmanned aerial systems (UAS) or remotely piloted aerial systems (RPAS), for observation, measurement, monitoring and mapping in various natural and built environments. Drones operate at a low altitude and improve the spatial resolution significantly over that obtained with satellites or manned aircrafts and can capture spatial data at a scale matched to traditional field survey and observations. Also in the late 1990s, airborne LiDAR (light detecting and ranging) technology revolutionised the collection of high-resolution and very accurate digital surface and digital terrain models.
