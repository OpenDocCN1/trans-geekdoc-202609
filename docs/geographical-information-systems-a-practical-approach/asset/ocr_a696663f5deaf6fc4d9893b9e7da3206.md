# Box 6.1 The Use of Standard Deviational Ellipses to Analyse the Changes to the Spatial Dispersion of Seasonal Tornado Activity in the United States

The growing intensity and frequency of severe weather events like extreme rainfall, extreme heat and drought can be directly attributed to climate change, but the link between tornadoes and climate change is currently not fully understood. A tornado is a type of storm in which powerful rotating winds form a column which extends from a cloud to the ground. It is formed by a combination of atmospheric instability and wind shear – changes in wind direction and speed at different elevations in the atmosphere. Challenges remain that prevent clearer attribution, including limited data collection methods, high year-to-year variability and difficulty in modelling key physical elements that help tornadoes form, as well as directly modelling tornadoes due to their small size. Despite these challenges, researchers from Towson University attempted to identify spatiotemporal trends in the United States by analysing seasonal changes to the spatial dispersion of tornadoes, or the area over which most tornadoes are dispersed in USA. Based on portions of the tornado record dated back to the 1950s, they calculated standard deviational ellipses (SDEs) of seasonal tornadoes rated 1 or higher by the Fujita or Enhanced Fujita ((E)F1+) damage rating scales between 1954 and 2017 and used the parameter values of the SDEs (including mean centres, orientations and areas) to characterise seasonal changes in the spatial dispersion of tornadoes over the period of time.
