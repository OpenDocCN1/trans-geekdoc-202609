Both the supervised and unsupervised classifications discussed in this section are on a pixel-by-pixel basis. They assume that the measurement vectors associated with different pixels are independent, while they are indeed spatially correlated. For example, a pixel classified as pasture is likely to be surrounded by the same class of pixels. Due to this assumption of ‘independence’, image classification often creates many misclassified isolated pixels inside class interiors, commonly referred to as speckles. This may result in a salt-and-pepper appearance, as shown in Figures 7.25 and 7.26a. In practice, image classification is usually followed by post-classification smoothing in order to remove the speckles and improve the quality of the classified image.

# 7.5.3 Object-Based Classification

The image classification methods discussed in the previous two sections are based on

the analysis of the spectral properties of individual pixels. These **pixel-based classification** techniques do not consider any of the information from neighbouring pixels, using only the spectral information available for each individual pixel. In contrast, object-based methods are based on information from a set of similar pixels called objects or image objects, using both spectral and spatial information for classification. Here, **image objects** are groups of pixels that are similar to one another based on the spectral properties (that is, colour), size, shape and texture, as well as context from a neighbourhood surrounding the pixels. They exhibit useful spectral and spatial features that single pixels lack.

**Object-based classification** involves two steps: image segmentation and object classification (Liu and Xia 2010). Image segmentation is the process of grouping pixels in an image into segments or image objects that have similar spectral and spatial characteristics.
