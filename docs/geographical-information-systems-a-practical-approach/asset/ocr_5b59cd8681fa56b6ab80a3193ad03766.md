Once eigenvectors are found, principal component images can be produced. In general terms, the pixel values for the kth principal component image are found from:

\( DN_{pk} (i,j) = a_{k1}DN_1(i,j) + a_{k2}DN_2(i,j) \),
\( ( \, ) + ... + a_{kn}DN_n (i,j) \). (7.19)

where \( DN_{pk} (i,j) \) is the DN value of the pixel \( (i,j) \) in the kth principal component image, \( n \) is the number of bands, \( DN_i(i,j) \) is the value of the pixel \( (i,j) \) in the ith band image \( (i = 1, 2, ... , n) \) and \( (a_{k1}, a_{k2}, ..., a_{kn}) \) is the kth eigenvector derived from the covariance matrix of the n-band image pixel values. The second principal component image contains far less variance, 18.93 per cent in the case of Woori Yallock. The third and succeeding component images have a further decreasing portion of the scene variance. Principal component images with high eigenvalues and large percentages of the scene variance provide significant information about the observed features, while those with small eigenvalues and low variances represent noise and provide little useful information. If the first k (< n) principal component images account for most of the variance in the multispectral image dataset, the original n bands of the image data can be set aside, and the subsequent image analyses can be performed using just the k principal component images.
