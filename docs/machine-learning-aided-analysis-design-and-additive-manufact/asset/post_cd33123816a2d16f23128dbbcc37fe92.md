## 16.3 Experimental results and discussion

### 16.3.1 Typical mechanical response of foam-filled tubes

Taking the specimen of UF-9 as an example, Fig. 16.5A shows the force–displacement curves of the thin-walled tube and individual foam. It can be seen that the curve of individual foam shows three stages. At the elastic stage, the force increases almost linearly with displacement. At the plateau stage, the displacement increases continuously while the force is maintained at a level. At the densification stage, the force increases rapidly causing displacement. For the thin-walled tube, there are three stages. The difference is that after the end of the elastic stage, the force reaches a peak, and then drops rapidly because the thin-walled tube buckles and collapses. Furthermore, Fig. 16.5B compares the force–displacement curves of the FFT and the sum of force with the sum of the empty tube and individual foam, despite that the FFT has overall higher capacity for the same mass. This is because the interaction between the thin-walled tube and individual foam contributes to the additional bearing capacity, as highlighted by the red area in Fig. 16.5B. Note that the compressive strength of the thin-walled tube is prominent lower than the interaction between the tube and foam is considerably higher than the bearing capacity of the tube (Fig. 16.5A). Therefore FFT can possess excellent energy absorption capacity while saving the weight of energy-absorbing structures.

As shown in Fig. 16.5B, the bearing capacity of FFT consists of three parts, including the thin-walled tube, individual foam, and the interaction between the tube and foam. Fig. 16.6C shows the deformation evolution of individual foam. As the loading proceeds, due to the irregular distribution of cell structure, the local stresses reach the maximum as compression progresses, the adjacent cell wall begins to buckle. The contact between the walls of buckled cells arrests this thus stabilizing the local microstructure. This leads to the collapse of cells in the neighboring local collapse zone. The process repeats until local collapse spreads to the whole specimen. This causes a nearly constant force level of the plateau region in the force–displacement curves (Fig. 16.5A). Finally, all cells are compacted together and original pores are filled, resulting in the densification stage with steeply rising strength.

### 16.3.2 The effect of the relative density of foam on foam-filled tubes

Force–displacement curves of tubes filled with foams with different relative densities are plotted in Fig.16.7A. The force increases with the relative density because the mass of FFT increases, resulting in a rise in the load-carrying capacity. Moreover, the higher the relative density, the more pronounced the strain hardening phenomenon (the force level as a function of deformation displacement). It is because the pores become smaller, the distance between cell walls occurs each other after plastic deformation, increasing the force level. The interaction energy is defined as the area enclosed by the curve of FFT force minus the force of the tube and foam used alone (Fig.16.5B). As shown in Fig.16.7B, the interaction energy increases as relative density increases.

## 16.4 Numerical results and discussion

### 16.4.1 The interaction between foam and tube in uniform foam-filled tube

The internal interaction between the cell wall of the foam and the thin-walled structure of the tube is largely monitored during the compression experiment. Therefore a numerical study is performed to reflect the deformation mechanism of interest. The action between the thin-walled tube and foam at the mesoscopic scale is shown in **Fig. 16.10**. A quarter of the model is hidden for a better view of the internal deformation. It can be seen that the buckling and supporting effect of FFT have undergone concentrated deformation as indicated by the red dotted box in Fig. 16.10. There is an obvious interaction between the extrusion of the buckled tube and the support of denied foam. Fig. 16.10B and C correspond to the extrusion of the buckled tube near the loading end and the supporting end, respectively. Part of infilled foam is sandwiched by the buckled thin-walled tube. **Fig. 16.10D** corresponds to the support of densified foam. These interactions enhance the energy-absorbing potential of FFT, making FFT better than the sum of tube and foam alone.
