### 10.1 Introduction

The development of lightweight structures is an active research field providing to solve the long-term dilemma between weight and stiffness. The recent innovations, as carbon fiber reinforcing systems [1] and new lightweight hy materials [2], are increasingly appreciated in the industrial practice, where the light yet stiff components are critically applied in the production of vehicle frames [3], aircraft skeletons [4,5], spatial building designs [6,7], etc.

The porous-matrix structures are among the most adopted lightweight forms in structural and mechanical engineering, and involve honeycombs, lattices, and foams in line with the geometrical characteristics. Compared to honeycombs [8] and lattices [9] which contain ordered (2D) or three-dimensional (3D) regular materials, foams are formed by random distributions of internal pore sizes, densities, and consequently relatively low manufacturing costs. In addition to the reduced weight (occasionally over 90% lighter), they also offer under multifunctional properties, including definite density, good energy absorption, and useful thermal and acoustic traits [10]. Integrating metal foams into one single structural component gives to the FG porous structures (metal based), which have been demonstrated to possess even more outstanding mechanical attributes [11–16].

202 FG porous composite structures

Despite the speedy advance in the research of FG porous structures, predictions of their properties have been continuously intricate, induced from the random nature of metal foams. Although the relative density is widely regarded as a dominant factor, the contribution from cell arrangements can be important and changing stochastic porosity distributions may result in evident variances when determining the overall foam mechanical features. For example, Young's modulus of closed-cell aluminium foams, discussed by Chen et al. [17], have a distinct variation up to 14.38% over the same relative density.

On the other hand, the rapid development of AI [21–23] has gained much attention in the last decade. With the high computation power and advances in deep learning, deep models could be considered as a universal solution for data. Deep learning models such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [24], convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [25], and others can be incorporated into various applications. This work adopts deep learning to measure the features they been widely adopted in various fields, that is, image segmentation, investigating the microstructure–property relations, and prediction of fault [26,27]. Following the deep learning, the structural uncertainty analysis is performed the bending response of an FG porous beam.

### 10.2 Al-enhanced finite element multiscale modeling

In this section, multiscale modeling using 2D finite element (FE) homogenization is introduced (10.2.1) centering on the details of RVE, followed by the establishment of database (10.2.2) which is used in the training of CNNs (10.2.3). #### 10.2.1 Representative volume elements for finite element homogenization

Homogenization is a commonly adopted approach for multiscale modeling, assuming that the overall mechanical constants of a small representative material region, that, RVEs, are equivalent to those else elsewhere [28–30]. Thus regardless of the irregularity in microscopic/mesoscopic views, the general material form may be regarded as homogenized and shares the features of RVEs. In this study, multiscale modeling is employed to investigate the Young’s modulus of closed-cell metal foams. Using Voronoi tessellation where the seed positions are generated using the standard uniform distribution, 2D square RVEs can be constructed by stochastic porosities via commercial software MATLAB (version: R2021a) and ANSYS/APDL (version: 2020 R2), illustrated in Fig. 10.1 for a typical example. Note that the edge is designed for the implementation of periodic boundary conditions (PBCs) requiring the node pairs on the opposite edges to move equivalently in both horizontal vertical directions, the internal cell wall morphologies and thicknesses jointly determine the RVE stiffness, while the influence of regularity edges may be ignored.
