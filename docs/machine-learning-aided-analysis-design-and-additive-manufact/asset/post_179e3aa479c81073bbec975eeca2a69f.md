
The free vibration problem can be considered as a subset solution for Eq. (12.5), by assuming a harmonic vibration as:

```
u(t) = \Phi e^{i\omega t} \quad (12.8)
```

where $\omega$ is the natural frequency (rad/s) of FGP plate; $\Phi$ is the normal mode. Substituting Eq. (12.8) into Eq. (12.5), the generalized eigenvalue problem can be formulated as:

```
K\Phi = \lambda M\Phi \quad (12.9)
```

where $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue, $\Phi$ is the corresponding eigenvector. The fundamental frequency of FGP plate is based on the lowest eigenvalue acquired.

## 12.3.2 Stochastic vibration analysis of functionally graded porous plate

For the randomness involved structural system, the stochastic parameters of material properties would be random and changed to $E(\theta), G(\theta), \rho(\theta)$ for the FGP plate, where $\theta\in\Omega$ is the random event. The global mass, stiffness matrices, and governing equation of free vibration analysis for the FGP plate can be transformed into stochastic format, which can be represented as:

```
M(\theta)\ddot{u}(\theta) + K(\theta)\mathbf{u}(\theta) = 0 \quad (12.10)
```

where $M(\theta), K(\theta), \dot{u}(\theta), \ddot{u}(\theta)$ are the stochastic formats of global mass, stiffness matrices, acceleration and deformation vectors. It should be noticed that, effected by the random properties $\rho(\theta), E(\theta)$ of FGP plate, the elemental mass matrix and stiffness matrix are also transformed into

```
M(\theta) = \sum_{r} M^r(\theta), \quad K(\theta) = \sum_{r} K^r(\theta) \quad (12.11)
```

```
M'(\theta) = \int_{v_r} \rho(\theta)N^T dV, \quad K'(\theta) = \int_{v_r} B^T D(\theta) dB \quad (12.12)
```

where $M'(\theta)$ and $K'(\theta)$ are the stochastic formats of elemental mass and stiffness matrix, $D(\theta)$ is the stochastic elasticity stiffness matrix, which are affected by the random values of density and Young's modulus of the FGP plate.

For the solutions of uncertain free vibration analysis of the FGP plate, the stochastic eigenvalue problem in Eq. (12.9) can be reformulated as:

```
K(\theta)\Phi(\theta) = \lambda(\theta)M(\theta)\Phi(\theta) \quad (12.13)
```

where $\lambda(\theta)$ and $\Phi(\theta)$ are the stochastic solutions of eigenvalue and eigenvector. it should be noted the above equation is a stochastic system with nondeterministic solutions, which cannot be solved directly through analytical or mathematical formulations. To provide a valid solution to the stochastic problem, the machine learning technique is considered as an alternative and introduced as follows.

## 12.4 Machine learning aided stochastic vibration analysis

### 12.4.1 Preliminary

For the machine learning aided stochastic vibration analysis framework, the machine learning technique is the main tool to investigate the underpin relation between the random inputs and the structural outputs under different conditions. Many types of machine learning techniques, such as response surface method (RSM) [10], artificial neural networks [11], and Gaussian process regression (GPR) [12] have been implemented into various physical mechanisms. Based on the traditional support vector regression (SVR) theory, the SVR is also proposed and illustrated effective in practical engineering uncertainty problems the distinct advantages of using machine learning technique can be summarized as follows:

- 1. Identifying the inherent relationship between systematic uncertainties and structural responses, and make statistical evaluations and reliability profiles over potential structural failures.
- 2. Implementing the calculation on the machine learning technique can largely reduce the computational costs, otherwise against complicated analytical derivations or repetitively finite element analysis simulations.

The traditional uncertainty quantification methods normally face analytical or numerical bounds with practical complex engineering applications, especially for nonlinear analysis involved three-dimensional degree-of-freedom objects. The emergence of machine learning technique serves as robust alternative to such problems, and the two of typical algorithms (i.e., GPR, X-SVR) are explained in this chapter for illustrations.

### 12.4.2 Gaussian process regression

For the Gaussian process, it defines that for any two datapoints within the function, the corresponding outputs behave a multivariate Gaussian distribution. In GPR, assume a group of input and output vectors as x and y, the relationship between is formulated as:

```
y = f(x) + \varepsilon \quad (12.14)
```

Machine learning aided stochastic vibration analysis of functionally graded porous plates where $\varepsilon \sim N(0, \delta^2)$. For Gaussian process regression, the relation function $f(\mathbf{x})$ is distributed as a Gaussian process:

```
$f(\mathbf{x}) \sim GP(m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})) \quad (12.15)$
```

where $m(\mathbf{x})$ is the mean equation and $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$ is the covariance equation, with detailed formulations as:

```
$m(\mathbf{x}) = E[f(\mathbf{x})] \quad (12.16)$
```

```
$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = E[(f(\mathbf{x}) - m(\mathbf{x}))(f(\mathbf{x}') - m(\mathbf{x}'))] \quad (12.17)$
```

where $E(\cdot)$ is the average of all values. The formulation $k$ is named as the kernel of Gaussian process. The appropriate kernel function could smooth the datapoints to higher dimension and provide with regression. One typical kernel based on GPR is the radial basis function, which is presented as:

```
$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2\lambda^2}) \quad (12.18)$
```

where $\lambda$ is the length-scale hyperparameter and $\sigma_f^2$ is signal variance, which are adopted to enhance and promote the resulting equation. Based on the mean and kernel function, the Gaussian process can be implemented to obtain the priori values between input–output.

### 12.4.3 Extended support vector regression

The SVR which was proposed by Vapnik [15] has been popular over past decades, exhibiting excellent ability in data pattern regression. Subsequently, success of support vector machine (SVM) and support vector machine (S-SVM) in classification problems inspired a new SVR model, the X-SVR by Feng et al. [13,14]. The decomposition method and the quadratic insensitive loss function has been implemented into the model with larger adaptability and stability.

For the input and output variables of $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$, the X-SVR creates a hyperplane $\hat(\mathbf{x})$ to describe the relationship between datapoints:

```
\hat(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{x} - \zeta \quad (12.19)
```

where $\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^q$ are coefficients; $\zeta$ is bias parameter. The above equation is suitable for linear regression with patterned inputs; for irregular ones one uses implicit mapping $\Theta(\mathbf{x})$ to transform the variables into high-dimension Euclidean space with regularized distributions:

```
\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m]^T \rightarrow \hat{k}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \Theta(x_1)^T & \Theta(x_1) \\ \Theta(x_2)^T & \Theta(x_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ \Theta(x_m)^T & \Theta(x_m) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} k(x_1, x_1) & k(x_1, x_2) \\ k(x_2, x_1) & k(x_2, x_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ k(x_m, x_1) & k(x_m, x_2) \end{bmatrix}
```

where $k(\mathbf{x})$ is the empirical kernel vector, and the kernelized form of X-SVR can be shown as:

```
\min_{\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{q}_i, \vartheta} \frac{1}{2} ||\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{q}_i||^2 + \gamma_1 k(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{q}_i) + \gamma_2 \frac{1}{2} (\vartheta^2 + \vartheta^2)
\text{s.t.} \quad k(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{q}_i) - \delta \leq y_i \leq \lambda + \vartheta,
\quad y_i - k(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{q}_i) \leq \delta \leq \lambda,
\quad \mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{q}_i \geq 0; \quad \vartheta \geq 0
```

where $\gamma_1, \gamma_2$ are the tuning parameters; $\vartheta$ is the tolerable variable; $k$ denotes the kernelized process. From the optimization problem adopted in the SVM model, the regression function can be simplified and considered as a quadratic programming problem by introducing a nonnegative Lagrange multiplier $\beta_k \in \mathbb{R}^{im}$ as:

```
\min_{\beta_k} \frac{1}{2} \beta_k^T \mathbf{P}_k \beta_k - \mathbf{L}_k^T \beta_k; \quad \beta_k \geq \mathbf{0}_{im}
```

where $\mathbf{P}_k$ and $\mathbf{L}_k$ are optimization vectors and matrices. Considering $\beta_k^* \in \mathbb{R}^{im}$ as the obtained global solution for the optimization problem, the overall kernel equation of the kernel-SVR model can be reformulated as:

```
\hat{f}_n(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{p}_n - \mathbf{q}_n)^T k(\mathbf{p}_n, \mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{v}_k^T \beta_k^* \\
\mathbf{p}_n - \mathbf{q}_n = \mathbf{v}(1:m) - \mathbf{v}(m+1:2m); \quad \mathbf{v}_k^T = (\mathbf{R}_k + \mathbf{I}_{lm \times k})^T \beta_k^* - \gamma_2 \mathbf{u}_k
```
