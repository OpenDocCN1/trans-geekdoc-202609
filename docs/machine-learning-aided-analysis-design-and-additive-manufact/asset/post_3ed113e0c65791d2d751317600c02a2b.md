## 13.4 Results and discussion

### 13.4.1 Mechanical’ properties of triply periodic minimal surface composite-based structures

Early research on lattice structures was based on a simplified analytical approach due to the limitations of fabrication techniques. Calladine and English [85] described two different types of structures. Type I cellular structures present a nearly constant (if not increasing) strength under loading, while type II structures show a softening caused by the buckling of cellular material and are more sensitive to inertial effects. The response type of a lattice structure is determined by the number of nodes and struts according to Maxwell Criteria [9]. To evaluate the mechanical properties of lattice structures (Ls), an analytical model has been described by Ashby and Gibson. They showed that relative density $(\rho*/\rho_s)$ is the most important characteristic in cellular materials [86]. Although the cell shape, size, edge, and face connectivity can affect the mechanical properties, they are neglected in this model, only considering ligament bending. Fig. 13.12 presents the Ashby-Gibson model (A) and its deformation in bending (B) and buckling (C)

Based on these assumptions, an equation to predict the relationship between elastic modulus and relative density is given by

```
\varnothing = C\rho^*^n
```

where $\varnothing$ is the property of interest, $\rho^*$ is the density of the bulk material, $n$ is the density exponent, and $C$ is the factor accounting for geometric imperfections. A geometric factor $C<1$ for the gyroid structure would indicate imperfections in the printed lattice, and the value of $C=1$ is the theoretical limit of the relevant property (i.e., defect-free). In terms of the postyielding behaviors of solid/network phase TPMS lattices, the property of base material plays a dominant role in it compared to the unit cell topology. For example, diagram structures show plateau stress when fabricated with a polymeric material in study by Maskery et al. [95]. In contrast, a significant drop in the stress after yielding is observed and related to the fracture of the struts for metallic TPMS lattices in study by Al-Ketan [3] and Yan et al. [89]. Fig. 13.13 presents the deformation of solid/network phase TPMS lattices manufactured by different materials. The differences in their postyielding behaviors and deformation mechanism could be explained by the fact that the base material has distinct ductility. When the deformation reaches a certain level, the struts in the lattice may be subjected to a higher local strain. As a result, local fractures may be observed and lead to propagating of cracks, causing catastrophic failure eventually if the base material is brittle. However, struts deform plastically when the base material is ductile. Meanwhile, there are other factors which may affect the deformation mechanism: (1) defects introduced by the fabrication process such as the microacks or void in the lattice and (2) the set-up of the experiments as creating lateral strain due to friction between sample and loading planes.
