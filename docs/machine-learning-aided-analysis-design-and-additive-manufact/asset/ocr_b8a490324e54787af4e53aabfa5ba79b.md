#  15.1.2 Dynamic loading

The Split Hopkins Bar has been widely used in testing the mechanical properties of materials due to its accurate measurement and constant loading rate [40,41]. Two specimens, including the direct impact test and the Taylor test, are considered here to investigate the crushing behaviors of additively manufactured Voronoi foams. In the direct impact test, a specimen is cemented on the support bar, and the impact bar is free to compress the specimen (Fig. 15.1A). In the Taylor test, the same specimen is cemented on the impact bar, and the support experiences dynamic impact (Fig. 15.1B). The two bars have the same diameter of 10 mm and are both made of Nylon PA66. The lengths of the impact bar and support bar are 400 and 300 mm, respectively. In both, a pair of strain gauges are mounted at a distance of 100 mm from the specimen-support interface to record the strain pulse $\varepsilon_{bar}(t)$. The semiconductor strain gauges, amplify the transmission pulses in Nylon materials, are observed; the loading force $F$ is calculated by $F = S_{bar} E_{bar} \varepsilon_{bar}(t)$, where $S_{bar}$ and $E_{bar}$ are the cross-sectional area and elastic modulus (513 MPa) of the bar. Finally, the normal stress $\sigma$ of specimens is calculated via $F/A$, where $A$ is the initial cross-sectional area of specimens. It should be pointed out that the strain pulse recorded by the strain gauges is equal to that on the specimen-support bar face. It has barely influences the experimental results [39].

In addition, a high-speed camera is used to capture the evolution of successive deformation during the dynamic test. The image resolution and frame rate are set to $512 \times 384$ pixels and 33,000 fps, respectively. From the recorded figures, the strain contours of specimens and the displacement-time histories of two bars can be obtained via the DIC. The relative displacement of two bars $\Delta L$ is applied to determine the nominal strain $\varepsilon$ of specimens via $\varepsilon = \Delta L/l$, where $l$ is the initial length of specimens.
