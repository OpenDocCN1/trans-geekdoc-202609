## 13.3 Manufacturing techniques

### 13.3.1 3D printing of composite materials

The TPMS topologies were discovered in the last century, but the manufacturing of TPMS-like structures has been challenging due to the limitation of conventional fabrication methods. With the recent advances in AM techniques, it is now feasible to fabricate complex TPMS-structures with high accuracy. This section presents the current manufacturing techniques for the fabrication of TPMS-based structures. The examples of TPMS lattices fabricated by different AM techniques are presented in Fig. 13.5.

**Figure 13.5** Triply periodic minimal surface lattice fabricated by (A) powder bed fusion technique [40], (B) stereolithography technique [41], (C) fused deposition modeling technique [42], and (D) material jetting technique [43].

Powder bed fusion (PBF) uses a laser or electron beam to selectively melt a metallic or polymeric powder to form the desired 3D structures. The PBF techniques can be categorized into selective laser sintering (SLS), selective laser melting (SLM), and selective electron beam melting (SEBM) according to energy sources and processes. The PBF process starts with evenly spreading a layer of powder onto the building platform. A laser or electron beam is used to melt or sinter the powder-based cross-sectional information by slicing 3D structures along the vertical direction. Once the first layer is completed, the process is repeated for layers until the desired structure is formed. A wide range of materials has been utilized to fabricate TPMS structures based on PBF techniques. For instance, maraging steel and stainless steel were used to produce both network/solid phase and matrix phase lattices [40–44], biocompatable materials and pure titanium (CP-Ti) were explored to manufacture TPMS lattices for tissue engineering [47–49], Titanium alloy (Ti6Al4V) were also used to manufacture lightweight structures [50,51]. Meanwhile, polymeric powders such as nylon were considered for the fabrication [52].

Stereolithography (SLA) also employed by researchers to fabricate TPMS lattices. In SLA, the liquid polymer is cured by a UV laser on a moving tray. Initially, the layer is placed in a tank filled with liquid polymer. A digital miller is used to manipulate the UV laser to produce the desired shape. Similar, after a layer is completed, the building tray moves up, and another layer is cured upon the previous layer. It is noticeable that the SLA technique is limited to polymers, but it is possible to fabricate thicker walls and struts with currently available PBF materials. Studies have produced polymeric lattices [54–56].

Material extrusion is also employed by researchers to produce TPMS lattices. The most commonly used extrusion technique is fused deposition modeling (FDM), in which 3D objects are fabricated by heating the filament to a semiliquid state and extruded from a nozzle to a building platform where the object is. Researchers have employed FDM techniques to fabricate polymeric lattices [17,57,58]. Material jetting is also employed by some studies to fabricate TPMS lattices [43]. In the material jetting process, droplets of photosensitive polymers are jeted into a building platform by inkjet heads and cured by UV lights. It is worth mentioning that in material jetting, wax support may be required and need to be removed by postprocessing with thermal treatment, while high-pressure water jets may be required to remove the support materials in some systems.

### 13.3.2 Additive manufacturing of concrete

Over the last decade, the rapid development of digital techniques has provided a new and innovative pathway to fabricating concrete structures in the industry, with aim of reducing the reliance on traditional formwork, decreasing material usage, and importantly, achieving high geometrical complexity of the concrete structure that is challenging to be realized through the traditional framework-based casting process. In this subsection, the methods of digitally fabricating concrete structures will be summarized and reviewed first. After that, the feasibility of each technique regarding the fabrication of lightweight concrete structures with complex geometry will be discussed.

#### I. Concrete digital fabrication methods

There are in general two categories of digital fabrication techniques for concrete structures depending on the usage of framework [60]. The first technique is called 3DCP. To begin, the freshly mixed cementitious materials are extruded out of a nozzle to form one layer. After that, the nozzle moves up by a height and repeats the extrusion process that allows the subsequent filament on the upper layer to deposit on the existing filament on the lower layer. Ultimately, the concrete structure is fabricated through the layer-by-layer deposition process, and therefore 3DCP is known as the No Formwork (free) fabrication method. A typical set of 3DCP consists of the following components:

- a concrete mixing system where raw materials (binder, aggregate, water, etc.) are mixed to produce the freshly mixed concrete;
- a pumping system where the freshly flowable concrete is transported by the pump to move through the hose that is linked to the printhead (nozzle);
- a robotic system with the controller and multiple-axis robotic manipulator, and the nozzle is mounted on the robotic arm that moves based on the designed tooling during the extrusion process [61].

An example of a 3DCP set is shown in Fig. 13.

Since 3DCP adopts a de-free printing process, the quality finishes the target concrete structure primarily depend on the material properties of fresh concrete. Here three important material properties are introduced, which are key to achieving the 3D printed concrete structure with adequate quality. The first property is pumpability, meaning before extrusion the concrete needs to be flowable enough to be transported to the nozzle without experiencing any blockage. The second property is extrudability, the concrete needs to be extruded as a continuous filament with an unaltered material. The third property is buildability, meaning after the extruded concrete needs to develop green strength to resist the self-weight of the upper layers (achieving the minimum deformation of the overall structure). Generally there have been ongoing research on the relationships between concrete properties and material properties [62]. Besides, there is also a growing interest in promoting the sustainable aspect of 3DCP by replacing the raw materials (cement and river) with recycled materials [33,63], including:

- supplementary cementitious materials (such as fly ash and silica fume), substituting cement [64,65];
- waste glass particles and rubber crumb substrate agent [66–68];
- cement-ferrite alkali-activated materials/geopolymer [69–71];
- limestone called clay cement (LC) [72,73].

The fresh properties (pumpability, extrudability, buildability) together with the mechanical properties of 3DCP have also been extensively investigated over the past few years.
