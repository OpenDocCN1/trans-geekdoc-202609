On the other hand, graded foams with a linear nonmonotonic gradient, the gradient distribution has a less influence on the mechanical properties. These compressive stress—strain curves of graded foams are almost identical (Fig. 15.12A). This indicates that when the gradient functions are settled, the mechanical properties of graded foams with similar relative density are independent of gradient distributions under quasi-static compression. As an example, the deformation evolutions of the specimen “M1-PG” at different nominal strains are shown in Fig. 15.12B, where the deformation bands are marked by dashed boxes. At a nominal strain of 0.15, the initial collapse appears in the region with the largest cell. As the nominal strain is increased, the deformation band extends to a smaller cell region. After the strain reaches 0.70, the graded foam reaches the final deformation stage.

Since the graded foams with similar relative density, the deformation mechanism of graded foams with different gradient distributions is coupled together. The strain contours at nominal strain 0.04 of graded foams with different monotonic and nonmonotonic distributions are illustrated in Figs. 15.13 and 15.14, respectively. Although the strain curves of these graded foams are different observed in the lowest-density region with the largest cell size. For instance, the specimens with different gradient parameters differ at two ends of foams (Fig. 15.13) and the specimen “1/2-L” differs firstly at the center of the specimen (Fig. 15.14D).

After that, graded foam collapses from higher to lower relative densities layer-by-layer, causing a distinct increasing plateau stage in the associated stress—strain curves.
