Duan et al. [55] investigated the effects of cell regularity on the mechanical properties and deformation mechanisms of Voronoi foams (Fig. 14.14). Experimental results indicated that random foams have lower collapse stresses and plateau stresses over Kelvin foams. This is because random foams showed localized deformations and cell-wall tearing failures while Kelvin foams exhibited layered deformations and no tearing failure. To decouple the deformation and failure mechanisms affecting the Voronoi foam properties, further numerical investigations without introducing artificial stresses were carried out. It found that random foams have collapse but similar plateau stresses compared to Kelvin foams. Therefore it can be concluded that random foams have randomly distributed localized deformations and thus can always mitigate the collapse stress but not lower the plateau stress compared to Kelvin foams because of their cell-wall tearing failures.

## 14.3.3 Honeycomb architectures

Honeycomb [59,60] has low density, low cost, and a large deformation range. It is usually used as a sandwich material and has been widely employed in light energy-absorbing structures [61,62].

Duan et al. [59] conducted a compression-shear test on honeycombs and Kelvin foams to compare their yielded elbows. The honeycomb and foam specimens with a similar relative density were fabricated using the additive manufacturing technique. Results revealed that the normal strengths of both honeycomb and Kelvin foam decreased when the shear strengths increased with the loading angle increasing. Garra et al. [60] analyzed the effect of cell patterns on the mechanical properties of honeycomb materials. As shown in Fig. 14,16, the analysis encompassed the 2D cell shapes, including Anti-tracheal, Circular, Hexachiral, Hexagon, Re-entrant Hexagon type I and II, Lozenge, Rotachiral, Sinusoidal Layers, Square Grids, ScroBO and Tetrahedral. ## 14.3.4 Lattice architectures

Lattice architectures [63–66] have high designability. By optimizing the structural elements, improved mechanical properties, including specific strength and specific stiffness, can be achieved. This makes lattice architectures popular in structural lightweight design [67–75]. Moreover, the mechanical properties of lattice materials are also known as geometric configuration rather than chemical components, lattice materials are also metamaterials. While traditional manufacturing techniques fail to satisfy the requirement of designing lattice materials with complex configurations, this gap is posed to be filled by additive manufacturing technique [76–82].

Abucciella et al. [63] focused on the cellular solids with tri periodic surfaces (TPMS). They designed three TPMS architectures including Schwarz Primitive, Schoen I-wrapped cubic (IWP), and Neovius as shown in Fig. 14.17. They produced these models using SLS with polyamide 12 as the base material. They reported that IWP and Neovius architectures outperformed others in mechanical properties and both of them had higher stiffness and strength than the Primitive architecture.

TPMS architectures were also investigated by Bonatti et al. [64]. They employed a parameterized surface-smoothing technique to derive three families of lattices including simple cubic (SC), face-centered cubic (FCC), and body-centered cubic (BCC) unit cells. They pointed out that the lattices can provide higher average stiffness, strength, and energy absorption capabilities than truss and. In addition, shell lattices had geometric smoothness and the stress concentrations can be avoided. This better fatigue life and strength were expected to be obtained for shell lattices.
