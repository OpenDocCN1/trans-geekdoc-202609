## 9.3.2 Response classification of representative 4× 4 units

## 9.3.3 Machine learning modeling validation and response prediction

As mentioned in Section 9.2.3, with the trained DNN, we can estimate the response curve of any pattern from test data. In Fig. 9.5A and B, we present an example of 20% and 40% training ratio, where the coordinate of each blue dot compares sample force $f_{pred}$ and the corresponding reference value $f_{ref}$. A closer position of a dot to the bisection line ($y = x$) indicates a more accurate prediction. The bandwidth formed by these blue dots reflects the overall performance. A low training ratio is acceptable and tolerable. In Fig. 9.5C, we plotted the average PDF of each training ratio and marked the corresponding values $P_t < 1$. It can be observed that the PDF is more elevated (more accurate) with a higher training ratio, but the probability is not significantly influenced. We further conducted regression analysis for each predicted curve and the reference curve. From this, the obtained regression coefficient is a good estimator of whether a predicted curve follows the trend. In Fig. 9.5D, we grouped all into five subsets where discrepancy still exists which may be caused by the geometric infills and their layouts. We showed the data with NMSE value from 0 to 0.3 and defined by six intervals for each subset. The majority of data for all subsets is characterized by NMSE value from 0 to 0.05, indicating a good prediction by our ML model. Above the MSE of 0.05, in particular between 0.15 to 0.3 (see the inset in Fig. 9.5D), it can be seen that subsets 10–12 (green) and 13–15 (purple) infills are more dominant compared to the other subsets, confirming that the prediction error is mainly controlled by patterns with larger infill numbers.

## 9.4 Conclusion

In this chapter, we investigated the response complexity of complex materials caused by nonuniform geometric design. Through a combined FE modeling and clustering analysis, we discovered the hidden connection between a geometric feature (such as layout and number of infills) and their mechanical responses for $3 \times 3$ cellular patterns. Guided by learned geometric rules, we further characterized the responses of $4 \times 4$ cellular patterns with varied infills and experimentally verified the linking between geometric features and their mechanical performance. As the design domain becomes larger and failure mechanisms become more complex, we introduce a ML method to accelerate the response of patterns from testing data using a 20% training ratio. The framework is not limited to certain material architecture or number of configurations, although we only validated the framework using $4 \times 4$ cells. We believe that our ML-based framework should be capable of accelerating the material design with properties at the local level and improving the performance and/or functionality at the global level.
