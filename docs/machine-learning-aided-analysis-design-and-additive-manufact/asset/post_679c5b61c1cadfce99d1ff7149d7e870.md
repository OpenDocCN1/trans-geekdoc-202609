After the formwork is printed, the concrete material with high flowability is fed into the formwork for casting purposes. In some cases, prior to the concrete casting reinforcements are placed into the printed formwork to enhance the structural durability of the concrete. The concrete structure is considered digitally fabricated once the concrete material in the printed formwork gets hardened. Here the *Eggshell* method developed by ETH Zurich is briefly introduced as an example to demonstrate the ideas behind the Formwork-based Concrete Fabrication. The Eggshell primarily comprises two stages: (1) 3D printing of shell with thermoplastic materials (such as polyethylene terephthalate glycol) to generate the formwork with complex geometry; (2) simultaneously filling the formwork with fast-setting concrete during the formwork process to promote the structure fabrication efficiency (simultaneous process), or feeding the finished plastic shell with set-on-demand concrete for casting (consecutive fabrication process) [74]. An example of a simultaneous process using the *Eggshell* method is the 1.8 m tall Branching Column composed of three separate parts, as detailed in Ref. [74], while the Future Tree [75] could be a typical example of a consecutive fabrication process as part of the Eggshell method. For more detailed discussions on the applications and research progress of 3D-printed formworks in concrete structure fabrication, readers are suggested to refer to Ref. [76].

## 2. Digital fabrication of lightweight concrete structure

This subsection discusses the application of different digital concrete fabrication techniques in the field of fabricating the new generation of lightweight concrete structures, such as the porous structure and other structures with high geometric complexity.

As for the form-free 3DCP method, research has shown the feasibility of 3D printing lightweight concrete prisms with hollow structures. Wang et al. [77] utilized a self-developed printing system to print lightweight concrete blocks (150 × 150 × 150 mm) and beams (100 × 100 × 400 mm) with different hollow patterns, including cellular, triangular, truss, and lattice patterns. Recently, there has another innovative study on the lightweight 3D-printed concrete filament via the extrusion of hollow-core filaments. Ramakrishnan et al. [78] combined two types of extruded hollow filaments, including the rectangular hollow shape and U-shape (Fig. 13.8). Nanoclay was added to the mix design of concrete that improves the stability of the overhang from the extruded hollow filament at a fresh state. The innovative design of the nozzle therefore has another pathway to produce hollow filament with less weight. However, future works are expected regarding the fabrication of an integrated structure using such hollow concrete.

When the 3DCP method is applied to fabricate the complex lightweight concrete at a large scale, topology optimization is frequently incorporated at the design stage prior to the practical fabrication to achieve an optimized material layout within the structure [80,81]. The most representative example is the concrete structure developed by Vantyhem et al., who proposed an integrated fabrication strategy consisting of layer-deposition 3DCP process, topology optimization, and postconditioning of concrete structure [82]. The overall structure was subdivided into several parts. The code of each subdivided part was generated and sent to the robotic arm as the guiding path at the printing speed of 80mm/s, with the printing duration lasting 24 hours for the completion of all parts. Finally, all printed parts were assembled, and joined using posttensioning methods, with the interior filled with grout mortar.
