Compared with honeycombs and lattices, foams are of relatively low manufacturing costs without the precise geometrical control, and can be further divided in light material composites for specific application exchange [9]; ceramic foams are used as thermal insulation and polymer foams are widely employed as thermal packaging, and absorption materials [10]; glass foams, produced by the recycled materials can be adopted as microwave absorbers and contribute to the environmental sustainability [11]; concrete foams are regarded as the new trend in the civil engineering construction and show great potential in the manufacturing of lightweight components and offshore structures [12]. Among all the foams, metal foams are one of the most well-developed composite forms and have been included in the production of crash absorbers in vehicles, supports in working platforms, thermal cooling devices, and buildings [13].

Due to light nature, porous materials are often used for soft cores bounded by hard skins in the sandwich structural design, as illustrated in Fig. 1.3. It effectively raises the structural weight while limiting the increase in weight, enhancing the mechanical strength of honeycomb cells, plates, and without a huge increment in the base material consumption. Numerous studies have been devoted into this topic by exploring various configurations, fabrications, and applications of sandwich structures [14–18]. It is acknowledged that the possible delamination between hard skins and soft cores poses a threat to the structural integration and is induced by the significantly different material characteristics of adjacent layers.

The continuously/piece-wise changing of pore size and density along one or more directions, as presented in Fig. 1.4. The initiative of this design is to adjust the material distribution with graded cellular geometries, focusing on the overall performance improvement and control.

Various FG porous materials have been examined in recent years. They can be built up with any of the geometric morphologies (foams, honeycombs, or lattices) and diverse material compositions. Hangai et al. [19] fabricated the FG foams containing multilayers of pure Al and Al alloys, and conducted the static compression tests to reveal their deformation behaviors. Zhang and Wang [20] developed FG porous structures with open and closed-cells, and identified the variations in Young's modulus induced by the porosity characteristics. Loh et al. [21] reviewed the 3D printed porous concrete highlighted by the graded radial density inspired from the palm tree, and noted its potential in lightweight structural products due to the superior strength-to-weight ratio. Hong et al. [22] presented the FG porous Al2O3 ceramics with dense surface layer and investigated the structures using changing freezing temperatures. Du et al. [23] suggested that the graded porosity is able to lower the reflection and scattering of FG porous glasses for applications.

FG porous materials are demonstrated to have novel mechanical [24], biological [25], thermal [26], acoustic [27], energy [28] properties. Their unique functionality in this book is referred to the tolerable mechanical performances for potentials in structural, automotive, aerospace, and defense engineering fields. For instance, graded porosities lead to the shifting of fundamental natural frequencies and can be employed in the vehicle designs to avoid resonance while remaining lightweight. The integrated advantages of FG porosities may offer solutions to problems that cannot be resolved with existing material forms.

### 1.2.2 Fabrication

The fabrication methods of FG porous composites highly depend on the base material compositions and desirable geometric parameters. Meanwhile, the manufacturing of graded foams or scaffolds based on steel, copper, and titanium can be achieved with a powder metallurgy route using NHHCO3 as space holders [31], a rapid manufacturing technique using ultrasonic-asspressured sintering and 3D printing [32], and the powder metallurgy process with sugar pellets as space holders [33]. [37] reviewed the fabrication approaches for FG porous ceramic-, metal, and polymer-nanocomposites.

Additive manufacturing (3D printing) provides the possibility to shape-perfect complex geometry [38] as the desired pore distribution efforts have made to use this new technique in the development of FG porous components across various geometric morphologies. Wang et al. [39] proposed a design framework for additively manufactured cellular lattice structures with the optimized mesoscopic features. Zhang et al. [40] presented the 3D printed reentrant honeycombs consisting of stainless and polymer. Geng al. [41] introduced 3D concrete printing in the realization of lightweight high-performing cement-based composites with porosity gradients.

## 1.3 Functionally graded porous composite structures

### 1.3.1 Structural forms

Apart from the efforts concerning FG porous material itself, a number of studies explored it in the structural innovation and developed FG porous composite structures featured with graded porosity distributions in the internal pore size, density, shape, cell thickness and/or material composition. The majority of current studies in this field devote to the performance examination on typical structural forms, such as beams, plates, arches, shells, and frames (see Fig. 1.5 for examples), concerning improvements in properties induced by FG poros. The corresponding material gradients are normally along the thickness of target structures but may also be established across other directions, such as the bidirectional FG panels with porosity variations in both thickness and streamwise directions [42]. These structural components are taken as basic elements for the assembly of a complicated system.
