# Mechanical behavior of additively manufactured functionally graded porous structures

Yu Duan$^{1,2,3}$, Bing Du$^{1,2}$, Xianhang Zhao$^{1,2}$, Bing Hou$^{1,2}$ and Yulong Li$^{1,2}$

## 15.1 Introduction

Cellular materials and porous structures have superior energy absorption capabilities. They have been widely applied in various engineering applications, including energy absorption, structural protection, and blast dissipation [1–5]. As a typical cellular material, foam exhibits excellent shock mitigation and energy absorption under velocities and impact loads [6–9]. It has been shown that the property of foams depends on their relative density, and their designability can be limited by using uniform-density distribution. Consequently, to improve the mechanical of foams, graded foams have gained attention in recent years [10–15]. By optimizing the distribution of cell size, morphology, or relative density, graded foams exhibit multiple mechanical properties without varying loads.

There have been some limited studies on the compressive behavior of foams [16–18], Gupta and Ricci [16] carried out a study on micro balloon foams with a graded volume fraction and wall thickness. Mackey et al. [17] constructed the mechanical properties of graded foams with uniform foams. Zeng et al. [18] suggested that placing the hardest layer in contact with the striker and the weakest layer as the impacted layer could achieve maximum energy absorption. However these graded foams possess a discrete gradient with a piecewise variation of density, which limits the performance of these materials. To manufacture foam with a continuous gradient, Hangai et al. [19] fabricated continuously graded aluminum foams by the stir casting route. Brothers and Duan [20] density-graded foams using the nonuniform foambuilder and the powder-structure hybrid, respectively. Both found that graded foams exhibited a smoothly rising plateau, which is due to the progressive deformation of each component layer. Once a weaker layer fails, a stronger layer would supplement to sustain the applied load and stress would exceed the strength of the current layer.

However, the aforementioned methods achieve the continuous variation of foam density in a monotonic or uncontrollable way.

The graded foams with nonmonotonic and controllable gradients can be obtained by finite element simulation [22,23] and theoretical models [24,25]. Cui et al. [26] studied the energy absorption of graded foams using the finite element method. Chen et al. [27] investigated the gradient effect on compressive property, deformation pattern as well as energy absorption of graded foams. Owen et al. [28] found that the single shock mode was observed in foams with a positive gradient while there was double shock mode in negatively graded foams. Yang et al. [29] and Ding et al. [30] optimized the crashworthiness of graded foams. Due to the difficulty in fabricating graded foams with continuous and controllable gradients, these numerical models can hardly be verified. Furthermore, in general, accurate models from experimental data need to be established It is worth noting that graded foams have great potential in energy absorption. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is a limited experimental study of continuously graded foams. The objective of this chapter is to conduct testing and investigate the mechanisms of graded foams using recently emerging fabrication methods, such as additive manufacturing techniques [31–35]. Among many additive manufacturing with desired complex structures, fused filament fabrication enables the construction of enclosed space models [36,37] and is chosen to produce foam. Uniform foams are both produced and tested. A systematic comparison of the mechanical between graded foams and uniform foams is performed. We establish an elastic, plastic-hardening model for graded foams based on experimental data and stress-strain relationship of analogous foams. The predictive models proposed in this work can be used to predict the stress-strain curves of graded foams in engineering applications.

The outline of this chapter is organized as follows. Section 15.2 introduces the production of Voronoi foams as well as the experimental settings. Then, quasi-static experimental results are presented in Sections 15.3 and 15.4, respectively, the compressive response and deformation mechanism of graded foams are both discussed in these sections. In Section 15.5, predictive models of stress–strain relations of graded foams are proposed and validated. Finally, the chapter is concluded in Section 15.6.

## 15.2 Materials and methods

### 15.2.1 Experimental set-up

#### 15.2.1.1 Quasi-static loading

A universal testing machine with 100 kN load cell is employed to conduct tests in displacement control with a strain rate of 0.001 s⁻¹. The tests are carried out based on the ASTM D1621 standard. The manufactured specimens are compressed between two circular plates up to a nominal strain of 0.8 (beyond the densification threshold). The applied loading force $F$ is measured by the load cell and the corresponding normal stress is calculated by $\sigma = F/A_0$ (where $A_0$ is the initial cross-sectional area of specimens). The cross displacement $\Delta L$ is measured by the optical extensometer and the corresponding normal strain is calculated by $\varepsilon = \Delta L/L_0$ (where $L_0$ is initial length of specimens along the loading direction). Two digital cameras are used to capture the global deformation evolutions for two faces of specimens. The frame rate and image resolution are 24 fps and $1280 \times 800$ pixels, the strain contours of specimens are then calculated by the digital image correlation (DIC) method. The DIC method matches the corresponding points between two images. To maintain conciseness, the detailed descriptions of the DIC method are not provided here and can be found in Refs., [38, 39].

### 15.1.2 Dynamic loading

The Split Hopkins Bar has been widely used in testing the mechanical properties of materials due to its accurate measurement and constant loading rate [40,41]. Two specimens, including the direct impact test and the Taylor test, are considered here to investigate the crushing behaviors of additively manufactured Voronoi foams. In the direct impact test, a specimen is cemented on the support bar, and the impact bar is free to compress the specimen (Fig. 15.1A). In the Taylor test, the same specimen is cemented on the impact bar, and the support experiences dynamic impact (Fig. 15.1B). The two bars have the same diameter of 10 mm and are both made of Nylon PA66. The lengths of the impact bar and support bar are 400 and 300 mm, respectively. In both, a pair of strain gauges are mounted at a distance of 100 mm from the specimen-support interface to record the strain pulse $\varepsilon_{bar}(t)$. The semiconductor strain gauges, which amplify the transmission pulses in Nylon materials, are observed; the loading force $F$ is calculated by $F = S_{bar} E_{bar} \varepsilon_{bar}(t)$, where $S_{bar}$ and $E_{bar}$ are the cross-sectional area and elastic modulus (513 MPa) of the bar. Finally, the normal stress $\sigma$ of specimens is calculated via $F/A$, where $A$ is the initial cross-sectional area of specimens. It should be pointed out that the strain pulse recorded by the strain gauges is equal to that on the specimen-support bar face. It has barely influences the experimental results [39].
