## 13.1 Introduction

Porous structures with microstructural architecture are in high demand for the construction industry as they contribute considerably to the overall performance in terms of high strength-to-weight ratio [1], energy absorption capacity [2], heat and sound insulation [3]. Many studies have been extensively considered on the design and optimization of lightweight cellular structures to maximize their tunable properties [4–6]. Tri periodic minimal surfaces TPMS are naturally optimized as the new generation of lightweight structures [7]. Recently, research activities on these functionally graded porous structures (FGPS) have grown significantly. Other types of FGPS are strut-based, body-centered cubic lattice, foam, and auxetic structures, which also offer exceptional features [3,8,9]. Furthermore, fractal-structures that were finely smoothed also offer an effective approach to increasing the design space as well as tailoring mechanical properties.

Among all, the TPMS cellular structures are found to be superior to others in terms of mechanical properties [6,11,12]. Lee et al. [13] studied the mechanical properties of TPMS cellular structures to show their higher stiffness compared to lattice structures. Tran and Peng [14] investigated various types of TPMS composites with metallic faces under blast loading, which showed better blast-resistance capacity than honeycomb unit functionally graded porous sandwich panels were also demonstrated in another study [15,16]. In a recent investigation, Peng et al. [17] used both analytical, numerical, and experiments to test the bending capacity and absorbed energy of 3D-printed sandwiches with TPMS cores (I-graph and wrapped package sandwich (IWP), and Neovius cell unit). The study indicated that the band with TPMS-Neo core provided better performance to other units.

However, the geometry of FGPS TPMS cellular structures is complicated due to its continuous-non-intersecting surfaces. The manufacturing process of these structures cannot be done by conventional methods [18]. Additive manufacturing (AM) or 3D printing—a recent advanced technology—has the ability to manufacture complex designs [19,20]. This technique is considered as one of the most important trends in digital construction, which allows the freer fabrication from a wide range of materials with mass distribution to directly print these highly complex geometries [21,22]. Besides, the structures can be optimized during the design process to save time and construction cost, while minimizing material waste, as well as satisfying safety and aesthetic criteria [23].

In recent years, the additive manufacturing of concrete (AMoC) has been employed to fabricate FGPS, such as TPMS cementous cellular structures [6,24,25], fractal-like structures [1], and Bologna structures [26,27]. It is not easy to practically implement the FGPS on a large scale, as the AMoC is still in its infancy [28]. Depending on the availability of framework in concrete fabrication, the AMoC method is divided into two categories: (1) AMoC with no 3D concrete printing (3DCP) that is obtained through layer-by-layer deposition [29], shot [30], powder-based technique [31]; (2) FGPS, including 3D printing framework [32]. However, the FGPS normally contain many parts, which can be difficult to achieve by using the 3DCP technique without framework. To overcome this limitation, 3D printing framework becomes the mechanism of fabricating complex and optimized structures like the FGPS [33]. By using the 3D printing framework technique, the mechanical performance of the TPMS cementous cellular structures was first conducted by Vuong et al. [6] using 3D-printed polyacrylic acid (PLA) molds. Besides, mechanical behaviors of different types of fractal-like cementous cellular blocks under uniaxial compressive loading were numerically studied, in which the 3D printing method was supposed to be employed [1]. The 3DCP process without framework was also used to print some FGPS like honeycomb, cellular panel, and Bologna structures to study their failure and inelastic deflection.

With the super mechanical properties of the TPMS, these structures can be promising for a wide range of engineering applications such as noise barrier wall, decorative walls, coastal protection blocks, and so forth. The aim of this chapter is to present a comprehensive review for evaluating the behaviors of these TPMS subjected to various types of loads including static and dynamic loading. A description of the design process and manufacturing techniques for these porous structures is provided in Sections 13.2 and 13.3, followed by the discussion in Section 13.1 in which performance evaluations of the TPMS structures under static and dynamic loading are investigated. The last section of this chapter is the Conclusions, regarding the results obtained, and potential applications of the TPMS structures.

## 13.2 Design of functionally graded porous structures

### 13.2.1 Topology design of triply periodic minimal surface

Mathematically, minimal surfaces are defined as surfaces with mean curvature of zero at a given point on the surface,

$H = 0, \quad (13.1)$

where $H = (k_1 + k_2)/2$; $k_1$ and $k_2$ are the principal curvatures. The Gaussian curvature is defined as:

$K = k_1 k_2 \quad (13.2)$

If the minimal surface is repeated infinitely in three directions, it is known as triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS) [34]. Several methods were explored to define TPMS using the Weierstrass-Enneper representation:

$x = \text{Re} \int f(w)(1-w^2) dw$
$y = \text{Re} \int f(w)i(1+w^2) dw$
$z = \text{Re} \int f(w)2w dw \quad (13.3)$

where $f(w)$ and $g(w)$ are holomorphic functions. However, the most common method to generate TPMS is based on trigonometric functions:

$\Psi(x,y,z) = \sum_{n,m,k} A_{nmk} \cos(nx + my + kz) \quad (13.4)$

where $A_{nmk}$ are the coefficients. For different types of TPMS, the equations are:

- Primitive: $\Phi_{Primitive}(x,y,z) = \cos(x) + \cos(y) + \cos(z) = c \quad (13.5)$
- Gyroid: $\Phi_{Gyroid}(x,y,z) = \sin(x)\cos(y) + \sin(y)\cos(z) + \sin(z)\cos(x) = c \quad (13.6)$
- IWP: $\Phi_{IWP}(x,y,z) = \cos(x)\cos(y) + \cos(y)\cos(z) + \cos(z)\cos(x) = c \quad (13.7)$
- Fischer-S: $\Phi_{F-S}(x,y,z) = \sin(x)\sin(y) + \sin(y)\sin(z) + \sin(z)\sin(x) = c \quad (13.8)$
- Diamond: $\Phi_{Diamond}(x,y,z) = \sin(x)\sin(y)\sin(z) + \sin(y)\sin(z)\sin(x) + \sin(z)\sin(x)\sin(y) = c \quad (13.9)$

In these equations, $x, y,$ and $z$ are spatial coordinates, $w_i = 2\pi\frac{n_i}{L_i}$ where $n_i$ determines the number of units along the directions $x, y, z$, and $L_i$ is the size of the structure in the corresponding direction.
