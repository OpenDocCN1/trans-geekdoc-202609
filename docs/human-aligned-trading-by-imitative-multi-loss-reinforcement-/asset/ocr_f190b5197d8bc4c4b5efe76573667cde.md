Of the five baseline models, the supervised learning model often produces better performance than the rest. This may be because the supervised model follows a simpler rule to execute a trading action entirely based on the prediction of stock movement over the next minute, whereas RL-based models have the action-taking mechanism embedded in the model itself which makes it harder to train. However, HAT’s outperformance proves that a well-trained value-based RL trading system with sufficient amount of human aligning features should execute more optimal actions over time than actions taken purely based on supervised learning outcomes for the fact that the Q value represents the reward over an entire trading period and not just over the next period. Separately, DQfD’s performance is not far off that of supervised learning. After all, it shares a similar modelling paradigm as HAT although it places less emphasis on actions multiple steps ahead.

Table 1 also compares returns by HAT to the buy-and-hold returns over the same periods. The finding is particularly interesting. For the five blue chip stocks of AAPL, COST, DIS, JPM, and NFLX, HAT outperforms a buy-and-hold in a market that is trending down when trading these stocks, but sometimes lags the buy-and-hold in an uptrend market. In a sideways-going market, HAT is marginally better.

In down-trending and up-trending markets, our results may be explainable. When the stock price is falling, holding a stock gives negative returns, but the model can take advantage of short-selling to deliver a cumulative return that is better than buy-and-hold. On the other hand, when the stock price goes through a strong upward movement during the day, the model often may match the return of a buy-and-hold, but there are times when it cannot do so, because of wrong actions taken to short-sell a stock when the price is actually going up. The situation in a sideways-going market indicates that, as much as we would have liked the model to deliver superb performances, a sophisticated model can ultimately be somewhat constrained by the training data set.

When there is no clear trend in the underlying data, the model’s actions appear less well-judged and hence deliver a set of returns that occasionally outperforms but other times simply match those of buy-and-hold.

On the other hand, the HAT model beats a buy-and-hold strategy most of the time with the five small cap stocks of NVAX, NVTA, APPS, RUN, and SFIX, which have less trading liquidity. We present an example trading day for Sunrun Inc (SUN) in Fig. 4 and it is evident that the model trades a lot less often with this stock. A study of historic tick data of these small-cap stocks reveals that their stock prices would stay unchanged over periods of minutes more often than their heavily-traded blue chip counterparts.
