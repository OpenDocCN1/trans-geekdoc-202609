
Although the above tools are effective, they scale poorly. Netcode works well for titles in which player inputs are fairly predictable, such as driving simulations, or those with relatively few players to synchronize, as is the case in most fighting games. It is exponentially harder to correctly predict and coherently synchronize the behaviors of dozens of players, especially when they are participating in a sandbox-style virtual world with cloud-streamed environmental and asset data. And large though the gaming industry might seem on a revenue basis, it remains highly constrained in reach. Even in the United States, which has only 4% of the world’s population and 6% of its land by surface area, yet 26% of its internet networks, Subspace estimates that barely three-quarters of broadband homes can reliably play high-fidelity games such as `Fortnite` or `Call of Duty`.^([11](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr11)) In contrast, the Middle East has 5% of the world’s population, 5% of its land, but only 3% of its networks, and fewer than one in four homes can reliably play the aforementioned titles.^([12](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr12)) It’s even harder to play in India, which has 18% of the world’s population, 2% of its land, and 3.5% of its networks, with only one in ten broadband homes meeting high-fidelity gaming requirements.^([13](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr13))

### The Battle between `H. sapiens` and `c`

Outlandish though it may seem, the fight to minimize network latency is, at its core, a fight against the speed of light. Consider, as an example, the quest to send just a single byte from a data center in downtown New York City to one in downtown Tokyo or Mumbai as rapidly as possible. At a distance of 11,000–12,500 kilometers, a light photon could hypothetically complete such a trip in between 36 and 42 ms. The physics of the universe, in other words, has only a roughly 6–12 ms margin over the physics of the human brain. In practice, this millisecond benchmark is impossible to attain.

One impediment is silica glass. Many assume that data sent via fiber-optic cables travels at the speed of light. This is technically true, but all glass fibers, unlike the vacuum of space, reflect light. For one thing, the path of a given beam of light is not straight even if the cable itself is perfectly straight. Instead, a beam of light travels in more of a tight zigzag as it bounces between the inside edges of a fiber-optic cable core. The result is a more than 45% elongation of a route, which produces a minimum possible latency of 52–61 ms. Although such a delay already tests the patience of avid gamers, this figure is hypothetical. In 2021, Amazon’s AWS, Microsoft’s Azure, and Google’s Cloud Platform averaged more than 110 ms when sending a packet one way from their northeastern American data centers to their Asia-Pacific data centers, which route to Mumbai and Tokyo. Travel inside the United States faces a comparable increase. Although beams of light could travel from New York City to San Francisco in 14 ms and through fiber optics in 20 ms, average transit times are over 30 ms. There are several causes for these increases.

While the phrase “internet backbone” brings to mind a largely planned-out and partly federated network of cables, the internet is really a loose federation of private networks with individual aims and little concern for collective efficiency. Furthermore, internet cables are rarely laid out as the crow flies. Instead, they must navigate environmental concerns, existing public and private infrastructure (cities, parks, freeways), governmental permits and ordinances, and the costs of given routes (a longer route may require only earthen trenches or may piggyback on an in-construction highway, while the shortest might require blasting rock). In general, the more direct the intended path of a cable, the more likely it is to be politically, socially, financially, or environmentally challenged. And in most cases, there’s not much of a business case for a specific network to be laid so that it is a few milliseconds faster. As a hypothetical, imagine a private broadband operator seeking to connect two cities or regions that are roughly 300 kilometers apart. The most direct path would allow data packets to arrive in 1.3 ms, but laying this cable might require digging through a river or under a school. An alternative route might follow local roads and be laid during an existing roadway repair process. This latter option might save effort and money, but add 100 kilometers and, with it, another 0.4 ms. These milliseconds add up when a user needs to reach a person or server many thousands of miles and many dozens of networks away, but the internet users directly connected by this hypothetical cable won’t pay for the 0.4 ms in savings, and most of the latency challenge comes from the aggregation of the networks in use, not that one segment.

Many countries and even major international cities lack a direct connection. New York City has a direct undersea cable to France but not to Portugal. Traffic from the United States can go directly to Tokyo, but reaching India requires jumping from one undersea cable to another on the Asian or Australian continent, a process that adds thousands of kilometers to the trip. To shorten this path, a dedicated line would need to be dug across at least Thailand, if not also Vietnam, Laos, and Myanmar. And again, such a connection would solve only for shore-to-shore transmission. In 2020, the US government effectively outlawed the construction of cables that directly connected the United States to Hong Kong or mainland China, prompting a still-under-construction cable funded by Facebook and Google to redirect to the Philippines and Taiwan.^([14](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr14)) The following year, the World Bank abandoned a cable project that would have connected three Pacific Island nations, after the United States protested its construction by a Chinese supplier.^([15](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr15))

## US Internet Backbone

![A 2023 map of active terrestrial internet networks in the United States. ¶ Infrapedia](OEBPS/images/pg140.jpg)

A 2023 map of active terrestrial internet networks in the United States. ¶ Infrapedia

The layout of the internet’s many networks is exacerbated by the Border Gateway Protocol (`BGP`), one of the core application layer protocols of `TCP/IP`. When a given device wants to send data to another unaffiliated device or server, at least one intermediary network is required, but within a large country, it’s common for data to travel across five to seven networks. International delivery might span one to two dozen network “hops” or more, depending on how remote and far away the two devices or servers are. These pathways are not fixed, either. Two emails sent from the same device in New York to the same device in Los Angeles might start on the same Wi-Fi network and Verizon FiOS wireline network and terminate on the same AT&T wireless network, but in the meantime, one email might travel through the midwestern United States and head south along the Pacific Coast, while the other might first head south to the Carolinas before traveling across the Southwest. The `BGP` dictates these pathways by helping every single network leg choose which network will pass a given packet of data. Accordingly, an American’s daily internet usage might involve hundreds or even thousands of the country’s more than 30,000 networks.^([16](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr16))
