
The goal of the Google/Stanford simulation was not to prove that bots can be more human than human—nor did it. Instead, the intent was to see if LLMs could be used to create AI that could populate “metaverses,” operate socially capable robots in the real world, be used to “train people on how to handle rare yet difficult interpersonal situations” or “test social science theories,” create “non-playable game characters that can navigate complex human relationships,” or serve as a truly personalized digital assistant.^([42](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr42)) The answer seems to be yes and the support these AI might provide continues to grow. In 2023, a pair of professors at the University of Pennsylvania’s Wharton School ran a study to determine whether MBA students or ChatGPT-4 would devise, on average, better business ideas. To do so, 200 students were each asked to create an idea for “a new product or service appealing to college students that could be made available for $50 or less.”^([43](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr43)) ChatGPT-4 was asked the same question, providing 100 total responses, after which the model was trained on a selection of successful ideas submitted by students in prior classes, and then provided another 100 ideas. The 200 human ideas took many human days of work to generate, whereas ChatGPT-4 took only an hour. Once all ideas had been tallied, the Wharton professors constructed a survey that asked potential customers how likely they were to purchase the related product. The average “purchase probability of a human-generated idea” was 40%, while the untrained GPT-4 ideas netted 47%, and the trained GPT-4 received a 49%. When considering only the ideas which were in the top 10% of purchase probability scores, only 5 were created by students, 15 came from the untrained GPT model, and 20 from the trained one.^([44](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr44))

## #6: Solving for Interoperability

Lastly, AI provides a pathway to overcoming one of the Metaverse’s most intractable problems—not scarce computational power, bandwidth limitations, battery life, or payment costs, but interoperability. Just as AI can recognize and create objects, while also intuiting their purpose and attributes, it is increasingly able to convert between file formats and conventions, fill in the information that’s lacking or discard that which unnecessarily burdens a CPU or GPU, separate parts of a single asset (e.g., a tie from a suit model), and individually customize them, too. Of course, AI can’t yet solve for the business models of interoperation, but mediating human disagreements and commercial incentives may forever remain out of a neural network’s digital grasp.

# [CONCLUSION](#contents.xhtml#con1)

“Technology frequently produces surprises that no one predicts. But the biggest and most fantastical developments are often anticipated decades in advance.” These words opened this book, and the pages since have hopefully demonstrated why and how the Metaverse is likely to fulfill them.

I’m certain that the future will be increasingly centered around real-time-rendered 3D virtual worlds and networks. Global internet bandwidth, latency, and reliability will all improve, even if the speed of light will remain the Metaverse’s greatest adversary irrespective of submarine cables, low-orbit satellites, atmospheric drones and balloons, and advances in AI or neural networks. The amount of computing power will grow, and that power will enable higher concurrency, greater persistency, more sophisticated simulations, and altogether new experiences. The locational mix of this computing power (i.e., remote, edge, local) is less clear, as is the nature of its operations (centralized, decentralized, quantum, or more fanciful concepts such as biocomputing). No matter the answer, I’d bet the supply of computing power will fall well short of the demand for it.

Interoperability will be achieved slowly, imperfectly, and never exhaustively or without cost. While the market will eventually coalesce around a subset of standards, these standards won’t convert perfectly into one another or support every use case, and each will have drawbacks, too. And before then, scores of would-be standards will be proposed, adopted, deprecated, and forked. AI will be an essential accelerant but is no panacea—least of all where human incentives, competition, and preferences are concerned. In the same spirit, it’s likely that economic interoperation is achieved not through virtual worlds “opening up.” Instead, platforms will strike bilateral “trade deals,” just as the United States has different policies with Canada, Indonesia, Egypt, and Honduras, in addition to the European Union (itself a collection of agreements spanning a finite set of “worlds”).

It will take longer than hoped for HMDs and other mixed-reality wearables to see widespread adoption—and even longer for this equipment to displace existing devices and do everything that science fiction authors and readers have dreamed for close to a century. And yet there is no doubt that the field continues to mature and that, overdue, its era will one day arrive. Given the cost of these devices, there are concerns that their adoption will produce greater inequality—especially in fields such as education. I suspect this will be true, though only temporarily.
