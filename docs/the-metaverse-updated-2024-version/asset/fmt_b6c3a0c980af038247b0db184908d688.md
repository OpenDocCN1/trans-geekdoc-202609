
Alongside the new devices that will be used to interact with the Metaverse are those that will help generate it. Leica Geosystems, a descendent of the storied camera manufacturer, now sells $30,000–$60,000 photogrammetric 3D cameras (and camera-equipped drones) that can capture hundreds of thousands and even millions of “laser scan set points per second” that are designed to capture the entire interiors of buildings such as malls and family homes, or office parks, with greater clarity and detail than the average person would ever see if they were physically on-site.^([86](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr86)) The satellite-imaging company Planet Labs, mentioned in [Chapter 7](#chapter7.xhtml), performs scans of nearly the entire earth daily across eight spectral bands, enabling not just high-resolution imagery but details including heat, biomass, and haze. To produce this imagery, it operates the third-largest private fleet of satellites of any company in the world, with over 200, many of which weigh less than 5 kilograms and are smaller than 10 × 10 × 30 centimeters.^([87](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr87)) Every photo from these satellites covers 20–25 square kilometers and is made up of 47 megapixels, with each pixel representing 3 × 3 meters. Roughly 1.5 GB of data is sent from each of these satellites per second and from an average distance of 1,000 kilometers. Will Marshall, the CEO and co-founder of Planet Labs, believes that the cost per performance of these satellites has improved a thousandfold since 2011.^([88](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr88)) Such scanning devices make it easier and cheaper for companies to produce high-quality “mirror worlds” or “digital twins” of physical spaces—and to use scans of the real world to produce higher-quality and less expensive fantasy worlds.

Also important are depth-sensing cameras that can track the precise movement of individual customers, equipment, and objects in real time and produce them inside a digital twin (more on this in [Chapter 12](#chapter12.xhtml)). Without such device, a digital twin is largely “fixed” in time or otherwise limited to information generated through location sensors (e.g., pressure sensors, UWB transmitters, NFC chips). Technologies such as Google’s Starline, as detailed in [Chapter 3](#chapter3.xhtml), also allow remote workers to be simultaneously “present” in the store (potentially from an offshore “Metaverse call center”), jumping across different screens to help the customer.

A final technology, which has seen limited deployment outside of immersive art installations thus far but also seems poised for popularity later this decade, is XR projectors. These devices are intended to transform any surface or collection of surfaces into fully rendered touchscreens—irrespective of how uneven or partially obstructed the surface might be, or its originally intended purpose. To do so, these projectors use various depth sensors to understand the dimensions of the surface they are projecting onto, and then use an onboard computer processor to alter the pixels that are being cast onto that surface so that the combined projection appears cohesive and undistorted. Depth sensors are also used to determine what parts of the projection the user is pointing to or touching, and to convert these gestures into inputs (traditional touch displays have onboard sensors to literally feel the user’s movements). As users often obscure part of a projection while attempting to use it, a network of XR projectors is typically needed to operate larger displays.

There are many potential applications and uses for XR projectors, spanning games, productivity, education, and engineering. For example, they can turn an entire wall into a chess board or iPad homescreen, or a kitchen table into a keyboard and trackpad, can transform a blank plastic (though potentially mechanized) mannequin into a fully clothed, animated, and seemingly sense-capable character, or highlight the real-time operations, weather, and topography of a physical 3D model of a city (as per Strategic Development Partners’ Water Street development). In November 2023, Reuters reported that Amazon was preparing a major push into the XR projector field, having bought projection mapping hardware start-up Lightform two years earlier, with Lightform’s co-founder and CEO, Brett Jones, becoming a head of product in Amazon’s devices group.^([89](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr89)) According to a job posting made by Jones earlier in the year, his group was looking for design technologists with “experience designing 3D spatial interactions for AR/VR or games using tools like Unity, Unreal Engine, etc.”^([90](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr90))

In January 2024, Disney publicly unveiled its HoloTile technology, which the gaming website Kotaku described as a computer-powered “omnidirectional treadmill-like floor” made up of a “layer of small, circular roller \[tiles\] that can shift and rotate as people walk on them.”^([91](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr91)) The purpose of HoloTiles is to enable users to “run, walk, and even dance” while remaining in the same position (give or take an inch or two), thereby offering users the ability to more fully immerse their bodies inside a virtual environment without needing to be physically present inside an enormous (and likely padded) room.^([92](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr92)) According to Disney, HoloTile can be used by multiple people at the same time and can be programmed to directly move the user (thus prompting a response) or alter the level of resistance (e.g., simulating thick muck or slick floors).^([93](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr93))

## Long Live the Smartphone

It’s fun to imagine all the brilliant new devices that soon enough will allow humanity to “jack into the Metaverse.” But, at least through the end of the 2020s, it’s likely that most devices for the Metaverse era will be updated versions of the same devices used in 2024 (and 2014). Most experts, including Unity Technologies CEO John Riccitiello, estimate that by 2030, there will be fewer than 250 million VR and AR headsets in use.^([94](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr94)) Of course, betting on such long-term prognostications is dangerous. The first iPhone was released in 2007, eight years after the first BlackBerry smartphone and at a point when smartphone penetration was less than 5% in the United States. Eight years later, the iPhone had sold more than 800 million units and driven US penetration to nearly 80%. Few in 2007 believed that by 2020, two-thirds of everyone on earth would have a smartphone.

AR and VR devices face not just significant technical, financial, and experiential obstacles but also the need to squeeze in. Behind the rapid growth of smartphones was a simple pair of facts: the personal computer was one of the most significant inventions in human history, and more than 30 years after it was invented, fewer than one in six people worldwide owned one. And those lucky few who did? Well, their computers were large and all but immovable. AR and VR devices will not be a person’s first computing device, nor even their first portable one. They are fighting to be a person’s third, fourth, fifth—and for a long time yet will probably be one of their least powerful, too.
