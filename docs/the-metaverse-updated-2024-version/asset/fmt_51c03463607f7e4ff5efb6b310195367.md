
# Chapter 9 HARDWARE, HMDS, AND THE HARDEST TECHNOLOGY CHALLENGE OF OUR TIME

A year before AOUSD, nearly three dozen companies—including competitors such as Meta, Microsoft, Epic Games, Unity, Adobe, and Nvidia, plus organizations such as the World Wide Web Consortium and Khronos Group—had come together to create the Metaverse Standards Forum, an organization intended to facilitate the creation of various standards groups such as OpenUSD. By its first anniversary in June 2023, MSF had amassed over 2,400 member organizations, prompting hope that Pixar would not be the last company to give away control of its proprietary technologies for the collective benefit.

For many, the most exciting aspect of the Metaverse is the new devices that might be used to access, render, and operate it. A great deal of attention focuses on head-mounted displays (HMDs), which are typically categorized in three ways. First are virtual reality (VR) headsets, which typically include a pair of one-to-two-inch displays directly in front of a user’s eyes which show a purely synthetic 3D environment. To aid immersion and visual clarity, the user’s eyes are usually fully surrounded by the headset, which thereby blocks out their peripheral view of the “real world.” In a simplified sense, a VR device is like watching a smartphone only an inch from one’s eyes and in landscape orientation, while wearing visual blinders and using head, eye, and other bodily motion to operate the device and potentially handheld controllers too. VR users are almost always stationary when using the device. While VR replaces “the real world,” augmented reality (AR) adds information on top of it. An analogy might be viewing the world with live and digital annotations, such as a virtual signpost that identifies an in-view statue and enables the user to press a digital button that plays an audio recording and perhaps a visual animation of the individual. There are two core methods of AR. In the first, the user sees through glass that is selectively illuminated to render AR information (“optical AR”). In the second, the user is looking at a live video of the real world that their device has captured and subsequently annotated (“passthrough AR”). As with VR headsets, AR devices are typically controlled, at least in part, by tracking a user’s hands, eyes, or body.

The last HMD category is mixed-reality (MR) devices, which are similar to AR but involve more direct interaction with the real-world environment. So a user might be able to play virtual tennis with the virtual ball, bouncing off both the real-world floor and the nearby wall, or collect fairies that have been programmatically placed throughout one’s home, hiding in drawers and flying around household plants. AR and MR devices are intended for both stationary and mobile use.

Many AR and MR headsets can also offer VR experiences by filling in all available pixels and using no visual inputs from the real world. Most, though not all, companies investing in VR, AR, and MR consider the ultimate goal to be a pair of lightweight MR glasses (not a “headset” or “goggles”) capable of sophisticated MR experiences, though such a device would not necessarily replace dedicated VR or AR devices, just as smartphones have not replaced the occasional need for a PC. As of yet, this goal is well behind schedule.

In 2016, Mark Zuckerberg repeated his belief that within a decade, “normal-looking” AR glasses not only would be a part of everyday life, but the devices would often displace the role of a smartphone for calls, sharing photos, and browsing the web.^([1](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr1)) A big-screen TV, meanwhile, would be replaced by a “$1 app” that would place an even larger AR screen on a family’s living room wall.^([2](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr2)) That same year, Tim Sweeney predicted that within five to seven years, VR devices would be as powerful as the PCs of 2016 yet a fraction of the price and shrunk down into glasses the size of Oakley sunglasses.^([3](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr3)) When Google unveiled its Google Glass smart glasses hardware in 2012, the company suggested that annual sales could reach tens of millions by 2015 and eventually appeal to the nearly 80% of people who wear glasses daily.^([4](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr4)) Instead, Google Glass became one of big tech’s most infamous flops, with sales failing to crack 100,000 units. In a similar vein, most estimates show that fewer than one million AR headsets or glasses have been sold in the decade since that market misfire. VR has performed better, with roughly 50–60 million in lifetime sales,^([5](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr5)) but many buyers stop using the device within weeks of purchase.^([6](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr6)) In October 2022, a few weeks before the Quest 2’s second anniversary since release, the *Wall Street Journal* reported that barely a third of the 20 million devices sold by Facebook were used monthly.^([7](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr7))

These poor results are not for lack of effort. In the nine years after acquiring Oculus VR in March 2014, Facebook has spent roughly $53 billion on its Reality Labs division. This spend spans marketing, manufacturing, and device subsidies—most Quests are sold below cost—but also includes investments into software, R&D, and M&A, some of which relates to devices, technologies, or experiences that have yet to see release, with another portion relating to products Facebook has since discontinued or shuttered. The resulting revenues are estimated at $7 billion, producing a $46 billion lifetime division loss, with roughly 20 million Oculus/Quest devices shipped over that same period. In 2021, Facebook partnered with Ray-Ban to release several models of co-branded glasses that enabled users to take photos and listen to music from the device, among other features. The *Wall Street Journal* has reported that 300,000 units were sold over the first eighteen months, with fewer than one in ten purchasers using them monthly.^([8](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr8)) In April 2022, Facebook was reportedly “rushing” to release its first AR glasses by the end of 2024,^([9](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr9)) but by February 2023, the company had pushed its target release date to 2027—11 years after Zuckerberg had predicted it would take less than a decade for the form factor to become quotidian.^([10](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr10))
