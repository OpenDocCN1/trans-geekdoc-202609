
To get a clearer sense of the challenge, imagine a user purchasing a good on Platform A and then attempting to use it on Platform B. Does Platform A provide Platform B access to an ownership record and asset maintained on Platform A’s servers, or transmit both to Platform B and then delete its own records? The latter method seems needlessly complex, as the user might then take the good to Platform C or sell it to a user on Platform D. But if Platform A maintains control, how is it compensated for this service? Who is going to pay for the related costs of storage, various transactions, and various legal risks? Keep in mind, the volume of requests to Platform A can be quite high, as they would come not just from Platform B but also hundreds of thousands, if not millions, of other platforms and independent worlds. Moreover, Platform A would also need to allow third-party platforms to modify this record and facilitate the sale of its underlying product to another user. And what if the user who was using an item from Platform A on Platform B wanted to sell it to a Platform B user who didn’t have an account on Platform A—then what happens?

The typical response is that some form of digital storage-locker service offered by virtual world producers and independent companies will emerge. These lockers might charge monthly fees, usage fees, or transaction-based fees or monetize through ads and data collection and arrange to permanently house and provide access to a user’s virtual goods. The issue with a “Metaverse storage locker” is that it’s a bit of a brand-based handwave. While it presumes more bespoke technology to support its supposed functionality, it doesn’t address the problems involved, nor ensure adoption. In addition, these wallets still need to be able to transfer items to other wallets (otherwise a user’s ever-growing investments would forever be stuck in and controlled by said operator) and receive them from various platforms.

Finally, there are the financial requirements of interoperability. Any platform that enables the exchange of valuable goods must comply with state-to-state “Know Your Customer” (commonly known as KYC) regulations, which help governments mitigate money laundering and ensure income is reported to tax authorities. Many virtual worlds also generate at least part of their revenue from the sale of virtual goods. Often, they operate complex in-game economies that use these goods, too. As a result, these virtual worlds’ makers and operators tend to tightly manage in-game economies by fixing prices and exchange rates, limiting what can be created, sold, or traded; and almost never allow users to “cash out” into real-world currency. For the developers of these worlds to put their businesses at risk, let alone allow third parties to modify their creations, they will need forms of compensation that ensure, not just pitch, considerable income from doing so.

## Interoperability Is a Spectrum

Given the many enormous challenges to interoperability, some doubt that the Metaverse will ever come into existence. Instead, its ideas will be limited to independent virtual worlds and virtual world platforms, such as `Roblox`, `Fortnite`, Nvidia’s `Earth 2.0`, and Google’s geospatial platform. There are both economical and historical reasons for optimism. But first, a little nuance is required.

Interoperability in the Metaverse is not binary. It is not about whether virtual worlds will or won’t share or whether standards exist. It’s about how many worlds share, how much is shared, when, where, and at what cost, with what standards and how many. This dynamic is not unique to the Metaverse.

Think about a piece of apparel in the real world. All belts are presumed to work with all pants. Exceptions exist, of course, but overall, most belts are compatible with most pants, irrespective of the year they were bought, the brand, or the country they’re from. At the same time, not all belts fit all pants equally well. And while there are common standards for pants and belts, a 30 × 30 pant from J.Crew, as an example, may fit differently from a 30 × 30 pant from Old Navy or any other apparel retailer—and dress sizes are known to vary even more widely. These standards vary globally too, hence those shoe labels that list several size categories—typically those of the United States, the UK, and Europe. Indeed, many differing technical standards exist, such as those for residential voltage, and for measurements of speed, distance, or weight. It’s also common for these rival standards to be intermingled. The UK uses miles for distances and pints for milk and beer but the metric system for most other measurements, while Canada uses proportionally more metric but continues to use feet and inches. The result of this measurement mixture is frustration or, more seriously, occasional errors and even losses.

In some instances, new equipment is required for a foreign device to be used in a local environment (as in the case of an electrical outlet adapter). In other instances, a government will require updates or replacements to a given part to ensure that it is locally compliant (e.g., updating a car’s exhaust system to meet tighter emissions regulations).

Even where compatibility is technically simple, friction and prohibitions remain. Pants work everywhere, but not every location will admit someone wearing jeans. Movie theaters allow almost any clothes and accept most forms of credit, but not outside food or drink. One can carry a shotgun in much, but not all, of the American outdoors, but rarely in cities and almost never in a school. Cars work on all roads in the United States, but to drive on a golf course a person needs to rent a golf cart (even if they privately own one, too). Not every business accepts every currency, but currencies can be exchanged for a fee. Many stores support some, but not all, credit cards and a few accept none. Most of the world now embraces trade, but not all of it, nor for all things, in all quantities, or for free. Identity is even more complicated. There are passports, credit scores, school records, legal records, employer IDs, state IDs, and more. Which of them are used for what, which of them are available to outside parties or can be affected by outside parties—it all varies, sometimes based on where a person is at a given time.

Though premised upon interoperability, the internet isn’t exhaustively interoperable, either. There are still public and private networks (and even offline ones) and sundry networks, platforms, and software that admit the majority of common file formats—but not all. While the most popular protocols are free and open, many are paid and private.

No interoperable system or standard is perfect in its design or complete in its adoption. Yet the arc of all global technologies is to interoperation and standardization, both of which typically strengthen or expand network effects and reduce costs. The aforementioned “protocol wars” are a great case study. From the 1970s to the 1990s, few believed that the many competing networking stacks would be replaced by a single suite, least of all one steered by nonprofit and informal working bodies. Instead, those who wanted to trade emails, files, or other data would need to contend with a “cyberspace divided.” Eventually, the utility and economics of a unified global networking suite proved too great.
