
Individual platforms and experiences inside the Metaverse may resemble standardized “hub worlds,” such as Tesla’s San Francisco simulations, Apple’s AR mapping solutions, or a future version of `Grand Theft Auto`. But the overall Metaverse is likely to lack such a uniform expression, directory, or gateway—just like the internet. The World Wide Web, for example, is exhaustively organized and indexed, with sites claiming “namespaces” that are reached at assigned server addresses that are located at assigned network addresses, yet there is no “starting point” for the web other than an empty browser, users do not need a website to surf to one, and there is no required pathway between one page and another. If a user wants to go directly to a given page, they simply type in the address or click a hyperlink that takes them there directly. Smartphones offer a unified view of a user’s apps and a directory of all apps, but there’s rarely any experiential link among them, only minimal standardization across them. And while both `Roblox` and `Fortnite` enable users to jump between worlds through portals and doors, most users access various worlds through 2D interfaces that more closely resemble a streaming video service than the Street.

Not only would a planet-based organizational model for the Metaverse be frustrating, but it’s also probably impossible. Many simulations, such as those of Tesla, Apple, and a city’s own digital twin, will cover the same user and real-world location and often interconnect, but will otherwise run in parallel. Fortunately, this model has many technical benefits as well. By splitting users or data across platforms, rather than building a virtual planet with billions of concurrent users and quadrillions of objects, many of the technical challenges discussed throughout this chapter, such as rendering, synchronization, and persistence, are greatly simplified.

## With Continuity of Data, Such as Identity, History, Entitlements, Objects, Communications, and Payments

Although establishing an interoperable network requires participants to exchange some data (e.g., identifier, address, location, etc.), it does not specify what data is then exchanged, let alone supported across its vast network. In general, visions of the full-spec Metaverse involve interoperable identities, histories (or records), entitlements (i.e., things a user owns or has the right to access), objects (e.g., virtual goods, avatars, or asset models), communications (just as emails work across all networks, domains, devices, etc.), and payment systems (required for virtual “trade”). Without such a suite, it’s argued there would be no “Metaverse,” just an internet with a growing number of 3D applications. A more 3D-capable internet has value, but it’s less likely to produce a true “parallel plane of existence” or produce an economy that might rival that of the “physical world” (while also underpinning much of it).

Interoperability is also essential to user rights and, in turn, the health of the Metaverse overall. If a user’s virtual entitlements or creations are locked to a given platform, then that platform’s de facto control over the user will grow as time passes and the user’s investment increases. For similar reasons, it will become increasingly difficult for any other platform, new or emergent, to compete with that platform. Interoperability is also needed to ensure that investments (time, money, energy, memories) in the Metaverse endure should a single virtual world or server within it be shut down. In the “real world,” consumers do not contend with these sorts of problems. Many underlying interoperable services and registries are government owned and non-excludable (e.g., passports, national identity numbers, currencies), individuals take physical possession of goods (unlike most digital goods) and these goods don’t disappear if their manufacturer closes, interoperability is physics based (a J.Crew belt works with Gap pants irrespective of any coordination between the two companies or permission from one), intermediaries are not required for most exchange (e.g., USD to Euro, a hat for a T-shirt), and so on.

There are many obstacles to establishing these standards. One is the current state of fragmentation. Almost all the most popular virtual worlds today use their own different rendering engines (many publishers operate several across their titles), with individualized scripting languages, tools, frameworks, and related services. The practical consequences of these engine-by-engine variations can be hard for non-developers to appreciate, but the depth of these differences is demonstrated by how the two most popular engines “disagree” on coordinate axes. In Unity, the `y`-axis in an X/Y/Z-based 3D model refers to up/down, while Unreal uses the `z`-axis for up/down and maps the `y`-axis to left/right. It’s easy for middleware to convert such practices, but the technical variations extend into how code is structured, inputs processed, particles simulated, and so on, rather than just the order of information.

Beyond the underlying logic and rules of these worlds is how the information that runs inside them is structured. There are no “JPEGs,” “MP4s,” “.XLS,” or “PDFs” for 3D objects, let alone environments or player data. As such, parties save their objects, textures, and player data into entirely different file formats and with only the information that they expect to need. Solving this requires not just a format but the philosophy around it. Take the idea of an “interoperable avatar.” It’s relatively easy for developers to agree on the definition of an image and how to present it, and as a static 2D unit of content made up of individually colored pixels, the process of converting one image file type (say, PNG) to another (JPEG) is straightforward. However, 3D avatars are a more complex question. Is an avatar a complete 3D person with an outfit, or is it made up of a body avatar plus an outfit? If the latter, how many articles of clothing are they wearing and what defines a shirt versus a jacket that goes over shirts? Which parts of an avatar can be recolored? Which parts must be recolored together (is a sleeve separated from a shirt)? Is an avatar’s head a complete object, or is it a description of dozens of sub-elements like individual eyes (with their own retinas), eyelashes, noses, freckles, and so on? In addition, users expect an anthropomorphic jellyfish avatar and a box-like android to move in different ways. The same applies to objects. If a tattoo is placed on the avatar’s neck, it should stay fixed to their skin irrespective of any movement they make—but a tie hung around that neck should move with (and also be affected by) the avatar. And it should move differently than a seashell necklace, which should also move differently than a feather necklace. Just sharing the dimensions and visual detail of an avatar is not sufficient. Developers need to understand, and agree on, how they work—then build code to support it.
