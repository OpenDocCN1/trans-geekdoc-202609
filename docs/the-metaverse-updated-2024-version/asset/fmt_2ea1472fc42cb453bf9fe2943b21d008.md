
# Consensus-based Actions and NFT Royalties

Consensus-based actions that target or disadvantage individual users or even groups are not uncommon in crypto. In May 2023, a group of `Decentraland` users put forward a motion to mark a given district as “stolen” on the platform’s marketplace, which would prevent its owner from selling the district on `Decentraland` and impair the price they might receive on third-party marketplaces.^([68](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr68)) The petitioners argued that the user who owned “District X,” `RobL#4045`, had contributed less than 0.1% of the district’s total real estate, gained control over the entire district through governance failures, removed the rights of all other contributors to the district after gaining control over it, then refused to provide financial disclosures relating to the district income and instead sought opportunities to sell it for personal gain in the “7 figures.”^([69](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr69)) The motion was denied, even though 37 users voted in favor, only 16 voted no, and 2 abstained. The problem? The nays had an average “voting power” that was 3.1 times higher than the yays.^([70](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr70))

## Blurry Seas

In some cases, dapp owners and users alike will target the rights of a minority group—and in doing so, potentially imperil the overall future of Web3. The best example here might be in NFTs.

Although many consider the royalty model of NFTs to be one of its most important features, these payouts are not programmatic or trustless, unlike the smart contract that operates the NFT and executes the transfer of an NFT. Instead, when an NFT is minted to the blockchain, code is included that specifies what portion (typically 5%–10%) of all subsequent sales of that NFT should be paid out to a wallet other than that of the seller. For years NFT marketplaces ensured that whenever they facilitated a transaction, these royalty payments occurred.

In October 2022, the Solana NFT marketplace Magic Eden announced that it would be shifting to a “royalty-optional” model, with sellers able to choose whether they would like to honor the royalty or instead keep all (or half) of it themselves. Though this policy shift angered NFT creators, few of whom knew that royalties could be ignored and many of whom launched NFTs precisely in order to reap these very payments, Magic Eden was responding to problems in its own market. Six months earlier, new competitors had emerged that used “royalty-optional” sales to differentiate from Magic Eden, which held 90% market share. And compete they did. By early September, Magic Eden’s market share had fallen below 80%, and by the time of its policy change, it was under 60%.^([71](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr71)) Stephen Hess, Magic Eden’s then CEO, defended his move by pointing out that enforcing royalties on his marketplace would have “little effect” if the blockchain ecosystem continued to support marketplaces that did not^([72](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr72)) and reminded critics that three months earlier, his company had touted an in-development NFT standard that would have guaranteed royalty payments, but had found little support.^([73](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr73)) Perhaps, Hess speculated, the future of NFTs would be multi–asset class—spanning untradable soulbound NFTs, royalty-optional NFTs, royalty-mandated NFTs, unchangeable NFTs, and so on.^([74](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr74))

A week after Magic Eden dropped its royalties, the Ethereum-only NFT marketplace Blur launched with the hopes of displacing market leader OpenSea, which supported NFTs across five different blockchains and had maintained 50%–90% market share among Ethereum NFTs throughout the crypto boom and crash.^([75](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr75)) Like Magic Eden, Blur enabled sellers to skip royalties. It also took no sales commission, undercutting OpenSea by 2.5%, and, like Sushiswap, announced it would issue `$BLUR` tokens to users based on their trading volume.

Within a month of Blur’s mid-October debut, OpenSea had lost 15 points of market share to Blur, which had just hit the 20% mark.^([76](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr76)) In response, OpenSea announced it would no longer enforce royalties for all NFTs—only those which were minted (or updated) to use its “Seaport protocol,” which would prevent these NFTs from being sold on marketplaces that did not honor royalties. “Make no mistake,” OpenSea’s CEO, Devin Finzer, wrote in a blog post, “technical decisions like this involve trade-offs: enforcing creator fees on chain requires sacrificing some of the censorship-resistant and permissionless nature of NFTs.” Finzer’s language was similar to that of Hess’s months earlier, who had written, “Royalty-enforcement solutions have trade-offs. Adopting them requires us to rethink what ‘ownership’ means. The concept would have to become more conditional, where creators would retain some rights to alter an NFT’s functions depending on user behavior.… In order for protocol-enabled royalty enforcement to work, a trend toward centralization will also need to occur. One key trade-off is that holders may not be able to freely transfer the NFT, \[especially\] as wallets don’t support these new technologies or standards at the outset.”^([77](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr77))

Days after OpenSea announced its blocking code, Blur announced that it would offer its own blocking tools, while also offering more `$BLUR` tokens to traders who chose to pay full royalties. By the end of the year, Blur provided an update that disappointed NFT creators—their token incentives had underperformed and most sellers were still not supporting full royalties. As a result, the marketplace would mandate royalties, but only at 0.5%—a tenth of what the average NFT specified—though Hess said he would try to slowly increase that rate to 1.0%, 1.5%, 2%, and so on should the market support such increases.^([78](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr78)) Blur continued to enforce full royalties on collections that were updated to include (or for newer releases, were issued with) marketplace-restrictive protocols or express permission lists.

By January 2023, Blur and OpenSea were consistently tied in market share at 40%–50% each. By February 2, Blur discovered a way to integrate OpenSea’s Seaport protocol into its marketplace, thereby allowing NFTs blocked from Blur to be sold there nevertheless (though Blur committed to enforcing full royalties for relevant collections).^([79](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr79)) A week later, Blur completed the first drop of `$BLUR` tokens, netting some users millions of dollars as a result.^([80](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr80)) Blur’s market share spiked to 75% by mid-February—all at OpenSea’s expense.^([81](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr81))

Not long after, Blur alleged that OpenSea “automatically sets royalties to optional when they detect trading on Blur,” which “forced” creators to choose between the two platforms. As such, Blur was updating its policy once again—this time to a four-category system.^([82](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr82)) The first category covered NFTs that were written to block OpenSea. In these cases, Blur would ensure full royalties. The second category covered NFTs that blocked neither OpenSea nor Blur, but did block royalty-optional marketplaces. These, too, would receive full royalties when sold on Blur. But if an NFT used the Seaport protocol to try to block Blur (and other royalty-free marketplaces), Blur would continue to use its workaround to make these NFTs available for sale on its marketplace, and these NFTs would receive only a 0.5% royalty. The last category was NFTs with no blocks whatsoever, which would also be limited to a 0.5% royalty. In response, OpenSea announced it would shift to a minimum of 0.5% royalty on all NFTs that did not use Seaport (or like substitutes), while removing Blur from its Seaport blocklist.^([83](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr83))
