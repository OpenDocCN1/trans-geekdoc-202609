
Start with identity. To use a Web 2.0 application, users must typically sign up for an account operated by that application or otherwise connect an account that’s operated by another Web 2.0 application (such as Facebook or Gmail). In contrast, Web3 users “connect” their crypto wallet, which is operated by the blockchain and controlled exclusively by the user, to a `dapp`. The user’s assets (images and videos), social graph, and data (transaction history) would also be stored “on chain,” with the user in control of when and how this information is exposed to a given `dapp`. Furthermore, much of the user’s interaction with the `dapp` would be stored on chain, too, including their transactions, likes, and so on. As a result, every `dapp` must be relentlessly and endlessly focused on offering the best possible experience, price, product, and array of services—or else an existing competitor or new entrant will come along to take their business. An example helps to bring this theory to life.

Uniswap was one of the first Ethereum `dapps` to gain mass adoption, having launched in 2018 and pioneered the automated market maker model that enabled users to exchange cryptocurrencies without the use of a centralized exchange or bank. Uniswap’s business model involved a 0.05% cut of all transactions while offering those providing liquidity—the supply of tokens available for exchange—a 0.25% spread on the market rate. In 2020, a competitor called Sushiswap emerged that had copied Uniswap’s predominantly open-source and straightforward code. But Sushiswap needed more than functional code to operate—it also needed cryptocurrency owners to make their holdings available for exchange and in such volumes that the exchange rate offered by the marketplace could be competitive with Uniswap. Undercutting Uniswap’s fees to users was impractical—even if Sushiswap waived its 0.05% fee, few users would even notice such savings, and the value of these savings would be dwarfed by the moment-to-moment changes in exchange rates that would be caused by insufficient liquidity on the exchange. Instead, Sushiswap announced a smart contract that would issue “Sushi tokens” to anyone willing to “stake” their cryptocurrencies on the exchange. This provided Sushiswap market makers with two means of generating a profit, an exchange fee per transaction and appreciation in the exchange itself, compared to just the former when using Uniswap. Later, Sushiswap announced an incentive that would specifically reward cryptocurrency owners for shifting their stake tokens from Uniswap to Sushiswap, which naturally increased its supply while also decreasing that available to the incumbent. This targeted offer was impossible for Uniswap to block, as records were publicly available on various blockchains, and easy for Sushiswap to provide, for the same reason. To defend its business, Uniswap announced that it would issue its own token, while also retroactively rewarding prior providers.

User-beneficial “arms races” are typical in `dapps`, and they often place the product on a path to progressive decentralization in which the community owns the product, rather than just its creators, and indeed directs it, too.

## A View to the Future

The vision of Web3 is compelling. It imagines a version of the internet that not only is decentralized in its operations and protocols but is literally run through that same network. This internet also enables users to store their identity, payments, and data on that network, instead of forcing it into databases under the control of massive for-profit corporations. In doing so, optimists believe that the internet can return to the principles of openness upon which it was founded—and forever stay that way. Chris Dixon, a crypto-focused venture capitalist at Andreessen Horowitz, is fond of saying that if the dominant ethos of Web 2.0 was “Don’t be evil,” Google’s de facto motto from the early 2000s until 2015, then the slogan of the Web3 era will be “Can’t be evil,” adding that “the only API you should trust is one controlled by a blockchain.”

Even if the benefits would be widespread, rebuilding the internet and its many applications is not going to be easy. Precedents also suggest that it’s unlikely. Free, open-source, and community-run technologies have been available for decades—and they almost always offer a more fair and prosperous future to developers and users alike. Despite this, these technologies tend to lose to paid, closed, and privately owned alternatives because the companies that operate those alternatives can afford enormous investments in services and tools, engineering talent, customer acquisition, and exclusive content. Such investments lead to an ineluctable feedback loop in which more users leads to more developers and vice versa. Once this feedback loop becomes powerful enough, developers and users are financially, creatively, or technically locked in—if not all three.

Blockchains can’t disassemble the Web 2.0 flywheels, but they do provide a mechanism through which significant and diverse resources—from wealth to infrastructure and time—can be easily aggregated by independent groups on a scale that contends with the mightiest of the private companies. Furthermore, these mechanisms have a baked-in economic model to compensate those who contribute to its success or ongoing operations, rather than relying on their altruism and empathy, as is the case with most open-source projects. And while the precise value of this compensation is not always known and is never guaranteed, it increases with the success of any given project. As such, early supporters can receive rewards commensurate with the risks they undertook, which are often competitive with the high salaries and certainty of traditional tech jobs. By the end of 2023, New Hampshire, Utah, and Wyoming had all legalized DAOs, with some technologists imagining it as the Metaverse-era version of the LLC (limited liability corporation) or 501(c)(3) (nonprofit organization)—a new structure that would forever alter (and ease) capital formation while enabling billions of otherwise disenfranchised individuals to participate in the high-growth tech economy.

Blockchains have also operated at extraordinary scale. In 2021 and 2022, more than $14 trillion in value was processed by the Bitcoin and Ethereum blockchains, several times the sum of Stripe, PayPal, Square, Shopify, and WeChat combined. All of these transactions took place without the need for a central authority, a managing partner, or even a headquarters. Also, the payments were made across dozens of rival wallets (rather than limited to a tightly controlled network, as is the case with peer-to-peer rails such as Venmo or PayPal), could be made at any time (unlike automated clearinghouses and wire transfers), were completed in less than a minute (unlike ACH), and required neither sender nor receiver to establish a bank account, let alone negotiate a long-term agreement with any specific blockchain, blockchain processor, or wallet provider. Many of these payments were programmed to automatically run applications, cast votes, lend money, fund projects, grant users access to a club, or trigger any number of other activities. Or payments were caused by these events. For this reason, the Web3 community argues that supposed “digital payment rails” such as PayPal, Venmo, and WeChat, as well as Apple’s IAP and Apple Pay, are little more than facsimiles of legacy ones. And unlike these rails, blockchains cannot be used to control users, extract added fees, or force the use of other products.
