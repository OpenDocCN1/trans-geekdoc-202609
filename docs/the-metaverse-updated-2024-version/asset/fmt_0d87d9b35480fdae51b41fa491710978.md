
Alongside the rapid deployment of commercial HMDs was an equally sudden, and even larger, surge in consumer demand. In May of 2019, Facebook released the Quest 1, the sixth VR device released by its Oculus division. Over its first 14 months on sale, fewer than 1 million total units were shipped. The Quest 2, which released in October 2020, sold 1 million units in under 100 days and crossed 10 million by its one-year anniversary. Moreover, the Quest 2’s sales figures were comparable to those of Microsoft’s Xbox S|X console, which had sold roughly 12 million units since its debut in November 2020, and not far off from the 16 million units sold by PlayStation 5, which also came to market in November 2020. The Quest 2’s growth over the Quest 1 also recalled the second iPhone, which outsold the first iPhone three to one and ushered in the smartphone era. In 2022, Mark Zuckerberg revealed that it was the success of the Quest 2 that inspired him to publicly unveil Facebook’s Metaverse ambitions and rename the company.^([56](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr56)) By the end of 2022, annual spend on VR applications was estimated at more than a billion dollars per year, three-quarters of which was billed through Facebook’s Quest Store.^([57](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr57))

For many, the clearest signal that the age of mixed-reality headsets was imminent came on June 6, 2023. At its Worldwide Developers Conference, Apple unveiled its long-awaited Vision Pro headset at its Cupertino, California, headquarters, with an average of 725,000 people watching the more than two-hour-long video.^([58](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr58)) The Vision Pro represented Apple’s most significant product launch since 2007’s iPhone, with tens of billions in R&D invested and more than 5,000 patents behind what the company called “the most advanced personal electronics device ever” and the start of a “new era of computing … spatial computing.”^([59](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr59)) Among the many use cases demonstrated were virtual movie theaters, mixed-reality school lessons, and immersive 3D “memories.”

Given the attention paid to the new 3D-focused devices, it has been easy to overlook the investments that added spatial functionality to traditional 2D form factors. In 2016, Apple released its first iPhone model with two rear cameras, enabling apps to focus on two fields of depth at the same time. Starting in 2017, the standard-issue iPhone came equipped with two rear cameras, with that year’s model also adding infrared (IR) sensors to the front-facing camera that could track and recognize 30,000 points on a user’s face in real time. To support this new hardware, Apple introduced `ARKit`, a software frame that helped developers build AR apps using the iPhone’s many cameras, computer and graphics processors, and motion sensors. The results were immediate.

In 2015, Snap launched AR filters, which used a phone’s camera and processor to understand a face and contextually layer on top a 3D effect, such as sunglasses, a beard, or blue eyes, all in real time. At the time, all filters were produced by Snapchat. Two months after the 2017 launch of Apple’s `ARKit` and IR-enabled iPhone X, Snap launched Lens Studio, which enabled third-party developers and eventually users to create their own lenses. Two years later, the company reported that users had created more than 600,000 AR lenses, with 142 million using at least one AR lens daily;^([60](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr60)) by April 2021, Snap said it had 200 million daily users of its AR lenses, with over a million AR lenses having been created.^([61](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr61))

Apple’s AR investments, which were rapidly replicated by Google’s Android operating system and ecosystem of device manufacturers, also enabled *`Pokémon Go`* to embrace “true AR.” Upon its release in July 2017, *`Pokémon Go`*’s “AR” was really just a 3D character or object layered overtop whatever the user’s camera was pointed to and without reference to what it saw, or its implications. A Pikachu might be seen on top of a user’s back lawn, but if the user tilted their camera to the sky or underground, Pikachu would still be there and not seem to know that fact, either. But starting three months after Apple’s `ARKit` and iPhone X, Niantic began a series of contextual AR upgrades. Now, Pikachu would be layered correctly on top of the user’s backyard, and if the user looked elsewhere, Pikachu would stay where it was. By 2020, Niantic had begun to roll out “occlusion,” whereby Pokémon might even hide behind a tree in the backyard, and a Pokéball thrown at Pikachu would appear to hit the tree and bounce away, rather than pass through it to the popular character.

In 2019, Apple added a third rear camera to its iPhone Pro line and also began to include ultra-wideband (UWB) transmitters and receivers into its full iPhone line, which enabled the devices to “see” through sound—just as a bat might. Specifically, Apple’s UWB transmitters were capable of emitting and processing up to a billion radar pulses per second. In doing so, these devices could generate spatial maps of a user’s home or workplace, precisely place itself within any preexisting map (whether generated by the user or available through a mapping app, such as those of Google or Apple), identify the location of other UWB devices within a few centimeters, and enhance the accuracy and safety of proximity-based smart home functionality (e.g., automatically unlock a home door when the user was approaching from outside but not when they were walking by inside the home).

Beginning with 2020’s iPhone 12 Pro, high-end iOS devices were also equipped with lidar scanners that enabled users to create high-fidelity 3D models by photographing real-world objects (a process called photogrammetry). The iPhone’s lidar-based photogrammetry capture is so powerful that many design and engineering professionals now use the device instead of $20,000–$30,000 dedicated lidar cameras. By the end of 2022, tens of thousands of private owners had used the device to produce high-resolution, immersive models of their homes, with the models then uploaded to real estate sites to better attract home buyers, or to furniture websites to help with interior decorating.
