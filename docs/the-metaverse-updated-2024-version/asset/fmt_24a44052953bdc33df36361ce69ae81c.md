
# Nouns DAO

Nouns DAO is another form of membership club, but instead of focusing on cultural events, this DAO manages the intellectual property rights of its eponymous NFTs. Each day, one new Noun—a cute pixelized avatar—is auctioned, with 100% of the net proceeds going into the Nouns DAO treasury, which exists exclusively to enhance the value of Nouns NFTs. The treasury does this by funding proposals authored by and voted on by the owners of the NFTs. In effect, it is a constantly growing investment fund governed by a constantly growing board of governors.

# CityDAO

A final example, which unites many of these themes, is CityDAO, which was formed in 2021 by several thousand outdoors enthusiasts (including Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin and entrepreneur Mark Cuban) to purchase a 40-acre plot of land near Yellowstone National Park in Wyoming, which had passed legislation recognizing DAOs as legal entities earlier in the year.^([16](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr16)) CityDAO’s purchase was successful, with the collective’s de facto figurehead telling the `Financial Times` that he hoped Wyoming’s embrace of the DAO structure and CityDAO’s experiment would “become this fundamental link between digital assets, crypto and the physical world.”^([17](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr17)) Two years later, CityDAO has largely lived up to this ambition.

Since its formation, all of CityDAO’s major decisions have been made through vote, including motions to programmatically collect dues from all members to pay land taxes and legal fees, acquire and assign land rights, and even dilute some members’ voting powers. In May 2022 CityDAO held a vote intended to start the transition from a governance model that gave individuals votes for every token they held to a more democratic “one person, one vote” model. The initial proposal, which passed with 88% support under the then-current “one token, one vote” model, involved the issuance of “voting credits” equal to the rounded-down square root of the number of tokens held. Users with 4 to 8 tokens would get two votes; those with 9–15 tokens would get three votes, and so on.^([18](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr18)) Two months later, the DAO approved a $10,000 purchase of a new parcel of land in Colorado which would then be split into smaller parcels with NFTs managing the leases. The goal of this latter purchase, which was completed in early 2023, was to serve as a template for the development of a crypto-native city that would be based on the purchase of a distressed American town, college campus, or remote retreat.^([19](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr19)) Around the same time, the DAO began accepting development plans for its first NFT leases in Colorado, which would be given free to the winning proposal.^([20](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr20)) In August, CityDAO began discussing another proposal to spend up to $1.5 million to purchase a property in Denver, Colorado, that would serve as the group’s “city hall.”^([21](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr21))

# A Tale of Android Alliances and Crypto Foundations

Even during times in which enthusiasm for crypto is in short supply and investment dollars even more scarce, blockchains regularly prove capable of new use cases and achieve new cultural, creative, and commercial highs. But to its advocates, blockchain’s core advantage is not in the new structures it enables but in what it prevents and preserves.

Forty years into the digital era, there is an expanding consensus that the natural arc of a platform is to extract ever more value from its users and developers, while also locking these groups in so that they can’t flee to a competitor or launch a plausible alternative.^([22](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr22)) The most significant case study here is not Apple’s iOS, which was never billed as “open,” let alone developer focused, but Google’s Android. Although the Android OS is based on code bought by Google in 2005 for “at least $50 million,” the project is technically open-source and is managed by the nonprofit Open Handset Alliance, a consortium of nearly a hundred device makers, network operators, and chip companies.^([23](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr23)) Any smartphone manufacturer, whether it is a member of OHA or not, is free to use Android in whichever (legal) way they like. In fact, an Android original equipment manufacturer (OEM) could fork Android to make it their own and then prevent others from using their variant. Or they could choose to use Android but take none of Google’s apps or services and instead exclusively integrate their own competing software suite.^([24](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr24)) In practice, though, nearly every Android OEM outside of China, with the exception of Amazon, which forked Android to create its Fire OS, uses Google’s end-to-end version of Android. This means that Google Search is the default search engine; the user’s smartphone account must be a Gmail account; Google’s suite of apps must be preinstalled and natively integrated; and the device must observe Google’s policies on data and security and the company’s various UI/X specifications too.

Android OEMs support Google’s version of Android on an end-to-end basis, because they can’t really do so on a piecemeal basis.^([25](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr25)) If an OEM wants their device to include the Google Play app store or support Google Maps or Google’s YouTube app, they must first support Google Play Services. To receive Google Play Services, the OEM must make Google Search the default search engine and preinstall a laundry list of Google apps and services (which the user is naturally blocked from deleting) and conform to a variety of policy requirements relating to how the Android OS can (and cannot) be visually customized, which end-user data is captured and how it is used, and which services and apps the OEM can themselves preload on their device or offer to the user who buys it. OEMs must also sign agreements not to fork Android or sell devices with a forked version of Android.^([26](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr26)) In exchange for these commitments, Google shares some of its Android revenue with OEMs—they receive a portion of search-related revenues from their respective devices and also a cut of app store profits.^([27](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr27))

Over time, Google has moved many of Android’s core functions, such as background services and libraries, into Google Play Services and also expanded Google Play Services to include health and fitness, payment processing, native advertising, and the like.^([28](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr28)) Many analysts considered Google’s de facto and creeping closure of Android to be a response to Samsung’s increasing success with the operating system. In 2012, the South Korean giant sold more than 40% of Android-powered smartphones (and the majority of high-end ones)—more than seven times as many as the second-largest Android OEMs, ZTE and Huawei.^([29](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr29)) In addition, Samsung had become increasingly aggressive with its alterations to the “stock” version of Android, producing and marketing its own interface (TouchWiz), while also preloading its devices with its own suite of apps, many of which competed with those offered by Google. Samsung even added its own mobile app store. Samsung’s success as an Android manufacturer is inarguably connected to these investments, but its approach was not dissimilar to “forking” in that it threatened to disintermediate Google from its developers and users. And not only did Google clamp down on its OEM policies in response, but it acquired one of Samsung’s biggest competitors, Motorola, which subsequently released co-branded Google-Motorola Android devices that received earlier access to the newest versions of Android than truly independent Android OEMs like Samsung, LG, or HTC, among others.
