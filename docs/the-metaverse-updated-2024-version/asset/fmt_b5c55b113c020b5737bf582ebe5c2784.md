
Shifting computing resources into the cloud also creates many new costs. One area of importance is temperature. Side-by-side, always-turned-on devices running at data centers create considerable heat—far more than the aggregate heat of those servers sitting in a living room credenza. Servicing, securing, and managing this equipment is costly. The shift from streaming limited bits of data to high-resolution, high-frame-rate content means substantially higher bandwidth costs, too. Yes, Netflix and others make the costs work, but they’re typically sending fewer than 30 frames of video per second (not 60 to 120) with a lower resolution (e.g., 1K or 2K, not 4K or 8K, which Google originally promised for Google Stadia), on a non-real-time basis, and from nearby servers that are storing files rather than performing intensive computing operations.

For the foreseeable future, what might be designated “Sweeney’s Law”—improvements in local compute will continue to outpace improvements in network bandwidth, latency, and reliability—seems likely to hold. Although many believe that Moore’s Law, which was coined in 1965 and states that the number of transistors in a dense integrated circuit doubles about every two years, is now slowing down, CPU and GPU processing power continues to grow at a rapid pace. Difficult though it is to believe, the iPhone 15 Pro has 19 billion transistors—19 times more than the entirety of Pixar’s 1995 industrial render farm.^([14](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr14)) These advances are also rapidly adopted by consumers, too, with the average smartphone worldwide replaced every 24–40 months.^([15](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr15))

## Outside the Law

Sweeney’s Law does not mean that all Metaverse-related compute will occur locally and/or on the relevant user’s device. As discussed in [Chapter 5](#chapter5.xhtml), industrial simulations tend to require more remote processing, and are more predictable in demand, less price sensitive than leisure-based simulations such as those used for video games or to create movies, and more tolerant of latency. As consumer usage of the Metaverse grows, especially in major cities and for non-leisure use cases such as AR shopping or travel, consumer demand for remote processing and rendering will vary less throughout the day, which improves utilization rates. Many generative AI models require too much hard drive space to be stored on a local device and cannot be processed on said device, either. In many cases, their outputs—a 3D model, or chatbot response—also do not require ultra-low latency.

The insatiable need for more processing power—ideally, located as close as possible to the user but, at the very least, in nearby industrial server farms—invariably brings to mind a third option: decentralized computing. With so many powerful and often inactive devices in the homes and hands of consumers, near other homes and hands, it feels inevitable that systems will emerge that share idle processing power.

Culturally, at least, the idea of collectively shared but privately owned infrastructure is already well understood. Anyone who installs solar panels at their home can sell excess power to their local grid (and, indirectly, to their neighbor). Elon Musk touts a future in which a Tesla earns its owner’s rent as a self-driving car when it’s sitting unused in their garage, expensive parking lot, or city street.

As early as the 1990s, programs emerged for distributed computing using everyday consumer hardware. One of the most famous examples is the University of California, Berkeley’s SETI@home, wherein consumers would volunteer use of their home computers to power the search for alien life. Sweeney has highlighted that one of the items on his “to-do list” for the first-person shooter *Unreal*, which shipped in 1998, was “to enable game servers to talk to each other so we can just have an unbounded number of players in a single game session.” Nearly 20 years later, Sweeney admitted that goal “seems to still be on our wish list.”^([16](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr16))

Although the technology to split GPUs and share non-data-center CPUs is still nascent, some believe that blockchains provide both the technological mechanism for decentralized computing and its economic model. The idea is that owners of underutilized CPUs and GPUs would be “paid” in some cryptocurrency for the use of their processing capabilities. There might even be a live auction for access to these resources, either those with “jobs” bidding for access or those with capacity bidding on jobs.^([‡](#chapter6.xhtml#footnote-029))

Could such a marketplace provide some of the massive amounts of processing capacity that will be required by the Metaverse? Imagine, as users navigate immersive spaces, their account is continuously bidding out the necessary computing tasks to mobile devices held but unused by people nearby, perhaps even those they are walking by, all to animate the synthetic experiences that surround them. And later, when this user is no longer using their device, they would be earning tokens as they return the favor (more on this in [Chapter 11](#chapter11.xhtml)). Proponents of this crypto-exchange concept see it as an inevitable feature of all future microchips. Every computer, no matter how small, would be designed to be auctioning off any and all spare cycles, 24 hours per day. Billions of dynamically arrayed processors will power the deep compute cycles of even the largest industrial customers and provide the ultimate and infinite computing mesh that enables the Metaverse. Perhaps the only way for everyone to hear a tree fall is for everyone to water it.

Whatever the prospects for decentralized computing, there are still those who believe that the full vision of the Metaverse, imagined by authors such as Stephenson and Gibson and tech pioneers such as Carmack and Sweeney, is simply impossible until quantum computing is possible, reliable, and scalable. In contrast to typical computing, which processes information using conventional bits of data (i.e., 0s and 1s) according to rules broadly understood in the 1830s and codified by Alan Turing in the 1930s,^([17](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr17)) quantum computing uses quantum bits and the more nascent field of quantum mechanics. “Qubits” can exist in multiple states simultaneously (superpositioned”), and experience quantum “entanglement,” in which the state of one qubit is dependent on another, even if they are a great distance apart.
