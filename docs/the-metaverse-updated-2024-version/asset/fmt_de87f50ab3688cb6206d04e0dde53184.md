
It’s likely that consumer spending today is constrained by the profound truth that no game lasts forever. Think of the many objects bought during a vacation that aren’t expected to make the trip home—a boogie board, a water bottle, a costume for Día de los Muertos—and how that fact affects the price that might be paid for these goods. Expected obsolescence always limits spending.

Enabling interoperable virtual goods is technically distinct from providing users with true “ownership” over these items, including the right to freely buy, sell, or trade them, but these functionalities largely follow from the enabling of interoperation; the ability for a user to move (or access) goods across independent account systems and platforms makes it impossible to prevent gray market sales. Fortunately, the right of resale (and profit) should increase the commercial value of virtual goods, as should clearer ownership rights. In early 2021, the *South China Morning Post* reported that Tencent “sued a game item trading platform to determine who owns the in-game currency and items.” Specifically, the company argued that these assets had “no material value in real life” and that in-game coins bought with real money were “effectively service charges.”^([4](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr4)) The result was outrage, with many gamers feeling mistreated and/or demeaned and understandably discouraged from making further investments.

One point of potential progress comes via Facebook, which is adding “interoperable avatars” to its Facebook Connect set of authentication APIs. Facebook Connect is better known to the public as “Log in with Facebook,” which allows Facebook users to substitute their Facebook log-in for a website or app’s own account system. Most developers would prefer that users create a bespoke account, as it provides the developer with greater information about the user and means that the developer controls this information and account (and not Facebook). However, Facebook Connect is far simpler and faster, and therefore it is the preferred solution for most users. As a result, developers benefit from an increase in the number of registered users—which beats having mostly logged-out anonymous ones. A comparable value proposition will exist for Facebook’s avatar suite, and the same is true of Snap’s Bitmoji and Apple’s Personas. If customized avatars are essential to user expression in 3D space, then few users will want to create a new, detailed avatar for every virtual world they use. It’s also unlikely that every platform—let alone the average platform—will boast avatar tools, or outfit choices, as capable as those of the big-tech giants. If so, the services that embrace cross-platform avatars will likely benefit, while also driving the standardization process.

Epic Games, meanwhile, is working to pioneer data standards that allow a single in-game “asset” (really, a right to data) to be repurposed in multiple environments. After acquiring Psyonix in May 2019, Epic Games announced that the publisher’s hit game *Rocket League* would go free-to-play in September 2020 and move over to the company’s online services suite, known as EOS, which would also allow the title to access *Fortnite*’s social graph.

To commemorate the free-to-play launch, Epic also unveiled the first of several two-week “Llama-Rama” crossover events in which *Fortnite* players could complete challenges in *Rocket League* that would unlock exclusive outfits and achievements that could be worn in and across either game. A second and third Llama-Rama began in March and December of 2021 and although there were no follow-ups in 2022 or 2023, Epic was at work expanding upon the model.

In March 2021, Epic bought Tonic Games Group, maker of *Fall Guys* and dozens of other games, as part of its investments “in building the Metaverse.”^([5](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr5)) Eight months later, Epic acquired Harmonix, the music-focused video game developer that owned *Rock Band*, with Epic’s news release declaring, “As we work to build the metaverse, \[Harmonix’s\] expertise is needed to reimagine how music is experienced, created and distributed.”^([6](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr6)) And then in April 2022, Epic announced that KIRKBI, the family-owned holding and investment company behind The LEGO Group, had invested $1 billion in Epic as part of a shared effort to “shape the future of the Metaverse” and “create new social entertainment exploring the connection between digital and physical worlds.”^([7](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr7))

Epic’s many behind-the-scenes efforts began to publicly converge in 2023. In March, the company unveiled an update to *Fortnite Creative* entitled Unreal Editor for Fortnite (or UEFN), which enabled developers to build *FNC* using the PC-based Unreal Editor typically used by Unreal developers, rather than through Fortnite’s more simplified (and child-focused) in-game editor. Using UEFN, developers could create fully custom sounds and textures, as well as inject custom code into their worlds to govern non-playable character AI, alter physics, and use a visual style that had little resemblance to *Fortnite: Battle Royale*. Epic also announced that independent developers would receive up to 40% of total *Fortnite* revenue based on an (unshared) equation spanning the total time spent playing *FNC* worlds, the purchasing behavior of its players, and the degree to which those worlds attracted net new players to the platform, among other measures.^([8](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr8)) Despite the increase in variety, users would still be able to wear their typical *Fortnite* avatars across all UEFN worlds (unless the developer behind this world prohibited it). To support *Fortnite*’s expansion toward the type of worlds and players typically found on *Roblox*, Epic also began to break its avatars down into more separable elements. For example, an avatar that previously included a holstered gun and belt of ammo would appear inside a PG world without its weapon and munitions, whereas some outfits were blocked altogether, thereby establishing a de facto system and taxonomy for avatar ratings and appropriateness.

On November 3, 2023, Epic launched *Fortnite OG*, a month-long event in which *Fortnite*’s original 2017 maps were brought back, prompting tens of millions of lapsed players to return too. On its second day, *Fortnite OG* hit a new record for Epic, with more than 44.7 million players logging on (including an estimated 33%–38% of all active PlayStation and Xbox players that day).^([9](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr9)) To commemorate the end of the mini-season, Epic held a live-in-game event featuring rapper Eminem that transported players across several different, never-before-seen *Fortnite* realms—all of which were about to debut. On December 7, Epic launched a new free-to-play game mode inside *Fortnite*, *LEGO Fortnite*, a sort of mash-up of *Fortnite*’s gameplay mechanics with the aesthetics and construction focus of *Minecraft* and real-world LEGO bricks. As with UEFN, players could continue to wear their *Fortnite* avatars in *LEGO Fortnite*—but now they would do so using a LEGO-styled version of that avatar. On December 8, another free mode was added, *Rocket Racing*, a spin-off of Psyonix’s *Rocket League* but focused on the kart-racing genre popularized by *Mario Kart*, alongside the ability for players to buy cars, decals, and wheels that could be used inside *Fortnite: Battle Royale*, *Rocket League*, *Rocket Racing*, and eligible UEFN titles. On December 9, Epic premiered its final free new mode, *Fortnite Festival*, which reimagined Harmonix’s classic musical rhythm game *Rock Band*. Epic also began selling virtual instruments, as well as popular songs that users could play inside *Rocket League* and *Rocket Racing* cars or while driving a car or dancing in *Fortnite*.
