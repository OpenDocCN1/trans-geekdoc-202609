
Other examples concern consumer-worn hardware such as lightweight optical AR glasses (which will become increasingly important to navigating a world run through AI-powered simulation). These devices will never be able to store enough referential information to recognize the entire world—or even a user’s typical environment—nor the attendant details of how to interact with its digital and AR inputs and controls. Instead, neural networks will be used to process the world in real time, with user “brain prints” helping the device predict what is and isn’t of use and prioritize computational resources to suit those conclusions. In the case of passthrough AR and MR, real-time artificial intelligence is already required to assemble and correct live video footage captured from various external cameras, each one facing a slightly different direction and from a different position, and to support foveated and varifocal rendering.

Another example harkens back to [Chapter 6](#chapter6.xhtml), which examined how game engines use “level-of-detail” and “occlusion” systems to constantly load and unload visual detail and even objects in order to minimize computational needs. Historically, these solutions have been purely rules-based—a tree’s bark texture would be loaded only if the player was within a given distance from the tree, and objects located behind an obstacle would be loaded only once the player changed position and could see them. The advent of hybrid cloud-local data storage has required applications like `Microsoft Flight Simulator` to predict where its users might go and preemptively download data to ensure its availability when needed. This model is similar to that used by many smartphone applications and digital assistants, which might pre-download a recommended video or preload the webpage leading to the second entry in a ten-item search result based on the expectation the user will select it, saving that user seconds in wait time. Some believe that device-side AI can also be used to improve frame rates and resolution fidelity without needing to transmit much (or any) incremental data to that device or construct it with far more powerful CPUs/GPUs. Imagine, as an example, playing a game of `Call of Duty Mobile` on a mid-range iPad that could render no more than 1K at 60 fps and whose asset information (e.g., a car, shrub, mountain) contained only mid-range-fidelity detail. As the user plays the title, computer vision might be scanning the entire rendered frame, determining what each individual item (the car, shrub, mountain) is likely to be, then generatively “upscaling” the detail. And after every rendered frame, the device’s processor(s) are generating (not rendering) another frame that is shown to the user before the next rendered frame and which sits in between the two renders, based on what is likely to happen in the interim. The result is a doubled frame rate, but effectively no increase in transmitted data or GPU rendering, and instead only a minor tax on largely unused CPU resources.

Finally, there are netcode algorithms, which were discussed in [Chapter 5](#chapter5.xhtml). Netcode is used to sustain online multiplayer gaming experiences in the event of spiking network latency or packet loss by predicting what would have happened and/or coherently resolving conflicting information. Unfortunately, modern netcode doesn’t scale well as the number of players, complexity of their inputs, or variability in latency increases—predicting the next second of a two-player 2D fighting game is much simpler than in fully immersive social venues, but some expect that transformer models or other solutions will improve these predictions and, in doing so, bring more users into the Metaverse.

## #5: Populating the Metaverse with AI Agents

A related area of focus is “non-playable characters” (NPCs), with developers using transformer models to imbue these agents with rich and dynamic personalities, skills, and desires. In April 2023, researchers at Google and Stanford published a paper summarizing a two-day simulation designed to test the believability of virtual agents powered by large language models. Specifically, the researchers assembled a town equipped with houses and dormitories, colleges and offices, grocery stores and bars, and populated with 25 autonomous agents powered by large language models modified “to store a complete record of the agent’s experiences using natural language, synthesize those memories over time into higher-level reflections, and retrieve them dynamically to plan behavior.”^([41](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr41)) During the simulation, characters organically formed routines, while also establishing friendships, sharing information learned, and developing professional interests. After researchers prompted one NPC to host a Valentine’s Day party, the NPC opted to invite her friends to the party, roping in customers at the café where she worked, too, and asked her closest friend to help them with decorating. The friend did so and revealed that she had a crush on another NPC, whom the NPC then also invited to the party. At the party’s scheduled 5 p.m. start, all five NPCs arrived on time, having rearranged their days to accommodate the event—including the crush, “Klaus.”

After the simulation concluded, each behavior of each agent was reviewed and evaluated for believability using common behavioral models (e.g., `TrueSkill`) and variance tests (`Kruskal-Wallis`). Each agent was also “interviewed” for “self-knowledge” (details on their life), “memory” (such as whom they met and what happened during the simulation), “plans” (what they will do and when), “reactions” (what they would do in case of a fire or upon seeing a friend on the street), and “reflections” (what inspires them or what they would buy for another agent’s birthday). Independent human “crowdworkers” were also hired and asked to watch each agent’s two-day experience and then role-play as these agents while answering the same questions. The Stanford/Google researchers found that the LLM agents consistently outperformed their human role-players and exceeded the believability benchmark.
