
The Metaverse is frequently conflated with “blockchain” and its related use cases, such as cryptocurrencies and NFTs, and with its overarching ethos, known as Web3. Yet the two concepts are, in a technical sense, distinct. In fact, there’s little consensus regarding whether they overlap at all—or should. Some executives, entrepreneurs, analysts, and investors consider blockchain an integral component to building the Metaverse. Others see blockchain as an effective (indeed, potentially ideal) solution without being in any sense strictly necessary. A third group views blockchains as a poor solution that may nevertheless become a working standard. Another group believes blockchains have no relevance to the Metaverse at all (and usually no future whatsoever). While more individuals have moved toward the fourth category than the first since the end of the 2020–22 crypto boom, the opposite was true during the 2010s, and it’s always possible that opinions may flip again as blockchain technology evolves. To examine these various and sometimes oscillating perspectives, one must first consider a technology used every day but rarely if ever consciously thought about: the database.

### Who Watches the Databases?

The modern world runs on billions of databases. These databases track everything from who owns which homes, to the mortgages on these homes, their addresses and tax liabilities, the utilities they consume, and which resources should be dispatched in the event of an emergency. The individuals who live in these homes have their medical histories stored in multiple databases managed by their family doctor, insurance provider, and even the ER they visited years earlier. And when they make a deposit in a bank, their money isn’t placed in a lockbox or tagged in a common vault; it is instead recorded in a database. A database also manages when these individuals are paid by their employer, in what sums, and through which payment rails. At least one database stores every email, photo, and document a user creates or receives.

Most databases are centralized—that is, they are managed by a single, definitive authority. In the United States, the largest retail bank is Chase, which manages over $2.5 trillion in assets, all of which is stored on a series of databases that track not just the current balances of its customer deposits but all of the records of how that balance accumulated. To be sure, Chase has many backups of this record (as users might too), and it really operates a network of different databases, but what matters here is that these digital records are managed and owned by a single party: Chase.

Blockchains are decentralized databases with no owner or operator. Instead, their information is recorded, maintained, and updated by a network of unknown computer “validators” that rarely know (or care) what information and transactions they are validating. Furthermore, the process of “validation” does not directly involve the relevant transaction—such as confirming a $5 coffee purchase at Starbucks. Instead, validators (i.e., computers) spend their time solving solutions to complex cryptographic problems that are automatically generated by the transaction. And while the answer to the problem is established through consensus across this network, each validator is in competition with every other to solve it first and, in doing so, to collect the largest share of the associated transaction fee (known as “gas”), which is typically paid in a blockchain’s native cryptocurrency.

The first blockchain, the Bitcoin blockchain, debuted in January 2009. The goal of this blockchain was expansive: a digital currency that could operate a global payment network capable of processing transactions in near real time 24 hours per day and 365 days a year; was accessible to all and excludable to none; could be used for any possible transaction; relentlessly minimized transaction costs; could resist tampering, corruption, and seizure; would not be affected by central banks that might pay off their debts by printing more money; and had no central authority, lead, or managing organization. For this reason, the sole focus of the Bitcoin blockchain was to operate a database that tracked ownership of a proprietary cryptocurrency, the lowercase `b` bitcoin, which also paid its validators in that same cryptocurrency, which appreciated in value as its usage grew.

Bitcoin’s creator, who is known only by his (presumed) pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto, designed its blockchain to have several interrelated attributes. First, Bitcoin would be a “permissionless” blockchain—anyone could become a network validator or bitcoin owner without needing to be invited or approved, and additionally, no one could be stopped from doing so. Second, Bitcoin’s code would be all but immutable—neither the blockchain itself nor any records/transactions on it could ever be changed unless roughly half of network validators chose to do so. Third, this underlying code would be fully transparent, which also meant that Bitcoin validators and bitcoin owners alike would know the maximum possible supply of bitcoin and when more would be released.

These first three attributes enabled the next two attributes, which in turn strengthened the first three. As more individuals and businesses purchased and used Bitcoin, it became increasingly decentralized. This theoretically stabilized its price swings and increased its utility. Moreover, the volume of bitcoin transactions grew, which increased the value of validating transactions and attracted more validators to the network. This not only drove transaction fees down through validator competition, but it further decentralized the blockchain, thereby ensuring its incorruptibility. Consider: If there were only seven validators, then four of them might collude to falsely “validate” transactions that never took place and, in doing so, rob the users. Alternatively, these four validators might “validate” only transactions that took place at their stores, effectively forcing users to hand over their bitcoin.

By 2023, the number of validators supporting Bitcoin on a daily basis exceeded more than a million, making it functionally impossible for its transactions and codes to be hijacked, and achieving Nakamoto’s final aspiration: trustlessness. With all of Bitcoin’s code transparently available, immutable to change, permissionlessly accessed, and operated by a widely decentralized network, no users, business, or validator need ever wonder whether a fee would be paid, a policy changed, or their business individually halted.

### A General-Purpose Blockchain

Not long after Bitcoin emerged, two early users, Vitalik Buterin and Gavin Wood, began developing a new blockchain, Ethereum, which they described as a “decentralised mining network and software development platform rolled into one.” It launched in 2015. Like Bitcoin, Ethereum pays those operating its network through its own cryptocurrency, known as Ether. Buterin and Wood additionally established a programming language (`Solidity`) that enables any developers to permissionlessly and trustlessly program on top of Ethereum. Most of these products are built around “smart contracts,” which are collections of code and data that reside at a specific address on the Ethereum blockchain.
