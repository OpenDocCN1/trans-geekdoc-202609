
# At the Foundation of Metaverse Misunderstandings

At the foundation of Metaverse misunderstandings is a simple fact: the term’s creator never defined it. The first appearance of the “Metaverse” was not in a computer science magazine, a technical schematic, or even a business plan, but instead in the 1992 science fiction novel *Snow Crash*, written by Neal Stephenson. And while much of *Snow Crash* takes place in or concerns the “Metaverse,” it is never concretely explained, only described—and from the perspective of the novel’s protagonist, a part-time hacker and part-time pizza deliverer for the Mafia.

What the satirically named Hiro Protagonist describes is a persistent and three-dimensional virtual world that reaches, interacts with, and affects nearly every part of human existence and much of the physical world. It is a place for labor and leisure, for self-actualization and physical exhaustion, for art alongside commerce. Users interact with the Metaverse primarily through virtual reality and augmented goggles, in addition to two-dimensional black-and-white displays (i.e., traditional computer monitors), experience it through a first-person perspective, and are represented by avatars that need not resemble their real selves at all. In totality, the Metaverse in Stephenson’s novel is more than 2.5 times the size of the earth and with no predefined bodies of oceans or lakes, thereby resulting in a planet with more than 8.5 times the available real estate. Stretched across that entire planet is “the Street,” a hundred-meter-wide road Stephenson refers to as “the Broadway, the Champs Élysées of the Metaverse.”^([1](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr1)) At any given time, there are roughly 15 million human-controlled avatars on the Street, with “a hundred million or so of the richest, hippest, best-connected people on earth” visiting “every day of their lives.”^([2](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr2)) As a point of contrast, there were fewer than seven million total users of the internet in the real world the year Stephenson’s novel was published and fewer than one million when he began writing it. It would take until 1998, six years after *Snow Crash* published, for the internet to reach a hundred million active users total and until the next decade for there to be a hundred million daily internet users.

## Stephenson’s Dystopic Vision

While Stephenson’s vision was vivid and, to many, inspiring, it was also dystopic. *Snow Crash* is set at some point in the early 21st century, years after a global economic collapse. Most layers of government have been replaced by for-profit “Franchise-Organized Quasi-National Entities” and “burbclaves,” a contraction of the term “suburban enclaves.” Each burbclave operates as a “city-state with its own constitution, a border, laws, cops, everything”^([3](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr3)) and some even provide “citizenship” based purely on race.

Stephenson never suggests that the emergence of the Metaverse prompted the aforementioned collapse, but he nonetheless suggests that the Metaverse worsened the world around it. Yes, Stephenson’s Metaverse offers opportunity and escape to millions—it is a place where a gig worker in the “real world” can be a talented swordsman with inside access to the hottest clubs—but readers are left with the unmistakable sense that it leads to further disengagement from the problems of the real world. Moreover, the technical demands of the Metaverse are so great that, at most, 16% of people can afford a computing device powerful enough to access it, and more likely, fewer than 1 in 20 can do so. Nearly all of the 100 million daily users of the Metaverse, in other words, are members of the economic or social elite.^([4](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr4)) Some Metaverse users choose to spend nearly all their waking life inside the Metaverse, entombing themselves in portable terminals, goggles, and other accessories that enable them to stay connected at all times. Stephenson calls these individuals “gargoyles” (none of whom are individually named) and writes that “nothing looks stupider,” they are “rude by definition,” “embarrassing,” and “no fun to talk to” as they just “pretend to make conversation.”^([5](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr5)) And what of the billions of people who don’t go to the Metaverse? “Most of them are making mud bricks or field-stripping their AK-47s.”

The Metaverse itself is administered by the inscrutable (and ultrarich) “Global Multimedia Protocols Group,” which owns all of the underlying virtual land of the Metaverse, as well as the machinery that enables it to exist. Access to the Metaverse, as with most global communications technologies, is controlled by the shadowy L. Bob Rife, whom many readers consider to be a riff on Scientology founder L. Ron Hubbard.

## Stephenson’s Influence on Technology and Industry

Though Stephenson never aspired to shape global policy or technology, his influence, like that of Vannevar Bush, only grows with time—even if he is and will likely remain mostly unknown to the public.^([\*](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-059)) Conversations with Stephenson helped inspire Jeff Bezos to found the private aerospace manufacturer and suborbital spaceflight company Blue Origin in 2000 (Stephenson was also the company’s first employee and remains a senior advisor a quarter century later). As of 2023, Blue Origin is the second-most-valuable private aerospace company in the world—behind only Elon Musk’s SpaceX. Two of the three founders of Keyhole, which was acquired by Google in 2004 and renamed Google Earth, have said their visions were informed (or inspired^([6](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr6))) by a similar product described in *Snow Crash*, and that they once tried to recruit Stephenson to the company (reports differ on whether this was as an advisor or just guest).^([7](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr7)) From 2014 to 2020, Stephenson was also the “chief futurist” at Magic Leap, which is widely considered the first high-profile start-up which sought to build the augmented and virtual reality devices that enable Stephenson’s Metaverse. By 2021, Magic Leap had raised more than $7 billion from the likes of Google, Alibaba, AT&T, and Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund, the last of which acquired the majority of the company in late 2022.

## Literary Influence and Related Works

Stephenson’s works have also been cited as an inspiration for dozens of projects and technologies that never appeared in *Snow Crash*. Two particularly notable novels came out in the seven years following *Snow Crash*. *Diamond Age*, published in 1995, is set in a world revolutionized by nanotechnology, operated on a “worldwide \[digital\] network that can transfer money anonymously,”^([8](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr8)) and centered on characters who have met through the virtual world and in many cases only know one another’s fictional identity. The pivotal technology of *The Diamond Age*, though, was an interactive book (a “ractive”) that generated live, CGI-based films, television series, and instruction lessons using the body movements and utterances of performers who might be tens of thousands of kilometers away. It wasn’t until 2002 that a film, *The Lord of the Rings: The Two Towers*, had even captured a performer’s motions in real time as part of the still-manual process of creating a CGI character.

Stephenson’s 1999 novel *Cryptonomicon* told a tale of cryptocurrencies, cryptographic program execution, and how money stores value, as well as the establishment of offshore data havens powered by ultrahigh-capacity submarine internet cables. Bitcoin, the first mainstream cryptocurrency, debuted a decade after *Cryptonomicon*.
