
# Android 的角色演变与区块链技术分析

## Android 的生态系统与商业模式

After 15 years, it’s clear that Android serves a different role than what was originally pitched in 2008. At the time, Google positioned the “open-source OS” as a collective effort to build a shared ecosystem. To OEMs, Android was not just a free alternative to licensing Windows Mobile but an opportunity to participate in mobile app revenues, steward the platform’s overall development, and develop OS-level services without needing to develop and manage the underlying OS itself. For wireless operators, it was an opportunity to participate in application-layer profits and diversify from Apple. Now Google is effectively paying its partners to deploy all of its services, not just Android, while also blocking them from touting competing services or deploying their own. And if they choose to support a rival, substitute their own services, or otherwise become noncompliant, then the OEM’s users—having already bought the OEM’s device—will lose access to all of Google’s apps and service-level integrations such as cloud backup and will also need to reinstall all third-party apps from an app store they’ve never used before and that might lack some of their apps.

## 元宇宙与平台垄断的警示

The history of Android serves as a warning. Though the emergence of the Metaverse offers the opportunity to disrupt today’s gatekeepers, such as Apple or Google, it’s possible that these kings will simply be replaced by another, be it Roblox, Epic, Facebook, or some as-yet-unknown entity. And the outcome may not be better, either. While Tencent’s WeChat’s 0.6% fee for real-world transactions is a fraction of that paid by consumers across the rest of the world, the company has used its control over digital payments and video games to charge 40%–55% for all in-app downloads and virtual items—a sum that far exceeds that of Apple, whose power Tencent was able to overcome with its own might, as discussed in [Chapter 10](#chapter10.xhtml).

## 去中心化区块链的特性

In contrast to most major technology platforms, decentralized blockchains have no central operating entity, let alone a for-profit authority. There is no “Ethereum Corp.” that might suddenly co-opt Ethereum transaction fees or impose new ones, deny an emerging technology or standard for its own benefit, launch a first-party service that competes with the most successful dapps, and so on. What’s more, Ethereum’s trustless and permissionless programming actually encourages developers to “compete” with its core functionality.

## 以太坊的扩容方案与竞争

As Ethereum became more popular throughout the 2010s, its transaction fees increased and its processing times slowed. This prompted some entrepreneurs to construct competing blockchains, such as Solana and Avalanche, both of which debuted in 2020. Other entrepreneurs instead built “scaling solutions” designed to support Ethereum, such as “Layer 2,” a “mini-blockchain” that sits on top of Ethereum, or “sidechains” that run parallel to it. These solutions process transactions in ways that are typically less secure than Ethereum but are faster and/or cheaper. One approach is to batch transactions, rather than process them individually. While this delays a payment or transfer, it also helps to minimize fees per individual transaction—and not all bills need to be paid on the spot. Another approach is to poll only a portion of eligible validators or operate a network with fewer total validators, both of which increase the risk of a transaction being hijacked but can be appropriate for less valuable transactions such as a $5 coffee (a similar approach is taken by most merchants: small purchases require only a credit card, larger ones require a zip code, and luxury purchases require address and photo ID). In theory, Layer 2s and sidechains are competitive with Layer 1s as they shift developers, validators, and users away from the “main chain.” Yet these would-be alternatives remain compatible with this Layer 1, run many of their operations through this Layer 1, and either keep “pegged” or associated tokens on or (eventually) move these assets to this Layer 1. These scaling solutions also help the Layer 1 use cases where its relatively high cost, slow speeds, and security are impractical, while also helping to reduce its network congestion, which in turn reduces its average transaction fees and time, to the aid of all developers and users on the Layer 1, which then helps the validators which support it. The model of “co-opetition” isn’t new to tech, but it’s rare that it ends well for the smaller party—especially if they run on the larger party’s own platform.

## 区块链协议的更新与“合并”

The lack of a single authority, or even group of authorities, also does not prevent a blockchain’s underlying programming from being revised, improved, or otherwise altered. However, updates require support from the community, which must therefore be convinced that any revisions are to their collective benefit. An instructive example of such a change occurred in 2022 and was dubbed “the merge.” Not only was the merge a massive overhaul of Ethereum’s operations, it also was one that involved substantial technical risks and potentially jeopardized the billions invested in “scaling solutions,” yet also offered the opportunity to dramatically improve the blockchain and resolve one of its most widely criticized problems.

## 从工作量证明到权益证明的转变

At its launch in 2015, Ethereum was based on a proof-of-work (PoW) system akin to that pioneered by Bitcoin in 2009. Specifically, validators would confirm a transaction (and therefore be paid) when they had solved the cryptographic problem that was automatically generated by a proposed transaction. Though this approach is considered secure, it’s also resource-intensive, as vast computing resources were required to solve the problem—which is precisely why Ethereum transactions typically involved high costs, slow processing times, and massive energy use. Moreover, this work was not performed by a single validator but rather many validators, all of which were racing to solve it, even though only one of them would be rewarded in any given situation.

Three years after Bitcoin’s debut and two years before that of Ethereum, an alternative model known as proof-of-stake (PoS) had been deployed by a different blockchain, Peercoin. In this case, the blockchain would pick a single validator based, at least in part, on the number of tokens that validator had “staked” on the problem. Though the model created the opportunity for validators to “confirm” false transactions, other validators would be able to “whistle-blow” by proving the “correct” transaction, which would result in the dishonest validator losing their staked tokens and the auditor collecting them. These incentives and costs naturally scale, too—the larger the transaction, the more likely it is that validators will stake to validate it, and the greater the bounty from successfully disputing it and the more likely a validator is to spoof it.

As Ethereum grew from 2020 through 2022, many developers called on the community to switch from PoW to PoS to reduce fees, improve transaction times, and mitigate energy usage and the related environmental impact. Furthermore, PoS-based blockchains, such as Cardano, which debuted in 2017, and Solana, from 2020, were rapidly attracting users and developers who might otherwise have leveraged Ethereum. Some developers, conversely, feared that a switch would erode Ethereum’s security-related advantages and produce widespread compatibility errors, if not outright data loss. The adoption of PoS also put at risk the value propositions of many scaling solutions, whose tokens were worth billions combined.
