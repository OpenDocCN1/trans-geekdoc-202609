## Chapter 5 NETWORKING VERSUS THE SPEED OF LIGHT

Discourse on the Metaverse typically focuses on the video games that are the most popular, yet the most technically impressive exemplar is a title that, over its lifetime, will likely count fewer players than `Roblox`, `Minecraft`, and `Fortnite` reach in an average day. In fact, the title’s aim is not socializing or the ability to traverse fictional realms or build virtual wonders. Instead, it’s designed to precisely reproduce an experience many deem unpleasant, dull, or terrifying: airplane travel.

### Bandwidth

The first `Flight Simulator` was released by Sublogic in 1979 for the Apple II computer, and though renders were limited to fewer than 200 colored lines atop a uniform black background, the title nevertheless amassed a cult following. In 1981—two decades before the debut of Microsoft’s Xbox video game console—Alan Boyd, the 32nd-ever employee at Microsoft and its head of acquisitions, approached Sublogic hoping to license the franchise and produce a new entry that would enable Microsoft to differentiate the still-to-come 16-bit Windows PCs from 8-bit computers such as the Apple II.^([1](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr1))

By 2006, Microsoft had released ten total entries in the rechristened `Microsoft Flight Simulator` (`MSFS`) line, and in 2012 *The Guinness Book of World Records* dubbed `MSFS` the longest-running video game franchise in history, beating `Space Invaders` and `Oregon Trail`, which were a year and a half older but had become dormant, and `Pac-Man` and `Donkey Kong`/`Super Mario`, which launched in 1980 and 1981.^([2](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr2)) Despite this heritage, it took until the 12th entry, which was released in August 2020, for `MSFS` to soar into the public consciousness. The *New York Times* wrote that the 2020 `MSFS` provided “a new way of understanding the digital world,” offering a view “that is more real than the one we can see outside \[and\] a picture that illuminates our understanding of reality.”^([3](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr3)) A number of publications, including `Time` magazine and Polygon, named `MSFS` one of the best games of 2020.

In theory, `MSFS` is what many people see it as: a game. Within seconds of opening the application, they’ll be reminded that Microsoft’s Xbox Game Studios developed and published it. However, the goal of `MSFS` is not to win, kill, shoot, defeat, beat, or score on another player or an AI-based competitor. The goal is to fly a virtual plane—a process that involves much of the same work as flying a real one. Players will communicate with air traffic control and their copilots, wait to be cleared for takeoff, set their altimeter and flaps, check for fuel reserves and mixtures, release their brake, slowly push the throttle, and so on, all before following their selected or designated flight path while managing conflicting routes and accommodating the flight paths of other virtual planes.

Every entry in the `MSFS` series has offered that sort of functionality, but the 2020 edition is extraordinary—the most realistic and expansive consumer-grade simulation in history. Its map is over 500 million square kilometers—just like the “real” planet earth—and includes two trillion uniquely rendered trees (not two trillion copy-and-pasted trees, or two trillion trees made up of a few dozen varieties), 1.5 billion buildings, and reproductions of nearly every road, mountain, city, and airport across the world.^([4](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr4)) Everything looks like “the real thing” because `MSFS`’s virtual world is based on high-quality scans and images of the “real thing” that are then transformed into 3D models using Microsoft’s Azure Stack.

`MSFS`’s reproductions and renders aren’t perfect, but they are still stunning. “Players” can fly past their own house and spot their mailbox or the tire swing in the front yard. When the “game” conjures a sunset that’s reflected across a bay and then refracted yet again by the plane’s wings, it can be difficult to distinguish between a screenshot from `MSFS` and a real-world photograph.

`MSFS`’s life-size and lifelike “virtual world” is made up of nearly 2.5 petabytes of data—roughly a thousand times larger than `Fortnite` and equivalent to more than 2.6 million gigabytes.^([5](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr5)) There is no way for a consumer device (or most enterprise devices) to store this amount of data. Most consoles and PCs top out at 1,000 gigabytes, while the largest consumer-grade Network Attached Storage (NAS) drive is 20,000 gigabytes and retails for close to $750. Even the physical space required to store 2.5 petabytes is impractical.

But even if a consumer could afford such a hard drive and enough space to house it, `MSFS` is a live service. It updates to reflect real-world weather (including accurate wind speed and direction, temperature, humidity, rain, and light), air traffic, and other geographic changes. This allows a player to fly into real-world hurricanes or to trail real-life commercial airliners on their exact flight path while they’re in the air in the real world. This means that users cannot “prebuy” or “pre-download” all of `MSFS`—much of it doesn’t yet exist!

`MSFS` works by storing a relatively small portion of the game on a consumer’s device—roughly 150 GB. This portion is enough to run the game offline, as it contains the game’s core code, visual information for numerous planes and cities, and generic textures and assets to populate a map the size of the earth. The trade-off of offline play is that users see mostly procedurally generated environments and objects, with landmarks such as Manhattan broadly familiar but populated with generic, mostly duplicated buildings that bear only an occasional and sometimes accidental resemblance to their real-world counterparts. Some preprogrammed flight paths exist, but they cannot mimic actual live routes, nor can one player see another player’s plane.

It’s when players go online that `MSFS` becomes a true wonder, with Microsoft’s servers streaming new maps, textures, weather data, flight paths, and whatever other information a user might need. In a sense, players experience the `MSFS` world exactly as a real-world pilot might. When they fly over or around a mountain, new information streams into their retinas via light particles, revealing and then clarifying what’s there for the first time. Before then, a pilot knows only that, logically, something must be around the corner.

Many gamers assume this is what happens in all online multiplayer video games. But the truth is that most online games try to send as much information as possible to the user in advance of them playing, and as little as possible when they’re actively playing. This explains why playing a game, even a relatively small one such as `Super Mario Bros.`, requires purchasing cartridges or discs that contain multi-gigabyte game files, or waiting tens of minutes or even a couple of hours downloading and installing these game files. From time to time, a player might be told to download and install a multi-gigabyte update before they can play again. These files are so large because they contain nearly the entire game—namely, its code, game logic, and all the assets and textures required for the in-game environment (every type of tree, every avatar, every boss battle, every weapon, and so on).
