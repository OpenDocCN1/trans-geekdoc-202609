Another example comes via credit scores. Banks and other financial institutions did not always share customer credit data—it was seen as far too valuable. In time, it became clear that pooling their data would be to their collective benefit, as every bank would have a fuller picture of a customer’s creditworthiness, including their history of payments and defaults and their outstanding liabilities. Competing homestay marketplaces Airbnb and Vrbo are now partnering with a third-party data provider to prevent guests with a history of poor behavior (loud noise, property damage, other rule violations) from making future bookings. Although this harms the individual offenders, all other guests see lower prices and better inventory as a result, while hosts limit their risk of rental incidents. Meanwhile, the two platforms face reduced legal liability and can offer more reliable income streams to their partners.

The top case study is language, one of humanity’s first and core technologies. By the early 2010s globalization had led many of the world’s largest corporations to mandate English as their official corporate language—including Rakuten, Japan’s largest e-commerce company; Airbus, an aerospace giant that counts the governments of France and Germany as its two largest shareholders; Nokia, the fourth-largest company in Finland; and Samsung, South Korea’s largest company. A 2012 poll by Ipsos found that 67% of individuals whose professional dealings involved communications with people based in other countries preferred to conduct their work in English. The next closest language was Spanish, at 5%. Crucially, 61% of respondents said they did not use their native language when working with foreign partners.^([1](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr1)) As such, English’s reported dominance does not stem from its noncommercial popularity. In fact, the language dominates despite the fact that fewer than one in ten people globally have English as a first language. Globalization has also driven standardization of currencies (the US dollar and the Euro, the latter itself a unification of nearly two dozen regional currencies), units (the metric system), and the import/export of goods (the intermodal shipping container).

## The March to Interoperability

When it comes to virtual worlds and 3D assets, there are many theorized drivers of interoperation and standardization. There is also observable progress.

For more than a decade after online console gaming emerged in the mid-2000s, the leading video game console, Sony PlayStation, refused to support so-called cross-play. Sony’s policy meant that even if one friend had the PlayStation version and another owned the Xbox version of the same title, they would never be able to play together, no matter how technically similar or compatible each title’s code was or the efforts by the developer to connect the two. Even if a single player bought two copies of the same game (say, one for their PlayStation and another for their laptop), their in-game currencies, in-game purchases, and in-game achievements would remain siloed in one or the other. In industry terms, Sony prohibited not just “cross-play” but “cross-purchasing,” “cross-progression,” and “cross-communications” with outside platforms, which also meant there were no cross-platform identities, either.

Critics of the policy argued that Sony’s stance was clearly a consequence of its dominant market position. If users didn’t need a PlayStation to play with other PlayStation users—which typically outnumbered those on rival consoles such as those of Nintendo and Microsoft—they’d be less likely to buy a PlayStation in the first place, and might abandon it even after they did. Sony’s president of interactive entertainment tacitly admitted as much in 2016 when he said “the technical aspect could be the easiest” part of opening up access to its PlayStation Network for cross-play.^([2](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr2)) And lo and behold, Sony launched full cross-play two and a half years later.

Sony did not change its mind because of internal preferences, financial straits, declining PlayStation sales, or customer feedback. Instead, it was effectively forced to do so by the success of `Fortnite`, which came from a company, Epic Games, that, not coincidentally, was built around an engine that could run on as many platforms as possible.

`Fortnite` had a number of rare attributes when it launched. It was the first mainstream AAA game that could be played on nearly every major gaming device globally, including two generations of PlayStation and Xbox as well as the Nintendo Switch, Mac, and PC; the following year, Epic expanded to iPhone and Android. The title was also free, so players didn’t have to buy multiple copies to be able to play on multiple platforms. `Fortnite` was also designed as a social game; it became better as more of a player’s friends used it. And it was built around live services rather than a fixed narrative or any offline play: the game’s content never ended and was constantly updated. This, plus superb creative execution, helped `Fortnite` become the most popular AAA game globally (excluding China) by the end of 2018. It was also generating more revenue per month than any game in history.

From the start, Sony’s console competitors opted to support cross-platform capabilities for `Fortnite`. Nintendo had negligible online networking business and therefore little concern about defending it, and there was little evidence that its handheld Switch platform was in direct competition with the PlayStation line anyway. In contrast to Nintendo, Xbox was in direct competition with PlayStation and had a thriving online multiplayer and services business—widely believed to be far superior to that of PlayStation. Despite this service leadership, Microsoft had long been an advocate of cross-platform gaming—after all, a majority of its customers were on its open Microsoft Windows platform, not the closed Microsoft Xbox. Microsoft also wanted to open up gaming for the very reason Sony had resisted doing so—to weaken PlayStation’s player lock-in and to give Xbox users the ability to play with all of their friends (a plurality or even majority of whom were on PlayStation). PC and mobile devices, meanwhile, had never blocked cross-platform functionality as neither were dedicated gaming services, to say the least. Moreover, these devices, unlike consoles, were not sold at a loss that necessitated video game purchases for recoupment.
