
As measured by the number of assets created, the fastest-growing category of virtual software may be those that scan the real world. Matterport is a multibillion-dollar platform company whose software converts scans from devices such as iPhones to produce rich 3D models of building interiors. Today, the company’s software is primarily used by property owners to create vivid and navigable replicas of their real estate on sites such as Zillow, Redfin, and Compass, affording would-be renters or homebuyers, construction professionals, and the like a better way to understand the space than is allowed by blueprints, photographs, or even live tours. Soon such scans will be used to determine the best placement of a wireless router or household plant, to test out a selection of different lamps (each one purchasable through Matterport), or to operate an entire smart home, including electricity, security, HVAC, and more. Another example is Planet Labs, which scans nearly the entire earth via satellite each day and across eight spectral bands, capturing not just high-resolution imagery but details including heat, biomass, and haze. The company’s goal is to make the entire planet, in all its nuances, legible to software and to update its data on a daily to hourly basis.

Given the number of virtual worlds that will compose the Metaverse, and given their diversity and the technical difficulty of constructing them, it’s likely that there will be dozens of major virtual world platforms, with many more underlying technology providers. This is good; there is danger to having a single virtual world platform or engine operating the entire Metaverse.

Recall Tim Sweeney’s warning about the scope of the Metaverse: “This Metaverse is going to be far more pervasive and powerful than anything else. If one central company gains control of this, they will become more powerful than any government and be a god on Earth.”

Though Sweeney’s remark is exaggerated, there is already widespread worry about the ways a handful of big-tech companies shape modern existence—from what their users buy to what they do, how they feel, and even what they believe to be true. As the Metaverse emerges, a growing share of human time, labor, leisure, friendships, memories, and spend will exist or occur in virtual space, rather than just be transmitted through it as with Zoom or Instagram today. Moreover, the Metaverse will help guide, inform, capture, and power the physical world, rather than just bring it online. It is hard to imagine any outcome in which this does not increase big tech’s control, and it is easy to fear one in which a single tech company not only defines the virtual laws of physics but also serves as landlord, treasurer, judge, and customs officer.

But if the Metaverse is to have many underpinning platforms and rival technologies, then there needs to be a way to interoperate among them.

## Chapter 8 THE TECHNOLOGIES, PHILOSOPHIES, AND ECONOMICS OF INTEROPERABILITY

It is common to hear the phrase “interoperable assets” when discussing the Metaverse, but no such thing exists, because virtual assets don’t exist. Only data does. And it’s here—at the very start—where the problems of interoperability begin.

Consider the “interoperability” of physical goods, such as a pair of shoes. The manager of an Adidas store in “the real world” could decide to prohibit a customer from wearing Nikes in their store. This would be a business decision, and obviously a poor one—and also one that would be almost impossible to enforce. A customer wearing Nikes can get inside an Adidas store by opening its door. This is because physics is universal, so atoms are “Write once, run everywhere.” That Nike shoes physically exist means that they are automatically compatible inside any Adidas store. The Adidas store manager would need to write a policy covering the wearing of non-Adidas shoes in the store, create a system to implement that policy, and then enforce that system.

Virtual atoms do not work like that. For virtual goods from a virtual Nike store to be understood in a virtual Adidas outlet, the latter would need to admit information on these shoes from Nike, operate a system that understood this information, and then run code to operate the shoes in the manner specified. Suddenly, the admission of sneakers has changed from passive to active.

Today, there are hundreds of different file formats used to structure and store data. There are dozens of popular real-time rendering engines, most of which have been fragmented further through various code customizations. As a result, almost all virtual worlds and software systems are incapable of understanding what each considers a “shoe” (data), let alone being able to use that understanding (code).
