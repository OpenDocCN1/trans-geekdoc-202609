
The development of easy-to-use, cross-company tools such as `UE` are key to establishing the Metaverse. Previously, building a virtual world required a developer/creator to acquire deep technical expertise and design their own tools, just like Epic Games or id Software, or build within a platform, such as `Second Life` or `ZZT`. The former approach constrained how many virtual worlds were created, and led to technical fragmentation that made interoperation difficult. The latter approach, meanwhile, produced networks within an application, rather than a network of applications.

In the quarter century since its debut, the Unreal Engine (now `UE5`) has become the most widely used “high-fidelity” engine for 3D games. `Unity`, a rival engine that launched in 2005, powers more than 50% of mobile games. And these engines are increasingly used outside of gaming, too.

Although the first few dozen virtual worlds were designed for scientific purposes, such as `Turochamp`, `Bertie the Brain`, `OXO`, `Tennis for Two`, `Spacewar!`, and `Maze`, nearly all of those created since were designed for video games. This skew is partly why so many liken the Metaverse to such experiences. It’s also a contrast with the traditional adoption curve for advanced computing and networking technologies.

The first computers were too large, expensive, and power intensive for anyone but governments and major enterprises to afford, let alone use. Moreover, these devices were too difficult for the average person to use and their utility was limited to (slowly) solving complex calculations that were relevant only to research labs and governments. Accordingly, it was more than 50 years until half of computers were owned by consumers and longer still for most usage to be noncommercial. The arc of the internet was similar. Over its first 20 years, bandwidth was too limited and latency too great for the internet to support consumer use cases such as photo sharing, let alone video calls or streaming, but it was more than capable of sharing word documents, spreadsheets, and messages, which were of immense value to the “information economy” and far superior to using couriers and postal services. And while early smartphones could barely play `Snake`, let alone support color displays, businesses and governments considered push email to be orders of magnitude more helpful than pager notifications or live phone calls.

In contrast to the path of computers and the internet, 3D simulations have limitations that historically prevented their adoption by industry and government services and instead confined them to consumer-focused implementation. All a “game” needs from simulation is to create a sense of fun; the physics of a building on fire, a car crash, or a shotgun pellet do not need to be realistic—and are sometimes better because they’re fantastical. Military personnel, surgeons, and infrastructure operators, meanwhile, need far greater fidelity. And it’s only over the past few years that this has been possible.

By the start of the 2020s, a company that had access to enough computing power could run large-scale, real-time simulations down to the particle level, enabling precise readings of heat absorption, transference, and dissipation, the effects of vibrations, the flow of sound, the reflection of light, the accumulation of radiation, and so on. The engines behind these simulations recently became “deterministic,” too—able to generate the identical outcome each time they were run, rather than nondeterministic, in which outcomes are largely but not precisely alike.

The many advances in real-time 3D simulation have resulted in new usages. In 2019, the Hong Kong International Airport produced a digital twin using the `Unity Engine` that simulates critical airport activity in real time, from passenger movement to planes and vehicles on the runway, as well as inbound traffic and their dependencies. This information is then used to predict and address potential backups and gate changes, and to prepare for and respond to emergency situations. In other cases, entire cities have been re-created and then connected to real-time data feeds for vehicular traffic, weather, and civic services, such as police, fire, and ambulance response. The goal of a digital twin is to enable city planners to make more informed decisions about zoning, construction approvals, and more. How would a new commercial mall affect travel times for emergency medical or police services? How might a specific building design adversely affect wind conditions, urban temperatures, or downtown light?

A good way to understand the practical potential of city-scale digital twins, unsurprisingly, came from video gaming. In December 2021, Epic Games released `The Matrix Awakens`, a playable technical demonstration that was intended to show off the functionality of the forthcoming `Unreal Engine 5` when running on the newly released `PlayStation 5` and `Xbox Series S|X`, which kicked off the “ninth generation of video game consoles” (the 1972 Magnavox Odyssey was first gen). `Matrix Awakens` was set in a procedurally generated and photorealistic city that was 16 square kilometers in area, spanning 7,000 buildings, 260 kilometers of drivable roads with 1,250 intersections, 50,000 cars (nearly all of which could be walked up to and driven), and 40,000 pedestrians.^([35](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr35)) “Players,” if one could call them that, could change the city’s time of day in real time and watch the sun’s rays shift and bounce across various buildings and windshields, as well as modify the number of pedestrians and cars, and then watch how the flow of traffic and people altered as a result. Some masochistic users even orchestrated traffic accidents so severe that hours later, they could observe how traffic jams had spread throughout the city.

In September of 2022, job postings at `Tesla` revealed the company was working to make Epic’s demonstration a reality by producing a live 3D model of the city of San Francisco using the `Unreal Engine`.^([36](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr36)) Unlike Epic’s demo, `Tesla`’s simulation doesn’t need to run on a single, consumer-brand video game console. Instead, it can be operated on `Tesla`’s private supercomputer cluster, or on the data centers of cloud computing giants such as `Amazon` and `Microsoft`. And `Tesla`’s virtual San Francisco is not made by designers or generative models, but assembled and maintained using the myriad real-time lidar scans and positional pings generated by the network of San Franciscan `Teslas`. The resulting simulation would aid city planning, autonomous driving software, and human navigation.

`Tesla` wasn’t the only tech giant to imagine a living Metaverse version of Google Street View; `Google`, too, was hard at work. In June of 2023, the company launched its `Geospatial Creator` tool, which let users create 3D/augmented reality experiences that could be “anchored” to real-world locations inside `Google`’s various mapping programs for other users to see and experience. To commemorate the launch, `Google` announced an AR-based reinvention of the 1978 hit video game `Space Invaders`. Unlike the arcade classic, however, the titular enemies would rain down not from the top of a CRT-display but from the skies of major cities such as Rio de Janeiro and New York, and they would be repelled not through joysticks and buttons but by smartphone-wielding citizens throughout the city. `Nvidia`, meanwhile, announced it was building “Earth-2,” a live digital twin that would model “the physics, chemistry and biology of the atmosphere, waters, ice, land and \[aggregate\] human activities” of the earth down to the “meter-scale resolution \[that\] is needed to simulate \[as an example\] clouds that reflect sunlight back to space.”^([37](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr37))
