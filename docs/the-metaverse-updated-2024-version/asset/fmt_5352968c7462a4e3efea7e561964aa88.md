
That such enormous variation could exist might surprise those familiar with common file formats such as JPEG or MP3, or who know that most websites use HTML. But the standardization of online languages and media stems from how late “for-profit” businesses came to the internet. Apple didn’t unveil iTunes until 2001, nearly 20 years after the Internet Protocol Suite was established. It was impractical for Apple to reject the standards already in wide use, such as WAV and MP3. Gaming is a different story. When the gaming industry began to emerge in the 1950s, no standards for virtual objects, rendering, or engines existed. In many cases, the companies producing these games were pioneering computer-based content. Apple’s Audio Interchange File Format (AIFF) is still the most common audio file format used to store sound on Apple computers; it was created in 1988 and was based on the general-purpose Interchange File Format standard devised by game maker Electronic Arts in 1985. Furthermore, video games were never intended to be part of a “network” like the internet. Instead, they existed to run on a fixed and offline piece of hardware.

Timing is not the only reason online virtual worlds have considerable technical diversity compared to the rest of the internet. Another critical factor is the intense computational, networking, and financial demands of modern simulations, which in turn encourages developers to code to their immediate needs and build whatever proprietary tech might better support their ambitions. In this sense, fragmentation is often essential, as the needs of AR and VR experiences, 2D and 3D games, realistic and cartoon-styled worlds, high-concurrent-user and low-concurrent-user simulations, high-budget and low-budget titles, and 3D printers can be quite different. As such, full standardization would likely mean underserving one application, falling massively short on another, and so on—and in totally unpredictable ways.

The challenge of interoperation goes beyond file formats and approaches questions that are more ontological. It’s relatively easy to agree on what an image is—it’s only two-dimensional and it doesn’t move (with video files being simply successions of images). But in 3D, especially with interactive objects, agreement is far harder. Is a shoe an object, or is it a collection of objects? If the latter, how many? Are the caps on a shoelace part of the shoelace or separate from it? Does a shoe have a dozen individual eyelets, each of which can be customized or even removed, or are they a single interconnected set? If shoes seem hard, just imagine avatars—would-be representations of real people. Forget trees—what is a person? Beyond visuals, there are other attributes that must be examined, such as motion or “rigging.” The bodies of the Incredible Hulk and a jellyfish should not move in the same way, but this means that the creator of these avatars needs to enshrine them with code detailing their movement, code that another platform can understand. To permit third-party objects, platforms will also need data that describes a good’s appropriateness (e.g., nudity, penchant for violence, language). A game for toddlers needs to differentiate between a PG-rated swimsuit and an R-rated one. Similarly, a gritty war simulator will want to know the difference between a sniper wearing a shrub-like ghillie suit and a “sniper” that’s actually an anthropomorphic bush. All of this requires data conventions—and probably additional systems. A 2D game will want to be able to import a 3D avatar but then restyle it according to its own logic. And vice versa. To support this functionality, a suite of interoperable standards, conventions, and systems are required. But these are still not enough.

To have a virtual goods economy, there must be mechanisms that record who owns various goods, support the sale of these records between users and their use across independent platforms, and can be modified or edited by both users and platforms. The difficulties involved are immense, which again reflects the difference between goods in the virtual and physical worlds (or between code and atoms).

In the “real world,” money can be exchanged physically with no intermediary; neither party is obligated to update their private ledgers for the transaction to be successfully completed. Digital payments involve and require a record but do not require the literal transfer of a specific dollar. Venmo does not operate individual bank accounts for each of its users but instead manages a series of corporate accounts that hold all user funds with an associated database recording how much of that pool is owed to each user. And when one Venmo user sends $100 to another, Venmo simply updates its record to reduce that user’s entitlement by $100, while the other is increased by $100. Interbank transfers are more complicated, but they too are bundled with and then netted against other inbound and outbound payments. Sending $1,000 from one bank to another may not, in fact, involve that sum leaving one bank and going to another, and certainly not the specific dollars in the account. This is possible because dollars are fungible; no one dollar is worth more than another and is instead fully substitutable with another.

When physical goods are sold, again, no intermediary is required, and the process involves one person’s literal dispossession of the good and another person’s literal acquisition of it. And while records are sometimes used—such as a house title or deed—they help affirm ownership but do not actually ensure it, nor are they required to use the asset.

Digital assets and ownership rights—that is, data—are trickier. While the internet enables a digital good to be transferred worldwide in a matter of seconds, there are no protocols that manage the ownership rights to that good. This problem is exacerbated by the fact that transmission results in the creation of duplicates—every device, server, and even network that is touched by the transfer gets a copy, and there is no record of provenance that autonomous owners or platforms might use to validate the “true owner” and ensure the value of the “true copy.” There are, of course, many permission-based services, but they manage access to a stored file; they do not enable the authoritative copy to be taken to a third-party service.

The only exception to the above is the internet’s domain system, which is managed by ICANN. It established the 1,500 or so top-level domains such as.com and.org; some 2,500 domain registrars, which have overseen the registration, purchase, and sale of close to 400 million second-level domains such as google.com or facebook.com; and a series of “dispute resolution providers” to manage ownership conflicts and rights. However, email domains are fairly basic (alphanumeric alternatives to IP addresses rather than executable code), are modest in transfer volume (roughly one every two seconds, mostly of low-value addresses), require the use of an intermediary to be completed, and cannot be altered by the owner.

Given these limitations, the rights to virtual goods are exclusively managed within a platform. `Roblox` effectively uses the Venmo model, managing which users own a `Roblox` asset; those assets are managed on `Roblox`’s servers and bought and sold using `Roblox`’s own currency, which is also stored on their servers. Not only does the Metaverse require the ability to send the sole copy of a good off of `Roblox` to a platform such as `Minecraft`, it must also be possible for the latter to then trade or customize it, with `Roblox` then able to reflect these changes.
