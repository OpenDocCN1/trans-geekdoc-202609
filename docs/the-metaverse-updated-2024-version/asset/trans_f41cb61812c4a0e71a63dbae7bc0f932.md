Some believe that the future of computing isn’t a pair of AR glasses or a watch but something much smaller. In 2014, only a year after its ill-fated Google Glass launch, Google announced its first Google Contact Lens project, which was intended to help diabetics monitor their glucose levels. Specifically, this “device” was made up of two soft lenses, with a wireless chip, a wireless antenna that’s thinner than a strand of human hair, and a glucose sensor placed in between. A pinhole between the underlying lens and the wearer’s eyes enabled tear fluid to reach the sensor, which measured blood sugar levels. The wireless antenna drew power from the wearer’s smartphone, which was intended to support at least one reading per second. Google also planned to add a small LED light that could warn users of spikes or drops in blood sugar levels in real time.^([74](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr74))

Google discontinued its diabetes smart lens program four years after it was launched, but the company claimed that the cancellation stemmed from “insufficient consistency in our measurements of the correlation between tear glucose and blood glucose concentration,”^([75](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr75)) which had been widely noted by researchers in the medical community. Regardless, patent filings show that the major Western, Southeast Asian, and other Eastern technology companies continue to invest in smart lens technology.

Fanciful though such technologies might seem in a world in which internet connections remain unstable and computing power scarce, they nevertheless feel within reach compared to so-called brain-to-computer interfaces (`BCIs`), which have been in development since the 1970s and continue to attract greater investment. Many purported BCI solutions are noninvasive—think of Professor X’s helmet in *X-Men*, or perhaps a grid of wired sensors hidden underneath the wearer’s hair. Other BCIs are partially invasive or fully invasive, depending on how close the electrodes are placed to brain tissue.

In 2015, Elon Musk founded Neuralink, of which he remains CEO, and announced that the company was working on a “sewing machine–like” device that could implant sensors that are four to six micrometers thick (roughly 0.000039 inches, or one-tenth the width of human hair) in the human brain.^([76](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr76)) In April 2021, the company released a video in which a monkey played the game *Pong* using a wireless Neuralink implant. Only three months later, Facebook announced that it was no longer investing in its own BCI program. In prior years, it had funded several projects inside and outside the company, including a test at the University of California, San Francisco, that involved wearing a helmet that shot light particles through the skull, then measured blood oxygenation levels in groups of brain cells. A blog post on the subject explained that “while measuring oxygenation may never allow us to decode imagined sentences, being able to recognize even a handful of imagined commands, like ‘home,’ ‘select,’ and ‘delete,’ would provide entirely new ways of interacting with today’s VR systems—and tomorrow’s AR glasses.”^([77](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr77)) Another Facebook BCI test involved a physical mesh of electrodes placed on top of the user’s skull, which enabled the subject to write at a speed of roughly 15 words per minute purely through thought (the average person types 39 words per minute, two and a half times faster). Facebook reported that “while we still believe in the long-term potential of head-mounted optical \[brain-computer interface\] technologies, we’ve decided to focus our immediate efforts on a different neural interface approach that has a nearer-term path to market for AR/VR,”^([78](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr78)) and that “a head-mounted optical silent speech device is still a very long way out. Possibly longer than we would have foreseen.”^([79](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr79))

The “differential neural interface approach” that Facebook referenced is likely CTRL-labs, which offers several comparative benefits including lower costs, easier upgrades, replacements and repairs, less operational risk, and, well, clearer ethics than BCI devices (especially those which are surgically implanted on a permanent basis). Still, BCI is expected to offer functionality that no other device type or sensors could reliably provide and which could transform not just interaction with virtual worlds, but the quality of human existence. For this reason, the most likely adopters of neural implants—at least early on—are those with severe physical disabilities. In May 2023, Neuralink received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration to perform its first (live) human trial and by the end of the year, the company was recruiting surgical candidates with “quadriplegia due to cervical spinal cord injury or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (`ALS`).”^([80](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr80)) On January 29, 2024, Musk announced that a robot had implanted Neuralink’s BCI technology into the portion of a patient’s brain that controlled the “intention to move,” and that a day later, the patient was “recovering well” and “initial results show promising neuron spike detection.”^([81](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr81)) Three weeks later, Musk reported that “progress is good, and the patient seems to have made a full recovery, with no ill effects that we are aware of. Patient is able to move a mouse around the screen by just thinking.”^([82](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr82)) In March, 2024, the patient, identified as 29-year-old Noland Arbaugh, began to speak publicly about his experience as a “cyborg” and “neuralnaut,” as well as his newfound capabilities.^([83](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr83)) “It makes—this is going to sound really kind of crazy—but it makes being paralyzed really not that bad,” Arbaugh reported in Neuralink townhall that was released by the company. “Anything that makes me more independent I’m all for and this is probably going to make people like me the most independent that they might ever be until it all gets cured—and I think that’s a very real possibility.”^([84](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr84)) Neuralink and Arbaugh also shared several videos show his ability to successfully play turn-based strategy games such as *Civilization VI* and even-fast moving competitive multiplayer titles such as *Mario Kart*, using only his thoughts.^([85](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr85))
