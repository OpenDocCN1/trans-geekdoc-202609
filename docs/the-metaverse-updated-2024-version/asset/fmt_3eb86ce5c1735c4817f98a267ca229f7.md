
For the typical online game, what actually comes from online multiplayer servers? Not much. *Fortnite*’s PC and console game files are roughly 30 GB in size, but online play involves only 20–50 MB (or 0.02–0.05 GB) in downloaded data per hour. This information tells the player’s device what to do with the data it already has. If a user is playing an online game of *Mario Kart*, Nintendo’s servers will tell the user’s Nintendo Switch which avatars their opponents are using and therefore must be loaded by the device. During the match, the user’s continuous connection to the server then enables a near constant stream of data on exactly where these opponents are (“positional data”), what they’re doing (sending a red shell), communications (e.g., a teammate’s audio), and various other information, including how many players are still in the contest.

That online games remain “mostly offline” comes as a surprise even to avid gamers. After all, most music and video is now streamed—people don’t pre-download songs or TV shows anymore, let alone buy physical CDs to store them—and video gaming is supposedly a more technically sophisticated and forward-looking media category. Yet it is precisely because games are so complicated that those who make them choose to marginalize the internet. Connections are not reliable, bandwidth is not reliable, latency is not reliable. As discussed in [Chapter 4](#chapter4.xhtml), most online experiences can survive this unreliability, but games cannot. As a result, developers chose to rely as little on the internet as possible.

A mostly offline approach to online games works well, but it imposes limitations. The fact that a player’s device can tell individual users which assets, textures, and models should be rendered means that every asset, texture, and model must be known and stored in advance by the same device. By sending rendering data on an as-needed basis, games can have a much greater diversity of virtual objects because they need not be preloaded to a consumer-grade hard drive. *MSFS* aspires for every town not just to differ from every other but to exist as it does in real life. And it doesn’t want to store 100 types of clouds and then tell a device which cloud to render and with what coloring; rather, it wants to dictate exactly what that cloud should look like.

When a player sees their friend in *Fortnite* today, they can interact using only a limited set of preloaded animations (or “emotes”), such as a wave or a moonwalk. Many users imagine a future where their live facial and body movements are re-created in a virtual world. To greet a friend, they won’t pick Wave 17 of the 20 waves preloaded onto their device but will wave uniquely articulated fingers in a unique way that has never happened precisely that way before. Users also hope to bring their myriad virtual items and avatars across the myriad virtual worlds connected to the Metaverse. As *MSFS*’s file size suggests, it’s simply not possible to send so much data to the user in advance. Doing so requires not only impractically large hard drives but also a virtual world that knows in advance anything that might be created or performed.

The need to “pre-send” an otherwise living virtual world has other implications. Every time Epic Games alters *Fortnite*’s virtual world—say, to add new destinations, vehicles, or non-playable characters—users have to download and install an update. The more Epic adds, the longer this takes and the longer a user must wait. The more frequently a world is updated, the more delays a user will experience.

The batch-based update process also means that virtual worlds cannot truly be “alive.” Instead, a central server is choosing to send a specific version of a virtual world out to all users, a world that will endure until the next update replaces it. Each edition is not necessarily fixed—an update might have programmed changes, such as a New Year’s Eve event or snowfall which increases daily—but it is pre-scripted. Finally, there are limitations to where users can go. During Travis Scott’s ten-minute event on *Fortnite*, some 30 million players were instantly transported from the game’s core map to the depths of a never-before-seen ocean, then to a never-before-seen planet, and then deep into outer space. Many people may imagine that the Metaverse operates in similar fashion—that users can easily jump from virtual world to virtual world without first enduring long loading times. But to put on the concert, Epic had to send users each of these mini-worlds in full, days to hours before the event, via a standard *Fortnite* patch (users who had not installed the update were denied entry to the event). Then, during each set piece, every player’s device was loading the next set piece in the background. It was notable that each of Scott’s concert destinations was smaller and more limited than the one before it, with the last being a largely “on rails” experience in which users simply flew forward in largely nondescript space. Think of this as the difference between freely exploring a mall versus traversing one via a moving walkway.

The Travis Scott concert worked so well because Epic Games, in addition to being one of the most creative game developers in the world, designed the experience in a manner that hid its technical limitations. Concerts are also inherently structured events—participants don’t expect to be able to do anything they can imagine but, instead, expect to be mostly guided through a predetermined set list that begins and ends at a fixed time. The decentralized and boundless Metaverse doesn’t have such conveniences.

Some might conclude from the popularity of *Roblox* and the immensity of *MSFS* that modern internet infrastructure can now handle Metaverse-style live data streaming. In truth, the model, too, is possible only in a highly constrained fashion and for specific use cases. *Roblox*, for example, doesn’t need to cloud-stream much data because most of its in-game items are based on “prefabs.” The game is mostly just telling a user’s device how to tweak, recolor, or rearrange previously downloaded items—just as all Lego builds are composed of fewer than 3,800 unique pieces (and 10 of these pieces constitute 20% of all pieces shipped to customers).^([6](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr6)) *Roblox*’s graphical fidelity is also relatively modest and therefore its texture and environmental file sizes are relatively small, too. Overall, *Roblox*’s data usage is much greater than that of *Fortnite*—roughly 100–300 MB per hour, rather than 30–50 MB—but still manageable. At its target settings, *MSFS* demands nearly 25 times as much hourly bandwidth as *Fortnite* and 5 times as much as *Roblox*. This is because it’s not sending data on how to reconfigure or recolor a preloaded house but instead sending a user’s device the exact dimensions, density, and coloration of a multi-kilometer cloud or a nearly exact replica of the Gulf of Mexico’s shoreline. Yet even this need is simplified in ways that won’t work for the Metaverse.

While *MSFS* requires lots of data, it need not arrive particularly quickly. As with real-world pilots, *MSFS* pilots cannot suddenly teleport from New York State to New Zealand, nor see downtown Albany from 30,000 feet above Manhattan, nor descend from the firmament to the tarmac in seconds. This provides the player’s device with lots of time to download the necessary data. In fact, the *MSFS* client can easily predict where the player is headed and preemptively download the related data. And even if *MSFS* doesn’t receive all of the data it requested on time, the consequences are modest: some of Manhattan’s buildings will temporarily be procedurally generated, rather than resemble the real thing, with the realistic details filled in as the user gets closer to the city.
