
While AR and VR may come to replace most of the devices in use today, that time is unlikely to be soon. Even if the combined number of VR and AR headsets (two very different device types) in use by 2030 tops one billion, four times the aforementioned forecast, they would still reach fewer than one in six smartphone users. And that’s okay. There are, in 2024, hundreds of millions of people spending hours each day inside real-time-rendered virtual worlds through smartphones and tablets. These devices are also rapidly improving—and not just in their computational power. Modern smartphones are packed with infrared sensors and lidar scanners, ultra-wideband and near-field communication chips, larger screens, more cameras, higher resolutions despite faster frame rates, and longer-lasting batteries.

Some believe that the future role of the smartphone includes serving as a user’s “edge computer” or “edge server,” providing connectivity and computation to the world around us. Versions of this model already exist: most of the Apple Watches sold today lack a cellular network chip and instead connect to their owner’s iPhone via Bluetooth. The approach has limitations: the Apple Watch cannot make a phone call when taken too far away from its tethered iPhone, nor play music to a wearer’s AirPods, download new apps, retrieve messages not already stored on the watch, and so on. But in exchange, the device is much cheaper, lighter, and less battery intensive—all because the user’s iPhone, a far more powerful device and one with greater cost per performance, is doing most of the work.

Given the shortage, cost, and importance of compute, it makes sense to focus on a single device’s capabilities, rather than invest in scores of others, especially when those devices have greater physical, thermal, and cost limitations. A computer worn on the wrist or face simply cannot contend with one in the pocket, just as that one cannot contend with one stored on a desk or under a TV stand. This logic applies to more than just compute. If Facebook wants users to wear a CTRL-labs band on each limb, why load each one with its own cellular and Wi-Fi network chips, if cheaper, less energy-intensive, and smaller Bluetooth chips are available that can send data to a nearby smartphone? Personal data may be the most important consideration. Though there are risks to concentrating information on a single device, such an approach may be preferred to a model where it’s shared across a wide network of devices, not all of which a user considers equally trustworthy. For these reasons, it’s likely that the first optical AR/MR glasses to go mainstream—whatever decade they materialize—will be possible only because they run on a device that long, long preceded it.

### Hardware as the Gateway

The many devices required and expected to support the Metaverse can be grouped into three categories. The first consists of “primary computing devices,” which for most consumers are smartphones but might someday be an immersive HMD. The next category is “secondary” or “supporting” computing devices, such as a PC or PlayStation or, for the foreseeable future, HMDs. Secondary devices may or may not rely on a primary device or be complemented by one, but they will be used less frequently than a main device and for more specific purposes. Last are tertiary devices, such as a smartwatch or tracking camera, which enrich or extend a Metaverse experience but will rarely operate it directly.

Improvements in each category will increase engagement and revenues in the Metaverse—and offer manufacturers an opportunity to generate a new business line. However, the massive investment in these devices—many of which are years from mainstream viability—has broader motivations.

The Metaverse is a mostly intangible experience: a persistent network of virtual worlds, data, and supporting systems. At the same time, physical devices are the gateway to accessing and creating the Metaverse. Without them there is no forest to be known, heard, smelled, touched, or seen. This fact provides the device manufacturers and operators with significant soft and hard power. The manufacturers and operators will determine what GPUs and CPUs are used, the wireless chipsets and standards deployed, the sensors included, and so on. Critical though these intermediary technologies are to a given experience, they rarely interface directly with a developer or end user. Instead, they’re accessed through an operating system, which manages how, when, and why the capabilities are used by a developer, what sorts of experiences they’re allowed to offer a user, and whether and to what extent a commission must be paid to the maker of said device. In other words, hardware is not just about what the Metaverse might offer and when; it is a fight to influence how it works and, ideally, to take a cut of as much of the resulting economic activity as possible. The more essential the device—and the more devices that connect to it—the greater the control afforded to the company that makes it.
