
All that said, traditional or “flat 2D” devices have proved surprisingly capable at enabling users to interact in 3D spaces. In 2022, Neal Stephenson wrote a series of tweets intended to answer a commonly asked question: Did the Metaverse he envisioned require an AR or VR headset?

> The assumption that the Metaverse is primarily an AR/VR thing isn’t crazy. In my book \[`Snow Crash`\] it’s all VR. And I worked for an AR company \[`Magic Leap`\]—one of several that are putting billions of dollars into building headsets. But I didn’t see video games coming when I wrote `Snow Crash`.… Thanks to games, billions of people are now comfortable navigating 3D environments on flat 2D screens. The UIs that they’ve mastered \[`keyboard` and `mouse` for navigation and camera\] are not what most science fiction writers would have predicted. But that’s how path dependency in tech works. We fluently navigate and interact with extremely rich 3D environments using keyboards that were designed for mechanical typewriters. It’s steampunk made real. A Metaverse that left behind those users and the \[`developers`\] who build those experiences would be getting off on the wrong foot.… My expectation is that a lot of Metaverse content will be built for screens (where the market is) while keeping options open for the future growth of affordable headsets.^([10](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr10))

### Error #3: Conflating the Metaverse with a video game platform such as `Fortnite`, `Minecraft`, or `Roblox`

As with AR/VR, this mistake is understandable. Nearly all of the top popular multiplayer video games of 2024 are shared, live, and 3D and boast some form of internal goods economy. In addition to these attributes, `Fortnite`, `Minecraft`, and `Roblox` each operate networks of tens of millions of virtual worlds, all of which are underpinned by an extensive technology suite that covers identities, avatars, virtual currencies, and communications, and can be accessed through nearly every mainstream computing device globally, are used for both work and play, and have millions of individual contributors supporting hundreds of millions of users as part of their multibillion-dollar economies.

Though there is a structural similarity between the Metaverse and these platforms, characterizing the former as the latter would be akin to saying Facebook is the World Wide Web. Yes, Facebook is a large, device-agnostic platform that spans billions of interconnected pages, with standardized identifiers (accounts rather than IP addresses), formats, conventions, and so on. And for some users, Facebook is most of what they do on the internet. Yet Facebook is a fraction of the overall internet, predominantly leisure focused, and entirely separate from the actual protocols of the internet. `Roblox`, `Minecraft`, and `Fortnite` are likely to be part of the Metaverse, resemble much of it, and pioneer some of its standards and user behaviors, but they are not a Metaverse, let alone the Metaverse.

### Error #4: Conflating the Metaverse with Web3, blockchains, cryptocurrencies, or NFTs

A recurring theme in this book is the difficulty of fully separating various technological eras, waves, or models. Nevertheless, technologists, sociologists, and investors typically differentiate between the early stage of the internet, so-called “Web 1.0,” which began with the creation of the World Wide Web in 1989, and “Web 2.0,” which started around 2004 or 2005, following the launch of Facebook and YouTube. Webs 1.0 and 2.0 can be broadly differentiated in terms of content, user activity, the role of platforms, and the technologies used.

During the Web 1.0 era, content was not strictly limited to text, but limitations in broadband speeds and computer processing power meant that images were scarce and low resolution, while audio was typically available only in excerpts and low quality, and video was hard to find and even harder to watch. The content creator typically hosted this content themselves on servers they owned, and these ran open-source operating systems such as `Linux`. The content itself was usually produced or displayed using common standards (e.g., `JPEG`, `GIF`, `JavaScript`, `HTML`), and distributed through open platforms (the World Wide Web, web browsers, `Microsoft Windows`, email protocols, etc.). Although the Web 1.0 era was the first time most people could widely distribute their content—and without intermediation by a publisher, producer, retailer, and so on—almost no internet users created content during Web 1.0. They just consumed it.

During Web 2.0, it became common to create, distribute, and access high-quality media formats, including live video and interactive media. Billions of users also established comprehensive internet-based identities (e.g., a `Facebook` account), as well as their own internet-based networks (e.g., adding friends on `Facebook`). The advent of cloud data centers meant a developer or user could easily hire a company like `Amazon` or `Microsoft` to host and manage their website or app versus buying, setting up, and maintaining their own server. `Apple`, `Google`, and `Facebook`, among other technology companies, made it easier for individuals to create, discover, share, or monetize a piece of content, page, application, and so on. These many advances led to a surge in online creation. Rather than just view the internet, as was the case in Web 1.0, nearly all users of the Web 2.0 internet also contribute to the internet. Some of these contributions are just an occasional social media post, but millions of independent developers and creators now earn a living through their social media pages, video channels, apps, and websites, while millions more work in the digital “gig economy.”

Despite these benefits, many in the tech community worried about the underlying trade-off of Web 2.0: though more prosperous and participatory, it was also becoming more closed and privately owned. Identities and networks were established not on `TCP/IP` (e.g., `email` or `IP addresses`) or other open protocols but instead on private databases and systems (e.g., `Facebook` log-ins). If a developer published a website to the World Wide Web, they could freely shift it from one host to another, change their domain, or shift their customer and mailing lists to a different provider, but on `Facebook` or `YouTube`, most of the user’s content, identity, and followers were stuck. The major mobile operating systems also dictated what sorts of apps could be distributed, using which technologies, and through which business models they could be monetized—and took 15%–30% of an app’s digital revenues, too. Though nominally still “open,” the World Wide Web was intermediated by web browsers that were also tightly controlled by mobile operating systems (i.e., `iOS` and `Android`); a search engine with 95% market share and a desire to foster its ecosystem, collect data, and sell ads (`Google`); and algorithmic social feeds that were even more opaque and represented the majority of time spent online for billions of users (`Facebook`, `Twitter`, `TikTok`, etc.).

The overarching goal of Web3 is to continue to expand and enrich the number of contributors to the internet, while also returning the internet to the decentralized principles upon which it was founded, and reducing the power of big-tech platforms and aggregators. The supposed result would be a “healthier” internet that gives more rights to the individual user, offers more profits to the independent developer, and reduces the influence and controls of the various big-tech platforms that typically sit between users and developers.
