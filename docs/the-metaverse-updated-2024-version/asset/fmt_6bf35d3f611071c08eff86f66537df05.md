
Despite the missed release dates, poor sales, and nearly $50–$100 billion in total industry investment, many technologists and tech companies remain hopeful that the era of HMDs is nearby. Even primitive AR devices are seeing sustained increases in deployment in vocational training, surgery, and engineering—and these uses are quite literally saving lives. Though user retention remains poor, the 2020 Meta Quest 2 outsold the 2019 Quest 1 by a factor of more than ten over its first 12 months, with lifetime sales reaching $20 million. A particular bright spot is the recently released Apple Vision Pro. Apple has a quarter-century history of being a relatively late entrant to categories as diverse as MP3 players, smartphones, tablets, and smartwatches but nevertheless cracking those categories open through innovations in both form factor and interface. Often, Apple discards features and functionality that prior entrants considered necessary, while embracing those that others had ruled out. The company also excels at branding and marketing, boasts the world’s largest and most valuable developer ecosystem, and mobilizes over 500 retail stores and 30,000 retail employees to help explain and sell its devices. But while Apple’s arrival seems to herald the start of a new computing category, its first device also suggests that mainstream use remains far out. At release, Apple Vision Pro was priced at US$3,500, with sales tax adding the equivalent of roughly US$450 in Canada and $700 in the UK—each figure more than the price of Facebook’s Quest 2—while users that require eyeglasses also need to purchase custom lenses that cost $99 to $149. This total price point, which excludes optional accessories, places it well outside the reach of the average person and suggests a lengthy path to widespread adoption. The Apple II, which was one of the first-ever commercially successful consumer computers and Apple’s first hit product, retailed for $1,300 at its 1977 release (equivalent to over $6,500 in 2024), and it was decades until the average American had such a device and longer still for the average person worldwide to own one. And despite this price tag, the Vision Pro has significant drawbacks, including the need to be plugged into a wall outlet or pacemaker-sized wearable battery pack (which lasts “up to two hours”) whenever it’s used. The Apple HMD is also heavy, weighing approximately 625 grams (or 1.4 pounds), most of which rests on the user’s face, with the battery pack adding another 350 grams.

By 2024, HMDs had become a Rorschach test for the Metaverse. Some argued that the failures of these devices were proof that consumers didn’t want the Metaverse—or worse, that big tech would force it anyway. To others, HMDs were overshadowing the growing popularity and capability of virtual worlds that supported consumer experiences (e.g., `Roblox` or `Minecraft`) as well as industrial operations (the digital twins of the Hong Kong International Airport and Tampa’s Water Street development project). Recall Stephenson’s tweet from [Chapter 2](#chapter2.xhtml). Even science fiction authors of the 1980s and early 1990s imagined that the average person would require an immersive headset to navigate a virtual space; remarkably, billions of people are adept at navigating 3D space using touchscreens, keyboards, and mice. At his 2023 Metaverse keynote at the Game Developers Conference in San Francisco, Sweeney implored the audience “to set aside the crazy hype cycle around NFTs and VR goggles; yeah, these technologies may play a role in the future, but they are not required. This revolution is happening right now.”^([21](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr21)) HMDs have also overshadowed the extraordinary advances of AR and MR on the world’s most popular computing device: the smartphone.

HMDs are a microcosmic example of the difficulty of building the Metaverse—a challenge that so many of the best-informed, wealthiest, and most technically capable companies in the world have routinely underestimated and that relies upon countless different innovations and advances to be realized.

### “It Looks Like `Wii Sports`”

More than half of all households in the United States own a video game console. In almost all cases, this console is the most powerful computing device owned, used, or even seen by the members of that household. This includes those households that own the most recent model of the iPad Pro or work in an office with a high-end enterprise PC or Mac. It’s also likely that this video game console is more affordable per unit of computing power than any alternative that the household might purchase. It often costs more to purchase a comparably powered gaming PC or even to replace the graphics card on an existing PC. This cost advantage stems from substantial economies of scale, with console makers shipping 50–150 million standardized units over the course of a decade. Purchasing individual components, each one individually packaged, marked up, and retailed, is expensive. Video game consoles are also subsidized, typically by $100–$200, as their manufacturers pursue a razor-and-blades model in which their hardware is sold below cost and profits are instead attained by taking a cut of all subsequent software purchases made by the hardware owner (e.g., a player), and by selling related services, such as online multiplayer. In contrast, no graphics card or monitor manufacturer gets a cut of a player’s Robux or V-Bucks, and few even offer paid gaming services.

Compared to everyday devices, a video game console has computing power so great that in 2000, Japan even placed export limitations on its own beloved giant, Sony, and its signature PlayStation 2 console. The government feared that the PS2 could be used for terrorism on a global scale, namely for missile guidance.^([22](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr22)) The following year, in touting the importance of the consumer electronics industry, US Secretary of Commerce Don Evans declared that “yesterday’s supercomputer is today’s PlayStation.”^([23](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr23)) Evans’s pronouncement was powerful—even though it was arguably backward: today’s PlayStation is often tomorrow’s supercomputer. In 2010, the US Air Force Research Laboratory built the 33rd-largest supercomputer in the world using 1,760 Sony PlayStation 3s. The project’s director estimated that the “Condor Cluster” was 5% to 10% the cost of equivalent systems and required only 10% of the energy,^([24](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr24)) with the cluster used for activities such as radar enhancement, pattern recognition, satellite imagery processing, and artificial intelligence research.^([25](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr25))
