
A prominent example is high dynamic range (HDR) mode, which is available on nearly all smartphones in use today. A photo is assembled out of a series of other photos that are rapidly taken at different exposures, then scanned by a machine learning algorithm that recognizes and separates individual objects and depths, then merges these many inputs together to create a final “version” that has a better balance of highlights and shadows—plus greater detail and clarity. More recently, techniques known as Deep Fusion, Photonic Engines, and optical flow use onboard neural engines to process images on a pixel-by-pixel basis. Computer vision also allows camera applications to accurately and automatically focus on horizon lines or humans, separate foregrounds and backgrounds, alter lighting, reduce blur, and time shots to avoid human blinks and capture the “best” facial expressions.^([\*\*](#chapter12.xhtml#footnote-000)) “Night Mode” isn’t concerned with capturing what the human eye can see but rather what a smartphone’s camera can find despite the scarcity of photons.

The result is not just “better” photos but photos that are better than is possible. Unlike graphical and computing processors, which benefit from ongoing miniaturization in transistors, a process that produces more computing power per unit of space while consuming less battery and generating less heat, photographs cannot improve as light is reduced. This is why smartphone cameras remain so much thicker than the rest of the device’s body, and why smartphones choose to add a second or third camera, instead of “making the first one better.” And in contrast to cloud (or quantum) computing, there is no way for a camera in a remote location to capture more light in support. Remarkably, AI can enable a smartphone application to substitute for the data (i.e., light) it lacks to produce a photo that looks as good as, if not better than, those produced by cameras that are thousands of dollars more expensive, have lenses several times larger, and whose exposure lasts an order of magnitude longer.

## The Meta Frontier

In the decades to come, AI will continue to construct increasingly detailed and multidimensional versions of human minds, identities, histories, and friendships, as well as those of society and the world at large. AI will also be instrumental in building and operating the Metaverse, and these contributions can, at least for now, be simplified into six categories: (1) Enabling a greater number of real-world items and environments to be virtualized, and at higher fidelity levels; (2) Enabling digital twins to perform more complex tasks and simulations, and for human operators to make better use of these capabilities; (3) Enabling a greater number of purely virtual items and worlds to be created, and with greater richness; (4) Enabling many Metaverse experiences to increase their fidelity, frame rate, responsiveness, and consumer usability by “beating” the laws of physics; (5) Enabling the Metaverse to be populated with highly sophisticated and responsive AI agents; and (6) Enabling greater interoperation without requiring fully standardized asset formats or codebases, or otherwise requiring developers and businesses to build a nearly infinite number of permutations for every world and item they create.

### #1: Virtualizing the Real World

In recent years, photo-based neural models have progressed from the 2D memorialization of 3D experiences to reproducing and then maintaining them. Apple’s Object Capture, which debuted in 2021, enables iPhone and iPad users to convert real-world objects into ultrahigh-definition virtual ones by taking a series of photos from different angles. Neural radiance fields, or NeRFs, can convert a few dozen 2D images into highly detailed, comprehensive, and immersive models of rooms, facilities, and even outdoor spaces. In this case, a neural model “fills in” information not contained in input photographs, such as the underside of a table, a chair’s unseen legs, or the knobs on a stove, but can also generate the materials in that image (reflective versus matte wood, say) and their attributes (firmness versus softness). Without these sorts of capabilities, Apple Vision Pro would not be able to offer immersive “memories” unless a user owned a half dozen cameras and staged them around their family gatherings, and it would be both financially and operationally infeasible for most companies to produce, let alone maintain, precise digital twins of the real world. *Microsoft Flight Simulator* is testament to that fact—its 500,000,000-square-kilometer map, 2 trillion unique trees, and 1.5 billion buildings were generated through Microsoft’s machine learning and geospatial computing suites. Most companies don’t need such a model—just a twin of their factory, office tower, or retail store—but they have far fewer employees and profit than Microsoft, too.

### #2: Improving and Using Digital Twins

Not long after the Metaverse hype cycle began in 2021, manufacturers of all kinds rushed to announce they were building their own digital twin—one that would enable both cost savings and productivity improvements, reduce jobsite injuries while growing throughput, revolutionize their industry but play to their existing strengths, and so on. Three years later, there’s little evidence of these achievements and considerably less chatter. Much of the disappointment stems from the fact that it takes humans far longer to learn how to effectively use a digital twin than to construct one (as is always the case with software). Another hindrance is the extent that the facility represented by the twin has sufficient capabilities (i.e., Internet of Things, or IoT, sensors and automation functionality) to make extensive use of this twin. The major constraint, however, is AI.

Unity Technologies describes twins as having five “levels.”^([31](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr31)) Level One twins are little more than a virtual version of a physical miniature model—they reflect a “real-world” space, typically with inconsistent proportions and some limited functionality (e.g., the ability to shift a truck from one stall to another). In some cases, these exist purely for demonstration purposes—intended to prove that a technical group can build such a model or to demonstrate the company’s commitment to “the Metaverse” to shareholders. More useful versions exist to help humans understand and explore a physical space without setting foot inside it, or to plan for that physical space (think back to Strategic Development Partners, Imerza, and the Unreal-based Tampa Water Street project from [Chapter 8](#chapter8.xhtml)). Regardless, the life span of Level One twins is usually short, as they represent a space at a fixed point in time; as that space changes, so does the utility of the model.

Level Two twins are “connected twins.” At this point, a virtual model receives and visualizes data from the real-world environment it represents. That data may be reflected through 3D assets (e.g., a truck may be represented as a 3D model placed in a stall) or labels (a digital placard indicating that a stall is occupied and by which truck) and may be generated through IoT devices and sensors, or submitted by human staff. Level Three twins are “predictive twins,” which are essentially models that use the data captured by connected twins to simulate what could happen, or (better) what is likely to happen, in the future. Such predictions can include predicting bottlenecks on a factory floor, daily productivity, overheating equipment, delays relating to late arrivals, and so on. Under some definitions or implementations, a predictive twin may deploy “AI,” whereas others might just be 3D representations of information that could be represented in a 2D Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.
