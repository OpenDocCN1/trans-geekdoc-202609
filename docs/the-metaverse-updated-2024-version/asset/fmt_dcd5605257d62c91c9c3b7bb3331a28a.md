# [PREFACE TO THE REVISED EDITION](#contents.xhtml#pre1)

Why update this book after only two years? Some numbers help answer this question. Close to 70% of the text in this “revised edition” is new. A quarter of these changes come from wholly new chapters. One example is [Chapter 12](#chapter12.xhtml), which focuses on artificial intelligence. In the first edition, AI flowed through the book—and it still does. But the extraordinary and unexpected advances of 2022 and 2023 warranted a dedicated discussion of computer vision, machine learning, and generative transformer models, in addition to the many ways in which they are accelerating and enriching the development of the Metaverse.

[Chapters 10](#chapter10.xhtml) and [11](#chapter11.xhtml), which cover mixed-reality hardware and blockchains, were in the original book, but have been entirely replaced. These fields are in their seventh and second decades respectively, but also saw remarkable evolutions in the span of only 24 months. Apple’s Vision Pro is not only the highest-profile “head-mounted display” since the early prototypes of the 1950s, but Apple’s most significant product launch since 2007’s iPhone. The company is also believed to have spent more than twice as long and 10–15 times as much developing this headset as it did on the iPhone, too, or roughly eight years and $20–$30 billion in total. Facebook has spent more than $60 billion on its suite of mixed-reality devices since 2014 and spent much of 2023 unveiling prototypes of devices that might appear later this decade—some of which more closely resemble the eyes of a dragonfly than a camera, but which help us understand the enormous sums spent on research and development and the role of hundreds of millions of years of evolution in charting humanity’s digital future.

In November 2021, the combined market cap of all publicly traded cryptocurrencies peaked at roughly $3 trillion. In April 2022, the field experienced its largest-ever decline—falling from $2.25 trillion to $825 billion in just ten weeks. That same year, Ethereum seamlessly completed its “merge,” which is one of the blockchain community’s most impressive and important achievements, only to then engage in a “royalties war” that may have upended the technology’s core value propositions. With over $80 billion raised by blockchain start-ups since 2012—only 20% of which was invested prior to 2020—each passing month provides substantially more evidence of blockchain’s feats and failures, as well as its challenges and potential, and the debates in between. By March 2024, nearly two years after crypto’s historic crash, its combined market cap had crept back to $2.6 trillion.

Not all Metaverse technologies were radically altered over the past 24 months, but each one progressed. In 2023, Google unveiled a new quantum computer that was 241 million times more powerful than its 2019 edition. Nvidia, a 30-year-old company that was originally focused on designing graphics processing units for video gaming, has become one of the world’s most important companies due to the aforementioned advances in AI, and its GPUs among the tech world’s most valuable resources. Nvidia’s founder and CEO, Jensen Huang, has predicted the economy of the Metaverse will one day exceed that of the physical world. In 2023, SpaceX’s Starlink launched its 5,650th satellite, with its internet constellation constituting close to two-thirds of all active satellites orbiting the earth. By 2024, Facebook is expected to own or de facto control close to 13% of the global submarine internet backbone, with a particular concentration in the African and Asian-Pacific continents. The leaps in computing and networking ([Chapter 5](#chapter5.xhtml) and [6](#chapter6.xhtml)) change the balance of power in the Metaverse, its likely business model, and global potential.

Interoperability ([Chapter 8](#chapter8.xhtml)), one of the most fraught and unpredictable aspects of the Metaverse’s development, also surged in 2022 and 2023. Epic Games unveiled Verse, which it envisioned as the “programming language of the Metaverse,” alongside its expanded Fortnite ecosystem, including LEGO Fortnite, Rocket Racing, and Fortnite Festival, which led to all-time highs in users, player hours, and revenues for the more than six-year-old title. Two months later, the Walt Disney Company announced a $1.5 billion dollar investment into Epic Games to produce a “new, persistent, and interoperable universe” that would be part of the Fortnite ecosystem, span franchises and brands from Avatar and Star Wars to ESPN and Disney Cruise Line, and enable users to create their own Disneylands and Disney Worlds, and watch and shop for Disney content and products. In parallel, Pixar, Adobe, Apple, Autodesk, and Nvidia, in partnership with the Linux Foundation, Epic Games, and Unity, also launched the OpenUSD format, which Nvidia had proposed as “the HTML of the Metaverse”—a comprehensive framework for describing, composing, simulating, and collaborating within 3D worlds.

Other updates to the book reflect reader feedback and areas of interest. The Metaverse now includes detailed histories of the founding of companies such as Epic Games and Oculus VR, and an examination of why it was that in 2021 the Metaverse began to surge to national headlines ([Chapter 3](#chapter3.xhtml)). [Chapter 4](#chapter4.xhtml), which unpacks my widely cited description of the Metaverse on a keyword-by-keyword basis, has been revamped for clarity and to differentiate between the “minimum” and “full-spec” Metaverse. The first edition of this book had four charts and exhibits; this revision has more than ten times as many.

The re-release also comes at a very different time in the macroeconomic cycle. Beginning in late 2022, interest rates rose faster than at any point since the dawn of the internet and to levels higher than at any point since the debuts of Amazon Web Services in 2002 and the iPhone in 2007—two events which are considered the starts of the cloud and mobile computing eras. Increases in the cost of capital and a squeeze on profits led many companies to reduce their Metaverse investments and focus instead on nearer-term business opportunities. This narrative shift has led to increased skepticism toward the Metaverse—and prompted many of the companies which once boasted the term and its ideas to substitute it for other decades-old buzzwords, such as “immersive” or “spatial.” Irrespective of the words used or sentiment, the trends are clear. The number of virtual worlds made each year, the number of people inside them as well as the money and time they spend there, and the cultural influence of those spaces all continue to grow. The deployment of real-time 3D-simulation and game-engine technology also continues to grow, as have their capabilities and business applications. These tools are increasingly used in healthcare, industrial design, civic management, automotive, aerospace, and artificial intelligence—to entertain and to improve lives and even save them. The Metaverse is a 100-year-old idea and 30-year-old word; no matter what it’s called, it gets nearer and clearer each day.

# [INTRODUCTION](#contents.xhtml#int1)

Technology frequently produces surprises that no one predicts. But the biggest and most fantastical developments are often anticipated decades in advance. In the 1930s, Vannevar Bush, then president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington, began work on a hypothetical electromechanical device that would store all books, records, and communications, and mechanically link them together by keyword association, rather than traditional, mostly hierarchical, storage models. Despite the enormity of its archive, Bush stressed that this “Memex” (short for “memory extender”) could be consulted “with exceeding speed and flexibility.”
