
Lastly, there is the “work” that these devices must do, which far exceeds that asked of a console. One baseline requirement of HMDs is to preemptively warn the user of key boundaries and dangers around them—including hard-to-parse reflective or transparent surfaces. This means that the device must understand the environment around it, not just see it. AR and MR devices must go further. They’re expected to understand not just the placement and shape of objects but also what they are, what they’re doing, what they’re likely to do, their relevance to the task at hand, and potentially detailed attributes such as their materials and composition. An HMD must also track the user’s hands, eyebrows, lips, and other body parts both precisely and quickly and reproduce them in real time inside a virtual environment. Often, these devices are asked to support video-calling—that is, displaying “real-world” video inside a simulated environment—or to reproduce the screen of a “real-world” device, such as a PC or iPhone, inside the virtual environment. Consoles, of course, are asked to do none of this. Instead, these devices can focus their relatively abundant computing power on “graphics.”

Then there are the graphical requirements of HMDs themselves. Due to their higher resolution, HMDs must render at least 11 and as many as 22 times as many pixels per second as the Nintendo Switch and up to 4 times as many as modern PlayStations and Xboxes. Yet the number of pixels is only half of the equation. The other half is how frequently they are updated. Most video games target 60 frames per second (technically, a 60 Hz display). Some titles will support 120 fps or more, though this typically means reducing the resolution in exchange. So `Fortnite` supports 4K in 60 fps, but if a player wants 120 fps (which helps with competitive play), the resolution drops to 1440p. On the Nintendo Switch, a relatively low-powered device, `Fortnite` maxes out at 30 fps. Games with particularly complex simulations are sometimes capped at 30 fps even on the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series S|X.

To avoid nausea, there is broad consensus that an XR headset must refresh at 120 fps, if not more.^([32](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr32)) This minimum threshold primarily stems from the movement of a user’s head and body as an input, which both activates the user’s inner ear (where balance is regulated) and makes the lag between action and response perceptible. But by increasing frames per second from 60 to 120 or 240, the total number of pixels that must be rendered by an HMD versus modern video game consoles grows by another two to four times—a massive burden on scarce battery life and computing power. The need to produce frames every 8 or even 4 milliseconds also reveals again the impracticality of cloud-based rendering, at least under today’s networking standards; even 20 ms of latency—a level that isn’t even discernible to six out of seven expert gamers—would result in three to five outdated frames.

Passthrough experiences face an additional computational challenge: the need to create the video that is passed through. Though it’s common for an HMD user to assume they are just staring through their device’s front-facing cameras, these video feeds must first be algorithmically assembled, adjusted, and corrected to account for the fact that these cameras are set tens of millimeters in front of the user’s eyes (a placement the brain struggles to appreciate) and often below or to the side, too (an even stranger placement that also results in light entering an artificial lens at an unfamiliar angle). The device must also work to remove visual artifacts and distortions caused by dirt or dust in front of a lens, “lens flare” from bright light directly in front of the camera, and sudden changes in brightness. And all of this must happen within milliseconds—ideally 20, if not fewer—otherwise users are likely to experience nausea and occasionally miss the real-world items they are trying to grab because their device is showing them frames that reflect where their eyes were, not where the brain knows they should now be.

## The Hardest Technology Challenge of Our Time

With a better understanding of what it takes to build a mass-market HMD, a few summarizing points can be made. First, HMDs face more technical trade-offs than almost any other kind of computing—there’s a weight budget and a heat budget, a minimum viable battery life and device price, the compute needed for pixels and that required to run software, and so on. In 2021, Mark Zuckerberg wrote that “the hardest technology challenge of our time may be fitting a supercomputer into the frame of normal-looking glasses.”^([33](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr33)) At the time, Zuckerberg’s forecast for widespread adoption of AR was already years behind schedule—and he would subsequently delay the release of the company’s first AR device at least three more. Second, the complexity and constraints facing these devices explain why the (exaggerated) criticism that, say, the graphics of the 2020 Quest 2 look like those of a 2007 Nintendo Wii, is actually a compliment—it’s a bit like saying that an adult ran 100 meters as fast as a 12-year-old, even though the adult was wearing a 50-pound backpack and solving math problems at the same time. Overall, “graphics” is a terribly unhelpful term in the first place because it refers to visuals rather than the simulation they reflect, let alone the other demands on a CPU’s or GPU’s finite power or the need to preserve battery life. Similarly, the aforementioned trade-offs mean that progress in these devices is not always obvious to users. A 10% reduction in weight or heat, a 10% improvement in battery life, a 10% improvement in eye tracking are all significant, yet hard for the average person to appreciate unless they use the device regularly and for extended periods of time.
