
In May of 2021, Google announced Project Starline, the company’s efforts to reinvent the stodgy telepresence booth using photorealistic 3D models and machine learning software. The “magic” of traditional telepresence hardware, which first emerged in the early 1990s, was rooted in hardware and context. A corporate executive would feel as though their peers were sitting across from them in the boardroom because they (as well as their peers) were staring at a series of life-size monitors stationed on the other side of a circular table that had been cut in half. Moreover, the camera trained on each participant would be angled according to their placement on-screen (e.g., someone on the far right screen would sit to the left of the camera pointed at them, rather than head-on). As with its spiritual predecessors, Starline depended on hardware—in this case, a dozen depth sensors and cameras (together producing seven video streams from four viewpoints and three depth maps), four spatial audio speakers, and a fabric-based, multilayered light-field display. These many components, mixed with Google’s suite of computer vision and machine learning AI technologies, enabled participants to be captured and then rendered using volumetric data, rather than flattened 2D video.^([62](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr62)) In effect, viewers were looking at a live 3D model (or a “photorealistic avatar”) of their family member, friend, or colleague, rather than 30 still images played per second. Though Google did not make Starline publicly available, the company’s internal research boasted about Starline’s improvements over traditional video calls. Starline users focused 15% more on those they were speaking to (based on eye-tracking data), displayed significantly greater nonverbal forms of communication (e.g., ~40% more hand gestures, ~25% more head nods, and ~50% more eyebrow movements), and had 30% increases in memory recall when asked to remember details from their conversation or meeting.^([63](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr63)) In testing against the peer-reviewed Zoom Exhaustion & Fatigue Scale, Google found Starline users had 31% lower fatigue compared to those on traditional 2D calls, and exhibited 12% faster response times when completing cognitive tasks after the call.^([64](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr64)) For those skeptical of why the internet needs broad 3D, or who have found Zoom, Microsoft Teams, or Google Meets exhausting or alienating, Starline offers one answer.

### Human Culture and Pastimes

While simulation technologies and devices increased in number and effectiveness, so too did the cultural embrace of 3D environments and socialization. As the world moved on from the COVID-19 pandemic in 2022, supposed “pandemic darlings” such as Zoom, Shopify, and Peloton began to shrink. Although many had predicted their categories were forever “pulled forward” by the global lockdowns of 2020 and 2021, this acceleration was illusory. In 2019, e-commerce constituted roughly 10% of total US retail spend and 15% of addressable retail spend. This share had grown in a fairly linear fashion from approximately 3.5% and 6% since 2009, gaining an average of 0.65 and 0.9 percentage points annually. In 2020, those sums surged to about 17% and 23% (+7 percentage points and +8 percentage points year over year). By 2023, however, e-commerce’s share was well below the level experienced in both 2020 and 2021, with roughly 15.5% and 22% (representing a decline of 1.5 and 1 percentage points over a three-year period). Furthermore, e-commerce’s share of retail sales in 2023 was almost exactly where pre-COVID trendlines would have predicted.^([65](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr65)) Digital’s share of ad spend looks similar. `Roblox`, in contrast, continued to see new highs. Every quarter since the pandemic, `Roblox` exhibited year-over-year (if not quarter-over-quarter) increases in users and usage. Moreover, `Roblox` was adding more users and user hours each quarter than ever before.

It’s likely that `Roblox` experienced smaller-scale pullbacks as the pandemic eased. Some users probably played less frequently, or for less time, or stopped playing altogether. Yet the platform was benefiting from generational changes, too. Each year, 140 million people globally turn nine, entering the `Roblox` demographic for the first time. This generational shift, which e-commerce began to experience in the 1990s, also helps to explain why `Roblox`, a platform that launched in 2006, took a decade to take off.

Think back to the first two years following the release of the iPad. As the device’s sales grew, it became common to see press reports and viral YouTube videos of infants and young children who would pick up an “analog” magazine or book and try to “swipe” its nonexistent touchscreen. Today, those one-year-olds are thirteen to fourteen. A four-year-old in 2010 is well on her way to adulthood. These media consumers are now spending their own money on content—and some are already creating content themselves. And while these once unintelligible young people now understand why adults found their futile efforts to pinch-to-zoom a piece of paper so comic, older generations are not much closer to understanding how the worldviews and preferences of the young differ from their own. Most adults look at `Roblox` and see an uninteresting and rudimentary “game.” Kids see something much different: a milieu. Just as prior generations grew up talking on the phone for hours after school and loitering in malls, or on text chats and message boards or, later still, social media, younger Gen Zs and all of Gen Alpha look to platforms such as `Roblox` to socialize and make friends. Certainly, `Roblox` benefited from various technological improvements since its release, not the least of which was the launch of consumer-friendly portable computers, but it’s no coincidence that `Roblox`’s core users are the very children who grew up “iPad native” and expect everything they touch to be fully interactive. Fortunately for `Roblox`, this cohort grows by nearly 400,000 people every day.

Although many Metaverse pioneers believe blockchain and cryptocurrency technology have no place in the Metaverse (and potentially no future at all), the growth in these technologies during 2020, 2021, and 2022 reinforced society’s embrace of virtual existence.
