
Level Four twins are “prescriptive twins.” Here, AI makes recommendations as extensive as how a facility should be organized, prioritized, scheduled, or staffed; how to optimize its use of materials; and how to triage burgeoning problems like late shipment arrivals, slow unloading processes, mechanical malfunctions, and so on. It is at Level Four that the twin shifts from exclusively supporting human work to guiding it. The final level is an “autonomous twin,” which involves the AI in Level Four being provided with the ability to partly “run” the facility. In this case, the AI might dynamically assign parking stalls, alter production flows, or preemptively cool rooms based on the knowledge of how many people will eventually be in it, for how long, and doing what activity. There is a parallel continuum that is not mentioned in Unity’s framework but applies to each of its upper four levels: integration into other digital twins. These outside twins might represent the operations of the city in which the twin is situated, the productivity (vehicular fleets) of the twin’s customers or suppliers, global meteorological conditions, and so on, all of which can aid prediction, prescription, and autonomous decision-making.

The value of a digital twin increases with each level, and as it increases, so does the number of outside twins it connects to and the degree to which it does so. These augmentations also substantially increase the reliance upon AI. Without AI, Level Five can’t be achieved, only rudimentary versions of Levels Four and Three can be attained, and integration into other twins quickly becomes impractical. Human operators simply cannot process as many decisions as reliably and accurately as a computer system, nor can they as quickly translate these decisions into actions. Still, the minimum threshold for AI is high. Beyond optimizing a facility’s operations, AI must be able to minimize the risk of human injury (and intervene when necessary), effectively explain a decision to humans in real time, and respond to human suggestions and requests, too.

### #3: Building Out the (Purely) Virtual World

The need for AI is also apparent when it comes to creating synthetic worlds that are entirely separate from the “real world.” With nearly six million virtual worlds made on `Roblox` annually, alongside the millions more created on `Minecraft` and `Fortnite`, it’s clear that the desire to create such spaces is widespread. Yet most of these worlds are basic in their functionality, detail, and scope and fall far short of the imaginations and aspirations of their creators. All great art, to be sure, requires extraordinary investments in training, experience, and, frankly, mental anguish. But the underlying elements of a virtual world are not as easily abstracted as a note, word, image, or number, nor as easily produced individually or assembled into a chord, sentence, picture, or spreadsheet. Moreover, the creation of a virtual world requires each of these other art forms—there is no point visiting a virtual world that has no music, imagery, or operations. Advances in generative AI, machine learning, computer vision, and semantic recognition will soon enable users to create, modify, and run entire virtual worlds in a matter of seconds and minutes using nothing but a few typed or spoken sentences (David Baszucki, Roblox’s founder and CEO, estimates that such functionality will arrive before 2028^([32](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr32))). The result will be an increase in the number of worlds in the Metaverse, and their quality will spike too.

AI’s ability to autonomously operate within the guardrails of a creator will enable further innovations. Hello Games’ 2016 title `No Man’s Sky` allows users to traverse a nearly infinite universe with up to 18 quadrillion unique planets, each one fully filled with its own geography, flora and fauna that can be named and harvested, and potentially the operating bases of other players. This scale—or anything remotely near it—is achieved only through procedural content generation that is capable of both generating incredible diversity and ensuring compatibility and coherence across these creations.

### #4: “Beating” the Laws of Physics

As detailed throughout this book, the construction and operation of the Metaverse is in constant competition with the actual universe (at least as it’s known today). Yet using AI, it is possible to perform tasks faster than physics would allow, or using less equipment than physics might require, or to higher fidelity, and so on.

Take Amazon’s much-touted “Just Walk Out” (JWO) technology as an example. Using a network of cameras (as many as 300 per 1,000 square feet) and sensors, powered by AWS’s computer vision and machine learning suites, Amazon is able to track which items users, as they wander around its grocery and convenience stores, pick up and hold on to or pick up and put back down again—and then charge the customers without ever needing them to “check out” or scan their items.^([33](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr33)) Crucially, its JWO did this without reliance on any facial scanning or biometric user data.^([34](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr34)) Instead, JWO tracked customers based purely on their body size, gait, and paths.^([35](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr35)) When it launched the first Amazon Go store in December 2016, Amazon reportedly hoped that as many as 2,000 stores would be using its JWO tools by 2026, including independent licensors of its technology.^([36](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr36)) In 2018, Bloomberg claimed that Amazon’s target had increased to 3,000 by 2021.^([37](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr37)) But fully two years after that target, JWO was being used in only 60 or so Amazon-owned stores, some of which the company announced would be closing.^([38](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr38)) It turned out that Amazon’s computer vision technology was costly to deploy and still required substantial support from human staff. The Information reported that there were as many as “1,000 people in India working on Just Walk Out as of mid-2022 whose jobs included manually reviewing transactions”—with customer bills featuring appropriate adjustments—“and labeling images from videos to train Just Walk Out’s machine learning model.”^([39](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr39)) In 2024, Amazon announced that newly constructed Amazon Fresh grocery stores would not offer JWO and that most of the company’s existing locations would be overhauled to “version two” stores that replaced JWO with “Dash Carts” that enabled customers to scan their grocery items for purchase as these items were placed into their shopping cart.^([40](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr40)) Amazon’s smaller, convenience store-styled Amazon Go stores were expected to keep JWO.

Humans may not need JWO for grocery shopping, but such technologies are essential for factories and plants such as those used to assemble cars or produce laundry detergent pods. To do so, computer vision will need to improve in accuracy, even as it increases the size of the environment it’s tracking, but without simply increasing the density and quality of the cameras and sensors that support the facility. Starline, as reviewed in [`Chapter 3`](#chapter3.xhtml), shows what’s possible. By leveraging two years of advances in AI, Google was able to maintain Starline’s image quality and fidelity, even as it substituted 12 high-end cameras and sensors with just a “few standard”—a change that not only substantially dropped the cost of the device, it shrunk it from the size of a restaurant booth to that of a midsize TV sitting on a minifridge.
