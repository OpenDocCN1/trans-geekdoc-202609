
Even after its fifth anniversary, the Epic Games Store’s path to profitability is hard to see. The company’s August 2020 profit-and-loss statement, leaked as part of its trial against Apple, revealed that Epic did not expect EGS to break even until 2027 at the earliest.^([25](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr25)) What’s more, EGS’s publicly reported gross revenues for 2020, 2021, 2022, and 2023 have subsequently fallen short of August 2020 forecasts. In addition, many top game publishers only selectively support the store (Take-Two’s catalog titles `Bioshock` and `GTA` are available, but not the annually updated `NBA 2K` series), while others skip it altogether (Activision Blizzard won’t even distribute its own store launcher through EGS). Without access to many of the most revenue-generative titles in gaming, revenues will be even harder to come by.

One could argue that because PCs are an open platform, no store can have a monopoly—and notably, the dominant online game distributor is independent from both Microsoft and Apple, which run the Windows and Mac operating systems and offer their own stores. At the same time, it’s telling that there is only one major profitable store, and its biggest suppliers struggle to exist outside of it. Few should consider this a healthy outcome, especially at a 30% fee or even 20% fee. This is because, as always, payments are a bundle that spans not just the processing of a transaction but also a user’s online existence, including their storage locker, their friendships, and their memories—in other words, a developer’s deepest obligation to their oldest customers.

## From Pac-Man to iPod

While `Pac-Man` cartridges, Steam MFNs, and `Call of Duty` exclusivity may seem of little consequence to the Metaverse, they serve as its economic precedent—and that of the digital era overall.

In 2001, Steve Jobs introduced digital distribution to most of the world through the iTunes music store. For his business model, he chose to emulate the 30% commission commanded by Nintendo and the rest of the gaming industry (though unlike consoles, the iPod itself had gross margins above 50%, not below 0%). Seven years later, this 30% was transposed to the iPhone’s app store, with Google quickly following suit for its Android operating system.

Jobs also decided, at this point, to adopt the closed software model that was used by the console platforms but had not been previously used by Apple’s Mac laptops and computers, or its iPod.^([†](#chapter10.xhtml#footnote-012)) On iOS, all software and content would need to be downloaded from Apple’s App Store, and as with PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo, and Steam, only Apple had a say over what software could be distributed and how users would be billed.

Google took a more permissive approach with Android, which technically allowed users to install apps without using the Google Play Store—and without third-party app stores. But this required users to navigate deep into their account settings, and grant permission for individual applications (Chrome, Facebook, or the mobile Epic Games Store) to install “unknown apps,” while warning users this made their “phone and personal data more vulnerable to attack” and forcing them to agree “that you are responsible for any damage to your phone or loss of data that may result from their use.” Although Google did not take responsibility for any damage or loss of data resulting from the use of apps distributed by its Google Play Store, the additional steps and warnings meant that while most PC users downloaded software directly from its maker, such as Microsoft Office from Microsoft.com or Spotify from Spotify.com, almost no one did on Android.

It took more than a decade for the problems associated with the proprietary app store model employed by Apple and, in a different way, by Google, to surface on the global stage. In June 2020, the European Union sued Apple, after Spotify and Rakuten, two streaming media companies, alleged Apple used its fees to benefit its own software services (such as Apple Music) and stifle competitors. Two months later, Epic Games sued both Apple and Google, alleging their 30% fees and controls were unlawful and anti-competitive. A week before the suit, Sweeney tweeted that “Apple has outlawed the metaverse.”^([26](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr26))

There are several interconnected reasons why it took so long for Apple’s App Store to receive scrutiny from competitors and governments. One was the unequal impact of Apple’s store policies, which primarily charged “new economy” businesses and waived fees on old economy ones. Early on, Apple had established three broad categories of apps when it came to in-app purchases (IAPs). The first category concerned apps which were used to purchase a physical product, such as buying Dove soap from Amazon or loading a Starbucks gift card. Here, Apple took no commission from a sale and even allowed these apps to directly use third-party payment rails to complete a transaction, such as PayPal or Visa, rather than Apple Pay or iTunes billing.

The second category was so-called reader apps, which included services that bundle nontransactional content (all-you-can-eat Netflix, `New York Times`, or Spotify subscription), or that allow a user to view content they previously purchased and could not alter, such as a movie rental that had been previously purchased on Amazon.com. If a reader app wanted to offer in-app payments, they were not able to use services such as Stripe, Square, PayPal, Visa, and so on. The only option was Apple’s iTunes billing, which took a 30% commission. If IAP was not supported, users would need to make all app-related payments via web browser or using the developer’s app on another platform (e.g., on a PC, Mac, PlayStation, etc.). However, Apple prohibited developers from telling users about these payment pathways from inside the app, or directing them to this pathway by, as an example, launching a web browser from within an iOS app. As a result, users would need to independently figure out (or already know) how to make a transaction, then do so. These obstacles typically led to missed sales for the developer, and required additional marketing investment, too. Still, a few considered the trade-off worthwhile to avoid Apple’s 30% commission and instead pay as little as 2.0%–3.5% to credit card and mobile payment networks—especially when the user’s payment information was used for a recurring subscription with a predictable “lifetime value” in the hundreds of millions, rather than a one-off transaction of a few dollars. Due to the cost savings, most developers who support Apple’s IAPs also offer their own billing solution, but user adoption is low due to the comparative difficulty of completing a transaction through that pathway (should they even know it exists). To improve adoption, developers will frequently offer substantial discounts compared to IAP prices, funded in part by the aforementioned 26.5%–28% savings, but again, Apple prohibited such information from being communicated in the app, or from developers running marketing campaigns which explicitly highlighted price differences. These constraints, in turn, forced developers to further decrease their web-based pricing and, in doing so, give up more of the supposed benefits from that channel.
