
In 2023, Facebook unveiled another prototype, Flamera, that was designed to improve passthrough video quality and reduce the frequency of distortions by using “bug eyes” that split incoming light beams into 106 apertures before they reached the device’s two light sensors. According to the company, this segmentation approach reduced visual artifacts and produced a more accurate passthrough video feed.^([70](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr70))

## Beyond Headsets

Alongside the many investments in immersive headsets are countless other efforts to produce new Metaverse-focused hardware that will complement primary computing devices, rather than substitute for them, as some imagine AR and VR devices might one day.

Most commonly, gamers imagine wearing smart gloves and even bodysuits that can provide physical or “haptic” feedback to simulate what’s happening to their avatar in a virtual world. Many such devices exist today, but they’re so costly and functionally limited that they are typically used for industrial purposes. Specifically, these wearables use a network of motors and electroactive actuators that inflate tiny air pockets which apply pressure on their owner’s skin or limit their ability to move.

Haptic vibration technology has advanced considerably since Nintendo introduced the Rumble Pak for the Nintendo 64 in 1997. A PlayStation 5 controller’s triggers can be programmed with context-specific resistance—a shotgun, sniper rifle, and crossbow will all feel different to “pull.” The crossbow might even fight back, with the user struggling to hold it down and sensing the vibrations of a virtual bowstring.

Another class of haptic interface devices emit ultrasonic sound (that is, mechanical energy waves beyond the audible range for humans) from a grid of microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), producing what a user might describe as a “force field” in the air in front of them. The force field produced by these devices, which looks a bit like a short, perforated tin box, is typically less than six or eight inches tall and wide, but its nuance tends to surprise. Test subjects claim to be able to sense everything from a plush teddy bear to a bowling ball and the shape of a sandcastle as it crumbles, aided in part by the fact that fingertips contain more nerve endings than almost any other part of the body. Crucially, MEMS devices can also detect the user’s interaction, enabling its sound-based teddy bears to respond to the user’s air-based touch, or the castle to crumble if it’s touched.

Gloves and bodysuits can also be used to capture a user’s motion data, rather than just relay feedback, thereby allowing the wearer’s body and gestures to be reproduced in a virtual environment in real time. This information can also be captured using tracking cameras. The problem is, such cameras require unobstructed views and relative proximity to the user, and they are prone to struggle if required to track more than a single user in rich detail. Many users—families, for instance—will want multiple tracking cameras in their “Metaverse rooms” and might add a few smart wearables to their wrists or ankles.

Such bands may seem clumsy—how could a bracelet or anklet substitute for a high-definition camera watching every finger?—but the current technology is in fact impressive. The sensors in an Apple Watch, remarkably, can distinguish between a user clenching or releasing their fist, or pinching one finger versus two fingers to their thumb, and the watch owner can use these movements to interact with the Apple Watch (and potentially other devices) without ever needing to touch or even look at them. In addition, people wearing the device can use the clenching motion to place a cursor on the watch face, then use the orientation of their hand to move it. The software involved, Apple’s AssistiveTouch, works using mostly standard sensors, including an electric heart monitor, a gyroscope, and an accelerometer. Other approaches promise even greater capabilities. Facebook’s most expensive acquisition since Oculus VR in 2014 was CTRL-labs, a neural interface start-up that produces armbands that record electrical activity from skeletal muscles (a technique called electromyography). Although the CTRL-labs devices are worn more than six inches away from the wrist and even farther away from the fingers, the CTRL-labs software enables minute gestures to be reproduced inside virtual worlds; individual fingers can be raised to count, point, or gesture to “come hither,” and pinches involving different combinations of fingers can accomplish various microtasks. Importantly, electromyographic signals on the CTRL-labs devices can go beyond reproducing human appendages. A famous CTRL-labs demo involves a user—in this case, an employee—mapping their fingers to a crab-like robot and then walking it forward, back, and side to side by flexing their fist and moving their fingers.^([71](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr71))

Facebook is also planning its own line of smartwatches. But unlike Apple, Facebook doesn’t see the device as secondary to, or dependent upon, a smartphone. Instead, Facebook’s watch is intended to have its own wireless data plan and include two cameras, both of which are detachable and intended to be integrated into third-party items, such as a backpack or a hat. Meanwhile, Google’s fifth-largest acquisition ever was the smart wearables company Fitbit in early 2021.

Wearables will shrink in size and increase in performance, and as their technologies improve, they will be integrated into everyday clothes. These developments will help users enhance their interactions with the Metaverse and enable them to interact with it in more places. Carrying a controller everywhere is not practical, and if the primary goal of AR is to make technology disappear into an everyday pair of glasses, then pulling out a thumb-stick or a smartphone to use it would defeat the purpose.

Although patent applications are an unreliable predictor of the future, a pair of December 2022 filings published by the US Patent and Trademark Office showed that Apple was working on a wearable smart ring capable of “self-mixing interferometry.” The field of interferometry uses interference of intersecting light rays to detect changes in the state of an object or environment. Apple imagined a ring with inward-facing sensors (and potentially a light emitter) that could be used to sense the wearer’s “physiological condition” by “\[detecting\] and/or \[monitoring\] … heart rates, blood oxygen saturation, blood pressure, sleep cycles, body temperatures” and/or “detect force and/or touch inputs, whether static or motions, speed, direction, force, displacement or other parameters of gestures.”^([72](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr72)) The second filing imagines such a ring being used to help “variously sense the presence of objects, distances to objects or proximities of objects, movements of objects (e.g., whether objects are moving, or the speed, acceleration, or direction of movement of objects) and so on” for AR, VR, and MR applications.^([73](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr73))
