
### The Next Internet

The simplest way to describe the Metaverse is as a 3D version of the internet. Whereas Vint Cerf, one of the “fathers of the internet”; Sir Tim Berners-Lee, the inventor of the World Wide Web; and Vannevar Bush, whose Memex inspired HyperText Transfer Protocol, all imagined the interconnection of networks, devices, and documents, a “3D internet” enables interconnection of virtual worlds.

Although there are many virtual worlds on the internet today, they are precisely that—applications on the internet. These worlds, as well as the billions of users fragmented across them, cannot exchange much information or even locate one another. Instead, they operate like a hotel whose rooms can call only other rooms (and if they’re lucky, maybe sister hotels). Or contrast today’s “internet” with the first mainframe computer networks, which emerged in the late 1940s, or even the first “network of networks” that was built and deployed across the Defense Department’s Advanced Research Projects Agency (commonly known as DARPA). These early networks are distinguished from “the internet” because the internet connected nearly all devices and networks globally. It’s for this reason that internet users do not worry about whether one email account can reach another, or need to check that they can link to a specific webpage, or wonder whether a given web browser can load a given page, an internet-enabled device can technically connect to a given network, or an American-based productivity solution can send out automated email alerts based on an online database hosted in Germany. The internet reaches and connects nearly all digital life.

Those building the Metaverse aim to extend this functionality into three-dimensional virtual worlds and spatial systems. Users would be able to seamlessly jump from virtual world to virtual world, whether from a science class at Harvard into a lecture run by Yale, from the AR experience inside a shopping mall into each of its stores and then the public park outside, from the planet Hoth on Disney+ into a fitness class on Peloton. Each of these worlds, meanwhile, would support and interact with the others’ avatars and virtual goods, and integrate into shared payment systems. Industrial simulations, too, would be capable of widespread and frictionless integrations. A self-driving Tesla could access the 3D mapping data of an underground parking lot, while exchanging spatial data from the other cars in the lot. Alongside city services such as electricity, water and sewage, police, fire, and ambulance would be a “virtual platform” that real estate developers, business operators, and civilians could access and build upon in daily life.

The purpose of this chapter is not to explain those use cases, nor their benefits, but instead to explain why the current internet does not properly enable them. To return to the hotel example: individual rooms can call phones outside the hotel not just because the building’s internal telephone network connects to that of the outside world, but because there is a global organizational hierarchy and numbering system for devices that support phone calls. For the internet, this framework is managed by ICANN, which assigns IP and network addresses, as well as manages the Domain Name System (DNS) that makes it easy for a user to contact these addresses. No such structures exist for virtual worlds or spaces on the internet.

Beyond being able to identify, organize, and contact virtual worlds, there need to be common ways of expressing and recognizing this information. Whereas the 2D web has myriad standardized file formats, such as JPEG for images or MP3 for audio, 3D and spatial information lack equivalent standards. Though there’s reason for optimism given the extent to which real-world documents have been digitized, it’s far harder to digitize the real world itself. Developers will need to describe what an object looks like, how and where it’s positioned, and its material composition, surface texture, temperature, purpose, activities, and more. There must also be consistent ways of designing and interpreting these virtual worlds. A JPEG, after all, is part of a website, not the website itself, and the World Wide Web has no “template.” Accordingly, almost all websites are built using HTML, a markup (not programming) language that tells a browser how to display the page’s content—including the color, font, and arrangement of its text, the orientation and dimensions of images, and its many hyperlink connections to the rest of the web. Without similar models for the Metaverse, its worlds and objects are just “data” that’s intelligible only to those who created it.

Other limitations stem not from what has yet to be established but from that which was. Though the internet’s founders envisioned many potential use cases—and were familiar with much of the science fiction work of Weinbaum, Dick, Asimov, Gibson, et al.—they were devising its protocols at a time when there was no worldwide data network and no agreement on whether it was even possible, let alone how it might work best. Moreover, delivery of any digital information across any network was unreliable, costly, and slow. And so the internet was built around a simple use case: asynchronously transmitting a copy of one file from one device to another device, and involving only the networks in between those two devices. Decades later, the internet has far exceeded this initial objective, but it remains constrained by it.

When logging on to the internet, the user is engaging in a series of short connections to outside servers—just long enough to download the requisite information, after which the interconnection is severed. Modern terminology reflects this—users say they “load” the `New York Times` homepage, not “connect to” the `New York Times`. Even when a user spends a half hour browsing the site, the process is more like leaving a string of voicemails with the `New York Times` server than carrying out a conversation. The user “calls” the `New York Times`, connects to their voicemail, leaves a message saying “Please send me the homepage,” then disconnects. Shortly thereafter, the `New York Times` calls the user back, and details the homepage on their voicemail. The same process applies to nearly all applications, even those that seem live. When browsing a “live feed” on the `New York Times` or Facebook, the initial page loaded includes code that instructs a user’s web browser or app to reconnect to the `New York Times` or Facebook server for an update after a predefined period of time, such as 3 or 30 seconds in the future. Each of these reconnections might travel a different route (i.e., over different intermediary networks) and thus travel at different speeds and over different distances, and is partly held up by the need to initiate a handshake of sorts. The result is that nearly all of the content viewed on the internet is “old” in one way or another. Two users sitting side by side in a coffee shop who load the `New York Times` a second apart may see entirely different homepages, photos, and/or headlines.
