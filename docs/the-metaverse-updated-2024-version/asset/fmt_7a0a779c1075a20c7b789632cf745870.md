
Think back to the example of the blockbuster film *Monsters University* from [Chapter 4](#chapter4.xhtml). To render the 2013 production, Pixar built a supercomputer comprising 2,000 conjoined industrial-grade computers with a combined 24,000 cores.^([1](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr1)) As each of the film’s 120,000 frames took roughly 30 core hours to render, Pixar’s supercomputer, which cost tens of millions of dollars to assemble, could squeeze rendering time down to about five seconds per frame—if the entire computer was assigned to that single frame.^([\*](#chapter6.xhtml#footnote-031)) Despite the enormous investment in *Monsters University*’s data center, Pixar replaced many of these computers and cores with newer and more capable processors over the following years. But rather than use this power to render frames more quickly, Pixar opted for more sophisticated renders. Consider that one scene in the studio’s 2017 film *Coco* had nearly eight million individual lights in the background, which required more than 1,000 core hours per frame to render. Eventually, Pixar opted to simplify the rendering process by “baking” a number of lights in 20-degree longitudinal and latitudinal increments—that is, reducing their responsiveness to the camera^([2](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr2))—which helped reduce rendering times to 55 hours per frame.

Another example is offered by the swelling physical size of video game consoles. The first Sony PlayStation, released in 1994, weighed 3.2 pounds (1,450 grams) and measured 10.75 inches by 7.5 inches by 2.5 inches (resulting in a volume of 202 cubic inches). In 2020, it was possible to manufacture a device as powerful as the original PlayStation for less than 5% of the inflation-adjusted cost and 3% of the size.^([3](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr3)) Instead, Sony’s fifth-generation PlayStation weighed 210% more than the first (i.e., 9.9 pounds, or 4,500 grams) and was 220% larger (though it was also 15% cheaper on an inflation-adjusted basis). Most of this increase related to the decision to place more computing power in the device—and larger fans for its high-end CPUs and GPUs as they performed their calculations. These investments enabled substantial improvements in graphical realism (e.g., light scattering and reflections) coupled with a doubling in frame rates and a nearly 30-fold increase in maximum resolutions. Excitingly, the most important improvements were gameplay related.

By the start of the 2020s, many of the most popular games in the world were those that supported unstructured, sandbox-style play such as *Roblox* and *Minecraft* and/or high levels of concurrent users, such as *Roblox*, *Fortnite*, and *Call of Duty: Warzone*. While these titles are incredible creative achievements, their underlying ideas are far from new. For decades, developers have imagined experiences with dozens of live players (if not hundreds or thousands) in a single, shared simulation and heretofore-unexplored virtual environments limited only by the imagination of the individual user. The issue was that these capabilities were not possible without enormous constraints, many of which were too great for the average consumer.

While virtual worlds such as *EVE Online* had included hundreds or even thousands of concurrent users (CCUs) since the late 1990s, individual players could not congregate via avatars. Instead, users directed large and mostly static ships that would relocate in space or exchange artillery fire. Dozens of *World of Warcraft* avatars can be rendered in the same place, but model detail is limited, the perspective is relatively zoomed out, and players have limited control over what each avatar can do. And if too many players have converged on a single area, the game’s server would temporarily “shard” it into concurrently operating but independent copies of that space. Some games even chose to limit real-time rendering to individual players and select in-game AI, with the entire background pre-rendered and thus impossible for players to affect. Engaging in any of these experiences also required a player to buy a dedicated gaming PC, which could run into the thousands of dollars. Even if such a device wasn’t strictly necessary, a user likely had to “turn off” or “turn down” the game’s rendering capability or halve the frame rate.

It was only by the mid-2010s that millions of consumer-grade devices could manage a game like *Fortnite*—one with dozens of richly animated avatars in a single match, each one capable of a wide range of actions, and interacting in a vivid and tangible world, rather than the cold vastness of space. It was around this same time that enough affordable servers were available that could manage and synchronize the inputs coming from so many devices.

Not only did these computational advancements lead to new or newly popular game genres, but the video gaming industry quickly expanded into the sorts of media experiences that were previously “IRL only,” such as concerts, and appealed to those who had historically shown little interest in games. By the end of 2023, *Roblox* had an estimated 355 million monthly active users^([4](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr4))—more than the entirety of PlayStation, Xbox, and Nintendo combined, and half as many as Snap.

But as mentioned in [Chapter 4](#chapter4.xhtml), these games feel so ahead of their time in part because of specific design decisions that allow them to work around current computational constraints. Most battle royales support 100 players, but they also use enormous maps with numerous “points of interest” to scatter players far from one another. This means that while the server needs to track what all the players are doing, each player’s device doesn’t need to render them or track or process their actions. And while players must ultimately converge on a small space—sometimes the size of a dorm room—the very premise of a battle royale means that almost all players have been defeated at that point. And as the map shrinks, it becomes harder to survive. A battle royale player might need to worry about 99 competitors, but their device faces far fewer opponents.

These tricks only go so far. The mobile-only battle royale *Free Fire*, which had as many as 100 and 150 million daily active users during parts of 2020 and 2021,^([5](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr5)) focused on players in Southeast Asia and South America, most of whom owned low-to-mid-range Android devices, rather than more powerful iPhones and high-end Androids. As such, *Free Fire*’s battle royale is limited to 50 players, not 100. When titles such as *Fortnite* and *Roblox* operate social events in a more confined space, such as a virtual concert venue, CCUs are typically capped at 50 or fewer. They also limit what users can do compared to the standard game modes. The ability to build might be turned off, or the number of dance moves reduced from the normal dozen or two to only a single preset option. And players who have CPUs and GPUs that are less powerful than average will face even more compromises; their game might not load the custom avatars of other players, as they have no gameplay consequence, and instead render each one as a stock character. For all the marvels of *Microsoft Flight Simulator*, fewer than 1% of desktop or laptop Macs and PCs can even run the title on its lowest graphical settings (and fewer still would be able to do so if its map was truly “alive,” rather than just its weather and airplane traffic).
