
In August 2023, OpenSea finally admitted defeat. In a blog post that was widely criticized online—even by OpenSea investor Mark Cuban—Finzer announced that his marketplace would no longer enforce royalties. “\[Blocklists\] depended on support from everyone in the ecosystem to be successful,” Finzer wrote, “and that just didn’t happen.” He was right. Over the previous 12 months, the effective royalty rate paid on NFTs across all major blockchains and marketplaces had fallen from a more or less stable 5% to around 0.55%.^([84](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr84)) In response, Yuga Labs, the maker of Bored Ape Yacht Club, announced that it would update its Bored Ape NFTs to block sales on OpenSea, having blocked Blur in January 2023.^([85](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr85)) Since 2021, the company had netted over $175 million in royalties but had seen the share of Bored Ape buyers that paid full royalties fall from 100% to 5% between August 2022 and February 2023.^([86](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr86))

While Blur is often blamed for the end of NFT royalties, its fight with OpenSea masks other truths. For one, there is no consistent evidence that `dapp`-versus-`dapp` competition is always good for the marketplace. And to that end, it’s notable that the `d` in “dapp” stems from the fact that they run on a decentralized network, not that their ownership structure is decentralized. In most cases, the `dapp`’s founder or founding team will hold a large portion of the `dapp`’s tokens out of the natural belief that the `dapp` will succeed and its tokens will therefore increase in value. As such, these individuals may have the ability to alter the `dapp` at will, or at least play a strong (and potentially blocking) role in its development over time.

The fight over NFT royalties also reveals the limitations of immutability and the realities of human commerce. That NFTs could be updated to prohibit various marketplaces or trades is proof that their much-touted code-based ownership rights are fewer than believed and that even when using decentralized records, customers are at the mercy of centralized entities. There are potential solutions to this issue, but none is enviable. The first requires NFTs to be minted without the ability for updates (or “upgrades”). This functionality has existed for years, but it’s rarely adopted because of the cost of permanence. If an NBA Top Shot, as an example, cannot be modified, then a moment that captured a 2D version of a Michael Jordan dunk could never be upgraded to VR—and only new NFTs could offer that sort of experience. Similarly, this NFT could never be transformed with use, or modified to unlock a product, experience, or any other sort of capability that had yet to be imagined at the time it was minted. Now, the owner of this “locked” NFT could be issued a new NFT, which provides the above functionality based on ownership of the original NFT but is essentially the same problem: the utility and value of the core NFT depends on the actions of a centralized entity.

Royalty enforcement faces similar challenges. While the market may eventually adopt a new NFT standard that does force royalties, workarounds will always exist. A user could sell an NFT worth $1 million, which would generate a $50,000 royalty, for $10 in exchange for a $999,990 transfer. Though this puts the buyer at risk, the two could write a `smart contract`, or form a `DAO`, to achieve the same effect.

As `Sandbox` and others demonstrate, the usage rights for (and thus also the value of) an NFT might always be modified, constrained, or otherwise affected by the original rights owner, whether it’s a nonprofit `DAO`, a for-profit Web 2.0 concern, or a Web3 company. Similarly, ownership of an NBA Top Shots NFT does not automatically give an independent developer the right to incorporate these NFTs to make an NBA game (just as buying a physical Disney costume does not give the buyer the right to make commercial films starring as or featuring Iron Man or Elsa).

The limitations of supposed “true ownership” are extensive. `Sandbox`’s terms of services specifies that “By using the Services, you grant TSB a worldwide, non-exclusive, royalty-free, perpetual, irrevocable, sub-licensable (through multiple tiers), transferable right and license to use, reproduce, publicly display, distribute and adapt the publicly shared Assets and Games for the purposes of developing, distributing, providing, improving, and promoting the Services, our activities, and your publicly shared Assets and Games. You further grant TSB the right to use your name and trademarks, if any, in connection with our use of your publicly shared Assets and Games.” In short, users might “own” the token associated with `Sandbox` items, but such ownership requires users to provide effectively unlimited (and alterable) rights back to `Sandbox`, including the right to reproduce the item and advertise its “owner,” and at no cost.^([87](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr87))

Crypto optimists believe that over time, the market will reward those companies that fully decentralize their ownership structures, provide the greatest rights to users, and exhibit the best behavior, forcing Web 2.0 operators to convert and/or exit the market altogether. This may still happen. In the interim, though, these forces do not appear to be at work. Instead, individuals, businesses, and organizations of all kinds operate under laws—hence terms of service, and government registrations—calling into question the purpose of partial decentralization. Trillions of dollars of value are exchanged annually through centralized databases that are more efficient and just as reliant on the law for enforcement.
