
Some journalists suggested that big tech’s sudden interest in the nebulous idea of the Metaverse was an effort to avoid regulatory action.^([33](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr33)) If governments around the world became convinced that a disruptive platform shift was imminent, then even the largest and most entrenched companies in history need not be broken up—free markets and insurgent competitors would disassemble them in due time and without the messiness of antitrust suits. Others argued that, on the contrary, “the Metaverse” is being used by those same insurgents to force regulators to open antitrust investigations into today’s big-tech companies. Various shareholders, meanwhile, feared that big-tech CEOs were using the vaguely understood term to justify billion-dollar pet R&D projects that were years from public release, probably further behind schedule, and destined for failure.

Cynics were aided by an unavoidable truth: there seemed to be widespread confusion and/or disagreement about the Metaverse among those who touted it. Some executives argued the Metaverse was decades old (Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick, whose company produced two of the best-selling franchises of all time, `Grand Theft Auto` and `Red Dead Redemption`) or had recently arrived (Satya Nadella). Others argued it was imminent (Microsoft founder Bill Gates) or would arrive by the end of the decade (Mark Zuckerberg). A final cohort cautioned against any deadline at all, instead saying the Metaverse would emerge in the coming decades (Jensen Huang, as well as Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney, whose company produced `Fortnite`, and Tencent senior vice president, Steven Ma, who leads its hyperreality vision and international games business^([34](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr34))). Moreover, the Metaverse was constantly conflated with video games, VR headsets, crypto, or 3D simulations of a building (known as a “digital twin” or “virtual twin”). Some companies spoke of “the” Metaverse, while others said they were building “a” Metaverse, a semantic difference of little consequence to the average person, but one that revealed significant philosophical disagreements. The companies that raised “the Metaverse” did share one talking point: the Metaverse would, naturally, make their company more profitable, their customers happier, and their competitors less threatening.

Metaverse doubters also noted what often went unsaid by its promoters: that the term originated in dystopic literature. Making matters worse, blockchain-based Metaverse companies and platforms seemed focused on the securitization of virtual existence—selling multimillion-dollar virtual plots of land or images. Not only did this focus strike many as absurd, but it was also offensive given the many, very real problems of the “real world,” such as poverty and climate change. And the digital world as it already existed was fraught with its own issues, from online harassment, misinformation, and data collection—why would anyone need to be online more, let alone exist online? Some recalled the rise of `Second Life` in the early 2000s, which had also been declared the start of virtual existence and appeared on the cover of national magazines, only to fade from memory by the end of the decade.

The skeptics, in short, had a real basis for their skepticism. And as of early 2024, much of it seems validated. Two and a half years after Facebook’s name change, it’s obvious the buzz has receded, if not turned negative. Life isn’t that different, Metaverse revenues are hard to trace, and there are few “Metaverse products” that can be bought that weren’t available for the past decade (and fewer still that are bought). Some companies have started backing away from the term altogether.

But behind the Metaverse hype is something that is both real and distinct from hype cycles of the past. The world’s largest companies never publicly reoriented themselves around the idea of 3D TVs or driverless cars, let alone renamed themselves 3D TV Platforms or Autonomous Vehicle Networks. Nor did they invest $50 billion—and promise to invest tens of billions more—in a speculative future they admitted was five to ten years away. `Second Life` may have fallen short of expectations, but in the 23 minutes it will take the average person to read to this point in the book, more people will have logged in to `Roblox` than used `Second Life` in a month during the peak of its popularity;^([35](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr35)) indeed, more people use `Roblox` in a day than have even set up accounts on `Second Life` in the two decades since its launch in 2003.^([36](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr36))

The term “Metaverse” is not the point. In the first edition of this book, I wrote that society would likely abandon the term “Metaverse” by the time it arrived—an easy bet given the extent it was already misused, its conflation with the vision and success of a specific company (Facebook), and negative associations with blockchains, big tech, and dystopic fiction. In the months leading up to the unveiling of the Vision Pro mixed-reality headset, Apple CEO Tim Cook was asked about Apple’s plans for the Metaverse and replied, “I always think it’s important that people understand what something is. And I’m really not sure the average person can tell you what the Metaverse is.” This is right. Products and experiences matter; terms don’t. Apple never called the iPhone a smartphone, but instead “a revolutionary mobile phone” that combined with a “widescreen iPod and an internet communicator.”^([37](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr37)) It still became the defining smartphone. And the iPod was not sold as an MP3 player, which had existed for years, but “a whole new category of digital music player” that “puts 1,000 songs in your pocket.”^([38](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr38)) The Apple Watch, unsurprisingly, was not a smartwatch but “a revolutionary product that can enrich people’s lives” and “Apple’s Most Personal Device Ever.”^([39](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr39))
