
# HMD领域的发展与应用

After decades of unsuccessful tinkering from toy makers, inventors, and public research labs, big tech began to plow billions into the HMD field in the early 2010s. In 2010, Rony Abovitz, who previously founded MAKO Surgical Corp., which designed and manufactured robotic arms to aid live patient surgery, launched Magic Leap, which designed and manufactured mixed-reality devices primarily aimed at industrial and governmental applications. By the time Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund acquired a majority stake in Magic Leap in 2022, the company had raised more than $4 billion (though reports suggested it sold for less than that sum). Also in 2010, Microsoft started development of HoloLens AR platform, which also focused on enterprise usage, with the first model releasing in 2016 and a follow-up in 2019, and Google kicked off its Google Glass smart glasses, which were designed for consumer users. The first Google Glass Explorer was released in 2013, with an enterprise-focused unit launching in 2019 and a reconceived and rebranded design publicly field-tested in 2022. Google’s Cardboard VR platform debuted in 2014, with Google’s second VR platform, Daydream, coming two years later. Sony PlayStation began development of its VR platform in 2011, and the PS VR came to market in 2016. In 2014, Snap acquired Vergence Labs, an AR glasses start-up that had been founded in 2011, and served as the foundation for the Snap Spectacles, which premiered in 2016, with the Spectacles 2, Spectacles 3, and Spectacles 4 releasing over the following five years. Amazon began development of its Alexa-based AR glasses sometime in 2016 or 2017. The first Echo Frames were released in 2019; the second edition arrived two years later.

As Magic Leap, Microsoft, and Google started their HMD divisions, Palmer Luckey, an HMD designer at the University of Southern California Institute for Creative Technologies, began developing a VR device that later became known as the Oculus. At the time, Luckey was rumored to own the largest personal collection of HMDs in the world, and his goal for the Oculus was to build a device more capable than any other available in the market at the time but also cheaper. At the time, John Carmack, the id Software founder, had also been investigating the HMD field and heard about Luckey’s work. After trying a prototype Oculus in late 2011, Carmack began to help Luckey with his research, and at the 2012 E3 video game convention in Los Angeles, the “father of 3D graphics” gave the first public demonstration of the Oculus (which was visibly held together by duct tape) and announced *Doom 3* would be ported to the device. The following year, Carmack joined Oculus as chief technology officer and left id shortly thereafter. A year after that, Facebook purchased Oculus VR for $2 billion, with Carmack staying on until 2022, when he left to found Keen Technologies, an artificial general intelligence start-up.

Despite the tens of billions spent on VR and AR HMDs by mid-2020, the lifetime sales of these devices were fewer than $30 million combined. But then, suddenly, these investments seemed to mature.

In June 2020, neurosurgeons at Johns Hopkins performed America’s first and second surgeries on a live patient using an FDA-approved AR device. The first was a spinal fusion surgery, during which six screws were inserted into the patient’s spine, while the second concerned the removal of a cancerous tumor on another patient’s spine. In both cases, the surgeon used a head-mounted AR device that rendered a 3D model based on a CT of the patient’s spinal column and internal bone structures, which displayed, in real time, the surgeon’s tools and insertions, plus the patient’s vitals. By the end of 2022, Dr. Timothy Witham, who performed Johns Hopkins’s AR surgeries and is the director of the hospital’s Spinal Fusion Laboratory, had completed more than 150 live patient surgeries using an AR device, with dozens of other such surgeries taking place across the United States.

In September of 2020, Mercedes-Benz announced it would deploy Microsoft’s HoloLens 2 AR device across its nearly 400 dealerships throughout the United States to local technicians performing complex repairs. Specifically, the device would provide technicians with on-screen schematics, automatically highlight the parts that needed testing or repairs, and guide installations and removals, while also warning of key risks, such as the potential for a burn. Technicians could also use the device to video-call Mercedes’s own centralized experts, who would be able to instruct the local technicians while watching their efforts in real time from the HoloLens’s front-facing camera.

Throughout 2020 and 2021, each of the ten largest upstream oil and gas companies began to incorporate VR devices into their training programs, especially for those who would be stationed on oil rigs and platforms. Not only are these facilities entirely foreign to most new hires, but they are in remote locations and dangerous; in the United States, oil rig and platform fatality rates are 25–30 per 100,000 (45–50 for drill workers), more than seven times the national average. One oil and gas executive reported that beyond reduced training time and accident rates, the use of VR devices in workplace onboarding had even led to new safety signage, placements, and instructions after eye tracing revealed that many trainees struggled to locate (or otherwise missed) emergency information in test scenarios.

In March 2021, the US Army announced a deal to buy up to 120,000 customized AR HoloLens devices from Microsoft over the following decade. This contract was valued at $22 billion—nearly $200,000 per headset (this sum includes hardware upgrades, repairs, bespoke software, and other Azure cloud computing services). In a press release, Master Sergeant Marc Krugh, who supported the army-Microsoft AR partnership since its inception, said the device would enable soldiers “to rehearse and train in more realistic scenarios.” The army-specific HoloLens unit, called IVAS (Integrated Visual Augmentation System), used HoloLens’s technology, while adding thermal imagery, additional sensors, GPS, and night vision to provide situation inputs and live training data to soldiers. Users could see where they were on the battlefield, as well as what and who was around them, by “projecting holographic images, three-dimensional terrain maps and a compass onto their field of vision.” Microsoft boasted that IVAS would enable users to shift their view to the simulated “vantage point of combatants” and evaluate not just their prospect for success but how they might be affected by daylight, moonlight, rain, and so on. The army was particularly keen on the ability to minimize the risk of friendly fire and improve the ability to escape a firefight. When paired with a weapon sight, IVAS allowed soldiers to see as their rifle might see, thereby providing the ability to “safely look around corners.”
