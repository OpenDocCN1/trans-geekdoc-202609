
Other areas are less ontological, but just as tricky. Interoperable economies—that is, virtual currency exchanges, goods trading, and so on—have complex tax implications, as well as regulatory burdens relating to money-transfer licenses, customer identification, securities licenses, capital reserves, and so on. For a virtual object on one platform to interoperate on another, systems are required to manage permissions and policies required to manage related liabilities. Harder still, architectures are required to sort exactly what happens (and doesn’t happen) to the relevant data and how. If a player buys an outfit on Platform A and wants to use it in Platform B, does Platform A send the outfit’s ownership record and files to Platform B, which manages it until it’s needed elsewhere, or does Platform A indefinitely manage the outfit and provide Platform B temporary rights to use it? If so, how does Platform A compensate? If the player sells the outfit to a Platform B user who doesn’t have a Platform A account, which company even processes the transaction? If the user alters the item from within Platform B, or adds an item to it from Platform B, how is the record on Platform A altered? If users have virtual items scattered across multiple titles, how do they ever know what, altogether, they own and where what they own can or cannot be used? Solutions are needed that not only establish and maintain an authoritative record but can transfer it to outside parties and provide authenticated access to that record and allow permitted parties to alter it, too.

Standards development is often considered the toughest hurdle faced by Metaverse builders because it is primarily a human process, not a technical one. Moore’s Law, which observes that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles every two years, charts predictable improvements in computing power and cost, while 6G mobile network specifications are already in early development and will doubtless be widely deployed, and the global internet backbone is constantly being expanded and upgraded.^([11](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr11)) Standards have no set timeline, inevitabilities, or obvious answers. They are agreed upon, not discovered, and therefore rely on debates and concessions, which are shaped by competitive leverage and deployment, produce unintended side effects, and sometimes result in rival standards that worsen fragmentation rather than reverse it.

Take the widely popular GIF format, first introduced in 1987. Although GIFs often compress their source video file to the point that many individual frames are discarded and the remaining frames have lost much of their visual detail, file sizes remain quite large. There are many alternatives to the GIF—from MP4 to APNG (animated PNG) and WebM—which are typically five to ten times lighter and provide far greater video clarity and detail, in addition to other benefits, such as the ability to be streamed. Despite this, GIF remains the more popular and most widely deployed form of moving image file, which makes it tough for any site or application to reject, thereby ensuring it endures. Yet this endurance has led to extra bandwidth use, more time waiting for files to load, and worse experiences overall. Given the intensity of the Metaverse’s computational and networking-related needs, such suboptimizations add up quickly.

On occasion, it’s clear which prospective standard is “best” before any one alternative sees wide adoption. Even so, the companies that steer or own a lesser alternative will typically resist embracing this rival for strategic reasons—and some of those who have already adopted the lesser alternative will continue to use it simply to avoid the burden of making a shift.

As one reviews the past four decades of the internet, it’s easy to imagine better structures or declare better formats. But the internet’s extensive interoperability was fostered by an irreplicable advantage: its foundational protocols, conventions, and formats were set long before it was commercialized. Accordingly, the internet of today did not need for-profit rivals to agree, let alone concede to, a rival’s proposal. And while many for-profit products on the internet are largely closed or use proprietary protocols, such maneuvers required the development of a product with benefits that far exceeded network effects. While many internet businesses opt out of data sharing or use proprietary protocols and services, the cost is a reduction in network effects and thus success largely depends on producing a product worthy of this trade-off. Instant Messaging services have routinely met this burden by offering greater security, functionality, speed, and reliability over SMS, but no closed alternatives to email have been able to do the same (even though companies such as Google and Microsoft might aspire for a world where all such messages ran through their systems). Conversely, today’s virtual worlds were all built without the intent, let alone ability, to interoperate. As such, opting in to such a model requires substantial technical shifts (or concessions) that provide, at least to start, only marginally greater network effects.

## Massively Scaled

For the internet to be “the internet,” it’s generally accepted that it must have a seemingly infinite number of websites and applications, and there must be myriad individual developers behind these pages, too. The Metaverse is similar. It must have a massively scaled number of virtual worlds if it is to be “the Metaverse.” Otherwise, it is more like a digital theme park—a destination with a handful of carefully curated attractions and experiences that can never be as diverse as, or contend with, the outside (real) world.

To most advocates, the size of the Metaverse will far exceed the overall size of the internet today—and the physical world, too. In 2021, Tim Sweeney told the *LA Times*, “Just as every company a few decades ago created a webpage, and then at some point every company created a Facebook page, I think we’re approaching the point where every company will have a real-time live 3D presence.… That’s starting to happen now. \[And\] it’s going to be a much bigger thing than these previous generational shifts.”^([12](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr12)) Jensen Huang imagines the Metaverse having countless compounding simulations; the US government might have a national simulation, with cities, building operators, and stores within the United States running their own partly overlapping and partly interconnected simulations. The larger the Metaverse, the more used and useful, but also the more difficult the problems of rendering, synchronization, standardization, persistence, and more.
