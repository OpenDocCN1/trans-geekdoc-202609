
Regardless, the broader history of the Metaverse in literature makes it easier to separate its ideas from the novel in which the term originates. Shared across the works of Stephenson, Cline, Gibson, the Wachowskis, et al. is the idea of a simulated reality that never stops, pauses, or resets, that touches nearly every living person (irrespective of whether they ever enter the Metaverse), that spans myriad experiences of both realism and fantasy, for leisure and labor, and that often directly touches the real world and its economy, complementing and sometimes substituting for parts of it.

There is another shared idea: that the Metaverse is dystopic, or at least the world that embraces it is a dystopia, and if so, the situation is exacerbated by the existence of the Metaverse. This commonality led to widespread criticism of big tech’s intentions when it embraced the term. Eleven days after Facebook’s name change, Alex Blechman, a staff writer for the satirical newspaper `The Onion`, posted a particularly popular tweet:

Alex Blechman

@AlexBlechman

Sci-Fi Author: In my book I invented the Torment Nexus as a cautionary tale

Tech Company: At long last, we have created the Torment Nexus from classic sci-fi novel Don’t Create The Torment Nexus^([24](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr24))

5:49 PM • Nov 8, 2021

On a more serious note, many asked why a company such as Facebook needed, let alone deserved, more reach into the lives of its users. Others wondered whether the present-day internet had already created the dystopias described by 20th- and 21st-century science fiction authors.

Though these questions are fair, the focus on the dystopic settings of Metaverse literature is misplaced. At the root of most fiction is drama—especially human drama—and a perfect society tends to produce little of it.^([§§](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-050)) It’s for this reason that so few books are set in utopias and that these supposed utopias later collapse or are revealed to be dystopic. The authors detailed throughout this chapter sought not to warn humanity of potential technologies but to predict and entertain through them. But more important than what various authors meant when they said “Metaverse” or “Cyberspace” or the “Grid” is what is meant by the technology executives and entrepreneurs who are trying to build it.

## Chapter 2 WHAT THE “METAVERSE” IS (AND ISN’T)

Stephenson’s neologism “Metaverse” comes from the Greek prefix `meta` and the stem “verse,” a back-formation of the word “universe.” In English, `meta` roughly translates to “beyond” or “which transcends.” Metadata is data that describes data, while metaphysics refers to a branch of philosophy “of being, identity and change, space and time, causality, necessity and possibility,” rather than the study of “matter, its fundamental constituents, its motion and behavior through space and time, and the related entities of energy and force.”^([1](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr1)) The “meta” and “verse” are intended to be a unifying layer that sits above and across all individual, computer-generated “universes,” as well as the real world, just as the universe contains, by some estimates, 70 quintillion planets.

To some technologists, philosophers, and sociologists, the Metaverse has existed for decades using another name: “the internet.” Today, the internet, a digital layer that spans nearly every country, company, and person, is used for labor and leisure, powers much of the physical world and extends much of its users’ senses of self, and has both a unified access layer (such as the web browser) and an underlying organizational framework (IP addresses, email addresses, etc.). The Metaverse Stephenson imagined in the early 1990s, and which was set sometime in the 2010s, is very different from the internet that’s been experienced since `Snow Crash` was published, but there’s obvious merit to the argument that humanity lives in it nevertheless.
