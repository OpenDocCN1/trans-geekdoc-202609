
# Second Life 的崛起与虚拟经济

For many, especially those in Silicon Valley, the idea of a parallel virtual existence was not seriously contemplated until the arrival of the aptly named `Second Life` in June 2003 (two months before the debut of the 2D social network MySpace). In its first year, `Second Life` attracted over one million regular users, and shortly thereafter, numerous real-world organizations rushed to add their own businesses and presences inside the platform. This included for-profit corporations such as Adidas, BBC, and Wells Fargo, as well as nonprofits such as the American Cancer Society and Save the Children, and even universities, including Harvard, whose law school offered exclusive courses inside `Second Life`. In 2007, a stock exchange was launched on the platform with the aim of helping `Second Life`–based companies raise capital using the platform’s Linden Dollars currency.

Unlike most virtual worlds before and after `Second Life`, its developer Linden Labs did not intermediate transactions between players, nor actively manage what was made or sold. Instead, transactions were made directly between buyers and sellers and based on perceived value and need. Overall, Linden Labs operated more like a government than a game maker. The company did provide some user-facing services, such as identity management, ownership records, and an in-world legal system. But its focus wasn’t on building out the `Second Life` universe directly. Instead, it enabled a thriving economy via ever-improving infrastructure, technical capabilities, and tools that would attract more developers and creators who would then create things for other users to do, places for them to visit, and items for them to buy—attracting more users and therefore more spending, which would in turn attract more investment from developers and creators. To this end, `Second Life` also offered users the ability to import virtual objects and textures made outside the platform. By 2005, just two years after it launched, `Second Life`’s annualized GDP exceeded $40 million.^([12](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr12)) By 2009, it exceeded half a billion dollars, with users cashing out $55 million into real-world currency that year.^([13](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr13)) Over `Second Life`’s first decade, a total of $3.2 billion in player-to-player transactions took place, with an average of more than 1.2 million individual transactions per day.^([14](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr14))

# Minecraft 与 Roblox 的主流普及

For all the success of `Second Life`, it was the rise of virtual world platforms `Minecraft` and `Roblox` in the 2010s that brought Linden Labs’ ideas to mainstream audiences. In addition to offering significant technical enhancements compared to their predecessors, `Minecraft` and `Roblox` were far easier to use, too, due to their focus on children and teenage players. The results have been astounding.

By the end of 2021, more than 150 million people were using `Minecraft` each month—more than six times as many as in 2014, when Microsoft bought the platform—with the title also becoming the best-selling game in history. Despite this, `Minecraft` was far from the size of the new market leader, `Roblox`, which had grown from fewer than 5 million to 225 million monthly users over that same period. Put together, the two platforms were averaging more than 6 billion hours of monthly usage, with more than 100 million different virtual worlds having been created, and more than $1 billion in royalties paid to `Roblox` developers alone. The second most popular virtual world on `Roblox`, `Adopt Me!`, was created in 2017 by two hobbyist players under the age of 25; in 2021 alone, `Adopt Me!` received more than 10 billion visits—more than seven times the average number of global tourism visits in pre–COVID-19.^([15](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr15))

# 虚拟世界的创造力与现实影响

In addition to being popular, the virtual worlds of `Roblox` and `Minecraft` consistently achieved new creative highs and made their presence felt in the “real world,” too. On a recurring basis, fans of franchises such as `Game of Thrones` or `Star Wars`, or sports teams such as Real Madrid or Manchester City, would spend weeks reproducing major locations brick by brick—such as the Santiago Bernabéu Stadium or even the 500 square miles of the fictional King’s Landing. One video game streamer, Aztter, constructed an original cyberpunk city out of an estimated 370 million `Minecraft` blocks, having worked an average of 16 hours per day for a year.^([16](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr16)) In 2015, Verizon built a cellphone inside `Minecraft` that could browse the web and even complete live (though blocky) video calls with people in the “real world.”^([17](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr17))

As the COVID-19 virus spread across China in February 2020, a community of Chinese `Minecraft` players rapidly re-created the 1.2-million-square-foot hospitals built in Wuhan as a tribute to the “IRL” (“in real life”) workers, receiving global press coverage.^([18](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr18)) One month later, Reporters Sans Frontières (also known as Reporters Without Borders) commissioned the construction of a museum within `Minecraft` that was composed of over 12.5 million blocks assembled by 24 virtual builders in 16 different countries over some 250 hours combined.^([19](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr19)) The Uncensored Library, as it was called, allowed users in countries such as Russia, Saudi Arabia, and Egypt to read banned literature, as well as works promoting free speech and detailing the lives of slain journalists such as Jamal Khashoggi.

# 其他平台的兴起：Pokémon Go 与 Niantic

Despite the enormous growth in `Minecraft`’s and `Roblox`’s audiences and developer communities, many other platforms emerged and grew in the second half of the 2010s. In the summer of 2016, Niantic released `Pokémon Go`, which shared the core mechanics of most `Pokémon` titles—finding, capturing, training, and battling Pokémon—but required players to leave their homes and traverse a virtual map layered atop the real world. To catch a Pokémon that swam in the ocean, for example, a player would need to visit an ocean-side beach; to find a bumblebee-styled Pokémon, they would need to wander a local park in the summer. Once the `Pokémon Go` application noticed the player was close to a Pokémon, a battle would trigger, in which the user could see a 3D version of the creature through their smartphone screen and camera, then throw an animated pokéball to capture the creature, which would also try to evade the player. By visiting various virtual locations set around physical landmarks, the user could also train their Pokémon, and battle other players at “gyms” based on key locations such as museums or statues. After six months, `Pokémon Go` amassed more than half a billion downloads and half a billion in revenue. By its sixth anniversary, lifetime revenue exceeded $6 billion with monthly players averaging between 75 and 150 million. The title was praised for its promotion of physical activity, helping local businesses grow as a result of increased foot traffic, and fostering in-person connection among players who didn’t know one another. Notably, Niantic was a spin-off from Google’s Geo division, which oversaw Google Maps, Google Earth, and Google Street View. Niantic’s CEO, John Hanke, had run the division until the spin-off, having joined with the sale of Keyhole, where he was co-founder and CEO.
