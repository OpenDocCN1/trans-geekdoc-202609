
Compare, for example, navigating the Roman Colosseum in a video game versus doing the same on Google Street View. Both provide 360-degree views and multiple dimensions of movement (look up or down, move left or right, backward or forward), but the former severely limits one’s choices—and if a user decides to look closely at a given stone, all they can do is zoom in on an image not designed for such scrutiny. It will be blurry, and the view angle is fixed.

Although real-time rendering enables a virtual world to be “alive” and to respond to input from a user (or a group of users, for that matter), this requires each visual frame to be rendered in a fraction of a second, rather than several seconds, let alone days. Moreover, more frames per second are needed depending on the screen that’s used and content that’s displayed. Whereas most movies, such as those of Pixar, have only 24 frames per second, most games require at least 30.^([†](#chapter4.xhtml#footnote-034)) Titles that are more dynamic and require more precise input, such as `Call of Duty`, aspire for a minimum of 60 frames per second. For head-mounted displays (e.g. virtual reality, augmented reality, and mixed reality headsets), many experts believe at least 120 and as many as 240 frames per second are needed to avoid nausea, disorientation, and related sensations.^([8](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr8)) According to a study from VRHeaven and compiled by Goldman Sachs, 7 percent of men and 23 percent of women who had tried virtual reality headsets in June 2020 reported that they “Frequently” experienced motion sickness when using these devices, and another 14 percent and 23 percent responded “Sometimes,” and 29 percent and 20 percent said “Rarely.”^([9](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr9)) Only 50 percent of men and 35 percent of women said “Never.”^([10](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr10)) At the time, nearly all VR headsets were limited to 75 fps or fewer. The incidence of nausea stems from many unique attributes of headset-based experiences, such as proximity of the device’s screen to the wearer’s eyes (2–5cm), the overall sense of immersion in the device (which means the user’s sense of balance is not necessarily “anchored” to the real world), and the use of head and body movements as an input (which can result in a disagreement between the brain’s sense of balance or “inner ear” and the experienced viewed on the display).

In totality, non-real-time-rendered films and real-time-rendered virtual worlds face the same resource constraints, but the latter requires far more, at far greater speeds, and at lower cost than the former. Whereas a Pixar film might have roughly 1,440 frames (24 frames per second × 60 seconds), a real-time-rendered world needs 1,800 (+25%) to 14,400 (+900%). It’s okay for Pixar’s frames to take seconds, minutes, or even days to render, but a real-time-rendered world’s frames cannot be rendered in advance and must therefore be produced between 99.5% and 99.999% faster. Pixar gets to use a world-class supercomputer that costs tens of millions to construct, but to reach the entire smartphone population, a virtual world needs to run adequately on a $100 Android device.

A Pixar film, like a video game, looks better as resolutions increase. A 2K display offers twice the detail of a 1K display, making circles sharper and smiles richer whether they are predetermined or interactive. And in both cases, a doubling in resolution doubles the frames required. But while the math is linear, the difficulty is not, given the aforementioned limitations on time, cost, and available resources. Furthermore, head-mounted displays require even greater resolutions than typical displays because the user’s eyes are only an inch or two away; the pixels of a 2K television are imperceptible from seven feet, but enormous when a user’s nose is pressed up to the screen.

## Interoperable Network

As with the internet, the term “Metaverse” does not describe any single system or even a group of systems. It instead describes a network of systems—and these systems are interoperable with one another. “Interoperability” refers to the ability for independent, autonomous, and heterogeneous systems to exchange and make use of one another’s data.

Interoperation requires network participants to adopt common technologies, such as the Internet Protocol Suite. These shared technologies are usually steered by nonprofit groups whose recommendations must then be voluntarily adopted by the various network participants. The Internet Protocol Suite is managed by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), a nonprofit with no formal membership or requirements and whose work is funded by its contributors and project sponsors.

Typically, network participants work to establish and/or support additional common standards and working conventions that aid the exchange of data. HTML, for example, is not part of the Internet Protocol Suite and thus does not, strictly speaking, enable the exchange of data. However, the widespread use of HTML ensures that most devices, users, and developers are able to coherently understand, consistently display, and easily use one another’s data. The widespread support of formats such as JPEG and MP3, meanwhile, ensures that most photo and audio files can be used on most websites and applications, irrespective of which website or application created them. Moreover, interchange solutions and frameworks emerged which helped convert between different formats, such as JPEG and PNG. To make the internet easier to use for consumers, rather than just for servers and networks, organizational hierarchies and registries were also established, such as the Domain Name System (which effectively converts an IP address into a text name like www.google.com). HTML is managed by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), a public-interest not-for-profit organization; JPEG by the Joint Photographic Experts Group, a not-for-profit committee in partnership with the United Nations’ International Telecommunication Union; MP3 by the Fraunhofer Society, a for-profit research organization which licenses the MP3 format; and DNS is managed by the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN), a not-for-profit multi-stakeholder group which also oversees IP and network addresses.

The healthiest instantiation of the Metaverse is likely one in which as many underlying protocols and standards are managed or guided by birds-of-a-feather groups, rather than by corporations or other private entities. This may take time. The easiest pathway to standards development is for a single party, especially one that already operates at scale, to lead said standards’ development and deployment. The IETF was originally established by the US federal government, which also developed the majority of TCP/IP in the first place, but by 1993, the organization had become fully independent. A related concern is how many protocols and standards are established; the internet could operate on fewer, but would likely benefit from more.

Although Neal Stephenson’s Metaverse was a protocol-based interoperable network, he imagined the Metaverse as having a unified and standardized experiential layer, whereas modern interpretations tend to assume it would not. In `Snow Crash`, the entirety of the Metaverse is contained on a single, geographically organized planet with a literal main street (and related benefits and real estate costs to the locations of various businesses), commutes to and from a user’s home, and programmatic regulations on the dimensions of avatars, buildings, and so on.
