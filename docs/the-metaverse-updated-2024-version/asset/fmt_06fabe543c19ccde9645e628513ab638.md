
Though powerful, consoles are limited. Consider the PlayStation 5 or Xbox Series X, both top-of-the-line video game consoles released in 2020. These devices are each nearly ten pounds and larger than a shoebox—much bulkier in comparison to other consumer electronics devices, but this is not usually a problem, given that these devices are generally placed inside a media shelving console and never moved. In truth, it’s an advantage! Precisely because these devices are so large, unsightly, and stationary, Sony and Microsoft have the leeway to place large and loud fans inside their consoles, which keep the consoles cool as they perform their intensive calculations. The fans are aided by large intake and exhaust vents. Sony and Microsoft can also keep component costs down as they don’t need to prioritize their size the way a smartphone manufacturer always must. And while Sony’s and Microsoft’s consoles are heavy, unlike most consumer devices, they never need a battery. Instead, they receive constant power from the electrical grid. This reduces both the size of the device and the heat it generates, which in turn means that the fan can be smaller, too, and also means they can run indefinitely, rather than just a few hours. Consoles typically support two forms of connectivity, Wi-Fi and Ethernet, but have no need for mobile network support—another chipset that takes up space, increases the device’s build costs, and drains battery power.

Video game consoles also don’t need to bring their own display; they hook into a television. This shifts cost to another purchase—no one thinks of a PlayStation as costing “$500 plus a TV”—while also enabling users to choose their own quality (e.g., 1K vs. 4K definition, LED vs. OLED), which accords similar flexibility to their budgets. Most TVs are also six to ten feet from the viewer, which means that a viewer with 20/20 vision can’t even see detail above 1080p unless the TV is larger than 45 inches (if at six feet) or 75 inches (at ten feet). These televisions also work in optimal or at least relatively stable environments—in a darkly lit living room, rather than in an office, let alone on the street, where lighting is uncontrollable and variable. Lastly, TV screens typically draw their own power from an electrical grid, just as a console does.

Video game consoles also off-load a portion of their work to other devices, such as a stand-alone controller for input or a separately purchased headset for audio (the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X don’t include support for Bluetooth audio, so headset purchases include Bluetooth dongles to add the functionality). Video game consoles don’t need to know or figure out very much. They don’t need to track the user’s motion (and especially not their eyes), or scan and process the environment the user is playing in. And video game consoles are leisure devices. It’s not important that they simulate physics precisely, generate “photoreal” renders, or support commercial “work.” And when a console runs an application, it’s often just running that single application, plus a few other supporting processes (e.g., audio chat), whereas PCs and smartphones are constantly running many concurrent applications.

The power- and hardware-related advantages of consoles must be kept in mind when evaluating the capabilities and supposed inadequacies of VR, AR, and MR headsets. It’s common to hear the critique that the experiences produced by these devices look worse than those produced by the consoles that were manufactured in the late 2000s and that could be purchased for only a few hundred dollars then and tens of dollars now.

When it comes to visually rendering a virtual environment, VR/AR/MR devices will always fall short of a contemporaneous video game console. Always. This is because the “work” performed by these devices is far, far harder while the constraints are far, far greater.

## Bulking Up and Slimming Down

Any extended reality (XR) device that aspires to regular use or long session times needs to be lightweight. There is broad consensus that the `tolerable` range for an HMD is about 300–700 grams (0.66–1.54 lbs.), depending on purpose (consumer vs. enterprise) and ideal session length (a user is more likely to use a device for several hours if it weighs 300, not 700, grams). Such a device must therefore weigh roughly a tenth the weight of a PlayStation 5 or Xbox Series X. AR glasses must be even lighter. While no one would reasonably insist that these devices must weigh the 15–40 grams of the average “dumb” glasses worn today, they still must be under about 150 grams if they’re going to be worn all day.

Not only do HMDs need to be far lighter than consoles, but they require considerably more components. These include batteries (which are particularly heavy relative to their size), displays, speakers and microphones, a multitude of internally and externally facing cameras (many experts currently believe roughly 12 total cameras are needed) that can track the user’s eyes, face, fingers, and local environment, as well as a variety of sensors (gyroscope, accelerometer, infrared, heat, ultrawide band, and so on), bifocal lenses, and, in the case of AR/MR devices intended for mobile use, wireless networking chips.

As is always the case, when these components are in use, they generate heat. As such, additional components such as thermal tunnels, fans, and heat sinks are required. But unlike consoles, which sit some feet from the user’s body, this heat is but millimeters away from a user’s head and eyes. For this reason, an HMD’s “heat budget” is strikingly narrow.

Fitting these many components into a single lightweight device is hard. It must also be done in an affordable, aesthetically pleasing, and comfortable fashion. This last aspect is where the distribution of weight matters. For many people, a 300-to-700-gram HMD is still too heavy—even for an hour. What’s more, roughly 85% of that weight is front-loaded (i.e., resting on the wearer’s nasal bridge and pulled to their forehead). Though traditional glasses are typically front-loaded, there’s a material difference between placing no more than 30 grams on a user’s nasal bridge and no more than 10 on their ears, and 630 grams on their forehead and another 70 across the rest of their head.

The tension between an HMD’s form factor and its functionality is made by its core feature: the display. When viewing a TV, computer monitor, tablet, or smartphone display, a user can look directly at the display with no intermediary required (though films or glass typically sit on top of LEDs for protective reasons). In contrast, VR and passthrough AR and MR screens require intermediary lenses that bend light toward the user’s eyes from only one to two inches away. This requirement forces a larger device. If a user wants to wear glasses while using an HMD, additional space and supporting material are necessary. New problems emerge, too. Another lens typically produces incremental blurriness, visual artifacts, and reflections—and the degree to which this occurs would also vary based on the quality (i.e., cost) and thickness (i.e., prescription) of the user’s glasses. For similar reasons, HMDs that support glasses cannot place their eye-tracking cameras in front of the user’s eyes. Instead, these cameras must be placed farther outside the HMD’s lenses and at an angle that leads to less accurate tracking unless more cameras are added to compensate.
