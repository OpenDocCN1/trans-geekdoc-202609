
Overall, very few online experiences require high bandwidth, low latency, and continuous connectivity other than real-time-rendered, multiuser virtual worlds. Most experiences just need one or, at most, two of these elements. High-frequency stock traders (and especially high-frequency trading algorithms) want the lowest possible delivery times, as this can be the difference between buying or selling a security at a profit or a loss. However, the orders themselves are straightforward, are data light, and don’t require a server connection to be sustained for seconds, let alone hours.

The major exception is videoconferencing, which involves many people receiving and sending high-resolution video files, all at once, and participating in a shared experience. Yet these experiences, too, can take advantage of software solutions that don’t work for real-time-rendered virtual worlds—and work even when these solutions fall short.

As with all networking activities, the data packets that make up a video call occasionally arrive late due to network congestion or temporary connectivity loss. In the case of videoconferencing, this means that at least one member of the call (if the network error occurred near their device) or many (if it happened near the speaker) might miss a few of the speaker’s words or receive a garbled version of them. This usually doesn’t matter, as humans are adept at deducing the speaker’s overall point (and potentially the exact words they said), but modern videoconferencing software, such as Zoom and Microsoft Teams, now deploys machine learning solutions to help out. If a user loses their connection, the application’s server will send the requisite audio once they regain connectivity, which is then played at roughly 1.5-times speed and with pauses programmatically edited out, so that the user can still hear what they missed and then catch up to being “live.” And because videoconferences have a single active participant and many passive viewers, this disruption typically happens without any other members of the call even knowing. Should the connectivity issues affect the speaker, everyone is affected, but another member of the call can take over or everyone can wait until the speaker reconnects. Everything is fine. If this issue continues, the application will usually stop uploading or downloading video from various members of the call, in order to prioritize what matters most: audio. And in the case of varying latency—that is, members of the call are receiving “live” video and audio a quarter, half, or even full second behind or in front of each other—it’s likely that users are annoyed, but with a little patience, they can figure out how to proceed.

In contrast to a video call, virtual worlds send more complex data, which is needed on a far timelier basis, both to and from a larger set of users, and with greater consequences resulting from network hiccups. Imagine, as an example, a multiplayer shooter game. If Player A lags 75 milliseconds behind Player B, they might shoot in a location where they believe Player B to be, but Player B and the game’s server know Player B has already left. This discrepancy means the virtual world’s server must decide whose experiences are “true” (that is, which should be rendered and persist across all participants) and whose experiences must be rejected. And if the user lags too frequently, or for too long, the server operating the virtual world might kick them out of the match altogether in order to preserve the experience of other players (though as any fan of team sports knows, the expulsion of a player disrupts the rest of the match regardless of why they were ejected).

The Metaverse is not a game, let alone a fast-moving shooter with gameplay that’s decided by as little as a single pixel or hundredth of a second. Yet the Metaverse’s synchronization requirements and obstacles are far greater. Unlike a match of `Call of Duty`, there might be millions of synchronized devices, users, sensors, and equipment, rather than just a few dozen. Users are more likely to be spread around the world, rather than between 10 and 1,000 miles apart, thereby elongating the travel times. There is also more data to synchronize and it’s of greater consequence and more likely to persist as well. Furthermore, human interaction is incredibly sensitive; a smile that happens a bit too late (or not at all) or misaligned gazes can quickly exhaust, alienate, or creep out users.

The Metaverse can’t really function as a parallel plane for human existence if many of those within it experience conflicting (and then invalidated) versions of it. But at present, only three-quarters of American broadband households can reliably participate in high-fidelity real-time-rendered virtual worlds. Fewer than one-quarter of broadband households in the Middle East can, and fewer than one in ten in India.^([13](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr13)) To improve these figures, many solutions are needed. As discussed in [Chapter 2](#chapter2.xhtml), the Internet Protocol Suite does not currently acknowledge, let alone prioritize, real-time transmission across the internet. Another obstacle is the physical internet backbone, which is shaped not to minimize delivery time, but by geographic, environmental, political, and economic realities. Indeed, even the speed of light is limiting.

## Unlimited Users and Individual Presence

“Concurrency,” or the number of active users at a given moment, is one of the foundational problems for the Metaverse, and for a fundamental reason: it leads to exponential technical burdens. Take synchronization as an example. It’s obvious that as more users join a simulation, there is more “work” to be done and more potential points of network failure. Yet adding a third user to a two-user simulation does not increase synchronization requirements by 50%, but by over 100%. In the first case, User A’s actions must be synchronized with User B’s, and User B’s inputs with those of User A, but in the latter case, User C must be synced with Users A and B, and each must be synchronized with C. Synchronization points grow from two to six. Adding a fourth user doesn’t increase synchronization work by a third but by more than double, as each of Users A, B, and C must sync with a third user and User D must sync with each of them, too. And as users increase, the number of events/actions to simulate per second tends to increase and their predictability decreases. This reduces computing resources available for logic processing, rendering, and persistence management.

Today, even virtual worlds that have no persistence, span fewer than ten square kilometers in surface area, tightly control user activities, and are operated by some of the most technically capable companies in the world can struggle to sustain more than 50 to 150 users at the same time. What’s more, 150 concurrent users is possible only because of how these titles are creatively designed.

In `Fortnite`, up to 100 players can participate in a richly animated virtual world, and each player controls a detailed avatar that can use more than a dozen different items, perform dozens of dances and maneuvers, and build complex structures tens of stories tall. However, `Fortnite`’s roughly five-square-kilometer map means that only one dozen to two dozen players will encounter one another at once—and by the time players are forced into a smaller portion of the map, most players have been eliminated and turned into data on a scoreboard. During `Fortnite`’s social events, such as its famous 2020 concert with Travis Scott, players converge on a much smaller portion of the map and cannot be eliminated. Accordingly, the title’s standard cap of 100 players per instance was halved, while many items and actions, such as building, were disabled, thereby further reducing the workload.
