# Part II BUILDING THE METAVERSE

## Chapter 4 A GUIDE TO THE “FULL-SPEC” METAVERSE

Part i of this book is dedicated to the history of the Metaverse, as well as its current state. [Part II](#part2.xhtml) focuses on its future. These chapters explore the various technologies, solutions, and agreements that will be needed to build the Metaverse—and the individuals and companies, philosophies and hypotheses, and complexities and histories that are behind each one.

With the above in mind, it’s helpful to move beyond the high-level definition of the Metaverse provided in [Part I](#part1.xhtml) and instead provide a description of a “fully realized” Metaverse. The Metaverse is …

“A massively scaled and interoperable network of real-time-rendered immersive virtual worlds that can be experienced synchronously and persistently by an effectively unlimited number of users with an individual sense of presence, and with continuity of data, such as identity, history, entitlements, objects, communications, and payments.”

The rest of the chapter explains this description in detail.

### “Virtual Worlds”

The Metaverse is made up of virtual worlds. The term “virtual worlds” refers to any computer-generated simulated environment. A virtual world can be presented in 2D (e.g., *Pac-Man*) or 3D (*Fortnite*), be purely virtual (*Super Mario*) or layered on top of the “real world” via augmented reality (*Pokémon Go*), or even be text-based (as in the case of a MUD). Some argue a virtual world need not be graphically rendered at all and instead can be limited to code (think of Neo viewing the Matrix in *The Matrix*). Stylistically, a virtual world can reproduce the “real world” exactly (this is typically called a “digital twin,” “virtual twin,” or “3D twin”) or represent a fictionalized version of it (e.g., the fantastical, quarter-scale Manhattan of PlayStation’s 2018 game *Marvel’s Spider-Man*), or represent an altogether fictional reality in which the impossible is commonplace (*Fortnite*). The graphics of this world may be “photoreal,” cartoonish, or stylized to imitate the works of Vincent van Gogh, Picasso, or any other artist, including one’s infant daughter.

The purpose of a virtual world can be “game-like,” which is to say there is an objective such as winning, killing, scoring, defeating, or solving, or the purpose can be “nongame-like” with objectives such as educational or vocational training, commerce, socializing, meditation, fitness, or operating physical spaces using a digital twin. In some cases, a virtual world has no individual users (as in the case of a Pixar film or when virtually simulating a closed ecosphere in a biology class), a single user (*The Legend of Zelda*), or many concurrent ones (*Call of Duty*).

The devices used to affect a virtual world vary: a keyboard controller, or motion sensor, network of cameras, voice, and more. The virtual world may run only on specialized hardware (e.g., a high-end supercomputer), a subset of broadly available computing platforms (e.g., PlayStation, but not Xbox), or across most possible endpoints (which likely involve large variations in the user’s experience by device).

Operationally, a virtual world may have a predefined run-time, after which it is fully reset (a level of *Super Mario*) or run in perpetuity (*EVE Online*), or it may operate somewhere in the middle, with only part of its world reset after a game or event (*World of Warcraft*). As with the real world, governance models vary widely. Most virtual worlds are centrally controlled by the person or group that developed and operates the world, which means they have unilateral control over its economy, policies, and users. In other instances, users self-govern through various forms of democracy. Some blockchain-based games aspire to operate as close to autonomously as possible after launch.

All virtual worlds face constraints. The most obvious is the difficulty and cost of development, operation, and updates. Any reduction to these barriers results in more worlds, with greater diversity, and more potential applications, which leads to a larger, more valuable, and more used Metaverse. Another constraint, which underpins much of this chapter, is the availability of computing resources (local or in the cloud). Naturally, an increase in these resources affects the virtual world’s cost structure, but it also determines the sophistication of its underlying simulation. In a simplified sense, one can think of a virtual world as having a maximum number of available “operations per second.” These operations can be used on graphical detail (i.e., shifting from low-fidelity graphics to photorealism), physics (e.g., simulating precise light reflections or damage to a car), environmental complexity (increasing the number of cars in an intersection from two to ten), the number of simultaneous users (which leads to more interactions produced per second), increasing the world’s level of persistence (discussed later in this chapter), computer vision (to understand the “real world” or a user’s hands), and so on. The “full-spec” Metaverse will simulate and include anything that an individual user or business might want to simulate and in whatever quality or fidelity is desired.

### Immersive (or 3D or Spatial)

The Metaverse is an immersive medium that enables users to do more than just surf “on” a page or look at an app. Instead, they will exist within it. As discussed in [Chapter 2](#chapter2.xhtml), these experiences do not require the user to adopt new form factors, such as a headset, but such devices would probably be an aid to adoption, usage, and functionality. Billions may be adept at using keyboards and controllers to navigate 3D spaces, but billions of others find those inputs impossible to use—and these inputs cannot match the precision, ease of use, or flexibility of a human hand or eye. Accordingly, companies such as Apple and Facebook are developing devices as wide-ranging as motion-tracking cameras and tracking rings, even sensors that can detect electrical signals traveling across skeletal muscles, and neural implants to read brain activity. Regardless, today’s devices, from smartphones to smartwatches, will also need additional and better sensors. The world will too. While digital twins and city-scale simulations can transform the world into the greatest software development platform in the world, fleets of Internet of Things (IoT) sensors and data pipelines will be required to both track the world in real time and translate it into 3D reproductions.
