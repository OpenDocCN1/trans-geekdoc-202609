
What I cannot predict is exactly what life will be like in the Metaverse—what we’ll do after we wake up and when we come home from work; how our jobs, relationships, and beliefs will change; and so on. Any attempt to describe the Metaverse with such precision would fail. Vannevar Bush had an uncanny ability to predict the devices of the future and much of what they might do, and he also had shrewd insights into the crucial role of government in making them useful and for the collective benefit. At the same time, his Memex was desk-sized and electromechanical—physically storing and connecting all the content a user might request—and did but a fraction of what we ask for from our iPhones. In `2001: A Space Odyssey`, Stanley Kubrick and Arthur C. Clarke imagined a future in which humankind had colonized space and sentient AI had emerged, but iPad-like displays were used for little more than watching TV while eating breakfast, and telephones were still dumb and required cords. Neal Stephenson’s `Snow Crash` has inspired decades of R&D projects and now guides many of the most powerful companies on earth. Yet Stephenson believed the Metaverse would emerge from the TV industry, not gaming, and Hollywood looks like it will be a mostly passive observer of this next great medium. Indeed, Stephenson imagined that socializing in the Metaverse would involve “people going to bars on the Street,” but “what we have now is `Warcraft` guilds” that go on in-game raids.^([18](#notes_split_012.xhtml#confr18))

Right now, we are at the least interesting and least diverse point of Metaverse software development. New ideas are focused on “What if App X or Service Y but in 3D” and the like. In time, entrepreneurs will learn how to take advantage of a world full of electromyographic sensors, volumetric screens, and passthrough displays. And consumers will respond to these creations, all the while revealing new use cases, wants, and needs to those same entrepreneurs. Eventually, a thing that seems trivial—a mobile phone, a touchscreen, a video game—becomes essential, and ends up changing the world in ways both predicted and never even considered. The iPhone is a brilliant demonstration of this point—and the last story I’ll tell in this book.

When he announced the first iPhone in January 2007, Steve Jobs described it as a revolutionary product. He was right, of course. But this first iPhone lacked an App Store and there were no plans to allow third-party developers to make apps. Why? Jobs told developers that “the full Safari engine is inside of iPhone. And so, you can write amazing Web 2.0 and Ajax apps that look exactly and behave exactly like apps on the iPhone.”^([19](#notes_split_012.xhtml#confr19)) But in October 2007, ten months after the iPhone had been unveiled and four months after it went on sale, Jobs changed his mind. A software development kit was announced for March 2008, with the App Store released in July of that year. Within a month, the million or so iPhone owners had downloaded 30% as many apps, in total, as the more than 40 million iTunes users had downloaded songs. Jobs then told the `Wall Street Journal`: “I would not trust any of our predictions because reality has so far exceeded them by such a great degree that we’ve been reduced to spectators just like you, watching this amazing phenomenon.”^([20](#notes_split_012.xhtml#confr20))
