
# One of the most important contributions to the ideas of simulated existence came via nonfiction

One of the most important contributions to the ideas of simulated existence came via nonfiction. In 1981, French philosopher and cultural theorist Jean Baudrillard coined the term “hyperreality” in his seminal treatise, *Simulacra and Simulation*. Baudrillard described hyperreality as a state in which reality and simulations were so seamlessly integrated that they were indistinguishable (just as Weinbaum had once imagined). Though many find this idea frightening, Baudrillard argued that what mattered was where individuals would derive more meaning and value—and speculated it would be in the simulated world.^([17](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr17)) Though Tencent did not mention Baudrillard when it unveiled its Metaverse vision, “hyper-digital reality,” the reference was quickly noted.

The same year Baudrillard published *Simulacra and Simulation*, the science fiction novelist William Gibson first read his short story “Burning Chrome,” which he subsequently expanded into the celebrated 1984 novel *Neuromancer*. The pair of stories popularized the term “cyberspace,” which had existed since the late 1960s but had not previously related to any digital technology. In *Neuromancer*, Gibson defined cyberspace as “a consensual hallucination experienced daily by billions of legitimate operators, in every nation.… A graphic representation of data abstracted from the banks of every computer in the human system. Unthinkable complexity. Lines of light ranged in the nonspace of the mind, clusters and constellations of data. Like city lights, receding.”^([18](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr18)) Early in the novel, the hacker Henry Case is poisoned by his employers as punishment for theft, with the mycotoxin damaging his nervous system so severely he can no longer connect to cyberspace. “For Case,” Gibson writes, “who’d lived for the bodiless exultation of cyberspace” and developed a “relaxed content for the flesh,” his body was now a prison.^([19](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr19)) When asked about Baudrillard’s influence on *Neuromancer* in 1991, Gibson said “he was a cool science fiction writer.”^([20](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr20))

As Baudrillard and Gibson were writing, the Walt Disney Company was in production on the family-rated feature film *Tron*, which released in 1982 and was written by Steven Lisberger and Bonnie MacBird, with Lisberger also directing. *Tron* focused on Kevin Flynn, portrayed by Jeff Bridges, a video game developer whose mind and body are accidentally uploaded into the virtual gameworld that he created. While trapped in his world, Flynn is forced into gladiatorial combat-to-the-death, and discovers sentient life as well as an AI dictator that has begun appropriating real-world infrastructure and programs to grow more powerful.

Though Flynn prevails, he is eventually stuck in “the Grid” for 30 years, leaving his young son fatherless. *Tron* was one of the first films to make extensive use of computer animation, with entire scenes composed exclusively of digital terrains, objects, and events, or with Bridges and a few physical items layered on top of its virtual world. The film was ultimately nominated for Best Costume Design and Best Sound at the 55th Academy Awards, but omitted in the Best Visual Effects category.^([††](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-052)) In 2010, Joseph Kosinski, who directed that year’s sequel to *Tron*, *Tron: Legacy*, told *Vanity Fair*, “There’s an interesting irony in the fact that the original *Tron* was disqualified from the visual-effects category … because \[members of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences\] considered using a computer cheating. The movie was ahead of its time; the ideas in it were ahead of their time. The idea of a virtual avatar, a personality for yourself that exists in an online world, at that point was very unique—something people couldn’t quite comprehend then but now it’s second nature.”^([21](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr21))

Originally envisioned as a graphic novel in 1989, *Snow Crash* was ultimately (and exclusively) published as a novel in 1992. And in the years that followed, many others continued to develop its themes. One such example was the 1999 film *The Matrix*, which was the fourth-highest-grossing film of the year globally. *The Matrix* took its name from *Neuromancer*, which had called visual abstraction of cyberspace the Matrix. In the film, which was written and directed by Lana Wachowski and Lilly Wachowski, “the Matrix” refers to a persistent simulation of the planet earth as it was in 1999, but that operates in the year 2199. The purpose of this simulation is to keep the minds of the incarcerated and incapacitated human race at peace so that their bodies can be used as bioelectric batteries by the sentient, but man-made, machines that conquered the planet earlier in the 22nd century. In the Wachowskis’ original shooting script, Morpheus, having recently “awakened” the protagonist Neo from the Matrix, explains that “as in Baudrillard’s vision, your whole life has been spent inside the map, not the territory.”^([22](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr22)) The line did not make the final cut, though the film retains a shot in which Neo picks up a copy of *Simulacra and Simulation* that has been hollowed out^([‡‡](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-051)) so it might better hide hard disks of his (illegal) hacking programs.^([23](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr23))

Another famous example came in 2011 with the publication of Ernest Cline’s *Ready Player One*, a sort of pop culture–infused version of *Snow Crash*. Set in the 2040s, the “real world” of *Ready Player One* suffers from various ecological, socioeconomic, and energy-related crises. In contrast to Gibson and Stephenson, Cline imagined that most of humanity had taken refuge in his imagined “Metaverse,” which he called the OASIS. Like the Metaverse, the OASIS was a digital and often fantastical simulation of reality that was accessed via a combination of VR headsets and various haptic peripherals, and which ran on a currency that was as valuable as those of the real world—and arguably more stable, too. The narrative of the book concerns the fight to control the multitrillion-dollar OASIS following the death of its owner, James Halladay, and keep it out of the hands of Nolan Sorrento, the CEO of IOI, which marshals a seemingly infinite number of human laborers inside the OASIS. The global film studio Warner Bros. optioned *Ready Player One* a year before its publication, with Steven Spielberg directing the 2018 adaptation.

## A Metaverse by Any Other Name

There is no answer for why Stephenson’s term “Metaverse” took hold while other terms from similarly popular, influential, and predictive novels did not. Having first entered the pop culture lexicon in the 1990s, perhaps “cyberspace” became popular too early to endure. Though a cult classic, *Tron* was far from a breakout success, limiting the impact of the “Grid.” The “Matrix” was probably contaminated by the R-rated themes of the Wachowskis’ hit film, as well as the term’s somewhat contradictory embrace by consumer-focused brands (in 2002, three years after *The Matrix* released in theaters, Toyota debuted the Matrix compact hatchback, which it produced for more than a decade). “Hyperreality” was likely too obscure and abstract, while “the OASIS” was too new. Moreover, it’s not yet clear—and is probably unlikely—that the term “Metaverse” will endure, even if Facebook’s new name does.
