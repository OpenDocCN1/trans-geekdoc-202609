
Specifically, Web3 advocates argue this dream is best enabled by (or at least most likely through) blockchains, which also use cryptocurrencies and tokens to compensate users who help “run” these blockchains. In this sense, a Web3 internet is one in which regular users not only consume the internet and create content on the internet, they also help to operate it and have an economic stake in it, too. Advocates of “Web 3.0” (note the small terminological difference from “Web3”) share similar aims, but do not see blockchains as necessary (or even useful) to achieving them—or to the need to securitize the internet’s operations. Vint Cerf says he has some skepticism about blockchains and the Metaverse, but believes that revising and expanding the Internet Protocol Suite, which has gone largely unchanged since the early 1990s, is key to the internet’s long-term health.^([11](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr11)) Sir Tim Berners-Lee, who believes the Metaverse is “going to be part of the future,” has called Web3 a “marketing hijack” that should be “ignored,” but insists that “Web 3.0” is “the real thing … a mid-course correction to take \[the internet\] back.”^([12](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr12))

The confusion between “Metaverse” and “Web3/Web 3.0” has several causes. Most obviously, the two terms rose to prominence at the same time and both are considered “successor states” to the internet as it’s known today. The evolution of online media formats from Web 1.0 to Web 2.0 also suggests that the embrace of a 3D/spatial internet would mean transitioning to Web3/Web 3.0. Some also believe blockchain technologies are required to actually run the Metaverse (though some Metaverse pioneers believe blockchains have no future at all), while others believe the Metaverse must emerge for the many blockchains and cryptocurrencies to justify their multitrillion-dollar combined valuation.

Despite their occasional similarities, the Metaverse and Web3/Web 3.0 remain definitionally distinct. The former does not require further decentralization of the internet (in fact, it would likely emerge more slowly as a result), while the latter does not require the development of a 3D internet. Regardless, the principles of Web3/Web 3.0 would likely produce a healthier Metaverse, even if it’s not technically required.

### Error #5: Considering the Metaverse a “replacement” to today’s internet, applications, and devices

Believing in the Metaverse does not require one to believe that society will stop using PCs or smartphones, abandon 2D applications and interfaces, or perform all digital labor and leisure inside 3D or spatial simulations.

Consider usage of the internet today. Though the “mobile and cloud” era of computing and networking is doubtless here, non-mobile devices such as laptops and PCs are still in use, “traditional” phone calls are common, most files are stored on local devices, billions still work “in person,” and restaurant, live sporting, and concert revenues have never been higher. And while more than half the traffic on the internet both originates and terminates on a mobile device, and over two-thirds of data is stored in the cloud, nearly all transmission of this data occurs via wired, not wireless, infrastructure, using a version of the Internet Protocol Suite that remains mostly unchanged since its formalization decades ago.

As was the case with the rise of both mobile networking and computing and cloud networking and computing, the Metaverse will likely result in new software, services, behaviors, use cases, and even hardware. In some cases, these new technologies will fully substitute for their predecessors. But more often, they will complement existing behaviors.

## Chapter 3 WHY NOW?

If the ideas behind the Metaverse are nearly a century old, if the Metaverse evolves from a technology more than a half century old, and if the term itself is 30 years old, it’s natural to wonder why the Metaverse came into focus in 2021—and to wonder when it might arrive.

New technological eras do not have launch dates. It’s possible to identify when a certain technology began development, when that technology first performed a given task, completed a public demonstration, or passed a sales milestone. But eras? They lack a singular starting moment. Instead, they emerge and are named only when the maturation of ecosystems proceeds to the point where sociologists, technologists, and regular people can identify widespread change. A great way to understand this dynamic is by considering the history of mobile computing.

### Dialing Back

The first mobile (i.e., cellular network) phone call was placed in New York City in 1973 using a Motorola cellphone. It’s difficult to argue the mobile computing era began that year, but the development of the two-and-a-half-pound prototype Motorola DynaTAC is nonetheless an essential starting point, in retrospect. To build its device, Motorola had to not just establish a new category but also make advances in portable, miniaturized, and rechargeable batteries and help establish new standards for long-range wireless data transmission. And the success of the first phone call, which was placed by Motorola researcher Martin Cooper as he walked between 53rd and 54th Streets on Sixth Avenue in Manhattan, also prompted companies such as Bell and NTT to build the wireless towers and networks that device manufacturers would need to be able to sell usable phones and, later, smartphones too.

In 1991, Norway launched the first “2G” network—the first network to support wireless data, rather than just voice communications (which powers SMS). The following year, IBM unveiled the first smartphone, the IBM Simon, which was made available for sale in 1994. That year also saw the release of Apple’s first portable computer, the Newton, though the device lacked a mobile networking chip and predated the creation of Wi-Fi by three years and, as such, could receive and send data only through physical cables and chips. By the late 1990s, Research in Motion’s various BlackBerry models had begun to take hold in governments, large enterprises, and information-focused businesses (e.g., finance, healthcare), with RIM going public in 1998. In 1999, the Wireless Application Protocol, or WAP, debuted, enabling a primitive version of the World Wide Web to be accessed from most phones—smart or not.^([\*](#chapter3.xhtml#footnote-047)) Though not part of the Internet Protocol Suite, WAP was the first mobile-specific protocol to see widespread adoption and facilitate widespread access.

In October 2001, a year and a half after the dotcom crash, Japan premiered the first commercial 3G network. The speeds enabled by 3G made it practical for smartphones to download more than text-based emails and WAP webpages, which in turn expanded the device’s appeal to consumers rather than just business users, and led to the development of the first major mobile-only media companies. December 2002, for example, is considered the start of the “ringtone” industry, after a Japanese wireless carrier launched its “Chaku-Uta” service, which let customers pay to download 45 clips of select songs, which could then be used instead of a traditional phone ringer. Mobile game downloads followed soon after.
