
Not long after Tencent publicly unveiled its vision of hyper-digital reality, the Communist Party of China (CCP) began its biggest-ever crackdown of its domestic gaming industry. Among several new policies was a prohibition on minors playing video games Monday through Thursday that also limited their play to between 8 p.m. and 9 p.m. on Friday, Saturday, and Sunday nights.^([22](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr22)) In addition, companies such as Tencent would use their facial recognition software and a player’s national ID to periodically ensure that these rules were not being skirted by a gamer borrowing an older user’s device. Tencent also pledged $15 billion in aid for “sustainable social value,” which Bloomberg said would be focused on “areas like increasing incomes for the poor, improving medical assistance, promoting rural economic efficiency and subsidizing education programs.”^([23](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr23)) Alibaba, China’s second-largest company, committed a similar amount only two weeks later. The message from the CCP was clear: look to your countrymen and women, not virtual avatars.

The Chinese government’s concerns about the growing role of gaming content and platforms in public life became more explicit in August of 2021, when the state-owned `Security Times` warned its readers^([§](#introduction.xhtml#footnote-061)) that the Metaverse is a “grand and illusionary concept” and “blindly investing \[in it\] will ultimately come back to bite you.”^([24](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr24)) Some commentators interpreted China’s various warnings, prohibitions, and taxes as confirmation of the Metaverse’s significance. For a communist and centrally planned country ruled by a single party, the potential of a parallel world for collaboration and communication is a threat, regardless of whether it’s run by a single corporation or decentralized communities.

Yet China’s approach to the Metaverse suddenly shifted in the summer of 2022. Regulators began to allow new game launches and even loosened some of its control over in-game content. China’s domestic giants, such as Alibaba, Tencent, and ByteDance, also resumed their talk of building the Metaverse. The first edition of this book was also approved for a local release in China, releasing within a month of its US publication, and four weeks later I was the cover story of the August 29, 2022, edition of `Bloomberg Businessweek China`.^([¶](#introduction.xhtml#footnote-060)) Alongside these apparent openings, however, was a new mandate for the Metaverse in China: it should be “independent and controllable,” while also increasing China’s influence over standards of a global Metaverse economy.^([25](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr25)) To this end, the government expanded its bans and prohibitions over blockchain technologies and similarly decentralized structures, and mandated that various national bodies, cities, and industry leaders collaborate to build Metaverse-focused computing, artificial intelligence, and 5G infrastructure.

Many of China’s stated ambitions were mirrored in Middle Eastern countries. Throughout 2022, the crown prince of Dubai, Sheikh Hamdan bin Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, spoke of his plans to make Dubai one of the top ten Metaverse economies globally, the Middle Eastern hub of the Metaverse, and a leader in the development of global standards for the Metaverse. In November, this strategy was formally presented to the nation and approved by Sheikh Mohammad bin Rashid Al Maktoum, the prime minister of the United Arab Emirates and Dubai’s ruler.^([26](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr26)) As part of this program, Sheikh Hamdan bin Mohammed announced plans to evaluate the country based on its “gross Metaverse product,” which “will be able to create billions of dollars in returns from Dubai without people being physically in the emirate but experiencing it in the Metaverse.”^([27](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr27))

As with the governments of China and South Korea, the European Parliament began to focus on the Metaverse in the months prior to Facebook’s name change. This interest was informed by the multiyear development of the EU’s Digital Markets Act, which was officially proposed to the European Commission in December 2020. The DMA was the largest overhaul of digital regulations in the internet’s 40-year history, many of which were intended to curb the powers and reach of the largest (and typically American) tech giants, such as Apple, Amazon, Facebook, Google, and Microsoft. The DMA thus helped propel these companies’ investments in virtual, augmented, and synthetic realities.

A week before the first public rumors of Facebook’s name change in 2021, Christel Schaldemose, a Danish member of the European Parliament who had also served as chief negotiator for the European Union on the DMA, told `Politiken` that “plans for Metaverse are deeply, deeply worrying” and that the union “has to take them into account.”^([28](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr28)) Scrutiny only increased after Facebook’s plans were revealed. In February of 2022, Margrethe Vestager, the EU’s commissioner for competition, told a group of German publishers that “the Metaverse is here already” and so the EU legislature and regulators must begin to act.^([29](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr29)) In September 2022, a report from the EU’s commissioner for internal market, Thierry Breton, stated the Metaverse was “one of the \[Union’s\] pressing challenges,” arguing that action was needed to ensure the Metaverse developed and operated in a manner consistent with European values and rules and with European influence, while warning of the need to prepare European internet infrastructure for the Metaverse. “We are ready to roll out our European ambition,” concluded Breton in his note titled “Europe’s Plan to Thrive in the Metaverse.”^([30](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr30)) By March of 2022, Vestager cautioned that the EU needed to be vigilant if it wanted to avoid a Metaverse dominated by American companies.^([31](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr31))

Regulators in the United States had similar fears to those of their EU counterparts—or at least fears that the existing tech giants would be able to use their cash reserves and market influence to ensure they dominated the budding Metaverse. In July 2022, the US Federal Trade Commission sued to block Facebook’s acquisition of Within, which was announced in October 2021. At the time, Within had fewer than a million estimated active users, but the FTC argued that in acquiring the company “Meta would be one step closer to its ultimate goal of owning the entire ‘Metaverse,’ ” which “poses a reasonable probability of eliminating both present and future competition. That lessening of competition may result in reduced innovation, quality, and choice, less pressure to compete for the most talented app developers.”^([32](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr32))

## Faster, Stronger, Harder, and Bound to Confuse

Perhaps no technology buzzword has ever risen so suddenly, so significantly, and with such expectations as “Metaverse” in 2021 and 2022. There have certainly been more popular terms, such as “mobile,” “cloud,” “personal computing,” and “the internet,” but interest in them built more steadily and it took years after the buzz began for companies such as Chevron or Panera Bread to seek related trademarks or patents, or for governments to take such strong preemptive action.

The shocking rise in usage of the term “Metaverse” led to understandable skepticism. Indeed, the tech industry has a history of using buzzwords that are hyped for far longer than they ultimately end up lasting in the market, such as 3D televisions, or that prove to be further away than originally promised, such as VR headsets or virtual assistants.
