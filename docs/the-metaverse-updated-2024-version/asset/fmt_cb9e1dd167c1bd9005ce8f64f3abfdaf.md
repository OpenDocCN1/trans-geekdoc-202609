
Until 1958’s *Tennis for Two*, all “video games” had been made to test computer-programming capabilities or evaluate human-computer interactions rather than for entertainment. Still, *Tennis for Two* had no leisurely origin story—and it still required several hundred pounds of professional equipment.^([22](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr22)) The title was created by William Higinbotham, an American physicist working at Brookhaven National Laboratory, which had been established by the US Atomic Energy Commission following World War II, and was based on research used to simulate the trajectory of missiles under various wind conditions. Specifically, *Tennis for Two*’s display ran on an oscilloscope, which graphically displayed voltage-based signals as waveforms. Higinbotham programmed his oscilloscope for three different voltage inputs—one each for the tennis court, its net, and a ball—that cycled so rapidly that a human player would see all three at the same time. The ball, meanwhile, was adapted from the missile arcs. As the name suggests, *Tennis for Two* was also a multiplayer title—the first in history. It was also the first video game whose graphics were in a constant state of change. Earlier titles with computer-generated graphics, such as *OXO*, changed only when a move was made.

The video game sector shifted from its infancy to childhood in 1962 following the release of the multiplayer combat title *Spacewar!* Originally created at MIT by Steve Russell and fellow members of the school’s Tech Model Railroad Club, *Spacewar!* became the first video game that was playable outside a single research institute and to be installed on multiple computers. In fact, Russell promptly open-sourced the title’s codebase, inspiring other computer scientists and hobbyists to use his creation to build their own.

The 1970s saw four major innovations in graphics-based simulations. The first was the release of the first video game console, 1972’s Magnavox Odyssey. For only $99.95 (equivalent to roughly $750 in 2024), a family could purchase an easy-to-use, shoebox-sized computer designed to play 28 games, including *Pong*, each of which was limited to white shapes on blank black screen and no sound. The second and third innovations came via MUDs and MUSHs, which pioneered both user-generated virtual world creation and the ability for multiple users to co-experience that world over the internet. The fourth advance was “3D,” though these early efforts focused on producing the sensation of 3D rather than “true 3D.” The first such example was *Maze*, which debuted in 1973 and was made by high school students Steve Colley, Greg Thompson, and Howard Palmer during a study program at NASA’s Ames Research Center.^([23](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr23)) *Maze* was a first-person-perspective title in which players navigated through a labyrinth of seemingly endless, identically designed hallways. Though users could move forward or backward, or take a left or right to travel down an intersecting hallway, their perspective was always straight ahead, with no ability to look up or down (vertical axis) or to the side (horizontal). When the player did make a turn, the very next frame would place them in the center of the new hallway, rather than show the user’s eyeline as it shifted across a doorframe and from one pathway to another. And if the player wanted to move forward or backward, they could do so only in fixed one-step increments. In truth, the maze didn’t even have a 3D model. Instead, it was composed of coordinate-based intersections of green lines that provided the illusion of angles and three dimensions.

In 1980, Atari released *Battlezone*, which let users take control of a tank viewport as they navigated a large two-dimensional map, that included various pyramid- and block-shaped obstacles, and fought enemy combatants. In contrast to *Maze*, players could look right and left, but the underlying mechanics were similar. Opponents and obstacles looked like they were 3D thanks to their geometric green outlines and 360-degree rotations, but this was an illusion similar to that produced by quickly flipping through a stack of papers that, sheet by sheet, portrayed a tank rotating from left to right. And while enemy tanks and hazards were often wider at the base than at their top, the tank could only ever shoot straight ahead and its shells, unlike the balls of 1958’s *Tennis for Two*, had no gravity-like arcs. As such, the varying widths were of purely aesthetic value (in fact, the game could have been played with only a single horizontal row of pixels). *Battlezone*’s background hills, meanwhile, would enlarge and shrink as the players moved backward or forward, but could never be reached.

In 1991, John Carmack, Adrian Carmack (no relation to John Carmack), Tom Hall, and John Romero founded id Software, which released the groundbreaking *Catacomb 3-D* in 1991, *Wolfenstein 3D* in 1992, and *Doom* in 1993. Though these titles seemed to be 3D—and offered full-color maps and models—they are not typically described as “true” or “full” 3D.

Imagine playing the classic *Pac-Man* arcade game, but with the camera (and all in-game assets) swung down 90 degrees from a bird’s-eye view into first person. The player, as Pac-Man, would see hallways dozens of feet long, several feet tall, and several feet wide, and be able to travel freely forward and backward, while also turning right or left at an intersection. Though this might seem 3D, the game’s ghosts, pellets, and fruit would still be flat 2D models with no sides or backs, let alone angles. These objects would sit squarely in the center of view at all times, too, and could not be ducked under or jumped over when moving forward. Meanwhile, the level itself would remain flat, with no stairs, hurdles, or even inclines or declines, with the player also unable to tilt their camera up or down. And because tilting the camera angle does not add any more detail to the map, moving forward or backward would, from a visual perspective, resemble zooming in or out from a given perspective (not unlike zooming in or out on a Google Street View image, which either tightens or widens the view, with the image blurring or sharpening accordingly).

id’s *Catacomb 3-D* effectively pioneered the *Pac-Man* example described above and, in doing so, created a thrill ride that felt not only 3D but new. The title was a fast-moving action shooter with large maps, swappable guns, depleting health and ammo meters that could be replenished through in-game items, secret doors the player could push through to reveal hidden pathways, and the ability for players to move “naturally” across its map, rather than jump across preset chess- or checkers-like positions.

In 1992, id debuted *Doom*, which maintained the 3D approach of 1991’s *Catacomb 3-D*, but seemed to offer players the ability to travel to higher and lower levels. Mechanically, it was akin to visually elevating half of a *Pac-Man* maze. The titular hero might seem to fall down from or jump up to this higher level, but there would be no real interaction between the two layers, and the process of moving from one to another was just an in-game animation. In the case of *Doom*, a player sitting on the top of a ledge would still be shooting straight, but their bullets would hit an enemy down below as though they were standing side by side. And if the player were below the ledge, they could not “shoot” up, just straight into the wall. And above the wall was just more wall that looked like the top of the ledge. Though these were tricks, the result was still stunning—especially given that *Doom*, unlike *Catacomb 3-D*, supported local and internet-based multiplayer.
