
All of the benefits of a Web3 internet apply to the Metaverse. Many are amplified. On top of these points, the blockchain is often seen as the only way to solve many of the Metaverse’s most intractable problems, such as its insatiable need for computing power. In 2017, long before the crypto boom of 2021, Tim Sweeney said that people will “come to the realization that the blockchain is really a general mechanism for running programs, storing data, and verifiably carrying out transactions. It’s a superset of everything that exists in computing. We’ll eventually come to look at it as a computer that’s distributed and runs a billion times faster than the computer we have on our desktops, because it’s the combination of everyone’s computer.”^([46](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr46))

NFTs are a particular area of optimism, with advocates seeing the potential to solve many of the interoperability issues reviewed in [Chapter 8](#chapter8.xhtml). Consider the adage “Possession is nine-tenths of the law.” Under centralized server models, a user can never truly take ownership of a virtual good. Instead, they are simply provided access to a good that’s held, via digital record, on someone else’s property, such as a server. And even if the user takes that data off that server and puts it onto their own hard drive, that’s not enough. Why? Because the rest of the world needs to acknowledge that data and agree on its use. Blockchains can do this by design; anyone who reads it sees the owner and the underlying structures. The sense of possession is augmented by another key property right: the unrestricted right to resale. When a user buys an NFT from a given game, the trustless and permissionless nature of blockchain means that the game’s maker cannot block the sale of that NFT at any point. They’re not even actively informed of it. Similarly, it is impossible for a developer to “lock” blockchain-based assets into their virtual world or technically prevent them from being used elsewhere—the reverse of the typical problem with virtual entitlements.

Token structures also solve for economic models. The blockchain would store assets, rather than individual servers, with network validators compensated for its incorporation into various apps and with crypto wallets consolidating a user’s assets across these many apps. The NFT model also ensures that even if a duplicate version of this virtual good is minted, the original remains distinct and “original”—like a signed and dated painting listed as one of one. Then there are smart contract–based royalties, which enable those who create an NFT to profit in perpetuity from their works and therefore also from their usage by outside developers. In January 2022, Ethereum’s Buterin also began development of a new form of NFT, “soulbound” NFTs, which can never be transferred and so will encourage the collection of NFTs for reasons beyond financial speculation.^([47](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr47))

## Present Reality

If the vision of Web3 is so positive and capable, what accounts for the widespread skepticism of and resistance to blockchain technology? For one, critics of the blockchain correctly point out that the supposed benefits often gloss over its limitations, drawbacks, and impossibilities. It’s also true that the crypto boom from 2020 through 2022 benefited from a poor understanding of these same issues, and many of the field’s greatest achievements are not technical innovations but legal or financial ones. To evaluate such arguments, it’s necessary to, again, start with database operations.

Irrespective of how transactions are validated (e.g., proof-of-work, proof-of-stake, or any other consensus system), it will always take more time and effort for multiple parties to validate a single financial transaction or nonfinancial record change than for a single entity to do so. Given the scarcity of computing resources today—and especially the needs of the Metaverse—even small inefficiencies have great costs. And modern blockchains are very inefficient by traditional measures. While Visa boasts that its network can process more than 24,000 transactions per second (TPS), the Bitcoin blockchain can manage only 7 TPS, while the proof-of-work version of Ethereum was limited to roughly 10 to 15 TPS.^([48](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr48)) Given this, transactions can take 15 seconds and five minutes to confirm, while Bitcoin averages ten minutes.^([49](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr49)) The fee for this work is also high and variable. During 2021, Ethereum’s gas fee averaged more than $5 and shot up to tens of dollars during periods of peak congestion—sums impractical for most daily purchases and many NFTs. That same year, Bitcoin averaged between $0.50 and $2.50, but was also prone to spikes that crossed the $40 mark (more to the point, these surges were typically caused by heavy volatility in bitcoin’s price, which meant that the ten-or-so-minute delay in a transaction could cost users dearly). And high though these gas fees could be, the price would be even greater if users wanted to bid their way up the transaction queue.

There is technical progress underway to address each of these issues. Ethereum’s 2022 merge increased transactional capacity to an average of 25 TPS, but in 2023, fees still swung as high as $40.^([50](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr50)) Buterin believes that various proposals to Ethereum 2 could increase TPS to between 2,000 and 3,000 within a few years,^([51](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr51)) with his current roadmap for Ethereum ending with 10,000 TPS.^([52](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr52)) Such promises seem fantastical, but keep in mind that work on Ethereum’s PoS network began before the PoW version of Ethereum even debuted, and the merge occurred 20 months after the test network launched—all of which is to say, preparations are long-running.

Many of Ethereum’s scaling solutions already help to reduce its congestion, costs, and speeds, too. Polygon, an Ethereum Layer 2, regularly achieves 7,000 TPS and claims to be capable of roughly ten times that, with fees in 2023 averaging less than $0.02.^([53](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr53)) Then there are the alternatives to Ethereum. Solana, a “high-performance” Layer 1 alternative to Ethereum, claims to have hit 65,000 TPS on its test network and regularly achieves 800 to 8,000^([54](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr54)) due to its novel proof-of-stake and proof-of-history architecture (though when normalizing its methodology to Ethereum, Solana’s TPS is more likely between 400 and 800^([55](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr55))).

Yet even if 100,000 or 1,000,000 TPS is achieved, the Metaverse will need many billions to operate “on chain” and, despite the need for distributed networks of computers to validate these transactions, to do so in tens of milliseconds, not ten seconds or two minutes. As Tim Sweeney tweeted in January 2021, blockchain technology “is going places” but the “state of the art is far from the 60Hz transactional medium needed for 100M’s of concurrent users in a real-time 3D simulation.”^([56](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr56)) Some doubt that blockchains will ever get there.

One common problem faced by all blockchains is the “trilemma,” so coined by Buterin in 2017, which refers to the interconnect trade-offs required for a blockchain to achieve “scalability” and “security.”^([57](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr57)) A blockchain can increase its scalability (high TPS count) by reducing decentralization or reducing security, but one of the two must give. And increased security means less scalability, as does greater decentralization.
