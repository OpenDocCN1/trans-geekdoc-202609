
In the 1990s, the average AAA open-world title might require only tens of megabytes (the 1998 Nintendo 64 title *The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time*), with the largest reaching perhaps 1.5 GB (the 1997 PlayStation title *Final Fantasy 7*, which required three CD-ROMs). By the late 2010s, the smallest of these titles would be 15 GB (2017’s *The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild*, which supported only 30 fps and 1080p) and the largest passing 100 or even 200 GB (2016’s *Final Fantasy XV*, up to 240 fps and 4K).^([17](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr17))

To aid the growing cost of virtual assets and the growing number of these assets used in a virtual world, new businesses and business lines emerged that were dedicated to producing assets for third-party licensing; there also arose a lucrative trade in marketplaces to aggregate those assets and sell their rights. The two largest such stores, which are operated by Unity and Epic Games, span several million assets. Many of these assets were originally designed for first-party use, but their developer later made them available for sale to help recoup the up-front investment in these assets. In 2019, Epic also made a trio of acquisitions intended to help build out its store. 3Lateral and Cubic Motion were used to create Epic’s “MetaHumans,” a sort of hyperrealistic avatar maker that can also be mapped to a smartphone camera or even professional-grade tracking solutions, while Quixel equipped the Epic’s asset store with “Megascans” of real-world environments that capture entire fjords and mesas down to every slab of stone, pebble, and scrape.

If asset stores are helpful when constructing fictional virtual worlds, they’re invaluable when making real ones. Few companies on earth could have produced *Microsoft Flight Simulator* save for Microsoft, which not only boasts the world’s second-largest cloud computing business but is also a market leader in the mapping and geospatial services that own and commission millions of satellite and aerial photos of the planet, in part for their use on its own Google Street View alternative, Bing Streetside. Despite this, neither *MSFS* nor Bing are entirely based on Microsoft’s proprietary data set. It also licenses traffic data, weather data, and supplementary maps covering topography and particularly remote regions of the world. The company also makes extensive use of OpenStreetMap, a free and open geographic database that spans thousands of underlying licenses. But even if companies such as Microsoft and Tesla can afford to build complete three-dimensional models of the world—or, say, specific large cities—such investments would be extraordinarily expensive and never ending because the world is constantly changing. This need, in turn, has led to assets stores, licensors, and marketplaces for the “real world.”

An example is Cesium, which manages and integrates a large pipeline of geospatial data (environmental scans, point clouds, photogrammetry data, and renders) from various sources (e.g., open libraries, its own database, private client clouds, dedicated mapping and licensing organizations). These data sets can then be cloud-streamed via any proprietary engine to create real-time visualizations using the 3D Tiles open standard. This allows any “developer” (a city, construction company, game maker, events company, etc.) to leverage simulations as part of its everyday work, such as site planning, measuring environmental change, tracking drivers in Le Mans, or launching a satellite.

Another area where economics are leading to 3D standardization is in game engines. Although the opportunity in virtual worlds has never been as large as it is today, that means that reaching the entirety of this market has also never been so difficult. In the 1980s, a developer might make a game for just one console and, in doing so, reach 70% of potential players; supporting two would mean reaching the entire addressable market. Today, there are three console makers, two of which operate consoles in two different console generations, plus a series of cloud-based consoles that use their own technology stacks, including Nvidia’s GeForce Now and Amazon’s Luna. There are also two PC platforms, Mac and Windows, that span dozens to hundreds of different hardware builds, and two dominant mobile computing platforms, iOS and Android, that span many more operating system (OS) versions, GPUs, CPUs, and other chipsets. Every additional platform, device, or build requires code that is unique to a specific set of hardware, but creating and supporting all this code is costly, time-consuming, and arduous—unless the developer writes it so generically that the title’s performance on any platform is constrained by the capabilities of any other one. Another option is to just ignore a large part of the market altogether (i.e., various OSs).

This challenge, when combined with the ever-increasing complexity of virtual worlds, is why cross-platform game engines such as Unity and Unreal have proliferated. They emerged partly as a response to fragmentation, and they don’t just solve the problem but do it at a low cost. Imagine a developer trying to pick a smartphone OS for their next game. Apple’s mobile ecosystem has 60% of smartphone share in the United States—80% share among teenagers—and over two-thirds of mobile gaming revenues globally. In addition, a developer can reach nearly 90% of iOS users by writing to only a dozen iPhone SKUs. The remainder of the global market is split among thousands of different Android devices. Forced to choose between these two platforms, a developer would always pick iOS. Unity lets the same developer easily publish their game across all platforms (including web), thereby increasing their revenue potential by over 50% at little incremental cost. In the abstract, Apple might prefer more exclusive games and games fully optimized for their own hardware, but it’s better for everyone, including iOS users and the App Store, that most mobile developers use Unity. By making more money, developers can build more and better games, prompting even more user spending on mobile devices.

The standardization of assets and the standardization of engines are intertwined processes. The availability of millions of detailed, diverse, and low-cost assets makes it harder for any virtual world builder to invest in—let alone develop or contribute to—fringe file formats or engines. But the potency of these parallel and intersecting processes might best be told through a specific example outside gaming.

### The Happiest Place(s) in Any Galaxy

Traditionally, films relied exclusively on non-real-time rendering software such as Autodesk’s Maya, Pixar’s RenderMan, or SideFX’s Houdini, rather than real-time alternatives such as Unreal or Unity. There were several rationales for this choice. First, real-time solutions were not designed for the levels of fidelity expected of IMAX-grade feature films and, until recently, struggled to produce such quality. Second, there was no need to produce a frame in milliseconds, and instead, it made sense to spend anywhere from a few seconds to several hours to render a frame if that extra time produced a more realistic or detailed image. And the goal of the computer graphics department was to virtually produce an already-known image (that is, one based on a storyboard). This means that there was no need to build assets for real-time usage—such as a model of the Avengers Tower in New York City that had the requisite detail to be viewed from any perspective, rather than just in a fixed position in a camera shot from a half kilometer away, or that might respond to any alien fire or attack instead of just those Joss Whedon had specified in his script. Building an entire, or even partial, model of Manhattan made even less sense.
