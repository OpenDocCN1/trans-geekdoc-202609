
The focus on non-real-time assets and rendering did come with a cost: a film or TV show’s digital models were almost always “single use”—even if a subsequent production would end up making a nearly identical model later on. Studios knew that this was theoretically wasteful, but there was no clear business case to make for additional investments in the hopes of later reuse, and later productions that might want to use those assets might use a different rendering software anyway. By contrast, Hollywood’s cost-benefit approach to physical sets, costumes, and items is typically just a matter of storage costs and there’s no worry of whether a sword from one Roman epic might be physically compatible with another. As such, it’s common for everything from a sword to a rock to a Victorian headpiece to be reused across scores of unrelated productions.

There will always be financial trade-offs between creating single-use assets for a given production and investing in detail/metadata that can more easily support future ones, and similarly, between creating new assets and repurposing or updating old ones. However, advances in real-time simulation and related hardware, the increasing value of transmedia franchises, and the growing importance of computer-generated imagery are collectively reducing the costs of developing real-time assets while increasing the return from investing in them.

The blockbuster 2019 Disney film *The Lion King* provides an illustrative use case. Although the entry looks like one of the company’s live-action remakes, such as *Mulan* or *Beauty and the Beast*, nearly all of the film’s sets and all of its characters are computer-animated. To provide the film with an “ultrarealistic, documentary-style” aesthetic, rather than just photorealism, director Jon Favreau had most of the film’s virtual set designed in Unity’s real-time game engine.^([†](#chapter8.xhtml#footnote-020)) This provided Favreau with an unprecedented ability to understand and test the film as it was being produced. In many cases, Favreau would put on a VR headset to explore as its many characters performed their roles, which experience enabled him to alter camera angles, lighting, blocking, and other aspects of the set. If he wanted to know what a scene would look like from the back of a gazelle or at night rather than day, he need not wait nor invest in potentially wasteful VFX work—the answer was available within seconds because nearly all objects and environments were in real time. *The Lion King* went on to gross $1.7 billion globally, more than any other film produced in the history of Walt Disney Pictures.

Only a few months after *The Lion King*’s release, Disney debuted Disney+, arguably the company’s most important single product launch in its nearly century-long history. And the service’s signature launch title? *The Mandalorian*, the first-ever live-action *Star Wars* TV series, which also happened to have Favreau as its creator and one of its key writers. Unlike *The Lion King*, *The Mandalorian* mostly starred human performers and made extensive use of practical effects and sets—but although the series spanned forests, deserts, and icescapes, every minute was shot on a single Manhattan Beach soundstage. This soundstage was also designed for game engines—Epic’s Unreal Engine this time—not green screens. The performers and physical sets of *The Mandalorian* were surrounded by a 20-foot-tall, 75-foot-wide, and 270-degree LED screen that projected, in real time, the show’s fantastic world, including its backdrops and some of its epic monsters, using the Unreal Engine. This technical approach meant that everyone inside the LED “volume” could experience what Favreau did in a VR—but without wearing a headset. It also meant that “real people” could be seen inside the environment too—rather than everyone just watching preplanned animations of Timon and Pumbaa. In addition, the cast could be affected by the volume’s LEDs; the light shining down from a virtual sun would recolor an actor directly and provide them with an accurate shadow, which would then not need to be applied or corrected in postproduction. A set could have the perfect sunrise year-round—and years later, that exact same setting could be summoned in a matter of seconds. *The Mandalorian* went on to receive an Outstanding Series nomination at the 2020 Primetime Emmy Awards, the first time a *Star Wars* entry was nominated for the highest creative honor in TV. It has remained the most popular original title for Disney+ across its first four years.

Hollywood’s adoption of “virtual production” doesn’t just make it easier to film a sequel—it makes it easier to make all derivative works. It’s no coincidence that Disney’s Star Wars: Galaxy’s Edge theme park attractions run on the Unreal Engine, and when the company adds AR and VR functionality to its parks, Unreal will be the likely choice then as well. By standardizing around Unreal and the associated asset formats and metadata conventions, it becomes easier for the company to repurpose existing models, such as those of Din Djarin’s Razor Crest or Nevarro City, and impractical to change technology stacks that require reconstructing these assets. And if Peloton wants to sell a course set on the Death Star or the Avengers Campus, it can repurpose (i.e., license, pay for) much of what Disney has already made—thereby saving money that, at a minimum, will make the resulting courses more affordable to Peloton’s customers, if not financially practical in the first place. If Tinder wants to offer virtual dates on Mustafar, the same logic applies. Instead of playing blackjack via the video-based iCasino, why not play on Canto Bight? Rather than launch a *Star Wars* integration in *Fortnite*, Disney will just populate its own miniworlds on *Fortnite Creative* using what they’ve already built. The many video game publishers that license Star Wars IP, too, might reconsider their own engine choices if using Unreal means access to the hundreds of millions in assets Disney has already developed. This is how standardization happens—decisions that were once made in isolation, such as a CTO’s preference for Old Technology A or New Technology B, are instead shaped by the activities of the broader market.

The network effects around engines and assets are particularly profound when it comes to real-world simulations. As an example, take the 56-acre, 20-building, multibillion-dollar redevelopment of Water Street in Tampa, Florida. As part of this project, Strategic Development Partners enlisted the XR real estate software company Imerza to produce a 17-foot-diameter, 3D-printed, modular scale model of the city, which was then supported by twelve 5K laser cameras that projected 25 million pixels atop this model, based on city data feeds for weather, traffic, population density, and whatever else.^([18](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr18)) All of this was run by an Unreal-based real-time-rendered simulation that could be viewed through a touchscreen or VR headset.
