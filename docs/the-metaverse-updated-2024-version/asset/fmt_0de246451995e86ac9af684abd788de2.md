
In 2019, the scientific journal *Nature* published a paper that argued Google had produced the first quantum computer to achieve “quantum supremacy”—the ability to solve a problem faster than any traditional computer. Specifically, Google claimed its Sycamore took 200 seconds to complete a task that would take the world’s most powerful traditional computer, the IBM-developed Summit at the Oak Ridge Laboratory in Tennessee, 10,000 years to complete.^([§](#chapter6.xhtml#footnote-028)) In 2023, Google unveiled an updated Sycamore whose quantum processor was reportedly 241 million times more powerful than its 2019 predecessor.^([18](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr18)) However, the quantum computing industry still faces many obstacles—and the extraordinary cost to construct these systems.^([¶](#chapter6.xhtml#footnote-027)) Qubits are incredibly sensitive to their local environment and can change state due to noise, vibrations, temperature changes, electromagnetic radiation, contact with neighboring particles, and more. The result is “quantum error” that is not just difficult (and costly) to address but difficult to notice—because it would take 10,000 years for a world-class computer to solve the problem attempted by Sycamore, Google could not directly verify its full results, and instead used extrapolations and spot examinations of key areas.^([19](#notes_split_006.xhtml#ch6fr19)) Now that’s some netcode.

## Chapter 7 VIRTUAL WORLD ENGINES AND PLATFORMS

A virtual tree falls in a virtual forest. The last two chapters explained what is required for the tree to be rendered, and for its fall to be processed and then made available to any observer. But what is this tree? Where is this tree? What is the forest? The answer is data and code.

Data describes the attributes of a virtual object, such as its dimensions or color. For Berkeley’s virtual tree to be processed by a CPU and rendered by a GPU, this data needs to be run by code. And if a user wants to chop down that tree and use its wood to build a bed or light a fire, that code must be part of a much broader framework of code^([\*](#chapter7.xhtml#footnote-026)) that operates the virtual world.

The real world isn’t altogether different. The laws of physics are the code that reads and runs all interactions—from the reasons a tree falls to how this produces vibrations in the air that travel to a human ear and cause nerves to relay information via electrical signal across various synapses. Similarly, a tree being “seen” by a human observer means it reflected light produced by (usually) the sun, light that is in turn received and processed by the human eye and brain.

But there is a key difference: the real world is fully preprogrammed. Humans can’t see X-rays or echolocate, but the requisite information already exists in the world. In a game, X-rays and echolocation require data and a lot of code. If a user goes home, mixes up ketchup and petroleum, and then tries to eat it or paint with it, the laws of physics take care of the results. For a game to manage the same interaction, it needs to know in advance what ketchup and petroleum do when they’re combined (and probably in generic ratios), or it needs to know enough about the two for the game’s logic to figure it out, assuming the game is capable. A virtual world’s logic might say that petroleum cannot be mixed with anything. Or that it can be mixed only with oil. Or that if it’s mixed with anything at all, it produces unusable sludge. But a more complicated result requires considerably more data and for the virtual world’s logic to be far more comprehensive. How much petroleum can be added to ketchup before it’s inedible? How much ketchup can be added to petroleum before it’s unusable? How do the resulting substance’s color and viscosity change based on the ratio of one ingredient to the other?

The fact that so many of these permutations have little value is hugely valuable to those who produce virtual worlds. Because the hero of *The Legend of Zelda* does not need to go to space, no space-based physics are required. *Call of Duty*’s players don’t need kayaks, enchantments, or baking ingredients; the game’s developer did not create the relevant code. Nintendo and Activision could focus more data and code on what their virtual worlds need and benefit from, rather than on infinite permutations that have limited practical value to their games.

For all its efficiency, this approach introduces obstacles to building Metaverse-like virtual worlds, and especially in establishing interoperability among them. In *Microsoft Flight Simulator*, a pilot can land a helicopter beside a football field, but there’s no football game he can then observe, let alone join. For Microsoft to offer such functionality, it would need to build its own football system, even though many developers have already done so and, with years of experience, are likely better at it, too. While *MSFS* could instead try to integrate these football-specific virtual worlds, each party’s data structures and codes are typically incompatible. In fact, it’s probable that the many developers who have built football-specific games, or have integrated football into their video games, couldn’t make much use of one another’s code, either, and must instead endlessly build and rebuild everything from the layout of a football field to the physics of a ball flying through the air. Not only is this disconnected work counter to the very idea of the Metaverse, but it’s also financially exhausting—especially as virtual worlds have increased in size, detail, and complexity. According to Nexon, one of the world’s largest video game publishers, the average number of staff credits for an open-world action game has grown from roughly 1,000 in 2007 to over 4,000 by 2018, with budgets growing by a factor of ten (roughly two and a half times faster).^([1](#notes_split_007.xhtml#ch7fr1))

The previous two chapters of this book covered the networking and computing power required to share, operate, and render the Metaverse. The next two will focus on the code that will constitute and unite it.

### Game Engines

The core of a virtual world’s code is typically contained in a “game engine,” a loosely defined term that refers to the bundle of technologies and frameworks that help to build a game, render it, process its logic, and manage its memory, among other functions. In a simplified sense, a game engine establishes the virtual laws of a virtual world’s universe—the ruleset that defines all interaction and possibilities.
