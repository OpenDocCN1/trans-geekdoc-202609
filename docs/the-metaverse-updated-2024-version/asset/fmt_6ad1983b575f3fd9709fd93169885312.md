
While Epic Games can rightly say that more than 12.5 million people attended this live concert, these attendees were split across 250,000 separate copies of the event (meaning, they watched 250,000 versions of Scott) that didn’t even start at the same time.

Another good example comes from `World of Warcraft`. To play, users must first pick a “realm”—a discrete server that manages a complete copy of the roughly 1,500-square-kilometer virtual world, and from which they cannot see or interact with any other. In this sense, it may be more accurate to call the game “Worlds” of Warcraft. Users can move between realms, thereby philosophically uniting these many worlds into a single “massively multiplayer online game,” but each realm is capped at several hundred participants, and if there are too many users in a specific area, the game creates several distinct and temporary copies of this area, while splitting groups of users among them.

The best example, though, is CCP Games’ `EVE Online`, a technical marvel that debuted months before `Second Life` or MySpace. Save for occasional downtime for maintenance or updates, `EVE Online` has run continually since its debut on May 6, 2003, and its virtual world has never reset. This virtual world is also enormous, spanning nearly 8,000 star systems and nearly 70,000 planets. And unlike `Fortnite` or `World of Warcraft`, all of its users are in the same game and at the same time, with no distinct matches or realms.

The scale of `EVE Online` is possible due to its innovative systems architecture, as well as its brilliantly flexible creative design. The virtual world of `EVE Online` is essentially just empty three-dimensional space with wallpaper backgrounds that look like a galaxy. Users cannot truly visit a planet and activities such as mining are more akin to setting up a Gmail account than constructing a virtual rig. As such, the persistence of `EVE Online` primarily concerns a relatively modest set of “entitlements” (an industry term), such as a player’s ships and resources. The result is less computational work for CCP Games’ servers, and for its users’ devices, which need not render a virtual world that is constantly changing form, just the few objects changing inside of it.

Furthermore, very little happens in `EVE Online` on a daily, quarterly, or even yearly basis. This is because the goal in `EVE Online`, to the extent one exists, is for various factions of players to conquer planets, systems, and galaxies. This is achieved primarily through the establishment of corporations, formation of alliances, and strategic positioning of fleets. Indeed, much of `EVE Online` actually takes place in the “real world,” via third-party messaging applications and emails, and not even on CCP’s servers. Users have spent years planning attacks, going undercover with enemy guilds in order to later betray them, and creating enormous personal networks that trade resources and construct new ships. While large-scale battles do happen, they’re remarkably rare—and involve the destruction of assets in the virtual world (e.g., ships) rather than of the virtual world itself. The former is far easier for a processor to manage than the latter because it is about removing objects in the world, not altering the world. The nature of space-based combat also means that action is limited in variety and relatively simple in mechanics. Ordering a ship to mine resources from a planet, or send a succession of blasts from and to a fixed position, is far less complex than a pair of individually animated avatars dancing, jumping, and shooting each other.

Because `EVE Online` is set in the vastness of space, most users are far away from one another—enabling CCP Games’ servers to effectively treat them as being in separate virtual worlds until necessary, just like `Warcraft`. And while players do sometimes want to converge on the same location—such as a battle site—the mechanics of `EVE Online` spread out this behavior and dissuade it, too. Depending on the ship and intended travel distance, it can take tens of seconds to several minutes for a given ship to warp to another system—and getting to its final destination might require several systems warps. The time involved in such flights has a direct and indirect benefit for CCP Games. First, it reduces the risk of too many ships arriving at any given moment and instead distributes these arrivals over a few minutes or more. Second, it discourages users from relocating a large number of their ships—or moving in the first place—as the user might not be able to return fast enough should an attack occur after they leave. “Warps” also deplete a ship’s capacitor, thereby producing a resource-related cost to relocation. To aid instances in which there are too many user activities (e.g., ship relocations) or instructions (e.g., to continue to fire on an opponent’s ship), `EVE Online`’s engine will deploy “time-dilation,” slowing the game’s passage of time from one in-game second being equivalent to one real-world second to as little as one-to-ten, which enables the game’s server to catch up and properly synchronize all inputs and outputs. While `EVE`’s time-dilation math is fictional, the concept broadly reflects the theory that spacetime dilates in response to the density of heavy and/or high-velocity objects.

`EVE`’s scale, reliance on real-world decisions, and focus on infrastructure, inventories, and locations, rather than virtual outfits or gunfights, makes it a better representation of what the “real world” Metaverse is likely to be like than games such as `Fortnite` or `Roblox`—and easier to operate than these titles, too. Even so, `EVE Online` inevitably encounters problems. At one point in the 2000s, a group of players realized that a specific star system, Yulai, sat near many high-traffic planets inside a major star cluster, making it an enticing spot to establish a new trading hub.^([14](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr14)) They were right. Not long after setting up shop, many buyers began to flock to the area, which attracted additional sellers, then more buyers, and so on. Ultimately, the number of transactions that were occurring inside this hub caused the CCP Games’ servers to start to buckle, leading the publisher to alter the `EVE Online` universe so that the destination would be less convenient to visit.

The lessons from “the Yulai Problem” helped CCP Games design, expand, and overhaul its maps in the years that followed. But in the real world, trade hubs can’t be moved, nor can travel distances be altered. And no matter how the universe of `EVE Online` is distributed, CCP cannot prevent another inevitable computing challenge: the sudden outbreak of battles so strategically important that thousands of users suddenly converge to save their faction or defeat another. In January 2021, the largest battle in `EVE`’s history occurred. It was more than twice the size of the prior record and the culmination of a nearly seven-month escalation between the Imperium Faction and a coalition of enemies called PAPI. Or at least, it should have been. The only real losers were CCP Games’ servers, which could not keep up with 12,000 players appearing in a single system, and any of those players who were hoping for a decisive victory.^([15](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr15)) Roughly half of the players were unable to ever enter the system, while many of those who did were placed in a sort of purgatory—if they logged in to the game, they’d likely be destroyed before having a chance to enter any coherent commands, while leaving meant that their server spot might be taken up by an enemy that would destroy their allies. There was an eventual winner—Imperium—but this was mostly by default, as the defender naturally wins in a battle that never actually takes place.
