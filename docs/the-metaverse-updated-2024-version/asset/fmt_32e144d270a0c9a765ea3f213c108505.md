
Fortunately, the nonprofit Ethereum Foundation had been investigating a potential switch from PoW to PoS even before the blockchain’s launch, with a parallel PoS “test network” (Ethereum 2) launching in December 2020. In April 2022, the foundation approved plans to merge Ethereum 1’s records into Ethereum 2, which would henceforth be renamed Ethereum.^([30](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr30)) In August of that year, the foundation announced the merge would occur the following month, take roughly 15 minutes, and have no impact on token holders, developers, or system capabilities, but would permanently reduce the energy needed to process a transaction by 99.95% and potentially double or triple network throughput, too.^([31](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr31)) The merge was completed successfully and on schedule, with no known errors or losses. Validators and developers were also free to remain on the newly named “EthereumW” (as in Ethereum Proof-of-Work, or EthW for short) and, crucially, the foundation could not shut down EthereumW even if it wanted to! The immutability of EthW meant that it could stop operating only if there were no validators to run its network. And as long as there were developers and users who preferred EthW, validators would be financially incentivized to support the network—especially if most validators shifted to Ethereum 2, but the community largely remained on EthW. Nearly all users, developers, and validators did shift to Ethereum 2, though EthW was still in operation in Q4 2023.

## Breaking the Cycle

Blockchain’s collectivist orientation extends down into the application layer, too, where it again stands in contrast to the arc of Web 2.0. When services such as Google Maps, Gmail, Facebook, and Instagram launched in the late 2000s and early 2010s, they were considered revelations for consumers. Not only were they far better than prior solutions—where those existed at all—but they also tended to be free. A decade or so later, the situation feels quite different. In return for “free service,” users provided these services with “free data” that was of considerable value and constitutes the foundation for corporations of unprecedented profit and control. Many users now believe these companies have also harmed the world through the spread of mis- and disinformation, rampant privacy violations, algorithmic manipulation, and the relentless pursuit of profit over happy users. Their products, too, seem to have become more expensive and less useful. Searches made on Google and Amazon are increasingly cluttered with “sponsored results”—and these results are often difficult to distinguish from the genuine article. Despite this, the products of “big tech” rarely face competitive pressure. There are many reasons for this, such as the large and talented staffs wielded by these companies, their influence over consumer hardware (as discussed in [Chapter 10](#chapter10.xhtml)), and the billions in data-center or warehouse costs required to operate a large-scale service in 2024 and attract customers. One of the easiest impediments to understand is the decades of customer data the tech companies have collected and retained.

Amazon’s recommendations are powerful because they are based on years of prior searches and can also leverage a detailed understanding of a user’s prior purchases (which offers clues about what they already own and the price they might pay). Lacking such data, competitors will always have a hard path to stealing away an Amazon customer—even if they did have the equivalent inventory, prices, and delivery networks. It is certainly true that Instagram and Facebook users can download all of their photos in the form of a ZIP file, then upload them to a competing service, but it’s not an easy process and there’s no way to carry over each photo’s likes and comments or reassemble one’s social graph. In the end, a user who feels that Facebook is adversely affecting their mental health has one true alternative: to abandon the service altogether and, with it, the many years of memories and to some degree friendships. As [Chapter 10](#chapter10.xhtml) summarized, Steam’s control over player entitlements, achievements, and social graphs allowed it to beat away competitors offering exclusive games at two-thirds the price and with greater rights to developers and players alike.

Even when applications do support external data sharing via APIs, they are typically shut down whenever the application seeks out the revenues of its one-time developer partners. Facebook long allowed Tinder users to sign up for the dating app using their Facebook account, which would also help populate their Tinder profile. In the abstract, Tinder would prefer that all of its users establish and fill out stand-alone profiles, which would provide the company with greater data independence, more insight into its users, and the ability to establish its own social graph. Weighed against these benefits were several considerations. First, Tinder, unlike Facebook, was not intended to be a lifelong service for its users—but instead one used for days to months at a time, with users typically deleting their account after use. Tinder also wanted to make creating a profile “effortless,” which would help distinguish the service from market leaders such as Match or eHarmony (which required users to answer hundreds of questions about their life, beliefs, and wants) and make sure that those who downloaded the app would try its signature feature: swipe-based matchmaking.^([32](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr32)) Not only did the use of the Facebook profile API enable Tinder users to instantly sign up, they could also quickly import their Facebook profile photos, which were among the user’s “best” photos and suitable for public view—a far better experience than asking the user to dig through years of cloud storage to find that same shot, or go through the clumsy (and quality-eroding) process of screenshotting their Facebook profile photo and then uploading it to Tinder. Facebook also allowed users to connect their social graph to Tinder, thereby enabling them to see if they had friends in common with a would-be match, and if so, who. Some users preferred matching with someone they could reference-check, for safety reasons. Others liked being able to go on a date to make a true “first impression”; such users “swiped right” only on individuals with whom they had no friends in common. In 2018, Facebook abruptly closed the API that enabled users to connect their social graph to dating apps. No reason for the policy change was provided, but that same year saw Facebook launch its own dating service, Facebook Dating, which naturally centered around its unique social graph and network. Facebook did continue to allow its users to log in to apps such as Tinder and to carry over their photos, too—but the preservation of this functionality was sensible despite (if not because of) the social network having launched a competitor. After all, Facebook cannot stop users from repurposing photos uploaded to Facebook, and, through “like counts,” the social network helps a user identify their best photos, too. Given this, Facebook might as well know which of its users were using dating apps—knowledge that would, at minimum, help the company recommend its own service.
