
Despite his far-reaching impact, Stephenson has consistently warned against a literal interpretation of his works—especially `Snow Crash`. In 2011, the novelist told the `New York Times`, “I can talk all day long about how wrong I got it,”^([9](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr9)) and when asked about his influence on Silicon Valley by `Vanity Fair` in 2017, he reminded the publication to keep “in mind that \[`Snow Crash` was written\] pre-Internet as we know it, pre–Worldwide Web, just me making shit up.”^([10](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr10)) And while Stephenson coined the term “Metaverse,” he was far from the first to introduce the concept.

## The Metaverse Before It Was “the Metaverse”

In 1935, Stanley G. Weinbaum wrote the short story “Pygmalion’s Spectacles,” which includes the first example of what modern readers would call a “virtual reality headset.” In the story, the inebriated Dan Burke^([†](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-058)) is approached by the mysterious Professor Ludwig,^([‡](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-057)) who speculates that Burke’s consumption stems from the desire to “make real a dream.” Ludwig goes on to tell Burke of the teachings of philosopher George Berkeley,^([§](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-056)) saying that all of “reality is a dream” and that “we do not see, feel, hear, taste the object, but that we have only the sensation of seeing, feeling, hearing, tasting.… \[The sensations\] are ‘mental phenomena’ and ‘interpretations.’ ”^([11](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr11)) Ludwig then promises that his device, which “vaguely resembles a gas mask,” produces a “movie that gives one sight and sound.… You are in the story, you speak to the shadows, and the shadows reply, and instead of being on a screen, the story is all about you, and you are in it.”^([12](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr12))

Eventually, Burke relents and tries on the device and is promptly stunned. After only a few moments, he loses all sense of time inside and struggles to accept that what he experiences isn’t real. Inevitably, Burke falls for an apparition, Galatea—and remains entranced even after he recognizes her name as a reference to the Greek myth of Pygmalion and Galatea. In the tale, the Cypriot king Pygmalion carves a sculpture so beautiful and lifelike that he falls in love with her, and the goddess Aphrodite eventually transforms her into a living woman, Galatea, whom the king later marries.

After finally removing the goggles, Burke is in a state “worse” than being “in love with a vision.… \[He is\] in love with a girl who had never lived, in a fantastic Utopia that was literally nowhere!”^([13](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr13)) Some months later, Burke finds Ludwig, who reveals his device was all “trick photography, but stereoscopic, as I told you—three dimensional,” with Ludwig carrying around a photographic apparatus in real time and his niece playing the part of Galatea. Ludwig remarks that its verisimilitude requires self-hypnosis, but Burke doesn’t care; an “ache had vanished; a pain was eased.” Galatea, Burke exclaims, is “attainable at last.” Ludwig, however, remains frustrated: “Fools! I bring it here to sell to Westman, the camera people, and what do they say? ‘It isn’t clear. Only one person can use it at a time. It’s too expensive.’ Fools! Fools!”

Ray Bradbury’s 1950 short story “The Veldt” continues upon (and darkens) Weinbaum’s themes. The story imagines a nuclear family living in an automated home that performs household tasks and entertains the two children, Peter and Wendy,^([¶](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-055)) using a virtual reality nursery.^([#](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-054)) Eventually, the parents fear that the Happylife Home’s AI has displaced them as parents and that their children might never develop self-sufficiency. After deciding to move to the country, Peter and Wendy trick their parents into the nursery, which subsequently traps and kills them. As they die, the parents realize the nursery had previously simulated, and thus prepared for, exactly this sequence of events.

Philip K. Dick’s 1953 story “The Trouble with Bubbles” is set in an era where humans have explored deep into the solar system but never succeeded in finding life. Yearning to connect with other worlds and life-forms, consumers begin to buy a product called “Worldcraft” through which they can build and “Own \[Their\] Own World,” with these worlds being cultivated to the point of producing sentient life and fully realized civilizations. Most Worldcraft owners eventually destroy their creations in a celebratory competition that Dick described as a “neurotic” “orgy of breaking” intended to “amuse some god suffering from ennui.”^([14](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr14)) The protagonist, Nathan Hull,^([\*\*](#chapter1.xhtml#footnote-053)) introduces a bill to the Directorate to ban Worldcraft on ethical grounds, while also arguing man ought to “try to find outlets that aren’t so artificial” and that “bottle-up” creative energy until it “sours.” Hull’s bill loses in a vote, 400 to 35. Not long after, an earthquake occurs, after which Hull realizes he is sentient life inside another life-form’s own Worldcraft-like product, destined to be one of the “obliterated countless millions.”

In 1956, Isaac Asimov’s novel `The Naked Sun` was published. In it, he described an alien society with long life spans, but where face-to-face interactions (“seeing”) and physical contact, including sex, are considered both wasteful and repugnant, with most socializing taking place through projected holograms and 3D televisions, and work performed by robots. By the end of the novel, the human detective Elijah Baley, who appears across Asimov’s Foundation book series, concludes the Solarian race is weak and largely incapable of exploration or cooperation. Three years after `The Naked Sun`, Dick published a novel, `Time Out of Joint`, in which the protagonist learns the paradise he inhabits is merely a constructed reality intended to protect his psyche from the fact that the planet earth is at war with lunar colonists.

In 1963, Dick wrote the short story “The Days of Perky Pat,” which was subsequently reimagined in the 1964 novel `The Three Stigmata of Palmer Eldritch`. In the short story, humanity has retreated into remote enclaves following global thermonuclear war. Nearly all of the survivors of the war spend their time playing Perky Pat, a dollhouse-based game in which their prior lives are simulated in physical “layouts,” and use most of their scrounged supplies to improve these layouts, adding features such as an electric lawn mower to Pat’s home or automatic door to the local supermarket. The winner of the game Perky Pat is decided through money, as there is “no other criterion by which one could tell if he had won or lost.” Occasionally, the survivors feel “shame” when playing “but only fleetingly; the shame, almost at once, was replaced by the desire to play more.”^([15](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr15)) The children of the survivors consider Perky Pat to be “utterly wasteful, stupid,” and instead focus on their lives, while speculating that the “grown-ups” would die if they had to take care of themselves (“Wouldn’t that be too bad,” one of the children thinks to himself; “Sure be sad”).^([16](#notes_split_001.xhtml#ch1fr16)) In Dick’s full-length novel, the world has been destroyed by global warming, not war, and the United Nations initiates a draft to help colonize nearby planets. To adjust, these colonists begin to take the illegal drug Can-D to experience shared hallucinations while using Perky Pats, both of which are manufactured by Layouts Inc. Eventually, pseudo-religious cults of Can-D users emerge, as well as a more powerful intravenous drug, Chew-Z, which seems to give God-like powers and entrap certain users.
