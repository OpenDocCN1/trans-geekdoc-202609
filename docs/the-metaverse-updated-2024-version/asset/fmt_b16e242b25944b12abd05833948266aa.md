
As the entire market embraced cross-platform gaming with the exception of Sony, its PlayStation offered the “worst” version of the game—yes, the PlayStation version had better graphics than that of Nintendo Switch, Apple iPhone, or Android, but it could be used to play with only a subset of a player’s friends. What’s more, PlayStation owners could play the cross-platform version of `Fortnite` from nearly any other device in their home—and as the title was free-to-play, they didn’t even need to pay another dollar to do so. This dynamic placed Sony in a tough spot. The company’s historical decision to block cross-play might have cost it the incidental sale—a PlayStation user whose friends all had Xbox might have skipped buying a $60 copy of the PlayStation edition of `Call of Duty` as a result—but it was better than enabling its competitors. Now, though, PlayStation was missing out on most of the revenue from the highest-grossing title in history and driving many players to competing platforms. Sure, PlayStation offered a better technical experience than the iPhone, but most players valued the game’s social elements more than its average specs. And on at least three occasions, Epic had “accidentally” activated cross-play on PlayStation—proving Sony’s lack of support was just a company preference, not a technological impediment, and prompting many PlayStation owners to demand Sony “open up.”

All of these factors led Sony to change its policies—and to the long-term benefit of the entire video gaming ecosystem. Today, most hit games can be accessed by nearly all computing devices globally—and so can be played by anyone, anywhere, anytime—without users needing to repay or to fragment their identity, achievements, or player networks. Furthermore, cross-platform play, progression, and purchasing means that every console competes primarily on hardware, content, and services, not rent-seeking. Even so, PlayStation is thriving, with the console generating over 45% of total `Fortnite` revenues and continuing to outsell Xbox by a ratio of more than three to one.^([3](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr3))

Crucially, Sony’s decision to open up its closed platform also provides a view into potential economic solutions to the challenge of interoperability. In order to avoid “revenue leakage,” Sony required Epic to “true up” its payments to the PlayStation store. If a `Fortnite` player spent 100 hours playing on PlayStation and 100 hours on Nintendo Switch (i.e., 50/50) but spent only $40 on PlayStation compared to $60 on Nintendo Switch (40/60), Epic would have to pay Nintendo a 25% commission on its $60 share (as per usual), and PlayStation a 25% commission on its $40 share (again, as per usual), and then pay PlayStation 25% on $10 that didn’t happen on its platform, but its share of time suggested the platform was owed. In other words, Epic pays twice on $10. It’s not clear whether this policy is still applied in 2024—the public knows it ever existed only because of leaked legal filings. Regardless, the model is an example of how the proliferation of cross-platform gaming aids all market participants.

Even after `Fortnite` opened up various cross-platform services across the major gaming ecosystems, some limitations remained. One console’s player network (say, Xbox Live) could not friend a player on another (PlayStation Network), except when adding them inside a specific game such as `Fortnite`. For similar reasons, players on each system could talk to or message one another only when inside a game. While this approach worked well enough when two players knew which single game they wanted to play before logging on, it didn’t work well for unstructured hanging out or enjoying ad hoc play, and it meant that players had to use alternative communications suites (chat, email) to communicate when they were not inside their gaming app of choice. The more central gaming was to someone’s lifestyle, the less this patchwork solution suited them.

`Discord`, an instant messaging and voice over IP platform founded in 2015, began to fill the gap that the major gaming ecosystems refused to fill. Unlike the social systems of PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo, and Steam, `Discord` was available on all major computing platforms—PC, Mac, iPhone, and Android—thereby enabling every single gamer to talk to every other gamer, irrespective of the game they were playing or their gaming device of preference, while also building an overarching social graph that superseded every game and device, and that even included occasional and lapsed gamers, too. Though `Discord`’s functionality was imperfect, users flocked to the service, which has since grown to have a larger and more active userbase than any other gaming platform. And crucially, there is no way for the platforms to stop users from using `Discord` in lieu of platform- or game-based communications suites, because users can and almost certainly do “second-screen” the app on their phones while they play.

As `Discord` became more essential to game discovery, many publishers began to integrate `Discord` application programming interfaces (`APIs`), sometimes even replacing their native communications suite with those of `Discord`. By 2022, Xbox and PlayStation announced native integrations of `Discord` in their closed platforms—a move that created a new “interchange” solution for their player networks, communication services, and socializing online.

The next steps for cross-play and identity are emerging. One such step harkens back to credit histories and homestay scores. Online toxicity is an unfortunate part of all social platforms and gaming services. Most of these platforms spend millions (sometimes tens of millions) a year on human moderators who review user reports of abuse and harassment, and ban severe or serial offenders. But there are key limitations to these operations. If Activision’s `Call of Duty` bans a user for abusive or racist language, that user can then go on to troll on Epic’s `Fortnite`—or just create a new account on `Call of Duty`. No gaming company wants to share their customer data with competitors, nor take on additional risks of losing this data—but no developer or player benefits from toxic behavior, and everyone is negatively affected by it. In that spirit, efforts are underway to build cross-publisher account-reporting systems and child-safety protocols.

There are also growing efforts to establish frameworks that would enable users to take an avatar or item from one virtual world to another. This area of interoperation is sometimes lampooned with reference to the apparent mismatches of the various titles, as in “Who wants to wear `Fortnite`’s anthropomorphic banana skin ‘Peely’ while playing `Call of Duty`?” Now to be fair, a giant, comically styled anthropomorphic banana doesn’t make much sense in `Call of Duty`, or in a virtual classroom, for that matter. But it is equally obvious that some users want some items, such as a Darth Vader costume, a Lakers jersey, or a Prada purse, in many different spaces. And they certainly don’t want to buy these items over and over again. They might be reluctantly willing to do so today, but virtual clothing is still in its infancy. By the end of the decade, billions of people will be sitting on numerous (effectively) duplicated outfits across their many previously played games—and will doubtless resist buying those outfits again. Liberating purchases from a single title will, the theory suggests, lead to both more purchases and higher prices. Put another way, would Disney sell more or less merchandise if it could be worn or used only in its theme parks? How much would someone pay for a Real Madrid jersey that could only be used in Santiago Bernabéu Stadium? Or how much lower would user spending be on `Roblox` if a player’s outfit was limited to a single `Roblox` game?
