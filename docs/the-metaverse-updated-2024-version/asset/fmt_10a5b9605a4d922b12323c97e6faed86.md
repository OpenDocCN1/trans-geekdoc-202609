
However, the Metaverse is more commonly—and best—positioned as an “evolution of” or “successor state to” the internet as it is known today, rather than a byproduct of the internet of the past 40-plus years. In particular, the “Metaverse as the next version of the internet” is the framework championed by Nvidia’s Jensen Huang,^([2](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr2)) Facebook’s Mark Zuckerberg,^([3](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr3)) Epic Games’ Tim Sweeney,^([4](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr4)) and Unity’s John Riccitiello, four central figures in the Metaverse’s development. Apple’s Tim Cook holds a similar view, though he eschews the term “Metaverse” for those of “spatial computing” and “immersive interfaces,” which make up the next great “digital era.”^([\*](#chapter2.xhtml#footnote-049)) At Microsoft’s Ignite 2021 conference, Microsoft’s Satya Nadella attempted to address confusion by stating that the term “Metaverse” “described both the new platform and a new application type; similar to how we talked about the web and websites in the early 90s,” but in this case, the Metaverse resulted from “the unification of digital networks and the physical world.” Nadella added, “The Metaverse enables us to embed \[connected\] computing into the real world and to embed the real world into computing.”^([5](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr5))

Although the successor state of the modern internet is not a literal adaptation of Stephenson’s Metaverse of 1992, it’s consistent across the relevant works of Weinbaum through Cline, each of which assumes a suite of technologies that come together to produce a new, multidimensional technological medium that spans nearly all of human existence. It’s also consistent with Stephenson’s underlying ambitions for the Metaverse of *Snow Crash*—that it would be a 3D medium more popular than the dominant medium of the 1990s (television, which has since been surpassed by the internet) and would bring “spatial arrangement to the World Wide Web.”^([6](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr6))

Regardless, anchoring the Metaverse in the internet only provides context for the Metaverse—it doesn’t explain or define it. Indeed, the claim produces a new challenge: even after decades of use, most people cannot define the internet. Instead, they can only describe their experience. And while many corporate executives understand the internet, they will only ever discuss it in the context of their business. An internet provider will talk about cables in the ground or wireless towers, or how the internet enables more people to communicate. To a media company, the internet is a direct-to-consumer channel for “streaming content.” A device manufacturer will speak of smartphones and touchscreens. A politician and day laborer, senior citizen and teen will give explanations that differ even more widely.

Of course, it doesn’t matter that the average person cannot define the internet—no part of their experience or usage of it is held back as a result. Eventually, the same will be true of the Metaverse. But without a basic understanding of how the internet works, it’s difficult to explain its still-in-formation successor state, let alone address misunderstandings. At a similarly early stage in the internet’s adoption, it was most commonly characterized as “the information superhighway,” which users “surf” with their keyboards and “mouse.” A quarter century later, it’s obvious this conception of the internet was a poor and misleading way to describe what was to come, but until most people could experience the full range of the internet, such accounts endured.

## Breaking Down Today’s Internet

The internet is the global system of interconnected computer networks that uses the Internet Protocol Suite (also known as TCP/IP) to communicate between networks and devices. There are interconnected networks other than the internet—such as those across a corporate or college campus—but the internet is by far the largest “network of networks.”

In total, the modern internet spans 110,000 networks across nearly 200 countries,^([7](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr7)) markets, and territories, connecting over 30 billion active devices and more than 6 billion people. Each of these users, devices, and systems can coherently, comprehensively, and consistently exchange information. More than 90% of internet users log on daily, using it for an average of more than two hours, while generating over a half trillion emails, accessing more than four million apps, and visiting many of the world’s two-billion-plus websites and many billions of webpages.

Crucially, “the internet” is not a formalized organization, collective, or even group of networks. The more than 100,000 networks that constitute this “network of networks” are almost all independently funded, operated, and otherwise autonomous. Most have no formal or financial relationship with one another. In fact, they rarely know more than a few dozen networks even exist. Despite this, many networks work in concert—broadcasting their location on the internet as well as other attributes such as their available capacity and speeds, and carrying one another’s data in accordance with the internet’s protocols. These protocols, meanwhile, have no formal licensing body or authority. Instead, they are typically steered by birds-of-a-feather working groups that recommend given processes, conventions, and standards, which the networks, alongside countless device manufacturers, application developers, wireless modem and router makers, all choose to support, too.

The internet is also aided by additional conventions that, while not part of the Internet Protocol Suite, are widely supported. For example, most internet applications support common file formats (e.g., JPEG, MP3), practices (metadata structures), and languages (JavaScript, HTML), thereby making it easier for the data shared across the internet to be widely understood. As with the Internet Protocol Suite, these so-called standards tend to be run by various consortia, working groups, and open-source communities, none of which can compel “the internet” to support one of these standards.

Despite—or indeed, because of—the internet’s decentralized architecture and collaborative principles, it has become one of the most important technological platforms in history. Though TCP/IP does not specify how it should be used or what should be built with it, nearly every part of modern life is now run through or supported by the internet. The World Bank and United Nations estimate that at least 15%–20% of world GDP is “digital,” with this segment growing two and a half times faster than the “physical” economy, and typically transforming it, too.^([8](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr8)) In many cases, the internet is used without users ever actively “logging on.” Nearly all Western point-of-sale terminals use the internet to authenticate and process payments, as do many passcode security devices, such as a building ID card, and most high-value goods are tracked through internet-connected tags. These experiences are also remarkably seamless. A user who places a video call using a Windows PC inside their apartment connected to an Ethernet cable can relocate to a restaurant across town, resume their video call on an Apple iPhone using the restaurant’s Wi-Fi or any available wireless network, continue to passively receive email and instant messages on this new device, and even resume downloads. The user and their device are instantly relocated on the internet, without interruption, obligatory fees, or even much difficulty. Several proprietary videoconferencing platforms, such as Zoom and Microsoft Teams, can call into one another, too, even if their users are on entirely different operating systems and/or using different browsers and in different countries. It should be noted, however, that while the internet is not inherently dystopic or utopic, it is doubtless used for ill and sometimes has ill effects.
