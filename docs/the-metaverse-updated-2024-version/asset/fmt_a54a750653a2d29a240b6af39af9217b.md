
Apple’s third app category was interactive apps that enabled users to affect or alter the app’s content (as is the case in a game or a cloud storage application). As with “reader apps,” interactive apps were prohibited from offering alternative in-app payments other than iTunes and could not inform users of any non-IAP alternatives. But unlike “reader apps,” interactive apps were required to offer IAPs and therefore pay Apple’s 30% fee for related purchases. As such, interactive app developers could avoid Apple’s 30% fee only by offering discounts large enough that users would notice the difference in dollar terms (as savings could not be advertised) and that would justify the friction. And in contrast to reader apps, which often relied on long-running subscription, users would need to choose this pathway for every purchase of a life, outfit, virtual currency, and more.

Various emails and documents from Epic’s court case against Apple revealed that the App Store’s multi-category payment models resulted primarily from where Apple believed it could exert leverage. But leverage also correlated with where Apple believed it could create value. Mobile commerce, of course, has been critical to the growth of the global economy for some time, but most of it was a reallocation from physical retail. For many people, the iPad form factor made reading the *New York Times* more compelling on the tablet than in print, but Apple didn’t enable the journalism industry. Mobile gaming was different. When the App Store launched, the gaming industry generated just over $50 billion per year—$1.5 billion of which was in mobile. By 2021, mobile was more than half the $180 billion industry and represented 70% of growth since 2008.

The economics of the App Store exemplify this dynamic. In 2020, an estimated $700 billion was spent using iOS apps, less than 10% of which was billed by Apple. Of this 10%, nearly 70% was for games.^([27](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr27)) Put differently, 7 of every 100 dollars spent inside iPhone and iPad apps were for games, but 70 of every 100 dollars grossed by the App Store were from the category. Given that these devices are not gaming-focused and are rarely bought for this purpose, and that Apple offers almost none of the online services of a gaming platform, this figure often comes as a surprise. The judge overseeing Epic Games’ lawsuit against Apple, which argued the company held monopoly power over the distribution of iOS apps and payments and abused this power, famously told Apple CEO Tim Cook: “You don’t charge Wells Fargo, right? Or Bank of America? But you’re charging the gamers to subsidize Wells Fargo.”^([28](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr28)) Of course, the major banks were powerful and significant when the App Store launched in 2008, but it took years for the leisure-focused games industry, which generated less than $75 billion worldwide in 2008 (and less than $15 billion on mobile devices), to become sufficiently noteworthy.^([29](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr29))

Because the App Store’s revenue came primarily from a tiny, but fast-growing, segment of the world economy, it also took time for the App Store to become a large business worth scrutinizing. Ironically, even Apple originally doubted how large the App Store business might become. Two months after its launch, Jobs reviewed the nascent business with the *Wall Street Journal*. In its report, the paper stated that “Apple wasn’t likely to derive much in the way of a direct profit from the business.… Jobs is betting applications will sell more iPhones and wireless-enabled iPod touch devices, enhancing the appeal of the products in the same way music sold through Apple’s iTunes has made iPods more desirable.”^([30](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr30)) To this end, Jobs told the *Journal* that Apple’s 30% fees were intended to cover credit card fees and other operating expenses for the store. He also said that the App Store “is going to crest half a billion, soon.… Who knows, maybe it will be a $1 billion marketplace at some point in time.”^([31](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr31)) The App Store passed this $1 billion mark in its second year, with Apple noting that it now operated “a bit over break-even.”^([32](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr32))

By 2020, the App Store had become one of the best businesses on earth. With revenues of $73 billion and an estimated 70% margin, it would’ve been large enough to be a member of the *Fortune* 15 if it was spun off from its parent company (which is the largest company in the world by market capitalization and the most profitable in dollar terms). And this is even though the App Store billed less than 10% of transactions flowing through its system, which themselves made up less than 1% of the global economy. Were iOS an “open platform,” these profits would likely have been competed away, at least in part. Visa and Square would offer smaller in-app fees, while competing app stores would emerge that offered services comparable to Apple’s but at lower prices. But this isn’t possible because Apple controls all of the software on its devices and, like gaming consoles, keeps it closed and bundled. And its only major competitor, Google, is just as happy with the state of play.

These issues aren’t exclusive to the Metaverse, of course, but their consequences for it will be profound, for the same reason Judge Gonzalez Rogers narrowed in on Apple’s gaming policies: the entire world is becoming game-like. That means it’s being forced into the 30% models of the major platforms.

Take Netflix, as an example. In December 2018, the streaming service chose to remove in-app billing from its iOS app. As a “reader app,” this was the company’s right, and its financial planning team had decided that while asking users to sign up on Netflix.com and manually enter their credit card would cost them some sign-ups versus Apple’s one-click in-app alternative,^([33](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr33)) this missed revenue was less than the 30% that it would have to send Apple.^([‡](#chapter10.xhtml#footnote-011)) In November 2021, Netflix added mobile games to its subscription plan, theoretically turning its “reader” app into an “interactive one.” But as Netflix bundled these games into its streaming service at no added cost (a Netflix choice), did not offer any microtransactions or paid expansions inside these games (another Netflix choice), and distributed its iOS-based games through stand-alone apps downloaded through the App Store rather than inside its streaming video application (per Apple requirements), the company was not obligated to restore Apple’s in-app payment option. Should Netflix ever choose to make its gaming subscription a paid add-on to its streaming service, or add in-game purchases—both of which many Wall Street analysts expect and which would likely reflect the success of the company’s expansion into video games—it would be forced to return to Apple’s payment service (or stop offering an iOS app altogether).

But why, exactly, does Apple’s 30% “outlaw” the Metaverse, to return to Sweeney’s pre-lawsuit remark? There are three core reasons. First, it stifles investment in the Metaverse and adversely affects its business models. Second, it squeezes the very companies that are pioneering the Metaverse today, namely integrated virtual world platforms. Third, Apple’s desire to protect these revenues effectively prohibits many of the most Metaverse-focused technologies from further development.

## High Costs and Diverted Profits

In the “real world,” payment processing costs as little as 0% (cash), typically maxes out at 2.5% (standard credit card purchases), and sometimes reaches 5% (in the case of low-dollar-value transactions with high minimum fees). These figures are low because of robust competition between payment rails (wire versus ACH) and within them (Visa versus MasterCard and American Express).
