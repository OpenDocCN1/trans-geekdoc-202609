
# In the years that followed this early research

In the years that followed this early research, Bush became one of the most influential engineers and science administrators in American history. From 1939 to 1941, he was vice chairman and temporarily served as chairman of the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics, the predecessor agency to NASA. In this position, Bush convinced President Franklin D. Roosevelt to establish what became the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD), a new federal agency that would be run by Bush, who would report directly to the president. The agency was provided nearly unlimited funding, primarily for secret projects that would aid the United States’ efforts in World War II.

Only four months after OSRD was founded, President Roosevelt approved the atomic bomb program known as the Manhattan Project, following a meeting with Bush and Vice President Henry A. Wallace. To manage the Manhattan Project, Roosevelt created a Top Policy Group consisting of himself, Bush, Wallace, Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson, Chief of Staff of the Army General George C. Marshall, and James B. Conant, who headed up a subbranch of OSRD previously run by Bush. In addition, the Uranium Committee (later named the S-1 Executive Committee) would report directly to Bush.

After the war ended in 1945, but two years before he left his role as director of the OSRD, Bush wrote two famous essays. The first, “Science, the Endless Frontier,” was addressed to the president and in it, Bush called for an increase in government investments in science and technology, rather than a peacetime reduction, as well as the establishment of the National Science Foundation. The second essay, “As We May Think,” appeared in *The Atlantic* and publicly detailed Bush’s vision of the Memex.

In the years that followed his essays, Bush stepped back from public office and public view, yet his influence matured as his various contributions to government, science, and society began to converge. Starting in the 1960s, the US government funded a variety of projects within the Department of Defense, in partnership with a network of external researchers, universities, and other nongovernment institutions, that together developed the foundation of the internet. At the same time, Bush’s Memex was informing the creation and evolution of “hypertext,” which enabled nearly all online information, content, documents, and pages to be interconnected and instantly accessed through clickable text links. The HyperText Transfer Protocol would later become one of the core protocols of the Internet Protocol Suite (TCP/IP) and defining aspects of the World Wide Web, which is typically written in the HyperText Markup Language (HTML). In 1986, partly inspired by Bush’s writings, the US federal government established the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) to guide the technical evolution of the Internet Protocol Suite.

While technological progress typically occurs out of common sight, science fiction often provides the general public with the clearest view of the future. In 1968, fewer than 10% of American households had a color TV, yet the second-highest-grossing film of the year, *2001: A Space Odyssey*, imagined a future in which humanity had compressed these fridge-sized devices into coaster-thin displays and used them idly during breakfast. Anyone watching the film today will instantly liken these devices to iPads. As with Bush’s Memex, this imagined technology took longer to arrive than was originally anticipated. iPads appeared in stores four and a half decades after Stanley Kubrick’s groundbreaking film was released, and more than a decade after the year in which the futuristic film was set.

By 2021, tablets had become commonplace and spacefaring had begun to feel within reach, with the competing efforts of Richard Branson’s Virgin Galactic, Elon Musk’s SpaceX, and Jeff Bezos’s Blue Origin appearing on front pages and inspiring dreams of civilian space stations and multiplanetary colonization. Yet 2021 saw another fantastical vision begin to take hold—one that promised to take humanity anywhere without ever having to leave their homes, let alone terra firma; that imagined not a new planet but a new plane of existence on top; and that focused on building new universes rather than just exploring the solar system. This vision was called “the Metaverse.” And while the term was nearly 30 years old and the ideas behind it older than even Bush’s Memex, the world’s most powerful companies and executives seemed to believe it would soon arrive.

## “A Metaverse Company”

For most people, the heralding of the Metaverse’s arrival began the same day they first heard the term. On October 28, 2021, Facebook, one of the most widely known brands in human history, announced it would subsequently be known as Meta Platforms.^([\*](#introduction.xhtml#footnote-064)) Facebook’s founder and CEO, Mark Zuckerberg, explained that the name change signified his commitment to transforming the 17-year-old “social media company” into a “Metaverse company,” and that he hoped the move would help rally the company’s users, partners, employees, and shareholders behind this mission.

Though some argued Zuckerberg’s announcement was little more than an attempt to shed Facebook’s controversial existing brand, the company’s pivot to the Metaverse was, in fact, already in its eighth year. In 2014, Facebook had purchased Oculus VR for approximately $2 billion—a sum twice what was paid to acquire Instagram two years earlier. By 2018, an internal report developed by Facebook’s executive team^([†](#introduction.xhtml#footnote-063)) and distributed to select board members declared that the Metaverse could be built by 2022 and was “ours to lose,” while calling on the company to “act first, and go big, or we risk being \[a\] wannabe.”^([1](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr1))

By the time of Facebook’s name change, the company had spent roughly $29 billion to develop, manufacture, and sell virtual reality (VR) headsets, augmented reality (AR) devices, brain-to-machine interfaces, and other technologies intended to build the Metaverse.^([2](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr2)) As Zuckerberg began to publicly detail these investments, he warned shareholders that the costs would reduce operating income at least $10 billion per year for several more years and that the Metaverse would not arrive for five to ten more years. But when it arrived, Zuckerberg promised, it would be worth trillions.

For many who were hearing about the “Metaverse” for the first time, Zuckerberg’s announcements were surprising. But those announcements were also backed by a decade and a half of success. Facebook was not just the sixth-most-valuable publicly listed company in the world; it was the youngest company to rank in the top 50. Facebook’s non-Metaverse businesses were generating close to $60 billion in operating cash flow—a sum that few companies had ever exceeded and that was so large it managed to make Facebook’s Metaverse investments seem modest. Zuckerberg also had a history of investing long before profits and building hundred-billion-dollar businesses out of products his rivals had considered profitless hobbies. And less than a decade ago, Zuckerberg oversaw one of the most successful platform pivots in technology history. In May of 2012, he declared Facebook would become a “mobile-first company,” and over the following five quarters, mobile’s share of Facebook’s revenue grew from between 3% and 5% to over 50%.^([3](#notes_split_000.xhtml#introfr3))
