
The disagreements regarding the “best” way to build an HMD computing platform is an echo of the early stages of the mobile era. Early leaders, such as Microsoft, with its Windows Mobile OS and the supporting manufacturing ecosystem, and Research in Motion’s BlackBerry, believed that the winning smartphone must have a battery that lasted for days, rather than one that supported only a few hours of use; that wireless data usage should be minimal, not used in abundance; that a smartphone would be the average person’s secondary computer, not their primary computer; that a physical keyboard was essential and a touchscreen optional at best; that the market would be led by business customers, not ordinary consumers; that the price had to be $200 or less, not $500 or more; that the device needed to be able to survive frequent drops (and especially not crack on its first), and so on. Apple took the other side of all of these bets. And while Apple was originally opposed to third-party apps on the iPhone,^([68](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr68)) the company reversed course within a year and, the year after that, released its famous “There’s an App for That” campaign to help sell the device. Apple’s legacy competitors, such as BlackBerry, had long supported apps but never imagined there might be thousands, let alone millions, of app developers for its device, and prioritized device security so greatly that developers needed notarized documents just to receive the application-development kit.

Apple’s unusual bets continued into its interface choices. Take the original iPhone’s most iconic design element—its home button. Although Jobs showed little interest in physical keyboards, he nevertheless decided to place a large home button on the front of the iPhone—taking up space that might otherwise have been used for a larger screen, a longer-lasting battery, or a more powerful processor. Jobs saw it as an essential part of introducing consumers to both touchscreens and pocket-sized computing. Not unlike closing a flip phone, a user knew that no matter what was happening on their iPhone’s touchscreen, pressing the home button would always take them back to the main screen.

In 2011, four years after it released the first iPhone, Apple added a new feature to its operating system: multitasking. Before this point, users could operate only a few predetermined applications at the same time. It was possible to listen to music through the iPod app while reading the *New York Times* app, but if the user then opened up their Facebook app, the *Times* app would close. If the user wanted to return to a given article in the *Times* app, they’d have to reopen the app, then navigate back to the article and scroll back down to where they left off. Doing so would mean exiting the Facebook app, too. Multitasking now allowed users to effectively “pause” an app while switching to another, all of which was managed by the home button. If a user clicked the home button, the app would be paused, and they’d return to the home screen. If they double-clicked, the app would still be paused, and the user would then be able to swipe through a tray of all paused apps.

The first few iPhones could have supported multitasking. After all, other smartphones with similar CPUs supported the feature. Taking the long view, Apple believed that it needed to ease users into the mobile computing era, and this meant focusing not just on which technology was possible but on when users were ready for it. To this end, it wasn’t until 2017, with the release of the tenth iPhone, that Apple felt comfortable removing a physical home button and requiring users to “swipe up” from the bottom of the screen instead.

There are no “best practices” in any newly established device category. In fact, many of the choices considered obvious today were once controversial—not just the iPhone’s touchscreen. Indeed, some early Android builds and apps used Apple’s pinch-to-zoom concept but believed it was backward—if a user is bringing their fingers closer together, shouldn’t whatever they’re looking at come closer, not move further out? It’s almost impossible to imagine this logic today, but that’s partly because billions of people have undergone 15 years of training in the opposite logic.

What might the future of HMDs have in store? A particular area of focus is minimizing the incidence of eyestrain. Increases in resolution and frame rate will help address this issue, as will reductions in heat. A more intractable issue relates to the very nature of HMDs. Human eyes “verge” as they look at objects nearer or farther away—that is, they move in opposite directions inward (looking at the tip of one’s nose) or outward (the horizon line). These movements automatically trigger the “accommodation reflex,” in which a person’s lens shape and pupil accommodate in order to establish focus (which also places non-focal objects and environments out of focus). When wearing an HMD, users often experience “vergence-accommodation conflict” (VAC) as their eyes converge and diverge to look at parts of a virtual world that are of varying distance but always in focus, which strains the accommodation reflex and leads to blurriness. The impact of VAC increases the longer an HMD is in continuous use. VAC is far less common when playing a video game on a monitor or TV screen, as the distance between the user and their display provides reference distances that stabilize the accommodation reflex and both the background and foreground of the virtual world are relatively closer to each other and therefore require less vergence.

To better address HMD VAC, Facebook unveiled a prototype in 2023, the Butterscotch Varifocal, which tracks a user’s eyes to determine where on a screen they’re looking, then uses mechanical actuators to move the screen forward or back. This movement, complemented by software that blurs the parts of a rendered scene proportionate to their distance from the user’s focal plane, reportedly reduces VAC-related weariness.^([69](#notes_split_009.xhtml#ch9fr69)) As is always the case, these additions have costs. Most obviously, the Butterscotch Varifocal’s actuators and rails add bulk and weight to the device, and increase battery usage. The HMD must also support rich eye tracking, which requires not just more cameras but cameras that are placed behind the device’s lenses (not on the periphery), which means many users will need to order prescription lenses.

Another emergent solution used by Facebook’s Quest Pro and Apple’s Vision Pro is “foveated rendering,” which takes advantage of the fact that human eyesight is sharpest at the center and blurriest at the periphery. By tracking the user’s eye, it’s possible for a headset to concentrate its computing power where the user is looking and reduce fidelity elsewhere. An 8K–16K display is still needed, as the user’s eyes might be directed at any point on the screen, but 90% of the screen might be rendered in only 2K–4K at any given time, saving battery and computing power. This functionality also requires eye tracking and is therefore not supported on Facebook’s non-Pro HMDs, such as the Quest 3.
