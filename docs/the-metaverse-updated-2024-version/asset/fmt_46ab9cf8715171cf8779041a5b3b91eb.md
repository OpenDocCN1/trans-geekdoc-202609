
For the Metaverse to serve as a platform for human socializing, as well as a computing layer for the real world, it must be able to support large numbers of users and IoT devices without compromising essential technical functions. Furthermore, participants must be individually represented within the Metaverse’s many simulations and able to affect it, too. If these requirements were rendered merely optional, the Metaverse would be technically easier—there would be fewer users to synchronize, fewer simultaneous events and inputs to process, and a reduced need for real-time bandwidth and delivery, and users would be able to share a rendered image instead of needing to generate their own custom view. However, such a limitation would make the Metaverse just a virtual diorama or a larger-scale version of the reality series `Big Brother`.

### Getting to the Full-Spec Metaverse

In “Pygmalion’s Spectacles,” Professor Ludwig’s sales pitch invokes the teachings of George Berkeley, an 18th-century subjective idealist who is best known for the saying “To be is to be perceived.” Today, Berkeley’s ideas are more commonly expressed through a thought experiment: “If a tree falls in a forest and no one is around to hear it, does it make a sound?” The question is commonly attributed to the philosopher but is at best a liberal reinterpretation of his writings. Regardless, Berkeley’s arguments and thought exercises have endured in pop culture for centuries because they are fun and hinge on important and ever-evolving technicalities and concepts.

According to an immaterialist such as Berkeley, the tree—standing, falling, or fallen—exists if someone (or God) perceives it. Others claim that what’s meant by “sound” is just vibrations that propagate through matter. As these vibrations exist irrespective of an observer, the tree does, in fact, make a sound whether or not someone is around to hear it. Another perspective alleges that “sound,” specifically, is a sensation experienced by a brain when these vibrations interact with nerve endings. If there are no nerves to interact with vibrating particles, there can be no sound.

One hundred four years after Berkeley’s death, the Frenchman Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville devised the phonautograph, the first device capable of recording sound waves as they propagated through a medium such as air or wood. From this point on, one could argue that many sounds were now heard by artificial observers, making the distinction between vibrations and sound sensations irrelevant. Nevertheless, 21st-century quantum mechanics largely agrees that without an observer, existence is at best a conjecture that cannot be proved or disproved—thus all that can be said is that the tree might exist—though Albert Einstein, who was instrumental in founding the field of quantum mechanics, took issue with this view.

Although 21st-century examples of `Fortnite` concerts and `EVE Online` battles are helpful to explain the technological requirements of the Metaverse, it’s Berkeley’s 18th-century tree that best reveals the difficulty of creating a virtual plane of existence for all of humanity. As William Gibson explained in `Neuromancer`, the Metaverse (or as he called it, the cyberspace matrix) does not really exist. Instead, it is a “consensual hallucination” that’s “experienced daily by billions.… A graphic representation of data abstracted from the banks of every computer in the human system.”^([16](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr16))

Accordingly, every aspect of the Metaverse, from its many trees to their individual leaves, and the forests in which they are situated, must be created by a human agent or a subagent thereof (such as a generative AI program). Every aspect of this data, too, must be set—from the specific coloring of the tree to its texture, the way it moves in the wind, what is required to chop it down, the sound it makes when falling, and so on. And for a user to experience this data, they must have systems capable of understanding and representing it. For the data to be part of a shared human experience, it must also be shared with billions of other people, each of whom can correctly interpret and affect it, while sharing their own data back.

In 2024, billions of people can observe a hyperrealistic virtual tree fall in a hyperrealistic forest. But if many users want to observe such an event, they must be separated into individual groups that each watches a different copy of that tree fall and hears the sound of that tree falling. Alternatively, the complexity of that tree may need to be reduced—its rough bark smoothed out into a textureless and uniform brown and its fall reduced to a generic thud. And those who do observe the same tree might see and hear it at slightly different times, thereby determining who responds to it first and last, and requiring some system to decide whose responses should be considered “true” or “false.” Some users might be able to hear the tree fall in spatial sound and see it in a high-resolution, high-frame-rate immersive headset, while another cohort might be treated to flattened sound and a lower-frame-rate, 2D display. And no matter how many see the tree fall, the Metaverse might forget this same fact and reestablish the tree minutes later as though it had never been felled. Or it might remember the tree as having fallen, but differently than its observers. And some systems within the Metaverse might not even recognize the tree as being a tree, let alone be able to use it, or understand who does and doesn’t own it.

The Metaverse will become “the Metaverse” only if it can support a large number of users experiencing the same event, at the same time, and in the same place, without making substantial concessions in user functionality, world interactivity, persistence, and rendering quality, and without fragmenting participants into different and occasionally conflicting instances. This does not require billions of people to share in any single experience at the same time, nor the ability to infinitely affect every particle of it—just as all real-world locations can have capacity limits, dress codes, and prohibited behavior. Yet it’s clear that the shortfalls of current technology are significant.

To close the gap between the present day and the long-awaited Metaverse-shaped future, numerous technologies are required. Some of these technologies exist today, but require substantial enhancements or investments. Some must be created. In other cases, new business models and coding practices are required.

The rest of this book is dedicated to these requirements, the potential solutions to them, and the ways they affect one another.
