
# 加密货币与人工智能的发展

In 2021, the combined value of all cryptocurrencies crossed $2.9 trillion, with the Bitcoin blockchain processing a remarkable $4.7 trillion in bitcoin transaction value that year, and Ethereum managing $3.6 trillion in Ether. Though these sums fell short of credit card giants Visa ($10.4 trillion) and MasterCard ($6 trillion), they were more than American Express ($1.2 trillion), and more than the combined payments volume of Stripe, PayPal, Square, and Shopify ($1.3 trillion)—even though nearly all crypto payments are for virtual-only assets, currencies, securities, and tokens, rather than groceries, cars, travel, and so on. In 2022, Bitcoin and Ethereum transfer value declined (−13% and −56% respectively), but average daily transfers were largely flat (−3.3% and +3.7%), with the gap largely due to the declining price of bitcoin and ether throughout the calendar year. In contrast, the US dollar inflated by an average 8% in 2022. Throughout 2021, over $40 billion was spent buying Ethereum-based NFTs alone, only 11% shy of the $50 billion spent on physical art globally, with 2022 sales hitting roughly $25 billion against $68 billion. MetaMask, the most popular crypto wallet, reported that its monthly active users grew from fewer than 500,000 in September 2020 to 10 million a year later, crossing 30 million by March of 2022.

Included in these multibillion- and multitrillion-dollar headlines were a number of significant cultural achievements, too. In July of 2021, the state of Wyoming legalized blockchain-based decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) as a corporate entity, having also been the first state to recognize the LLC business structure back in 1977. The following month, several thousand outdoor enthusiasts used a DAO to purchase a 40-acre plot of land near Yellowstone National Park, which the collective promptly turned into a public park funded and operated by its membership. Three months later, a crypto collective named ConstitutionDAO was formed after Sotheby’s announced that one of 13 surviving first editions of the United States Constitution was set for auction and expected to sell for between $15 and $20 million. Though ConstitutionDAO lost to Ken Griffin, the billionaire founder of the multinational hedge fund Citadel, it raised roughly $47 million in cryptocurrency from nearly 20,000 anonymous contributors in less than a week—none of which required a legal contract, lawyer, or even bank account.

## Artificial Intelligence

A final area that saw sudden, multifaceted advances at the end of the 2010s and the early 2020s was artificial intelligence, which had been in active development longer than not just the Metaverse but the internet, too.

As early as the 1930s, philosophers and mathematicians began to debate whether a machine that processed logic through shuffling symbols as basic as 1 and 0 could be said to “deduce” or “reason.” In 1948, the University of Manchester unveiled the Manchester Mark 1, one of the first computers capable of storing (and thus recalling) a program, prompting the press to refer to the device as an “electronic brain.” As discussed earlier in this chapter, it was possible to play games such as tic-tac-toe against an AI by 1950, with checkers following in 1951.

In 1955, American computer scientist John McCarthy coined the term “artificial intelligence,” and in 1956, IBM researcher Arthur Samuel created one of the first adaptive computer programs, the Samuel Checkers-Playing Program, which improved its checkers performance by playing against itself. The year 1956 also saw the debut of Logic Theorist, the first automated reasoning program, which was developed by Allen Newell, Herbert A. Simon, and Cliff Shaw and is considered the first “true” AI. To test the device, its creators had Logic Theorist attempt to prove the first 52 theorems of Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell’s *Principia Mathematica*. It successfully proved 38 (or 74%) of them, and even established several new—and shorter—proofs than had been created by any human mathematician at the time. In 1958, McCarthy began development of the LISP programming language, which spent decades as the preferred language in the AI field, and is now the second-oldest high-level programming language still in use. In 1959, Samuel coined the term “machine learning.”

The first industrial robot, Unimate, was invented in 1950 by George Devol and debuted on a General Motors assembly line in 1961. Five years later, Unimate joined Johnny Carson’s *Tonight Show* to hit a golf ball (which, tragically, lipped out) and pour a beer (done to perfection). In 1966, MIT computer scientist Joseph Weizenbaum unveiled ELIZA, the first publicly demonstrated “chatbot” that understood natural human language and could credibly attempt the Turing Test. Weizenbaum named his program after the ingenue Eliza Doolittle in George Bernard Shaw’s *Pygmalion*, which took its name from the same Greek myth Stanley Weinbaum had referenced in “Pygmalion’s Spectacles.” In Shaw’s play, the working-class Doolittle is successfully trained to pass as a member of high society, after which the phonetician Henry Higgins deems her a successful experiment.

In 1986, the German professor Ernst Dickmanns unveiled the first autonomous car, a reengineered Mercedes-Benz van called VaMoRs, equipped with cameras, sensors, and a computer capable of real-time computer vision as well as steering, throttling, and braking. By 1987, the Benz’s computer vision was capable of processing enough imagery to drive at 60 miles per hour.

In 1996 and 1997, the IBM chess computer Deep Blue beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov across two six-game matches, winning 4–2 and 3½–2½. Deep Blue’s victories came nearly a half century after Turing had tried to create a program that could just play a game of chess but could not find a computer powerful enough to even test Turochamp’s code. The year 1997 also saw the release of British programmer Rollo Carpenter’s Jabberwacky (named after the famous nonsense poem “Jabberwocky” from Lewis Carroll’s *Through the Looking-Glass, and What Alice Found There*), which became the first popular internet-based chatbot. The express purpose of Carpenter’s Jabberwacky was to develop artificial intelligence by learning from interactions with humans rather than just applying a predefined ruleset and finite knowledge base. Later that year, Dragon Systems unveiled NaturallySpeaking 1.0, the first consumer product to offer continuous speech recognition, and by the end of the decade, various speech-recognition systems had surpassed the vocabulary of the average American.

In 2001, ActiveBuddy, Inc., launched SmarterChild, the first natural language chatbot that was integrated into messaging platforms such as AOL Instant Messenger (AIM) and MSN Messenger and given a distinct personality. Within its first year, nine million users added SmarterChild to their friend list (roughly 2% of all internet users at the time), with a quarter of a million engaging with it daily to look up information, play word games, and pass time. Six years after the launch of SmarterChild, Microsoft acquired ActiveBuddy.
