
# Prior to Starlink’s Commercial Launch

Prior to Starlink’s commercial launch in 2020, Facebook, Google, and various start-ups aspired to build internet networks using solar-powered drones or high-altitude balloons in the stratosphere. The benefits of such equipment would include significantly shorter distances to customers compared to lower-earth-orbit satellites and far lower launch costs.

Beginning in 2016, Facebook’s Aquila project deployed a series of drones roughly 140 feet wide and weighing 900 pounds that flew at altitudes between 18,000 and 27,000 kilometers (93% closer to the ground than Starlink’s satellites) and were intended to operate continuously for 90 days. Facebook hoped that drones might one day provide internet to four billion people, most of whom lacked acceptable alternatives.

Beginning in 2011, Google’s Project Loon launched 50-foot-wide and 40-foot-tall superpressure helium balloons to altitudes between 18 and 25 kilometers, which operated for an average of 100 days before needing to return to earth.

In 2018, Facebook halted development on Aquila, while Google shut Loon down in 2021, citing various technical obstacles, rival priorities, and the deployment of 4G networks across the developing world. Despite the failure of these projects, there is still hope that stratospheric internet will prove viable in the decades to come.

To this point, Starlink’s satellites not only boasted considerable advances over the satellite models of the 2000s and early 2010s, but the company also realized the very vision once touted by the Bill Gates–backed Teledesic.

Between 1994 and 2002, Teledesic had raised over $1 billion as part of its $9 billion plan (equivalent to $19 billion in 2024 dollars) to launch 850 satellites into orbit roughly 700 kilometers above the earth.^([30](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr30)) By 1997, the company’s plans were shrunk to 288 satellites at 1,400 kilometers, and in 2002, Teledesic suspended operations.^([31](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr31))

# Improving the Border Gateway Protocol

In theory, improving the Border Gateway Protocol is the cheapest and fastest way to improve delivery times. The Internet Engineering Task Force does not dictate standards development or mandate the adoption of any updates. Instead, it helps organize working groups who put forward various proposals. And while these proposals sometimes receive tacit or explicit approval from IETF leaders or sponsors, their adoption—and thus ability to become standards—requires the voluntary embrace of the many companies that constitute “the internet.”

In the case of BGP, this would involve hundreds of computing-device and modem/router manufacturers, over 110,000 network operators, millions of application developers, and so on. Not all of these groups would need to adopt a change for there to be benefits—better routing on two of seven network hops between a user and a server would nevertheless save precious milliseconds—but a critical mass of adoptees is required to have any meaningful effect.

And although the benefits of a real-time protocol or packet classification is obvious, the specific solution is less clear. Remember, BGP has multiple criteria in its rank-order preference. Should BGP prioritize real-time traffic that has the greatest distance to travel or the lowest plausible tolerance? Should real-time traffic be moved to the “front of the line” or be boosted by some fixed percentage (and if so, how much)? Should real-time traffic be boosted irrespective of network congestion, thereby significantly worsening the experience for non-real-time traffic? And because the internet is decentralized and largely ungoverned, what system might avoid applications and/or networks coding their traffic as real-time irrespective of its latency needs? Such coding would aid delivery reliability and speed, which benefits most developers and users, even if it’s not required—but it would also take up scarce capacity needed by traffic that did need real-time delivery.

# Real-Time Bandwidth Standards and Big-Tech Infrastructure

A potential solution for real-time bandwidth standards and protocols may stem from the growing ownership of key backbone infrastructure by big-tech companies. In 2012, data sent to and from Amazon, Facebook, Google, and Microsoft constituted less than 10% of traffic transmitted on submarine cables. A decade later, these four companies were more than two-thirds of total traffic, with companies such as Nvidia, Netflix, and Apple taking up much of the rest.

In response to bandwidth fees as well as the desire to improve connectivity across the developing world through non-stratospheric means, these companies began to acquire and purchase the future capacity of existing submarine cables, and co-finance or fully fund the laying of new ones.

By the end of 2024, Facebook is expected to have the greatest share of global submarine infrastructure among tech giants, having increased from 0% in 2015 to partial, complete, or de facto (i.e., capacity-based) ownership of 13%—a portion representing more than 170,000 kilometers of cables and connecting nearly 33 countries across 47 connections, while reaching 36% of the global population, mostly in Africa and Southeast Asia.^([32](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr32))

# Chapter 6 COMPUTING ENTIRE UNIVERSES

Sending enough data in a timely fashion is just one part of the process of operating a synchronized virtual world. The data must also be understood, code must be run, inputs assessed, logic performed, environments rendered, and so on. This is the job of central processing units (CPUs) and graphics processing units (GPUs), each and together broadly described as “compute.”

Compute is the resource that performs all digital “work.” For decades, there have been increases in the number of computing resources available and manufactured per year. Despite this, computing resources have always been and will likely remain scarce—because when more computing capability is available, more calculations and more complicated calculations tend to be made.
