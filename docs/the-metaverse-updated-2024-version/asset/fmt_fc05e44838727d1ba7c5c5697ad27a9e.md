
# Persistence in Virtual Worlds

In virtual worlds, “persistence” refers to the permanence of events, activities, or data. Most video games today are only selectively persistent. Throughout a match of `Fortnite`, players build or destroy various structures, set fire to forests, or kill wildlife, and so on, but after the roughly 20-to-25-minute match concludes, all of these changes are effectively deleted by Epic Games. The map once enjoyed and affected by 100 players will never be re-experienced, or even accessible, again. In fact, even within a given match, `Fortnite`’s virtual world is only partially persistent. A bullet mark on an indestructible rock might appear for only 30 seconds before it, too, is deleted, and thus also any evidence that combat had even taken place at that location. Although the world of `World of Warcraft` is “continuous,” in contrast to the match-based model of `Fortnite`, it operates similarly. If a player enters a specific part of the `World of Warcraft` map, defeats their enemies, leaves, and returns, they’ll typically find that those enemies have respawned. An in-game tradesman who sold a player a rare item only a day earlier might offer them a second as though it would be their first. The worlds of `Fortnite` and `World of Warcraft` can have lasting changes only when their developers deploy an update—and the players have little to no control over whether a given choice or event endures indefinitely.

Persistence is partly a creative challenge. The goal of most virtual worlds is entertainment, and most large-scale entertainment products are tightly produced and managed. By limiting persistence, a game’s developer can better predict what might happen and ensure it’s “fun,” as well as plan future updates that will keep the title both fun and fresh. However, persistence is a technical challenge, too. When a tree is cut down in the physical world, there is no concern for whether or not the systems of Mother Earth can handle such an event or remember it, or whether anyone else might be able to see the felled tree at a later date or interact with it. Physics adhere to their own laws, but they never run out of “processing power.” The production, operation, and memory of a virtual world are all confined by what the relevant computers can store, render, compute, and synchronize. Moreover, the effects of persistence compound. In a simple case, a tree being felled means that it is no longer there. But what of its stump? Does it remain? And does the trunk of the tree now lie on the ground? If still visible, do other users see which side it was chopped from, or just that it was generically cut? And does it “biodegrade”? If so, how—generically, or in response to its local environment? How should the tree affect other items which occupy its same space?

These questions of what should persist and how are not philosophical. A programmer (or AI) must make a decision and build the technology to support it. The answer also determines what a user can and cannot do, as well as the computing resources available for other tasks in the simulation, which affects what the world can and cannot do. In games, the answers might seem trivial, but many of the most popular social platforms, such as `Roblox`, thrive in part due to their comparatively high levels of persistence, which effectively add a fourth dimension (time) to play, and enrich the contributions of any additional user. Digital twins, such as those for a city or factory floor, have particularly high persistence and detail requirements (recall that such models might precisely simulate light, heat, sound, etc.). Networked simulations are also limited by the lowest common denominator of persistence across their network—a good reason for every participant to store as much data as possible, but that also means they must process, render, and synchronize it, too.

## Synchronous

For the Metaverse and its individual experiences to be shared, every relevant participant, network, and device must be synchronized in real time. This requires the user to maintain a continuous^([‡](#chapter4.xhtml#footnote-033)) (i.e., sustained and uninterrupted) connection to a virtual world’s server (both to and from) and that can deliver vast volumes of data (“high bandwidth”) in near real time (“low latency”).

These may not seem like outlandish requirements. After all, there are a hundred million people streaming high-definition video at any given moment, while much of the global economy ran via live and synchronous videoconference software throughout the COVID-19 pandemic. And broadband providers improve the availability, bandwidth, and latency of their networks each year, with internet outages becoming less common each day. However, the internet works so well today because of the many tricks deployed to cover up moments of low bandwidth, high latency, and dropped connections. Spotify, for example, used to start a track’s progress bar immediately after a user selected the track, leading them to believe it was playing even though it had yet to even load.

Although the playback of a Netflix stream is continuous, the company is sending users distinct batches of data, many of which travel through different network paths from the server to that user. By using excess bandwidth to send the user data before it’s needed—such as minutes 45 and 46 when the user is only on minute 44—Netflix can ensure playback continues in the event the user’s device loses its connection to a local Wi-Fi router or cell tower, or if there’s congestion somewhere along the route between Netflix’s server and the user. Because Netflix receives video files anywhere from months to hours before they’re made available to audiences, it is also able to analyze these titles to determine what information can be discarded in the event a user’s bandwidth suddenly drops. Specifically, Netflix’s machine learning systems will scan each frame to simplify its elements—a background sky might be composed of 500 shades of blue which, though beautiful, could be simplified to 200, 50, or 25 without significantly harming the viewer experience. The streamer’s analytics even do this on a contextual basis—recognizing that scenes of dialogue can tolerate more compression than those of faster-paced action, and that the size and resolution of the viewer’s screen matter too. In addition, Netflix will preload content at local nodes. When a user asks for the newest episode of `Stranger Things`, it might be just a few blocks away and therefore arrives immediately.

These approaches work only because most Netflix content is prerecorded; it’s not possible to “pre-do” anything that has yet to occur. It’s for this reason that live video is substantially less reliable than on-demand streams. And even streaming services that deliver live video have tricks. In most cases, transmissions are delayed by 2 to 30 seconds, which means there’s still the opportunity to pre-send content in case of temporary congestion. Commercial breaks can also be used by both the content provider’s server, or the user, to reset the connection in case the prior one proved unreliable. Most live videos require only a one-way continuous connection—for instance, from CNN’s server to the user. Sometimes there is a two-way connection, as in the case of a Twitch chat, but only a sparse amount of data is being shared (the chat itself) and it’s not of critical importance, as it does not directly affect what’s happening in the video (remember, it probably happened 2 to 30 seconds earlier).
