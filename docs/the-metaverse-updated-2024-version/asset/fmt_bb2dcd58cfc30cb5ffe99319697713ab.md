
# Helium 与去中心化网络

Another example is Helium, which the *New York Times* has described as “a decentralized wireless network for ‘internet of things’ devices, powered by cryptocurrency.”^([9](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr9)) Helium sells a $500 hot-spot device that allows the secure rebroadcasting of a home internet connection—at speeds up to 200 times faster than a traditional home Wi-Fi device. This internet service can be used by anyone, from consumers wanting to check Facebook to infrastructure (a parking meter processing credit card transactions) and transportation networks (whose tens of thousands of bikes, scooters, and mopeds often encounter mobile network “dead zones”).^([10](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr10)) Those operating a Helium hot spot are compensated with Helium’s HNT token in proportion to usage. As of the summer of 2023, Helium’s network spanned more than 400,000 active hot spots, up from fewer than 25,000 at the start of 2021, distributed across roughly 50,000 cities in 175 countries.^([11](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr11)) Notably, the company was founded in 2013 but struggled to gain adoption until it pivoted from a traditional (i.e., unpaid) peer-to-peer model to one that offered contributors direct compensation via cryptocurrency. The long-term viability and potential of Helium remains uncertain; most internet service providers (ISPs) prohibit their customers from rebroadcasting their internet connection, and while the ISPs typically have ignored such service violations as long as the connection was not resold and total data usage was low, there is no guarantee that the ISPs will continue to ignore such violations by users of Helium or any analogous system. Regardless, the company, which has pivoted to making deals directly with ISPs, serves as a further reminder of the potential of decentralized payment models.

# Dapp 代币与激励机制

The tokens issued by a dapp can reward network participants for more than just providing computing and networking resources, as is the case with Bitcoin, Ethereum, RNDR, and Helium. These tokens can also be awarded for contributing personal time, delivering new users (customer acquisition), data entry, IP rights, capital (money), bandwidth, good behavior (community ratings, perhaps), or helping to moderate, and may include governance rights and, of course, are likely to appreciate alongside the underlying project.

# 去中心化社交网络与代币模型

To some, such systems offer a potential solution to the harassment and toxicity that is so prevalent on large-scale online social networks. Imagine a model in which Twitter users were awarded valuable Twitter tokens for reporting poor behavior or reviewing previously reported misbehavior, and lost them for violating the rules. Instead of relying on tips or promotional tweets on behalf of advertisers to generate income, superusers and influencers could be awarded tokens for hosting events. By the end of 2021, Kickstarter, Reddit, and Discord had all publicly described plans to shift to blockchain-based token models. Kickstarter and Discord later abandoned these plans due to user backlash, but in July 2022 Reddit announced the launch of its first NFT avatar collection, starring the company’s familiar “Snoo” mascot; users could purchase, sell, or earn through contributions to the community, which could be managed in a Reddit-branded crypto wallet. After a year, 20 million avatars had been minted across four “generations,” with nearly 17 million users purchasing or earning an avatar and close to $40 million in peer-to-peer (“secondary”) sales taking place.^([12](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr12)) Not long after, the company announced it was depreciating all of its non-crypto-based loyalty, points, and reward systems, implying a shift to a mostly blockchain-based framework.^([13](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr13))

# 去中心化自治组织（DAO）

Smart contracts serve as the basis for decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs), collectives that use these programs to raise capital, maintain and administer governance processes, complete investments, and issue dividends—all without hiring a lawyer, performing credit checks, establishing a bank account, confirming payments, or tracking membership. The diversity of these DAOs and their achievements have been pretty remarkable.

Many of the most expensive NFTs have been purchased not by individuals but by DAOs made up of countless pseudonymous crypto users who could never have made the purchase on their own. The most notable of these DAOs, ConstitutionDAO, was formed in November 2021 to bid on a physical copy of the US Constitution that would be put up for auction less than a week later, which Sotheby’s estimated would sell for between $15 and $20 million. In the span of three days, over $47 million was raised from 20,000 mostly anonymous contributors. Despite the striking scale of its treasury, ConstitutionDAO ultimately lost the auction, though Bloomberg reported that the auction had “showed the power of the DAO,” which has “the potential to change the way people buy things, build companies, share resources and run non-profits.”^([14](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr14)) Such prognostications take time to be proved or disproved, but a potential example might be the Komorebi Collective, a DAO-based venture capital fund formed in late 2021. The purpose of the DAO is to invest in crypto start-ups with “exceptional female and nonbinary founders”—two profiles that are chronically overlooked by traditional funds. Among Komorebi’s founding 36 members are high-profile “Web 1.0” and “Web 2.0” venture capital giants such as Kleiner Perkins, the design and consulting firm IDEO, and former executives from companies such as Spotify and Twitter, in addition to individuals who don’t typically participate in the financial sector, such as journalists and human rights workers.^([15](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr15))

DAOs have also been used to establish crypto-based social clubs, such as Friends with Benefits, which requires its members to purchase FWB tokens to access the club’s Discord channel and events. Some have argued that by requiring users to buy tokens to gain entry, FWB is simply replicating the centuries-old “membership dues” model of every exclusive club that came before it—with extra juice in the form of “crypto” hype. However, members do not pay annual “dues.” Instead, they need to buy a certain number of FWB tokens to gain entry—and then hold them to remain members. As a result, every member is a part owner of FWB, and can leave at any time by selling their tokens. Because these tokens appreciate as the club becomes more successful or desirable, every member is incentivized to invest time, ideas, and resources into the club. And as the effective entry fee goes up, the practicality of joining the club just to spam goes down. Appreciation also means that the club must work harder to earn its ongoing role in a member’s life, too. If an individual joins a club by buying $1,000 in tokens, they intuitively see enough value in their membership to forgo access at least temporarily to $1,000 of their money (and potentially see its value decline if the club’s popularity declines). If these tokens quadruple in price, the club must be thriving, but it also has to offer enough value to those who joined at $1,000 to keep them from selling and taking a profit—which would create downward pressure on FWB’s token price while publicly broadcasting that members were leaving and membership was generally unstable. Finally, many social DAOs use smart contracts to issue tokens to individual members for their contributions or to those who cannot afford to join the collective but are deemed worthy by its members.
