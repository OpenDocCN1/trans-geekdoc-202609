
Alongside the leaps in transformer-based AI were equally impressive, if less heralded, achievements in neural networks. One example is neural radiance fields (`NeRFs`), which were originally developed at Google in 2019 and 2020. `NeRFs` use neural computing networks to produce large-scale, realistic immersive models out of a few dozen 2D photos, with the network filling in the many unseen angles and elements, such as the underside of a wood table or a partly obscured stove. In 2023, Google announced its `NeRF` system would be used to supplement its Google Maps and Street View solutions with full immersive models of major cities, and the interiors of popular destinations such as particular restaurants or museums, as well as the ability to “time slide” through different weather and traffic conditions in order to aid trip planning.

As is reviewed in detail throughout [Chapter 12](#chapter12.xhtml), AI is a critical aspect of the Metaverse and serves several functions. From a philosophical perspective, it reflects and accelerates the multidecade trend toward synthetically aided existence. It is also required to overcome many of the physics-related challenges to building the Metaverse. In May 2023, for example, Google announced that advances in AI had enabled it to shrink its two-year-old Starline device from the size of an enclosed restaurant booth to that of a midsize TV sitting atop a minifridge, while also shifting from a dozen high-end cameras and sensors to only a “few standard” ones.^([86](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr86)) As a result, Google promised, the device would be both easier and cheaper to deploy but would still maintain its performance benefits versus traditional 2D displays. In other instances, improvements in AI will enable computer processors to “beat” the speed of light and better contend with unreliable and low-capacity networks (detailed in [Chapters 5](#chapter5.xhtml) and [6](#chapter6.xhtml)) by predicting what transmitted data will tell it to do before it arrives.

AI will also enable there to be more and better virtual worlds across the Metaverse. In 2022, `Roblox`’s roughly 275 million average monthly users created nearly 5.5 million worlds, though nearly all fall short of what users imagined. `Roblox` co-founder and CEO David Baszucki believes generative AI will enable users to type or speak entire worlds into existence by 2028.^([87](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr87)) And difficult though the creation of purely fictional worlds is today, it’s even more difficult to translate the ever-changing “real world” into virtual assets. With solutions such as `NeRF` and photogrammetry, it may soon be possible to produce an “Earth 2.0” that covers nearly every square meter seen by humankind.

Just as AI can help generate and operate virtual worlds, it will also help interoperate between them—enabling otherwise incompatible file formats, coding conventions, and systems to seamlessly interconnect with one another without needing human developers to conform to a single standard or produce endless permutations of every world, asset, or data set they built.

Another related role for AI is in populating virtual worlds. Transformer models from companies such as OpenAI and Facebook are now being used to create virtual agents, characters, and entire communities with detailed personalities that grow with their experiences and can serve as friends, colleagues, or mentors. Lastly, humanity will rely on AI systems to operate large-scale digital twins and simulations, as they can process more information and make more decisions than any human could, and guide users through them.

## Why the Metaverse Matters

When Ray Bradbury, Isaac Asimov, and Philip K. Dick began to build upon the ideas of Stanley Weinbaum and Vannevar Bush, virtual worlds were limited to single-purpose computers, played by single users, rendered in barely changing 2D monochrome, and were accessed by no more than a dozen scientists and researchers a year. Today, there are vast cloud-based networks that span tens of billions of dynamic virtual worlds that reach more than half a billion people on a regular basis and operate multibillion-dollar economies. And in the decades to come, these worlds will continue to grow in realism, diversity, functionality, economic value, and cultural influence. Meanwhile, the technologies behind these worlds will improve in capability, expand in deployment, and more deeply integrate into the physical. Precisely when “the Metaverse” arrives is a philosophical question; that it’s coming faster than ever can be said with certainty.

Another certainty is the many wars for supremacy in and over this new computing era. They will be fought between tech giants and insurgent start-ups via hardware, technical standards, and tools, as well as content, digital wallets, and virtual identities. The wars will be motivated by more than just revenue potential or the need to survive the “pivot to Metaverse.”

In 2016, a year before his company’s release of `Fortnite` and long before the term “Metaverse” entered public consciousness, Epic’s Tim Sweeney told reporters: “This Metaverse is going to be far more pervasive and powerful than anything else. If one central company gains control of this, they will become more powerful than any government and be a god on Earth.”^([88](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr88)) It is easy to find such a statement hyperbolic.^([##](#chapter3.xhtml#footnote-036)) The history of the internet, however, suggests that it may not be.

The foundation of today’s internet was built over several decades and through a variety of consortiums and informal working groups composed of government research labs, universities, and independent technologists and institutions. These mostly not-for-profit collectives typically focused on establishing open standards that would help them share information from one server to another, and in doing so make it easier to collaborate on future technologies, projects, and ideas. The internet’s decentralized design also reflected the philosophies of individuals such as Vannevar Bush, who hoped to exchange rather than control data, and the necessities of the era during which it was built, the Cold War, which prompted the adoption of transmission protocols and technologies that could operate even if substantial portions of the network were destroyed or commandeered.^([89](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr89))

The benefits of this approach were far-ranging. For example, anyone with an internet connection could build a website in minutes and at no cost using pure HTML, and even faster using a platform like GeoCities. A single version of the site could be accessed by every device, browser, and user connected to the internet. In addition, no user or developer needed to be disintermediated—they could produce content for, and speak to, anyone they wanted. The use of common standards also meant that it was easier and cheaper to hire and work with outside vendors, integrate third-party software and apps, and repurpose code. The fact that so many of these standards were free and open-source meant that individual innovations often benefited the entire ecosystem, while placing competitive pressures on paid, proprietary standards, and helping to check the rent-seeking tendencies of platforms sitting between the web and its users (e.g., device manufacturers, operating systems, browsers, and internet service providers).
