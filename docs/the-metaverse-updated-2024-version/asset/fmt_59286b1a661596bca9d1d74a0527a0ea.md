
In October 2022, Apple formally revised its policies to allow NFT sales in iOS apps, but many catches applied. Most notably, NFTs could not be used to “unlock” functionality, as would be the case with NFTs used inside games, or to access content, such as a streaming video service.^([48](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr48)) Apple would also be owed 30% of the fee from the transaction, which would paid in a traditional currency rather than a cryptocurrency (Apple’s typical store rules, such as the prohibition on telling users of alternative payment methods, continued to apply). No major marketplaces or applications accepted Apple’s proposal, citing several issues. A key matter was that marketplaces could not practically determine (and therefore prevent) NFTs which had, or might soon have, unlocking capabilities. It was also impossible to imagine any user would choose to purchase or sell a multi-thousand-dollar NFT, let alone a multimillion-dollar one, through an app that added thousands or millions in added fees, rather than do so via browser. And even if both options were available, it was obviously problematic for a “marketplace” to simultaneously offer two very different prices—not only would this produce confusion, but it would lead to angry customers demanding a partial refund after buying through the “wrong channel.” Worse still, a 30% fee would eventually devour the entirety of an NFT’s value if it traded hands enough times. As a hypothetical, imagine an NFT that sells for $100 in ether, netting Apple $30 and the seller $70. If the buyer then wanted to sell the NFT, it would need to sell for $143 (+45%!) to break even on the $100 purchase, as Apple would collect another $43 (or $73 total on an NFT worth $143). If the buyer could not resell the NFT for more than $50 (which might be due to declining demand and/or erosion in the underlying cryptocurrency), they would net only $35, while Apple would collect $15 (cumulatively $45 on an NFT worth $50). Lastly, such a transaction would require the buyer, seller, or marketplace to pay Apple in cash. Web3 advocates disliked this requirement as it would likely require selling off some of their cryptocurrency as a result (which would create downward pricing pressure on the ecosystem), and told Apple the “real-world” identity of the NFT’s owner.

In December of 2022, Apple reportedly proposed a new App Store rule whereby select (i.e., not all) marketplaces (not games) would be allowed to support blockchain-based in-app payments for select NFTs (those that did not have unlocking functionality), and Apple would take a 30% cut of the associated “gas fee,” rather than the value of the NFT.^([49](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr49)) Though this fee would be far more modest in dollar terms, marketplaces again refused Apple’s rules and, as such, declined to update their apps to support in-app purchasing or trading. To these marketplaces, Apple’s willingness to take a comparatively lower fee solved only one of the aforementioned problems with its IAP solution—and created several others. For example, gas fees can run from tens of cents to tens of dollars or more based on network congestion, making Apple’s fee both highly variable and uncorrelated to the services being rendered. Apple’s fee would also have to apply to NFTs that were gifted to users or simply transferred between a user’s own wallet, as there was no way for Apple to distinguish between transactions.

Although the major platforms sometimes suggest that their refusal to support or distribute blockchains stems from a concern about user safety, government regulations, or energy use, such claims don’t hold up to scrutiny. Sony’s music label has invested in NFT start-ups, and created its own NFTs, while Microsoft’s Azure offers blockchain certifications and its corporate venture arm has made numerous start-up investments. Apple CEO Tim Cook has admitted that he owns cryptocurrencies and has been interested in the field for a “while.”^([50](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr50)) Instead, it’s obvious that these platforms refuse blockchain games because they simply do not work with their revenue models. Allowing *Call of Duty: Mobile* to connect to a cryptocurrency wallet would be akin to a user connecting the game directly to their bank account, rather than paying through Apple’s App Store or Sony’s PlayStation Store.

The impact of these many bans and limitations has been profound. Web3-focused games could run natively only on the PC, were limited to the browser on iOS, and were not available on the Microsoft Xbox, Nintendo Switch, or Sony PlayStation. It’s for this reason that nearly all of the hit blockchain games of 2020 and 2021 were focused on collecting (virtual sports cards, digital artwork, and so on) or otherwise limited to simple 2D graphics and turn-based play (consider *Axie Infinity*, a sort of reimagining of the 1990s hit *Pokémon*). Furthermore, there have been no high-profile “AAA blockchain games,” despite industry leaders such as Electronic Arts, Konami, Nexon, Roblox, Sega, Square Enix, Ubisoft, and more having once announced plans to launch such a title or blockchain-related capabilities. To this end, claims that “gamers beat NFTs”^([51](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr51)) are misleading. Even if gamers don’t want blockchain games, they haven’t been given much of an opportunity to play them.

## Digital First Requires the Physical First

At the core of the virtual payment rails problem is a conflict. The very idea of the Metaverse supposes that the “next platform” is not based on hardware, or even on an operating system. Instead, it is a persistent network of virtual simulations that exist irrespective of, and in fact are agnostic regarding, a given device or system. The difference is that between a *New York Times* app that runs on a single user’s iPhone and an iPhone used to access a living *New York Times* universe. There is evidence of this transition today. The most popular virtual worlds, such as those of *Fortnite*, *Roblox*, and *Minecraft*, are designed to run on as many devices and operating systems as possible, and are only lightly optimized for any specific one.

Of course, no one can access the Metaverse without hardware. And every hardware player is fighting to be a (if not *the*) payment gateway to this multitrillion-dollar opportunity. To win this fight, the market leaders forcibly bundle their hardware and operating systems with first-party services, third-party software distribution, payment rails, browser engines, identities, and entitlement management solutions. The combined force of this bundle enables a platform to limit to development of threatening business models, technologies, or competitors, while also protecting lucrative payment-processing fees, as WebGL, cloud gaming, NFC, browser engines, blockchains, and so on demonstrate.

There are always justifications for an individual policy, but they’re impossible for the market to validate when there are only two smartphone platforms and their respective stacks are so extensively bundled. Even regulatory efforts to introduce more competition to individual service offerings tend to be stymied by platform power. In August 2021, a bill passed in South Korea banning app store operators from requiring their own payment systems, arguing such a requirement was monopolistic and harmed both consumers and developers. Three months later, and before the law changes were set to come into effect, Google announced that apps which chose to use an alternative payment service would have to pay a new fee for using their app store. Its price? Four percent less than the old fee—almost exactly the cost of its old fee, less the fees charged by Visa, MasterCard, or PayPal. As such, any developer who chose to use another payment rail would end up with savings of less than 1%—a margin so small that changing payment systems would be senseless, and no price cut to consumers would be possible.
