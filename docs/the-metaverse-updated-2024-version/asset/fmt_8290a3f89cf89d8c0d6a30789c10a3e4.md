
Despite the silent friction inherent in the internet’s design, most non-live experiences on the internet still feel live because the volume of updates sent and requested is high (as in the case of instant messengers), the delivery time is typically rapid and delays often hidden behind design tricks, users don’t necessarily know when information is out of date, and update requests occur more regularly when an application is open and thus under the eye of the user. Unlike web browsing and instant messaging, however, truly live online experiences require a user’s device to establish a sustained or continuous connection to the relevant third-party server. It’s for this reason that video calls, live video streaming, and video games are not always possible, let alone reliable in performance. Yet it’s these experiences, not browsing the `New York Times`, that best reflect the data needs of the Metaverse. The Metaverse is a live world and live experience and therefore requires both live (i.e., real-time) information and a continuous connection to its source. And unfortunately, neither the internet backbone nor the Internet Protocol Suite was designed for these applications. As an example, TCP/IP’s Border Gateway Protocol, which routes traffic on the internet as it jumps from one network to another, neither routes nor prioritizes traffic that needs to arrive quickly or whose connection must be sustained. Indeed, BGP doesn’t even receive that information.

Making matters more challenging, the Metaverse is imagined as a shared and multiparticipant experience. As such, the information sent by a given user or server must be synchronized across all relevant users, networks, and devices in real time. Again, though, the internet was designed to connect no more than two devices or servers at once, and for only finite periods of time.

This structure has worked fairly well over the past 40 years because there are relatively few shared online experiences. When visiting an office cafeteria, many users are on the company’s corporate Wi-Fi, but their experiences are entirely disconnected and irrelevant to one another. When two users scroll BuzzFeed together on their respective devices, they are sharing a real-world experience, but not a digital one. In some cases, the internet is co-experienced—but these experiences are typically simple, computationally limited, and tolerant of delays. Live text chats, such as those on Twitch, are uncoordinated between users, barely customized, and unaffected by which entry appears above or below another. Google Docs are coordinated, but it’s rare that they are edited concurrently, and even rarer that the same part of the document has several simultaneous authors.^([†](#chapter2.xhtml#footnote-048)) Video calls are an exception to the above as they are both live and, by definition, multiuser experiences. Yet these applications have an important advantage: there is usually just one focal participant at any given moment, with all others serving primarily as spectators, not “collaborators” in the experience.

What makes the Metaverse particularly difficult from a networking perspective is that every additional user or device has a linear impact on the number of required continuous connections—which is already hard—and an exponential impact on the number of interactions that must be synchronized in real time. And unlike a video call or Google Doc, these interactions are not video frames or text, but contribute to a complex and dynamic computer model. Recall that the Street protocol of Neal Stephenson’s could “handle” more than ten million simultaneous users.

A related problem is that of “copies.” The Internet Protocol Suite as it exists today is designed to share copies of files. As such, every message, document, or photo that is sent is ultimately copied to various recipient devices. When a user sends a photo from their iPhone using Gmail, a copy exists on their device and Gmail account, as well as the recipient’s email service, and any number of devices they use to download it on or view it. Both users’ iCloud accounts may also store a copy of the photo, too, which may or may not sync with whether the device keeps or deletes the photo. There is no mechanism in TCP/IP that establishes, let alone manages or enforces, the canonical version of a file, ownership of it, or various access and permission rights. Instead, these properties and rights are possible—on a limited basis—only when the content remains on a single platform on the internet. If a `Roblox` user wants to trade an avatar to their friend, `Roblox` can ensure the friend receives it, the sender loses access to it, and so on. If that user were to export the avatar, then the copy problem begins. To use a “real-world” analogy, Chase Bank can reassign the rights to a $100 bill across its customers’ bank accounts, but neither Chase nor the US Treasury can control who has it, takes it, or might try to forge it when it leaves Chase Bank.

There are some bodies that administer the “canon” of the internet. The domain system managed by ICANN is a literal database which enables various agents (e.g., GoDaddy or Name.com) to register domains on behalf of private individuals and shift their websites from one domain to another, or sell their domain to another person or company. The DNS record is singular, authoritative, and dynamic, having expanded from only 7 top-level domains in 1998 (.com,.org,.net,.edu,.gov, .int, .mil) to more than 1,500 a quarter century later, but neither technical systems nor governance structures of ICANN can scale to manage infinitely expanding inventories of virtual goods worth trillions per year.

The Metaverse does not mean securitizing or canonizing all content on the internet. It’s not just okay, it’s good that many copies of a photo might exist, and that User A cannot alter a photo they sent to User B after User B received it, or take it back. But for the Metaverse to have a network-level economy for any virtual goods, or any creations, that spans more than a single platform, it requires altogether new systems for object rights and exchange.

To summarize: a basic definition of the Metaverse might be a “shared, live, and 3D version of the internet that supports an open economy of virtual goods.” [`Part II`](#part2.xhtml) of this book will progress from this definition to a description that better illuminates the Metaverse and its technological needs. But for the time being, this stated definition should suffice to explain the “next internet” in contrast to the current one.

## What the Metaverse Is Not

Having established a baseline, if still simplified, understanding of both the internet and the Metaverse, it’s helpful to address common Metaverse myths and misunderstandings. Several points should now be obvious to readers, but it’s helpful to address them directly.

### Error #1: Using the singular article “a” Metaverse, plural form “Metaverses,” or possessive construction such as “Company A’s Metaverse”

Companies such as Amazon, Apple, Facebook, Google, and Microsoft are not “the internet,” do not operate “the internet,” control the protocols of the “internet,” or have their own “internets.” Instead, these companies operate large, hardware- and software-based, horizontal and vertical platforms that are on the internet, help users access the internet, and provide things to do with the internet. And as vast and mighty as these various platforms are, they represent just a small portion of the overall internet—even when combined. This is the right way to think about the Metaverse, too.
