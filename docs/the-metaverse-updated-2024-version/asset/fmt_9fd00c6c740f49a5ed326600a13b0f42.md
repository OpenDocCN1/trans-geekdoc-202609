
At the time of Sweeney’s post, the Metaverse was already years behind not only the fictional forecasts of *Snow Crash*, which supposed that the Metaverse was fully operational and inhabited by more than a hundred million people by the early 2010s, but also the real-world hopes of many tech entrepreneurs. But as the histories of mobile computing and electrification remind us, eras begin when many different technologies converge and ecosystems are built up around them. And by the early 2020s, there was growing consensus that the Metaverse was about to hit its version of the 1910s in electricity or the 18-month period that followed the iPhone’s debut in 2007. The modern history of virtual worlds, real-time graphical simulation, head-mounted displays, human culture and pastimes, and AI also helps to explain this belief, too.

### Synthetic Worlds

Most accounts of the history of the Metaverse begin in the 1970s, with the advent of Multi-User Dungeons (or MUDs), a sort-of software-based version of the role-playing game Dungeons & Dragons, but which was experienced purely through text and the internet. Using text-based commands that resembled human languages, MUD players could interact with one another, explore a fictional world populated by non-playable characters and monsters, attain power-ups and knowledge, and eventually retrieve a magical chalice, defeat an evil wizard, or rescue a princess.

The growing popularity of MUDs inspired the creation of Multi-User Shared Hallucinations (or MUSHs) and Multi-User Experiences (MUXs). Unlike MUDs, which asked players to carry out specific roles in the context of a specific and usually fantastical narrative, MUSHs and MUXs enabled participants to collaboratively define the world and its objective. Players might choose to set their MUSH in a courtroom, while taking on roles such as defendant, attorney, plaintiff, judge, and members of the jury. One participant might later decide to transform the relatively mundane proceedings into a hostage situation—which would then be defused by a poem mad-libbed by the other players. As late as 1993, traffic analysis suggested that 10% of data transmitted on the internet was for MUDs, MUSHs, and MUXs—a remarkable figure given that these simulations transmitted little data in each message compared to emails, financial files, and research reports.^([5](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr5))

The next great advance came in 1986 with the release of the Commodore 64 online game *Habitat*, published by Lucasfilm, the production company founded by *Star Wars* creator George Lucas. *Habitat* was described as “a multi-participant online virtual environment” and, in a reference to Gibson’s novel *Neuromancer*, “a cyberspace.” Unlike MUDs and MUSHs, the world of *Habitat* was graphical, thereby allowing users to actually see virtual environments and characters, though only via pixeled 2D. It also afforded players far greater control over the in-game environment. “Citizens” of *Habitat* were in charge of the laws and expectations of their virtual world, and had to barter with each other for necessary resources and avoid being robbed or killed for their wares. This challenge led to periods of chaos, after which new rules, regulations, and authorities were established by the player community to maintain order.

Though *Habitat* is not as widely remembered as other 1980s video games, such as *Pac-Man* and *Super Mario Bros.*, it transcended the niche appeal of MUDs and MUSHs, ultimately becoming a commercial hit. The title was also the first game to repurpose the Sanskrit term *avatar*, which roughly translates to “the descent of a deity from heaven,” to refer to a user’s virtual body. Decades later, this usage has become convention—in no small part because Stephenson reapplied it in *Snow Crash*.

In the 1990s, virtual world platforms began to transcend the flatness of 2D text and imagery. *Web World*, released in 1994*,* was the first open-ended virtual world platform that was rendered in “2.5D.”^([6](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr6)) This graphics approach produced the illusion of 3D through the use of isometric art styles,^([†](#chapter3.xhtml#footnote-046)) mathematically precise distortions in the virtual world’s map and objects, and tilting the virtual world’s map and/or the camera angles a player used to see it. In 1995, Worlds, Inc., brought true 3D to the category and was expressly focused on all forms of virtual socializing, rather than game-like objectives.^([7](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr7)) That same year saw the debut of *Activeworlds*, the first virtual world platform that enabled multiple users to build a given world at the same time using 3D drag-and-drop objects, rather than just commands and code.^([8](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr8)) Notably, *Activeworlds* also had the express purpose of building Stephenson’s Metaverse and asked players to both enjoy its virtual world and help expand and populate it.^([9](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr9)) In 1998, *OnLive! Traveler* launched with spatial voice chat, allowing users to hear where other players were positioned relative to other participants, and for an avatar’s mouth to move in response to the words spoken by the player.^([10](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr10))

In 1999, Intrinsic Graphics, a 3D gaming software company, completed the spin-off of Keyhole, whose titular product was a virtual production of the entire planet earth. Five years later, Google acquired the company after concluding that more than a quarter of Google Search queries were for geospatial information, such as the location of a supermarket or the directions to it.^([11](#notes_split_003.xhtml#ch3fr11)) In 2005, Google renamed Keyhole “Google Earth” and made it available for free to consumers worldwide. In the ensuing 20 years, Google’s many upgrades to Google Earth brought the product ever closer to the “Earth” application of Stephenson’s *Snow Crash*, which had inspired at least one of Keyhole’s founders to start the business. In 2007, Google began its Google Street View project, which eventually enabled Google Maps users to view 360-degree photos staged every few feet (in cities) or dozen meters (rural areas) on nearly every street in the world. By 2020, Google was updating these photos every year (cities) or three (rural areas). In 2008, Google added live traffic information across its mapping products. At first, Google’s data set relied on licenses to traffic sensors owned by various governments and private infrastructure operators, but the company eventually factored in data generated by the billions of smartphone-based Google Maps users, which provided greater insight into real-time transit, scheduled transit, and typical patterns. In 2012, Google Earth launched a 3D mode for select cities, such as Manhattan and Paris.
