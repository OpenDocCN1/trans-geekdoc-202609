
By the start of 2024, Epic had yet to integrate *Fall Guys* into *Fortnite*—a move that seemed likely, but was technically difficult and time consuming. The seemingly rapid integration of *Rocket League* was aided by the fact Psyonix had built the game on Unreal.^([\*](#chapter8.xhtml#footnote-021)) In contrast, *Fall Guys* was built on Unity. As such, integration required Epic to either rebuild the title on its engine (while ensuring it “felt” and “played” the same), and/or invest in a series of translator tools—and do so while sustaining (if not growing) *Fall Guys* player base and defending against many copycat competitors that emerged after the title became a hit. Regardless of when (or whether) *Fall Guys* relaunches as part of *Fortnite*, it appears that through its cross-title assets and achievements model, Epic is trying to set a precedent similar to the ones it established in cross-platform gaming. The company believes that there are benefits—that is, more profits and players—to reducing the friction to accessing different games, making it easier for players to bring their friends and items across these games, and giving them a reason to try out new games. Players will then spend more time gaming, with more people, across a greater diversity of titles, spending more money along the way. If so, an ever-expanding network of third-party games will want to connect to Epic’s virtual identity, communications, and entitlements systems (which are a part of Epic Online Services), which helps to make Epic’s various offerings the standard. And only two months after the launch of *LEGO Fortnite*, Epic’s ostensible strategy received external validation: The Walt Disney Company agreed to invest $1.5 billion into Epic as part of a broader partnership that would involve the creation of a “new, persistent, and interoperable universe” connected to *Fortnite*.^([10](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr10)) In its promotional artwork, Disney displayed a network of worlds based on franchises and films such as *A Nightmare Before Christmas*, *Avatar*, Marvel, Monsters, Inc., and *Star Wars*, as well as brands like Disney Cruise Line, ESPN, and 20th Century Studios, while promising fans would be able to “play, watch, \[and\] shop,” as well as “create their own stories and experiences.”^([11](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr11))

In support of these efforts, Epic had a lower-profile but potentially more significant product launch in December of 2022, Verse, a new programming language “designed for the Metaverse” that Epic also open-sourced.^([12](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr12)) Speaking to developers about Verse at the 2023 Game Developers Conference, Sweeney said it “is about solving the larger problem of the metaverse.”^([13](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr13)) “When you release an app, you use a bunch of \[different code\] libraries. But you compile one piece of code, you ship it, and it doesn’t change again until you release a new version,” he remarked, but “the Metaverse, we think, will be the combination of millions of objects written by millions of different developers—a lot of code, a lot of content that’s constantly being updated—in which vehicles from one developer, together with characters from another developer, have to work together, even if those programmers have never met each other or never tested their content before \[and use different engines\].”^([14](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr14)) To address this, Sweeney argued the Metaverse needed a “special kind of programming foundation”—one that was designed to be easy to learn, could better detect errors before being implemented into a live experience, was modularized, backward compatible, and widely interoperable, could scale to billions of users in a single simulation, be correctly interpreted by different clients just as different web browsers agree on the look and functionality of a webpage, and could support a decentralized open economy.^([15](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr15)) Following *Fortnite*’s December 2023 expansion, Sweeney declared that the sixth version of Unreal Engine was effectively “Unreal Engine 5 + Verse + rough deployment parity into *Fortnite* and into standalone products + metaverse economy + standards + ?? magic TBD.”^([16](#notes_split_008.xhtml#ch8fr16)) In addition to driving asset and economic interoperability, Epic is also driving the “interoperation” of competing intellectual properties, which is a philosophical, rather than a technical, problem (cross-platform gaming is a reminder that this is the harder of the two challenges). As virtual platforms like *Fortnite*, *Minecraft*, and *Roblox* have grown into culture-defining social spaces, they’ve become an increasingly necessary part of consumer marketing, brand building, and multimedia franchise experiences. Over its first five years, *Fortnite* has produced experiences with the NFL and FIFA; Disney’s Marvel Comics, *Star Wars*, and *Alien*; Warner Bros.’ DC Comics; anime hits *Dragon Ball* and *Naruto*; Lionsgate’s *John Wick*; Microsoft’s *Halo*; Sony’s *God of War* and *Horizon Zero Dawn*; Capcom’s *Street Fighter*; Hasbro’s G.I. Joe; Nike; and Michael Jordan, Travis Scott, and on and on. But to participate in these experiences, brand owners must embrace something they almost never allow: unlimited-term licenses (in-game outfits are kept by players forever), overlapping marketing windows (some brand events are mere days apart or overlap entirely), and as good as no editorial control. In sum, this means it’s now possible to dress as Neymar while wearing a Baby Yoda or Air Jordan backpack, holding Aquaman’s trident, and exploring a virtual Stark Industries. And the owners of these franchises *want* this to happen. By 2023, it was common for many blockbuster games—even those which ostensibly prioritize realism, such as *Call of Duty* or *PUBG: Battlegrounds*—to incorporate and intermingle popular IP.

To counteract the adverse effects of interoperable goods, several solutions have been proposed. One idea is “programmed degradation”—that is, for virtual goods to wear out with use or time or transfer, which would help keep such goods “scarce” and provide creators (or underlying license owners) with enduring price and supply power. An alternative approach would involve royalties paid to an item’s creator every time a trade occurs. Developers can (and would) also produce virtual goods that are not interoperable, cannot be resold or traded, or are altered when transferred. Keep in mind, too, that enabling interoperable assets does not mean embracing all of their functionality. An avatar that is able to fly in one virtual world is not automatically capable of doing so in another—the latter’s code would need to support it. Such qualifications ensure that no game or environment is “broken” through interoperability.

## Beyond Virtual Goods

Most of the narrative around an interoperable Metaverse focuses on the objects described above—avatars and in-game items. The more important area of standardization and interconnection is at the 3D tools and design layer, which also happens to be developing more rapidly, too.

Take 3D assets. Over the past 30 years, the cost of developing an AAA video game has grown from single-digit millions to hundreds of millions of dollars. There were many causes for this increase, including the increasing use of motion-capture performance, recorded dialogue, and engine development, but a large portion stemmed from greater scale, diversity, and visual fidelity of a game’s virtual world.
