
Indeed, there is disagreement as to whether transformer models are the right pathway to AGI. In August 2022, the 3D graphics and VR pioneer John Carmack told Russian American computer scientist and podcaster Lex Fridman, “I am not a madman for saying that it is likely that the code for artificial general intelligence is going to be tens of thousands of lines of code, not millions of lines of code. This is code that conceivably one individual could write, unlike writing a new web browser or operating system.”^([21](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr21)) Four months later, Carmack officially left his role as consulting CTO of Oculus at Facebook to found Keen Technologies, which, like OpenAI, aspired to build AGI. However, Carmack seems skeptical that transformer models and existing AI players will be able to build it.

In his first interview after starting the “fourth major phase of his career,”^([#](#chapter12.xhtml#footnote-001)) Carmack told Dallas Innovates that the number of “billion-dollar off-ramps” for AI technologies has become a de facto obstacle to achieving true AGI.^([22](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr22)) “There are extremely powerful things possible now in the narrow machine-learning stuff,” he said, “\[but\] it’s not clear those are the necessary steps to get all the way to artificial general intelligence.”^([23](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr23)) “Most of what the mainstream is doing \[this research and development is\] because it’s fabulous, it’s useful … \[but\] there is this kind of groupthink,” Carmack went on, “that is really clear, if you look at it, about all these brilliant researchers —they all have similar backgrounds, and they’re all kind of swimming in the same direction.”^([24](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr24)) In contrast, Carmack was optimistic about “a handful of ideas that are not the mainstream … some work from like the ’70s, ’80s, and ’90s that I actually think might be interesting, because a lot of things happened back then that didn’t pan out, just because they didn’t have enough scale. They were trying to do this on one-megahertz computers, not clusters of GPUs.”^([25](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr25)) Even more radically, Carmack believes “the source code, the computer programming necessary for artificial general intelligence, is going to be a few tens of thousands of lines of code.”^([26](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr26)) In contrast, the source code for the Chrome browser is 31 million lines, while Twitter is roughly 20 million, and operating systems can reach up to 100 million (depending on the definition of what’s in the operating system versus an application packaged with it).^([27](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr27)) “You literally cannot type enough in your remaining life to write all of that code,” Carmack reported to Dallas Innovates, “but it’s my belief that I can really back up that the programming for AGI is going to be something that one person could write.”^([28](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr28))

## Synthetic Realities

Though “Tesler’s theorem” means AI is a moving target, and thus more a philosophical concept than an achievable state, it is the best way to understand the diffusion of these technologies and humanity’s shift to synthetic existence. Each year, the AI products once considered “magic” are normalized to the point that they’re considered unremarkable—even as they take on a greater role cataloging and directing human life. Meanwhile, the systems behind these AI are being created, maintained, and often updated in real time by those very same lives.

The most visible example is online recommendations. Search engines like Google, Yahoo, and WebCrawler once relied purely on “objective” data, including the number of sites linking to a given page or overall traffic, just as earlier cataloging methodologies used defined taxonomies (e.g., Dewey Decimal Classification) or alphabetization. Contemporary engines sometimes use this information, but it’s often secondary to what the engine knows about its user, including their favorite websites and their overall interests and antipathies—but always, crucially, in a comparative sense, assessing how that user compares to others making that same query. As such, the results provided by a search engine operate not by general-purpose rank-order systems but rather by AI-powered responses that target people as individuals. YouTube, Netflix, and TikTok operate similarly.

In 2024, there are myriad copies of every internet user’s personality stored on computers around the world. And though the “brain prints” established by these companies are based on pattern matching and inference, just like a transformer model, and are isolated from one another rather than cohesive, they are often a more accurate and detailed representation of how a user thinks than anything the user, their loved ones, or their psychiatrist might devise. A few years after Facebook launched, Mark Zuckerberg reportedly built a model that examined “friend relationships and communications patterns” that “could determine with about 33 percent accuracy who a user was going to be in a relationship with a week from now.”^([29](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr29)) Facebook never confirmed the existence of this tool; if it existed at the time, it probably does not today. Yet an updated model would doubtless be more accurate—and more capable. Facebook would likely be able to predict not just which prospective mate a newly single individual would date (or at least the basics of what they’d look like) but also when and where the meetup might take place—all of which might be recommended and/or fulfilled by Facebook’s various services, in fact. And that’s before Facebook and others launch suites of devices that can track a user’s face and eyes, electrical signals skittering across their skeletal muscles, and eventually, even brain signals.

In addition to generating mental models of humanity, internet users are digitizing the real world. Facebook’s facial recognition software is based on users manually tagging their friends in billions of photo albums, a feature that launched in 2006. By 2010, Facebook was capable of automatically generating tags for users; in 2017, the social network’s facial recognition software was so accurate that it notified users whenever they were included in a photo uploaded by another user, even if that user didn’t manually tag them.^([30](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr30)) Google first deployed facial recognition in 2008 through Picasa, with Google’s own Photos app adding the functionality in 2015 and Apple Photos doing so a year later.

In some cases, users have actively, but still unwittingly, trained AI. In 2009, Google bought the company reCAPTCHA, which used CAPTCHA challenge-response tests to validate whether an internet user was human in order to thwart bot attacks and spam. Most reCAPTCHA users did not realize that their inputs were also being used to help Google manually digitize old books and ingest its Street View scans, learn how to identify and properly orient boats, cars, and penguins, decipher human speech, and build autonomous (i.e., computer vision–based) vehicles.

Amazingly, the real-world photos that billions of users have uploaded, tagged, categorized, rotated, and transcribed also have enabled the creation of AI that can now create photographs of the real world that are more detailed than physics allow—and use these same photos to build detailed models of the real world, too.
