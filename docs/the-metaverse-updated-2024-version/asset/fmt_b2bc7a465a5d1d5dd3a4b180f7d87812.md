
# Like the internet, the Metaverse is not any one network, platform, device, application, or even group of them

Like the internet, the Metaverse is not any one network, platform, device, application, or even group of them. It is instead a network of myriad interconnected and interoperable networks, platforms, devices, applications, and so on. While it’s common to hear that Company A or B is building “the” Metaverse or building “a” Metaverse, they are typically just building hardware and/or applications (and maybe contributing to development of a Metaversal Internet Protocol Suite). Now, there are some important nuances to this taxonomy and philosophy.

## First, there are rival efforts to build the foundational technologies of the Metaverse

First, there are rival efforts to build the foundational technologies of the Metaverse. Company A and Company B, for example, may envision and advocate for competing protocols and standards for the Metaverse, which would in turn affect how the Metaverse operates or is commercialized. As such, it could be argued that these two companies are contributing to the development of Metaverses (certainly, Web3 developers would say they are building a very different Metaverse than the likes of Facebook and Microsoft). History suggests that separate initiatives can endure for some time, before eventually merging or establishing a single winner. From the 1970s to 1990s there were many proposed and operating working alternatives to the internet, some of which were supported by different US federal departments even though the Department of Defense had created and still spearheaded much of the internet. Few technologists at the time expected that a single global winner would emerge from these so-called “protocol wars,” but the utility of a unified digital economy was too great, and holdouts eventually switched or integrated into the internet. It’s even less likely that over the coming decades, the common foundations of the current internet would split into multiple Metaverses.

## Second, there is likely to be some regional fragmentation across “the” Metaverse

Second, there is likely to be some regional fragmentation across “the” Metaverse. The so-called “Chinese internet” uses the same Internet Protocol Suite (TCP/IP) as the rest of the world, yet the experience of using the internet in China is unmistakably different due to various regulations, firewalls, software giants, and cultural norms. If the Metaverse proves as essential and valuable as many predict, it’s likely the Chinese Metaverse will be even more distinct from that of the rest of the world than the internet is today (as mentioned in the introduction, the Chinese government has already stated that its domestic Metaverse should be “independent” and “controllable”). Still, the Chinese Metaverse will be built on top of the Internet Protocol Suite, likely leverage many of the same technologies that are also used for the Metaverse outside China, and interoperate into parts of the non-Chinese Metaverse. As such, the “Chinese Metaverse” is best described as a regional expression or variation of the Metaverse, rather than a wholly different Metaverse.

## Third, one company may still establish effective control over the Metaverse

Third, one company may still establish effective control over the Metaverse by designing and/or operating its protocols or access devices. Though the foundations of the internet are both open and without an owner, it has become increasingly closed and commercialized over the past 40 years—and the Metaverse is likely to exacerbate this trend, rather than reverse it. This dynamic is discussed throughout this book and is a point of focus in [Chapters 3](#chapter3.xhtml) and [10](#chapter10.xhtml).

## Error #2: Conflating the Metaverse with a virtual or augmented reality headset, operating system, or experience

Technically, the term “virtual reality” refers to computer-generated simulations of three-dimensional objects or environments with seemingly real, direct, or physical user interaction.^([9](#notes_split_002.xhtml#ch2fr9)) The underlying mechanics of processing and rendering computer-generated simulations do not fundamentally differ based on the type of screen they’re viewed through, or how the user’s inputs are collected. In current usage, however, virtual reality refers to a computer-generated simulation that is rendered on a screen contained within a headset, rather than a TV screen or monitor, with the user interacting with the simulation using a mix of facial or body movements, rather than typing on a keyboard or operating a controller. VR headsets provide a greater sense of immersion inside a computer-generated simulation, and greater ease of use, as the user’s senses (i.e., their eyes and ears) are enveloped in the device, and they can interact as they do in the real world (e.g., look right to shift the camera angle right, turn body right to turn the character right).

In contrast to VR devices, augmented reality devices layer computer-generated simulations on top of the real world, rather than replace it altogether. For example, an AR device worn in a supermarket might layer floating, 3D labels above produce that identify various items and estimate their ripeness. As with VR, AR does not require a headset. Many applications provide rich AR experiences today through a standard smartphone camera and screen. Popular examples include Snapchat (which enables users to capture videos wearing AR costumes and makeup), Pokémon Go (which enables users to “see” and interact with Pokémon that appear to exist in the real world), and Google Maps (which can help users find their way by placing directional arrows on top of landmarks and streets). As with VR, it is assumed that wearable headsets are the most intuitive way to use AR functionality, just as glasses are easier to use than monocles stored in pockets.

VR and AR devices are often conflated with the Metaverse because of their role in novels such as Neuromancer, Snow Crash, and Ready Player One, as well as their centrality to Facebook’s Metaverse strategy. There is also a widely held belief that VR/AR headsets will eventually become the “best,” “most popular,” and “preferred” way to access the Metaverse. Regardless, the Metaverse is not a device, nor a specific interface or type of experience.

To better understand the relationship between VR/AR devices and the Metaverse, consider the relationship between touchscreen smartphones and the internet. Neither the internet nor even the mobile internet requires either a touchscreen or the use of apps—users could, if they preferred, use BlackBerry-style clickwheels and physical keyboards. In fact, no part of using the internet even requires a visual interface; billions of people globally can and do occasionally access the internet simply by saying “Siri” or “Hey Google” or “Alexa.” Apps are another good example. Though the most popular way to access digital content and experiences, users could mostly just use a single app to engage with the internet: a web browser.

Though the internet does not require touchscreens, smartphones, or apps, it’s generally recognized that they are the best way to access it and have been key to expanding who uses the internet, for what, and how often. Without a touchscreen, keyboards would take up half of a smartphone’s display and there would be little ability to tailor any interface to a specific application—whether the user was playing a game, accessing a medical record, or scrolling TikTok, they would need to use an alphanumeric keyboard, a half dozen symbol-based keys, and some form of trackwheel or thumbpad, rather than benefit from a tailor-made touch user interface (UI). This limitation would have made general-purpose smartphones ill-suited to many specialized tasks and kept many children and senior citizens from adopting the devices in the first place. Though most internet products and services are available via web browser, most users prefer to access these products and services via apps, which are purpose-built for these products and services and therefore offer substantial performance, stability, and interface-related improvements. VR/AR headsets will likely play a similar role to touchscreens and apps in the Metaverse.
