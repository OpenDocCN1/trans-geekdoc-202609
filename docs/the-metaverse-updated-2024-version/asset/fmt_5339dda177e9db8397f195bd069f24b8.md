
For the first few decades, most developers created bespoke engines for their virtual worlds. But as summarized in [Chapter 3](#chapter3.xhtml), an alternative model emerged in the 1990s in which developers could license an engine, such as Epic Games’ `Unreal Engine` (1996) and, later, Unity Technologies’ `Unity` (2005).

Using these engines has a cost. `Unity` charges every individual developer that uses it an annual fee that ranges between $400 and $4,000 depending on the features required and the size of the developer’s company. `Unreal` typically charges 5% of net revenues.

Fees aren’t the only reason to build your own engine. Some developers believe doing so for a given game genre or experience, such as realistic and fast-paced first-person shooters, ensures that their games “feel better” or perform better. Others worry about the need to rely on another company’s pipelines and priorities, or fret that their vendor has such a detailed view into their game and its performance. Given the concerns, it’s common for large publishers to build and maintain their own engines (some, such as Activision and Square Enix, even operate a half dozen or more).

Most developers see significant net positives to licensing and then customizing `Unreal` or `Unity`. Licensing allows a small or inexperienced team to build a game with an engine more powerful and extensively tested than they might ever build—and that is less likely to break and can never run over budget.^([†](#chapter7.xhtml#footnote-025)) Developers can also focus more of their time on what will differentiate their virtual world—level design, character design, gameplay, and so on—instead of the basic technology required for it to run. And rather than hiring a developer and then training them to use or build on a proprietary engine, a game maker can instead appeal to the millions of individual developers already familiar with `Unity` or `Unreal` and immediately put them to work. For similar reasons, it’s also easier to integrate third-party tools. An independent start-up that makes, as an example, face-tracking software for video game avatars doesn’t design their solution to work with a proprietary engine they’ve never used, but instead to work with those chosen by the largest number of developers.

A good analogy is designing and building a house. Neither the architect nor the decorator designs proprietary lumber dimensions, assembly hardware, measurement systems, blueprint frameworks, or tools. Not only does this make it easier to focus on the creative work, but it makes it easier to hire carpenters, electricians, and plumbers. If the house ever requires a renovation, another team can more easily modify the existing structure because they don’t need to learn new techniques, tools, or systems.

This analogy has a key shortfall. Houses are built once and in one place. Games, conversely, are designed to run on as many possible devices and operating systems as possible—some of which haven’t yet been released or even developed. As a result, games must be compatible with, say, different voltage standards (e.g., the UK’s 240 volts and the United States’ 120 volts), measurement systems (imperial and metric), conventions (aerial telephony wires and buried ones), and so on. `Unity` and `Unreal` build and maintain their game engines so that they’re not just compatible with every major platform but tailored to each platform’s strengths and shortfalls so that a developer can easily tap into each without needing to build to the lowest common denominator of computing power.^([‡](#chapter7.xhtml#footnote-024))

In a sense, one can think of independent game engines as a shared R&D pool for the industry. Yes, Epic and Unity are for-profit companies, but instead of every developer sinking part of their budget into proprietary systems to manage core game logic, a few cross-platform technology providers can concentrate a portion of their budgets into a more capable engine that supports, and benefits, the entire ecosystem. As the major game engines developed, another type of independent gaming solution emerged: live services suites. Companies such as `PlayFab` (now owned by Microsoft’s `Azure`) and `GameSparks` (`Amazon`) operate much of what a virtual world needs to “run” online and multiplayer experiences. This includes user account systems, player data storage, processing of in-game transactions, version management, player-to-player communications, matchmaking, leaderboards, game analytics, anti-cheat systems, and more, all of which work across platforms. Both `Unity` and `Epic` now have their own live services offerings, too, which are available at low to no cost and are not limited to their engines. `Steam`, the world’s largest PC game store and a key point of discussion in [Chapter 10](#chapter10.xhtml), offers its own live services product, `Steamworks`.

As the global economy continues to shift to virtual worlds and environments, these cross-platform and cross-developer technologies have become quietly ubiquitous and essential. And the next wave of virtual world builders—not game makers, but retailers, schools, sports teams, construction companies, and cities—are particularly likely to use these solutions, rather than build their own. This places the companies that own them in a favorable strategic position. Most obviously, they become a sort of standard feature, or lingua franca, for the virtual world—think of them as the “English” or “metric” of the Metaverse. Just as it is likely that the average reader of this book uses some English and some knowledge of the metric system when traveling internationally, odds are that if someone is building a virtual world today, they are using—and paying—one or more of these companies. More broadly, who better to establish common data structures and coding conventions across virtual worlds than the companies that govern their logic? Who better to facilitate exchanges of information, virtual goods, and currencies among these virtual worlds than the companies that power the same inside them? And who better to create an interconnected network of these virtual worlds, as `ICANN` does for web domains and IP addresses? [Chapter 8](#chapter8.xhtml) will return to these questions, but first, it’s helpful to examine a path some think of as the easier, and best, way to build out the Metaverse.

## Integrated Virtual World Platforms

As both independent game engines and live services suites developed over the past two decades, other companies combined these approaches into a new one: integrated virtual world platforms (IVWPs), such as `Roblox`, `Minecraft`, and `Fortnite Creative`.

IVWPs are based around their own general-purpose and cross-platform game engines, similar to `Unity` and `Unreal` (`Epic`’s `Fortnite Creative`, like `Fortnite`, runs on the company’s `Unreal Engine`). In this case, though, they are further “productized” so that no actual “coding” is required. Instead, games, experiences, and virtual worlds are built using graphical interfaces, symbols, and objectives. As an analogy, consider the difference between using the text-based `MS-DOS` and visual `iOS`, or designing a website in `HTML` versus creating one in `Squarespace`. In short, the IVWP interface enables users to create more easily and with fewer people, less investment, and less expertise and skill. As `Roblox` enthusiasts know, many of the game’s creators are kids, and nearly ten million have created and distributed their virtual worlds on `Roblox`’s platform.
