
A number of Metaverse aspirants do still see the blockchain as the “most plausible path” to an open Metaverse. In December 2022, Activision Blizzard’s chief operating officer Daniel Alegre decamped to become president and COO of Yuga Labs, which the company said would “expedite its ambitious vision for blockchain gaming, metaverse development, and community-building.”^([117](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr117)) Four months later, Mike Seavers, Epic’s executive vice president of development and Riot Games’ former chief technology officer, announced he was also joining Yuga Labs, taking the position as CTO.^([118](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr118)) In 2019, Sam Altman, the cofounder and CEO of OpenAI, cofounded Worldcoin, which intended to provide online authentication to all of us humanity using iris scans that were secured and validated by its Ethereum-based Layer 2 blockchain, which also issued users with its namesake token in exchange for scanning their iris. By May 2023, Worldcoin had raised nearly $250 million from venture funds such as Andreessen Horowitz, MultiCoin, and Khosla Ventures, as well as individuals such as FTX cofounder Sam Bankman-Freid and LinkedIn cofounder Reid Hoffman, with the company exiting beta two months later.

And Neal Stephenson? In 2022, he announced that he was co-founding a new blockchain-focused company, Lamina1, with former Bitcoin Foundation chairman Peter Vessenes. ## Chapter 12 THE ROLE AND ACCELERANTS OF AI

Chapter 3 provided a brief timeline of artificial intelligence, from the philosophical theory of thinking computers in the 1930s to the emergence of transformer models capable of answering complex queries, crafting detailed images, and writing everything from short emails to sophisticated code and erudite essays in a matter of minutes.

One of the more overlooked pioneers in AI’s history is the American cognitive and computer scientist Marvin Minsky.^([\*](#chapter12.xhtml#footnote-006)) In 1959, Minsky co-founded the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory^([†](#chapter12.xhtml#footnote-005)) with John McCarthy, who had coined the term “artificial intelligence” four years earlier and would debut his LISP programming language in 1960.^([1](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr1)) Minsky’s 1969 book, *Perceptrons*, which was co-authored by South African mathematician and computer scientist Seymour Papert, is regarded as one of the foundational texts of the field of artificial neural networks. In the run-up to the book, Minsky had served as an advisor on Stanley Kubrick’s landmark 1968 film, *2001: A Space Odyssey*; the character Victor Kaminski was named after Minsky.^([2](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr2)) In Arthur C. Clarke’s novelization of the film, which expanded and updated his 1951 short story “The Sentinel,” Minsky is mentioned directly, with Clarke predicting that he would achieve breakthroughs in neural networks that led directly to the creation of HAL 9000.^([3](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr3))

In the 1950s, Minsky developed^([‡](#chapter12.xhtml#footnote-004)) one of the first widely accepted definitions of AI—“the science of making machines do things that would require intelligence if done by humans”—which reflected what *MIT Technology Review* characterized as his belief that “the human mind was fundamentally no different than a computer.”^([4](#notes_split_012.xhtml#ch12fr4)) Minsky’s definition was never universally embraced by AI researchers of his era, and contemporary computer scientists have largely discarded it. One issue is that tasks that were once viewed as evidence of or benchmarks for AI advances often become so routine that they are reclassified as mere “algorithms” or “computation,” rather than any form of “intelligence” at all—much less “human intelligence.” Examples here include playing chess against a human opponent or passing the Turing Test, but a particularly fun case is autocorrect.

Early versions of the autocorrect feature arguably lacked any form of “AI.” Instead, a word processor or email client simply looked up every would-be “word” a user wrote against a database of known words, identifying those that did not match any stored entries, and then listing off the entries with the greatest fit according to a ruleset that focused primarily on the number of letters shared between the nonword and a real one or the ordering of those letters. Later, spellcheck began to take into account common spelling errors, such as an *ff* that should have been a *gh*, which would otherwise mislead a correction system that was based purely on letter overlap and sequence. Still, this was not “intelligence” but a more sophisticated ruleset.

In 1993, Microsoft shipped Word 6.0, which included not just spellcheck, which had shipped with the very first edition of Word a decade earlier, but also “grammar check.” While this new feature understood various forms of “logic,” including syntax (the sequence of subjects, objects, and verbs in a sentence), conjugation (tense), and agreement (alignment among quantities and genders in a sentence), its system was still just following rules, albeit now against a more elaborate directory that included word classes (nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs), genders (in applicable languages), exceptions, and idioms. Over time, spellcheck continued to improve. By 1997, Microsoft Word could understand identity errors relating not to spelling per se but to homophones like using “accept” instead of “except.” This required the application to understand a word’s specific meaning and context in a sentence. Later, spellcheck added the ability to strive for “clarity” or “concision,” which involved understanding which words or phrases might be confusing or unnecessary. In the mid-2000s, autocorrect systems began to incorporate aggregate user behavior into their systems by measuring which words or phrases were typically selected when an error was identified—the word “lichens” might be closer to the misspelling “lichense,” but users were far more likely to pick “license” instead. This functionality enabled spellcheck programs to register and then use new words and slang, and eventually to recognize typos caused not by poor spelling but by the typical layout of a keyboard. By the 2010s, many spellcheckers could also “learn from” and then tailor their recommendations to individual users, which was particularly helpful to users writing in a nonnative language, were left-handed, suffered from a disability, or had poor typing methods, all of which tended to produce more fringe typos.
