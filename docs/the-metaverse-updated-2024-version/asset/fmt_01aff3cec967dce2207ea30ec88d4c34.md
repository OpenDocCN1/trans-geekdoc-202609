
MSFS is a remarkable creative experience, technical achievement, and case study on why so many came to view the Metaverse as a practical opportunity in 2020 and 2021—not just an inevitable part of some distant future. Nevertheless, the 2020 MSFS is still far from “the Metaverse.” Overall, the title has more in common with a diorama than Neal Stephenson’s bustling Street. Only a handful of MSFS’s 1.5 billion buildings can be entered, and none appear operational (let alone do anything). And though AI-operated vehicles can be seen on its countless roads, no traffic jams ever occur, nor does the AI adhere to any realistic patterns, such as rush-hour traffic or 3:30 pickups at the local school. Indeed, if a user lands their plane on the local freeway, nearby cars will not only drive as though there was no such emergency landing, they pass through the plane altogether. There are no baseball or football matches playing on the millions of fields a virtual pilot might see while flying (least of all ones the pilot could join). Altogether, a user can engage with no more than 49 other players—and no one can modify MSFS’s virtual world (creating a new map is out of the question).

There is nothing wrong or inherently disappointing about MSFS’s limitations. The title’s goal is to let users traverse a surface-level model of the earth, not engage in an earth-like ecosystem. But if users want to use a platform capable of the latter functionality, they will need to be able to download far more data per hour than even the fullest version of MSFS demands today—and they will need it on a timelier basis, too. A computer might be able to guess what a Manhattan skyscraper’s windows look like—and a user might be able to wait for the real thing—but the Metaverse is more than just an empty model of the known world. And this means tackling the least understood element of internet connectivity today: latency.

## Latency

Bandwidth and latency are often conflated, and the mistake is understandable: both impact how much data can be sent or received per unit of time. The classic way to differentiate the two concepts is by likening an internet connection to a highway. “Bandwidth” is the number of lanes on the highway, and “latency” is the speed limit. If a highway has more lanes, it can carry more cars and trucks without congestion. But if the highway’s speed limit is low—perhaps due to too many curves or because it’s laid in gravel rather than pavement—then the flow of traffic is slow even if there’s spare capacity. Similarly, a high speed limit with only one lane results in constant congestion—the speed limit becomes an aspiration, not a reality.

The challenge with real-time-rendered virtual worlds is that users aren’t sending a single car from one destination to another. Instead, they’re sending a never-ending fleet of cars tethered together both to and from that destination (as [Chapter 4](#chapter4.xhtml) detailed, a “continuous connection” is needed). It’s also not possible to send these cars in advance because their contents (i.e., data) are created only milliseconds before they hit the road. Furthermore, these cars must drive at their fastest possible speed because if they arrive late, the rest of the fleet behind them will carry the wrong packages, too, as will many of the other cars on the road. And should any portion of the fleet ever be diverted to a relief highway or road, it might be necessary to restart the entire fleet’s trip—even if this new route is equally fast.

As “speed” is an abstract concept, it’s helpful to specify how the latency requirements of the Metaverse differ from those of the internet today. In 2024, most internet usage involves a single device pulling static copies of information from a single server, such as a consumer’s iPhone downloading an email or loading a webpage. As the user is not engaged in a simulation or any live experience, a 500-millisecond (or half-second) or longer delay has no consequence other than mild frustration—and tolerance is surprisingly pretty high. Most users don’t notice a 150–200 ms delay between a single button press, such as that of a Netflix pause-or-play button, and the relevant application’s response to that input. Moreover, latency is typically the least valued networking attribute; when watching Netflix, a user would likely prefer higher-resolution video (“bandwidth”) and uninterrupted playback (“reliability”) to an 80 ms–faster start (“latency”). Even when users are participating in a shared experience, latency requirements are typically low. It rarely matters whether an iMessage or WhatsApp message arrives within 50 ms rather than 500 ms—and such a margin is inconsequential in contrast to the speed in which the recipient reads it. Latency in a video call is unquestionably annoying but also straightforward to manage; participants just learn to wait a bit after the speaker stops speaking. Even a second (1,000 ms) is workable.

But the human tolerance for latency in interactive experiences is incredibly low. When controlling a virtual character, a user’s brain must feel as though this avatar is a direct extension of the user’s inputs, rather than merely the accurate execution of them. This is the difference between waving a flag and pressing a button that waves a flag. As these inputs are happening in a continuous fashion, unlike a one-off press of a play-or-pause button, delays are even more noticeable to users, as is variability in response times.

Recall that many modern 3D simulations aspire for at least 60 rendered frames per second, equivalent to at least one frame every 16 ms. The inevitable outcome is that even a seemingly modest 100 ms delay can lead to a dozen consecutive frames that do not reflect what a user has already done and what their brain expects to see. This problem is exacerbated by the fact that more real-time-rendered virtual worlds are multiuser. As such, variations in latency among users mean that certain users (or devices, or networks) are effectively ahead of or behind one another, which results in some users being able to respond to information that others haven’t even received. To put what is still an abstract conversation about speeds and milliseconds into perspective, avid gamers typically notice that latency affects their play at 48 ms, while some can correctly note delays after only 5 ms.^([7](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr7)) Crucially, these determinations do not correlate with skill, just total playtime (i.e., experience). And on average, even novice players will realize that latency, rather than their video game skills, are to blame for their poor play by 100–110 ms.^([8](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr8)) At 150 ms, games that involve quick responses are simply unplayable. In contrast, the average person does not even notice when the audio and video of a movie or TV show are out of sync unless the audio is 45 ms early or 125 ms late (170 ms total variance). Acceptability thresholds, as they’re generally called, are even wider, at 90 ms early and 185 ms late (275 ms).

The Metaverse isn’t a fast-moving pixel-precise game like Fortnite or Call of Duty, but its requirements are nevertheless quite high. The more realistic an avatar—such as the hyperreal “personas” created by the Apple Vision Pro’s face scan—the more sensitive humans are to the movement, detail, and timing of a smile or eye movement. To this end, few movie watchers are put off by the mouth movements of a cartoonish Pixar character, but many are creeped out by a photoreal CGI human whose lips don’t move exactly right (animators famously call this the “uncanny valley”).
