

# Apple App Store 新条款分析

Under these new terms, developers would be allowed to distribute their apps inside the App Store, exclusively through unaffiliated third-party app stores, or both.^([68](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr68)) Whenever these apps were installed by a user through Apple’s App Store, Apple would take a 17% commission in place of its prior 30% commission.^([69](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr69)) Apps distributed through Apple’s App Store would also have the choice of offering Apple’s in-app billing services, or those of a third-party payment provider, or both. In the case where a developer chose to offer Apple’s IAP, Apple would also charge another 3% to eligible transactions to cover payment processing, for 20% total.^([70](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr70)) Apple would not take any commission from an app distributed through a third-party app store, but apps distributed through these stores `or` through the App Store under its modified commissions model would also encounter a new charge called the “Core Technology Fee.”^([71](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr71)) This fee would be equal to €0.50 for every user’s first installation or an existing user’s first update in a 12-month period, with fees waived for the first one million qualifying installs/updates.^([72](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr72)) Alternatively, a developer could choose to continue to operate under the pre-DMA business terms of 30% (inclusive of transaction fees) and no Core Technology Fee and inclusive of transaction fees; however, this would require the developer to stick to all of Apple’s other App Store policies too, which meant the developer could not distribute their app on other stores or educate the consumer about alternative payment options inside that app.^([73](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr73))

While Apple estimated “99% of developers would reduce or maintain the fees they owe to Apple” and “<1% of developers would pay a Core Technology Fee on their EU apps,”^([74](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr74)) Apple’s plans were widely criticized as “egregious” and “punitive … will burn its political and developer goodwill” by top technology journalists,^([75](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr75)) a “heads I win, tails you lose” strategy by mobile app experts,^([76](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr76)) “Malicious Compliance^([#](#chapter10.xhtml#footnote-008)) … forcing developers to choose between App Store exclusivity and the store terms, which will be illegal under DMA, or accept a new also-illegal anticompetitive scheme” according to Tim Sweeney,^([77](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr77)) and “masquerading as compliance with the law … a complete and total farce,” according to Spotify, which had been a key advocate for DMA-like regulations in the EU and around the world.^([78](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr78))

David Heinemeier Hansson, a Danish programmer who created the Ruby on Rails web-development framework and Hey, a digital calendar and email application, wrote that “once you start peeling the onion \[of Apple’s new policies\], you realize it’s stuffed with poison pills so toxic you can scarcely believe Apple’s chutzpah.… Might be the most blatant extortion attempt ever committed to public policy by any technology company ever.… Explicitly designed to ensure that no second-party app store ever takes off.”^([79](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr79)) Nikita Bier, a serial entrepreneur who sold his start-ups to Facebook and Discord, highlighted that an app that chose Apple’s new IAP program and was installed 10 million times in a year, while also generating $10 million in in-app revenue that year, would owe Apple $6.2 million (62%), with only $3.8 million left to cover sales tax (typically 21%, or $2 million of this $3.8 million) and all operating expenses.^([80](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr80)) “If you make less than $0.57 per user—which is most apps—you will end up negative and you will owe Apple money,” Bier added.^([81](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr81)) Hansson also highlighted that in the EU, Instagram, a free app with roughly 250 million monthly users, would need to pay $135 million to Apple annually if it chose to adopt Apple’s new terms, rather than $0—even if no part of the app changed. Facebook would also need to pay this fee for each of its EU apps, such as WhatsApp, Facebook, Meta Quest, and so on.^([82](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr82)) Companies that planned to launch their stores by placing their popular apps inside said store would end up having to pay the fee twice. Epic, as an example, would have to pay one fee when a user installed the Epic Games Store on iOS, and then again when the user downloaded `Fortnite`.

Apple’s nonfinancial terms were also considered obstructive and unnecessary. “While Apple is also under an obligation to allow ‘sideloading’ (i.e., the ability to download apps directly from a website, as software can be downloaded from websites on PCs),” Damien Geradin of the European competition and digital-regulation law firm Geradin Partners wrote in a blog post, “\[Apple\] interprets this obligation in an extremely narrow way: ‘sideloading refers to downloading iOS apps outside of an official app marketplace—and in the EU, users will have the option to download alternative marketplaces that offer apps for download.’ ”^([83](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr83)) And if a developer chose to make their app available outside the App Store, users would need to delete the version of the app they had already downloaded before they could install that new version—a confusing, unclear, and unfamiliar requirement that would likely lead to customer loss and annoyance and impede competition.^([84](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr84))

Third-party app stores also had to be “mainly app stores,” blocking apps such as Instagram and Snapchat from selling games or digital products inside their apps as Tencent’s WeChat does in China.^([85](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr85)) And while a third-party app store could focus on a single category of apps, such as games or photos, it must “be open to submissions from any developer in that same category” and could not define that category such that only their own apps qualified.^([86](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr86)) Even when apps skipped Apple’s App Store entirely, developers would still need a developer account issued and managed by Apple, and “all apps and every update must still be submitted to Apple for notarization. After review, Apple will then forward apps to each marketplace where the app is distributed \[not the developer\].”^([87](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr87)) Apple’s review would involve both automated checks and human reviews, and though non–App Store apps would no longer be subject to Apple’s content restrictions (enabling pornographic and pirated-content apps, among others, on iOS), Apple would still enforce technical controls regarding background processes, permissions, APIs, and other design choices.^([88](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr88))
