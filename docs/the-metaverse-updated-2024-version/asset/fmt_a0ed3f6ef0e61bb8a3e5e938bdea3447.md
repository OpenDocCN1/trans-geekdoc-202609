
As a category, VR and AR devices are particularly intolerant of latency. In contrast to “2D” displays, such as those of a PC, television, or smartphone, which use keystrokes, touchscreen taps, and mouse movements for inputs, HMDs often rely on a user’s head, eye, and body movements. These latter methods often activate a user’s inner ear, which in turn makes them more susceptible to disconnects between what they are doing (turning left or right, squatting, waving, and so on) and what their eyes can see. The result can be nauseating in a literal sense—less so for experiences where both the user and their virtual avatar remain in a fixed position, as in the case of a meeting, watching streaming video, and some stand-in-place fitness experiences, but brutally so when the user is making steps forward, backward, and side to side and/or their avatar is leaping, climbing, running, and so on. For similar reasons, VR and AR headsets aim not for 30 to 60 or even 90 frames per second but 120–240 fps, which aids visual clarity but also increases the number of “old frames” seen by a user if latency increases.^([9](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr9)) AR device experiences, even those on a smartphone, face another challenge—their renders sit atop the “real world,” which “renders” at the speed of light (an object 100 meters away therefore arrives in 0.0003 ms, or 0.0000003 seconds). A device that is constantly loading information after a viewer has looked elsewhere is useless, and one that is partly lagging is likely to be more frustrating than helpful.

Separate from the issue of what’s tolerable is how latency affects usage. Subspace—a real-time bandwidth start-up whose executive team consists of the former senior vice president of infrastructure and security at `League of Legends` maker Riot Games, the former head of software at SpaceX, and the former head of video infrastructure at Google—says that video game players typically increase or decrease their weekly play time by 6% for every 10 ms decrease or increase in latency. This correlation holds beyond the point at which even “expert” gamers consciously notice latency levels, too.

Fortunately, not all Metaverse applications need ultra-low latency. Digital twins, such as those that oversee an airport terminal or factory floor, have higher acceptability thresholds because they simulate slower-moving and more predictable agents and objects. A relatively tiny portion of their simulations involves synchronization with distant users, devices, or networks. And while industrial AR devices still face high frame-rate requirements, their usage is typically concentrated to a few areas and they can therefore preload more of the data that might be needed, rather than query a distant server for real-time support. That said, the role of latency should not be underestimated. The more powerful a digital twin, the more powerful the computational power that’s required (as discussed in the following chapter). Most companies outsource their compute needs to cloud computing companies such as Amazon’s AWS, whose data centers might be thousands of kilometers away, and charge heavily for real-time bandwidth delivery due to its scarcity. And while industrial simulations often move slower than their fictional counterparts, latency-related inaccuracies compound over a higher volume of interactions, decisions, and AI recommendations.

With a greater understanding of latency requirements, it’s possible to reassess the current state of delivery time. The median time for data to be sent between the 25 largest cities in the United States is roughly 18 ms,^([10](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr10)) which meets nearly all requirements for ultra-low latency experiences but doesn’t tell the full story of delivery times. For example, the specific city pairing matters greatly. Dallas to Houston averages only 2.5 ms one way, while New York City to Los Angeles takes 30 ms, and Orlando to Seattle typically exceeds 35. In addition, these sums reflect only city-to-city or data-center-to-data-center transit time, and therefore excludes the time required for a device outside these locations to send in their data or to receive it. This “last mile” is typically the slowest and least reliable part of delivery. Unfortunately, many buildings or neighborhoods still rely on low-capacity (i.e., low-bandwidth) coaxial copper cable and can easily congest. Households and devices that rely on cellular networks for data face 20 or even 40 ms in added latency compared to wired fiber optics. And while Wi-Fi typically covers only the last few feet of delivery, Wi-Fi routers add 1–2 ms—a small margin, but one that not all users can afford.

Moreover, all connections suffer from “jitter,” variances in delivery time relative to the median. While most jitter is tightly distributed around the connection’s median latency, it can spike latency severalfold due to a congestion at a local internet node, or an event as trivial as a neighbor or family member turning on Netflix or receiving a FaceTime call. Though temporary, such a jump can sever a network connection or otherwise ruin a shared experience.

International delivery is even more difficult. Median delivery times from data centers in the US Northeast to those in Northeast Asia are as long as 170 to 200 ms, and it takes up to 350 or 700 ms for a packet to go from a user in one locale to a user in the other, with jitter also more common, long-lasting, and severe.

Given the many latency-related obstacles faced by internet users some 40 years after the formalization of TCP/IP, it might come as a surprise that there are hundreds of millions of people who frequent online virtual worlds. Yet this is possible only due to numerous tricks and solutions that, unfortunately, don’t work well in the Metaverse. Case in point: Most high-fidelity multiplayer gaming is “match made” around server regions. By limiting the player roster to those who live in the northeastern United States, or Western Europe, or Southeast Asia, game publishers can minimize latency within each region. And because gaming is a leisure activity and typically played with one to three friends, this clustering works well enough. Multiplayer games also use “netcode” solutions that correct for synchronization issues and conflicts. Delay-based netcode will tell a player’s device to artificially delay its rendering of its owner’s inputs until the most latent player’s inputs arrive. This will irk players with muscle memory attuned to low latency, but it works. Rollback netcode is more sophisticated. If an opponent’s inputs are delayed, a player’s device will proceed based on what it expects to happen. If it turns out the opponent did something different, the device will try to unwind in-process animations and then replay them “correctly.”
