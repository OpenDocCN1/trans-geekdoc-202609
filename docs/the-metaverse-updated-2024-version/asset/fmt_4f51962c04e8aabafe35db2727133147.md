
# From 2020 through 2023

From 2020 through 2023, more than $80 billion was verifiably laundered using cryptocurrencies, according to Chainalysis.^([99](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr99)[\*](#chapter11.xhtml#footnote-007)) At least $35 billion was verifiably stolen via hacks (primarily by attacking DeFi protocols) and through scams in which the quasi-anonymous founders or ringleaders of a DAO, dapp, or DeFi project raise money and then abscond with the proceeds.^([100](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr100)) North Korea perpetrated at least $3.4 billion of this theft^([101](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr101))—a sum made even more remarkable when contrasted to the country’s $25–$30 billion GDP,^([102](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr102)) inclusive of less than $150 million in annual exports.^([103](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr103)) At least $15 billion in customer funds has been lost as a result of legitimate organizations that, perhaps due to operating outside of typical financial regulations, mismanaged the funds.^([104](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr104))

Scant regulations are also said to have inflated many of crypto’s shocking sales figures. Some studies have found that as much as 70% of cryptocurrency trades may be “wash trades”^([105](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr105))—those in which the buyer and seller are the same person, or otherwise affiliated—as are 45% of NFT sales.^([106](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr106)) Washing has been illegal for nearly a century in the United States due to its distortive impact on trading volumes and prices and its role in tax evasion.^([107](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr107))

Due to the risk of theft (or immutability of mistakes), need for regulatory compliance, general difficulty of blockchain systems, high and unreliable gas fees, and transaction times, most cryptocurrency trading continues to occur through “centralized exchanges” (CEXs) such as Binance, Coinbase, and Robinhood, in addition to traditional banks such as Bank of America and JPMorgan Chase, rather than “decentralized exchanges” (DEXs) like Uniswap and Sushiswap, which run on the blockchain. Between January 1, 2021, and September 1, 2023, close to $44 trillion was traded through CEXs, with DEX volumes less than $3 trillion (or 6%).^([108](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr108)) CEXs effectively defeat the purpose of blockchains in the first place, as they manage user funds no differently than a traditional bank does US dollars or Euros—through centralized database entries that reallocate monies between users and accounts, rather than by shifting these resources to and from them. CEX trades also do not involve any updates to the blockchain—which is precisely why they are so fast, reliable, and low in fees. To protect its customers from fraud, stolen wares, and counterfeit NFTs, OpenSea has also launched various verification, banning, and “copymint” services—none of which are unreasonable solutions, but they too illuminate the limitations and problems of “trustless,” “permissionless,” and “immutable” code and the centralization that springs up to address them.

## To the Moon, Back Down to Earth, and Then Maybe Back Again?

Having long been conflated with the Metaverse, crypto’s role in building it is now unclear. Though its systems continue to improve, blockchain funding has become scarce, many once-promising start-ups now languish, and users are hard to come by. Many blockchain advocates now exhibit disinterest, disappointment, or even disdain.

Not long after Jensen Huang declared in February 2022 that “blockchains \[are\] going to be here for a long time and \[will\] be a fundamental new form of computing,”^([109](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr109)) he stopped mentioning the topic altogether. Throughout 2022, Facebook added the ability for select users to display, mint, trade, buy, and sell Ethereum, Solana, Flow, and Polygon-based NFTs on Instagram and Facebook.^([110](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr110)) In March 2023, Facebook removed this functionality before it was even launched to most users, with the company stating that instead, it would focus on “areas where we can make impact at scale.”^([111](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr111)) Having described blockchains as “the combination of everyone’s computer” in 2017, Sweeney pronounced in early 2021 that a “blockchain based underpinning for an open Metaverse … is the most plausible path toward an ultimate long term open framework where everyone’s in control of their own presence, free of gatekeeping,” though he called cryptocurrency investment “a wild, speculative mess.”^([112](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr112)) Eight months later, Sweeney remained optimistic about the potential in blockchain, but was also discouraged by its misuse, declaring that Epic Games wasn’t “touching NFTs as the whole field is currently tangled up with an intractable mix of scams, interesting decentralized tech foundations, and scams.”^([113](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr113)) After Steam banned games that used NFTs, Sweeney announced that “Epic Games Store will \[continue to\] welcome games that make use of blockchain tech provided they follow the relevant laws, disclose their terms, and are age-rated by an appropriate group. Though Epic’s not using crypto in our games, we welcome innovation in the areas of technology and finance.”^([114](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr114)) By the end of 2022, Sweeney’s thoughts were best summarized by the one-word tweet “Craptocurrency,”^([115](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr115)) which he quote-tweeted in early January 2023 with “Kleptocurrency.”^([116](#notes_split_011.xhtml#ch11fr116))

To many in the crypto community, the retreat of big tech and “traditional finance” from Web3 is a mostly positive development. It reduces the influence of these companies, while revealing their talk of openness to be just that: talk. Going forward, development will be slower, less well funded, and less high profile, but it will continue and perhaps benefit from less day-to-day scrutiny.

Those who still believe in blockchain-based decentralized computing are right to point out that its technologies are immature. The first NFT debuted in 2014, with Ethereum launching in 2015, the first dapp following in 2016, and the modern NFT standard (ERC-721) defined in 2017. Most usage of these technologies has occurred since the start of 2021, as has two-thirds of funding, but there has been little change to underlying structures or protocols during this time. For that matter, it’s important to reiterate that the “best” technical solution rarely wins—those that are adopted do. It’s hard enough to create any interoperable entitlements system, let alone one that sees widespread usage and scales into trillions of transactions. Perhaps it’s better to prioritize deployment, rather than blueprint altogether new solutions that, if adopted, would be superior.
