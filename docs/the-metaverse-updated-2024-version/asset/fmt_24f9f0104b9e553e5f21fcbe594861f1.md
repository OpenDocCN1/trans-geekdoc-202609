
Although BGP successfully routes traffic across more than 110,000 networks globally, it does not know what data is being sent (e.g., an email versus video call), whether it’s an initiation or response (e.g., a user sending a request for the *New York Times* homepage, or the *New York Times* website sending that data back), its urgency (the relevance of 10, 50, or 200 ms), the impact of congestion (whether a key connection might be severed), and so on. Instead, the internet’s “air traffic controller” applies a standardized methodology for routing data, which essentially weighs the shortest path, the fastest path, and the cheapest path, with a general preference for cost.

BGP’s ruleset reflects the internet’s original asynchronous network design. Its goal is to ensure that all data is transmitted successfully and inexpensively. But as a result, many routes are far longer than necessary—and inconsistently so. Although a *Fortnite* player might be located only 500 kilometers from a local server and have a shortest possible network path of 650 kilometers, their data might take an 800-kilometer path while their neighbor ends up traveling 50% farther. Moreover, one of these connections might end up severed, or suffer from recurring bouts of 150 ms latency, all to prioritize traffic that doesn’t need to be delivered in real time, such as an email.

### Not FTL, but Hopefully Fast Enough

The many factors listed above explain why a data packet that could travel from New York City to San Francisco, Tokyo, or Mumbai in 14, 36, or 42 ms is instead limited to a minimum of 20, 52, or 61 ms and actually averages 30, 110, or 130 ms. Improving these times will be expensive, difficult, and slow. But there is reason for optimism.

Internet access continues to improve on an annual basis, with average bandwidth increasing, latency falling, and prices (in real terms) declining. The emergence of what many accountancies, consultancies, and investment banks expect will be a multitrillion-dollar Metaverse economy, alongside additional growth in the digital economy, will lead to even greater investments.

In the average year, close to $3 billion is spent laying new submarine cables, many of which reach populations that had previously lacked high-speed internet.^([17](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr17)) The average fiber pairing per cable has grown from 4 to 14 over that time, too, substantially increasing capacity.^([18](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr18))

Compared to wireline infrastructure, it’s comparatively easy to upgrade wireless towers and stations. 5G networks are primarily billed as offering wireless users “ultra-low latency,” with the potential of 1 ms and a more realistic 10 ms expected compared to the 10–20 ms average of today’s 4G networks. This is not a complete solution, as it reflects only the latency from a user’s device to a tower, after which the fixed-line internet backbone takes over. However, it is a substantial leap for mobile devices as well as rural homes without access to in-the-ground broadband.

Starlink, SpaceX’s satellite internet company, promises to provide high-bandwidth, low-latency internet service across the United States and eventually the rest of the world. In contrast to prior satellite internet companies, such as Viasat or EchoStar/Hughes Network Systems, Starlink satellites are an average of 550 kilometers above the earth, rather than 35,700 kilometers,^([19](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr19)) which reduces the theoretical transit time required for a customer to send their data to a satellite and for that satellite to send the data back to earth from roughly 250 ms to 4 ms. As Starlink’s lower-earth-orbit satellites are less than a tenth of the weight of their geosynchronous competitors, the company’s launch costs are substantially lower, which enables more of these satellites to be sent into orbit, which then reduces the distances between a customer and the nearest available satellite.

Though an unprecedented technical achievement, satellite internet doesn’t achieve ultra-low latency, especially at great distances. Though Starlink aspires to 10–20 ms one-way latency (20–40 ms round trip), at the start of 2023, it averages more than 30 ms (60 ms+) when sending data to and from a US server or device—more than double the industry average.^([20](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr20)) In Puerto Rico, latency averaged about 60 ms (120 ms) when sending to and from local computers, four and a half times the local average, while the Philippines was 80 ms (160 ms), seven times the average.^([21](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr21)) Starlink’s actual latency far exceeds its theoretical minimum for several reasons. For example, a given packet of data might need to daisy-chain across several Starlink satellites before being sent down to earth, after which it must still travel through terrestrial networks to reach its destination—and then repeat the process coming back. In some cases, Starlink even exacerbates the speed-of-light problem compared to purely terrestrial alternatives. New York to Philadelphia is around 150 kilometers in a straight line and potentially 200 kilometers by cable, but over 1,200 kilometers when traveling to a low-orbit satellite and back down.^([\*](#chapter5.xhtml#footnote-032)) Compared to fiber-optic cables, Starlink also faces severe capacity constraints. Residential fiber-optic cables can support up to 100 gigabytes per second in bandwidth, while submarine cables can reach into the single-digit terabytes per second. Starlink satellites max out at 20 Gbps, which allows them to support a maximum of 2,000 simultaneous users and experience speed degradation as usage grows. Between Q1 2021 and Q3 2023, average download speeds for US Starlink customers fell from roughly 90 Mbps to 65 Mbps, due to increases in users and usage, with one-way latency growing from 20 ms to 30.^([22](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr22)) Due to lower average population density, Canadian customers experienced no material decline over that period.

As SpaceX founder Elon Musk acknowledges, Starlink is focused on “the hardest-to-serve customers,” people whom telecommunications companies “otherwise have trouble reaching.”^([23](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr23)) With this in mind, satellite delivery should be seen as enabling more people to meet the minimum latency specifications for the Metaverse, rather than offering improvements for those who already meet it. Still, the impact of the technology continues to grow, as do its minimum service levels. In 2018 Musk estimated that Starlink would require $10 billion in investment until achieving sustainable profitability.^([24](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr24)) By mid-2021, Musk’s estimate had grown to more than $30 billion^([25](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr25)) as the company ratcheted up its global ambitions. At the end of 2023, Starlink’s network spanned an estimated 5,600 satellites—over 60% of all active satellites^([26](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr26))—and planned to launch another 6,500 by 2025, with the hopes of eventually operating more than 42,000.^([27](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr27)) In 2019, Amazon announced Kuiper Systems, which has similar aims to Starlink and leverages Amazon founder Jeff Bezos’s aerospace company, Blue Origin. Amazon expects to launch over 3,000 satellites by 2030,^([28](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr28)) with an investment budget of more than $10 billion.^([29](#notes_split_005.xhtml#ch5fr29)) SpaceX’s and Amazon’s satellites may relay traffic to each other, just as independent fiber networks do today. But even if these rival constellations remain fully separate, growth in their fleets will increase the number of customers that can be supported and the average bandwidth they’re afforded, while also reducing latency by shortening the average distance between satellites, and from satellites to industrial receiver stations on earth.
