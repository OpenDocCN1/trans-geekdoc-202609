
New development suites (such as the aforementioned iOS ARKit), software languages, and standards (discussed later in this chapter) will also be needed. Google’s Starline device is a great illustration of the utility of 3D, but it requires novel software and AI not just to create a realistic live avatar but also to share it. Starline creates many gigabits of data per second, several times greater than the average US household can download per second. Accordingly, Google developed a new compression algorithm that rapidly shrinks this data by more than 100 times and is still compatible with the common WebRTC standard. And to use with 3D interfaces, developers will need to create new interaction models and design principles, just as Apple pioneered user experience (UX) elements such as “pinch-to-zoom” and “swipe-to-unlock.”

Not all use cases or applications will benefit from spatial, immersive, or 3D implementations, nor will those that do benefit do so in all cases. Yet many examples are already clear, including Google’s Starline, VR training for dangerous work environments, and AR-assisted surgery or repairs. It’s also evident that among those native to devices such as the iPad or video game consoles, 3D/AR socializing is often preferred to 2D alternatives whether based on text, image, or video. But there is particular hope that a 3D internet will enhance categories that have historically resisted digital disruption.

For decades, theorists have expected education to be transformed by the internet—that Harvard University would offer online-only degrees that rivaled its in-person equivalents in both imprimatur and test scores, yet was available at a fraction of the cost and to far more students. Harvard has abstained from such a model in part due to brand- and legacy-related concerns, but no effective alternative has emerged. Indeed, the COVID-19 pandemic revealed the value of the classroom and peers-based learning just as it demonstrated the inadequacies of learning through large video calls, on-demand videos, multiple choice, and even interactive e-books. Between 2020 and 2023, the National Center for Education Statistics reported that average reading and math scores for American children fell 5 points in their standardized testing rubric (220 to 215), the largest drop since 1990, while math scores fell by 7, the first-ever-reported drop.^([1](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr1)) Among 13-year-olds, scores fell by 4 (260 to 256), more than double the largest-ever-recorded drop, and 9 (280 to 271), the second-ever-recorded drop and three times larger than the first.^([2](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr2))

The suddenness of the shift to remote education is partly to blame, but for all the power of computers and the internet, the educational sector has seen no measurable productivity improvements since the internet and PCs. Students are not taught in less time, nor are more students taught effectively per instructor. Student outcomes have not seen substantial improvements, either. In comparison to 1999, the average reading score for 9-year-olds had grown only from 212 to 220 and math grew from 232 to 241; scores for 13-year-olds saw even smaller gains, with reading increasing from 259 to 260 and math from 276 to 280.^([\*](#chapter4.xhtml#footnote-035)) Since 2012, ACT scores have routinely hit two-or-three-decade lows.^([3](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr3)) In fact, no other sector has seen greater increases in costs, with college tuition and fees up more than 825%,^([4](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr4)) while the second-closest sector, medical care and services, is up 450%,^([5](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr5)) versus 210% for the Consumer Price Index overall.^([6](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr6)) Worse still, there have been few improvements in education equality or access.

At-home or at-distance education will never fully substitute for in-person experience, or for the effects of geography or income on learning. But the gap is likely to close for the same reasons VR training is so impactful in industries such as automotive repairs and oil and gas rigging: humans learn best by doing and through physical instruction. Immersive simulations enable virtual classrooms to be accessed worldwide, while also affording participants the ability to be “present” in these classrooms and to make eye contact with a teacher or peer. Furthermore, the virtual classroom can be stocked with infinite and no-cost Bunsen burners to light and frogs to dissect. The classroom need not exist at all, either. Instead, students could learn on the surface of the moon or the right ventricle of the heart. Everything once imagined in *The Magic School Bus* becomes virtually real—and it will be staffed by live versions of Miss Frizzle, motion-captured recordings of other teachers and instructors, and a suite of personalized AI assistants.

To be clear, education-related use cases are not a necessary part of any Metaverse description (nor are any comparable examples in fields such as healthcare, engineering, and so on). But for the Metaverse to be “the Metaverse,” it needs more than just technological enablers to be in place. Developers must also produce new experiences out of whole cloth for it which are subsequently adopted by society.

## Real-Time Rendered

For a virtual world to be visible, it must be “rendered.” Rendering is the process of generating a visible 2D or 3D object using a computer program. The goal of this program is to “solve” an equation made up of many different inputs, data, and rules to determine what should be rendered and when, and by using various computing resources, such as a graphics processing unit (GPU) and central processing unit (CPU). As is the case with any math problem, an increase in the resources available to solve it (in this case, time, the number of CPUs and GPUs, and the power of these CPUs and GPUs) means that more complex equations can be tackled, and more detail provided in the solution.

Take the 2013 Pixar film *Monsters University*, which not only boasted beautiful graphics and vivid worlds but was designed to look great even on an IMAX screen more than 125 feet tall and 70 feet wide. Even when using a top-of-the-line industrial-grade computing processor, Pixar concluded that the film’s 120,000-plus frames would require an average of 29 hours each to be rendered. In total, the film would therefore require two years to be rendered, assuming no single rendering was redone, let alone any scene changed. In response to this conclusion, Pixar built a data center of 2,000 conjoined industrial-grade computers with a combined 24,000 cores that, when fully assigned, could render a frame in roughly five seconds.^([7](#notes_split_004.xhtml#ch4fr7)) Most companies, of course, can’t afford such a powerful supercomputer and therefore spend more time waiting. Many architectural and design firms need to wait overnight to render a highly detailed model. An alternative approach is to render a less complex or detailed image, object, or scene.

Prioritizing visual fidelity is sensible if one is creating a Hollywood blockbuster that will be shown on the silver screen, or selling a multimillion-dollar building renovation. However, experiences set in virtual worlds require real-time rendering. Without real-time rendering, the size and visuals of virtual worlds would be severely constrained, as would the number of participating users and the options available to each user. Why? Because experiencing an immersive environment through pre-rendered images requires every possible sequence to have been pre-made—just as a choose-your-own-adventure novel can offer only a handful of choices, rather than infinite ones. In other words, the cost of greater visuals is less functionality and agency.
