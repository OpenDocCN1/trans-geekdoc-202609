## Chapter 10 PAYMENT RAILS, PLATFORM PREFERENCES, AND PUBLIC POLICIES

Most Metaverse fiction provides little detail of the economies of its synthetic realities, or the physical world in which these fantastical worlds exist. This is a reasonable choice. Though writers such as Neal Stephenson and William Gibson hope to provoke their readers, their primary ambition is to entertain, not educate. And with rare exception, their novels are also set in a version of the 20th- or 21st-century earth that has suffered some sort of economic collapse—making the odd fictional lesson of dubious applicability to a would-be “real Metaverse.” But if the “real Metaverse” is ever to generate the trillions in annual revenue that so many expect, its economic underpinnings are of crucial importance. They will define the nature of competition and the cost of doing business, and sometimes pick winners, shape business models, and decide what’s “fair” and which disruptions are even possible.

Many of these underpinnings are legal or regulatory. Others are by convention or culture. Some of the most powerful are known as “payment rails”—a technology that, like the internet, is composed of a complex series of systems and standards that are deployed across a wide and decentralized network, are largely automated, and support trillions of dollars in economic activity each week. These ecosystems are difficult to build and even harder to displace, but unlike the internet, they are typically privately owned. Visa, MasterCard, and Alipay are each among the 50 most valuable public companies in the world, with most of their peers being the likes of Google, Apple, Facebook, Amazon, and Microsoft, as well as financial conglomerates such as JPMorgan Chase and Bank of America, which hold trillions in deposits and manage the transfer of trillions more in financial instruments each day.

Unsurprisingly, there is already a fight to become the dominant “payment rail” in the Metaverse. What’s more, this fight is arguably the central battleground for the Metaverse and potentially its greatest impediment, too. To understand this dynamic, one needs to first understand how various payment rails operate today, their role in the modern gaming industry, their influence over the payment rails of the mobile computing era, and the ways mobile payment rails can and often are used to control emergent technologies and stifle competition ([Chapter 11](#chapter11.xhtml) will then discuss these points in the context of blockchains and cryptocurrencies).

### The Major Payment Rails Today

Over the past century, the number of distinct payment rails has grown because of new communication technologies, the increase in the number of transactions made per person per day, and the fact that most purchases are not in physical cash. From 2009 to 2022, cash’s share of US transactions fell from over 45%^([1](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr1)) to 18%.^([2](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr2))

The most common payment rails in the United States are `Fedwire` (formerly known as the Federal Reserve Wire Network), `CHIPS` (Clearing House Interbank Payment System), `ACH` (Automated Clearing House), credit cards, `PayPal`, and peer-to-peer payment services like `Venmo`. These rails have different requirements, merits, and demerits, having to do with the fees that are charged, network size, speed, reliability, and flexibility.

Start with the classic payment rail: wires. Two years after the Federal Reserve System of the United States was established in 1913, it began development of a proprietary payment network that spanned each of the 12 Reserve Banks, the Federal Reserve Board, and the US Treasury. Early versions of this system were telegraphic and used Morse code, but by the 1970s, `Fedwire` began to move to telex, then computer operations, followed by proprietary digital networks. Wires can be used only between (and thus through) banks, so both the sender and receiver must have a bank account. For similar reasons, a wire can be sent only on non-holiday weekdays and during business hours. While a sender can set up recurring wires (say, sending `$5,000` every Tuesday), there is no such thing as a “wire request.” As such, wires cannot be used to automatically pay recurring bills or other invoices. Once money is sent via wires, that sending cannot be reversed. Even if this were possible, other limitations discourage frequent use of wires, such as the often substantial fees charged to both sender (`$25–$45`) and recipient (`$15`), in addition to other fees for non-USD wires, failed wires, and confirmations (which are not always possible). The banks themselves are charged as little as `$0.35` to `$0.90` per transaction by `Fedwire`. The size of these fees, which are mostly fixed, makes small dollar-value wires impractical. But for larger sums (individuals can wire up to `$100,000`), wires are the least expensive option.

In the 1970s, the major US banks also established a competitor to (and customer of) `Fedwire`, named `CHIPS`, in part to reduce their transfer costs. This meant departing from `Fedwire`’s “real-time settlement,” in which a sender’s wire would be instantly received by and usable to the recipient. In contrast, each bank holds outgoing `CHIPS` wires until the end of the day, at which point they are grouped together based on the recipient bank, and then netted against all of the incoming `CHIPS` wires from that same bank. In simple terms, `CHIPS` means that rather than Bank A sending Bank B millions of wires per day, and Bank B sending Bank A millions of wires per day, they wait until the end of the day and make a single transaction. With this system, neither the sender of a wire nor its recipient has access to the wire’s funds (and for up to 23 hours, 59 minutes, and 59 seconds). Only the bank does, and the bank collects interest on much of it over the course of the day. Naturally, banks typically default to `CHIPS` for their wires. Due to time zones, money-laundering protections, and other governmental restrictions, international wires typically take two to three days.

Wires (inclusive of `Fedwire` and `CHIPS`) are typically the most complex and time-consuming way to send money, as extensive information is needed from the recipient. The irreversibility of the transaction, combined with the lack of (or delayed) confirmation, also means that mistakes are even more time-consuming to correct. Yet wires are still typically considered the most secure way to send money, as `CHIPS` is limited to only 47 member banks and involves no intermediary, while `Fedwire`’s only intermediary is the US Federal Reserve. In 2021, `$992` trillion in value was sent via US `Fedwire` across 205 million transactions (an average of roughly `$5` million each),^([3](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr3)) while `CHIPS` cleared over `$450` trillion across an estimated 130 million transactions (an average of `$3.5` million).^([4](#notes_split_010.xhtml#ch10fr4))

An `ACH` is an electronic network for processing payments. The first `ACH` emerged in the United Kingdom in the late 1960s. Like wires, `ACH` payments can be made only during business hours, and they require the sender and the recipient to each have a bank account.
