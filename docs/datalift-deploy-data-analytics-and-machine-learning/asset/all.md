Railway maintenance and autonomous vehicles more similar and more different than you’d think Irina Vidal Migallón, Technical Lead AI & Computer Vision, Siemens Mobility Moderated by Sven Krüger M onitoring the state of rail tracks and autonomous driving have many tasks in common: detect obstacles, detect people, detect assets, detect damages. As pre-production work, it may seem a no-brainer to approach them in similar ways. The devil is in the details, though, which happens to be 90% of the product. Sensors, deployment platform, processing hardware, feedback loop, laws: they have a more significant impact than the machine learning technique itself.   
[Siemens Mobility](https://www.mobility.siemens.com/global/en.html) has a division focusing on computer vision and machine learning for different products around mobility, especially also in the railway domain. Products range from passenger assistance to autonomous driving. For example, we increase comfort for travelers by finding spots for a wheelchair or detecting aggression to improve safety. For driving, our approach includes handling typical issues like obstacle detection and track segmentation. The difference deployment makes We often see issues around computer vision and machine learning that look similar, actually, deceivingly similar. I say ‘deceivingly’ because even though the challenges may point to the same machine learning solution, the solution is part of differing products, and we deploy each differently. Deployment is more than a click. You iterate to find the best solution for your customers and users and, for example, with your DevOps to check for feasibility.   At Siemens Mobility, we are working on different use cases for a variety of users. Consider the usual machine learning cycle from data processing on to training and then, finally, to deployment. That final step, the deployment, makes the difference. It makes every use case different.   
  
![Siemens Mobility use cases](Image00002.jpg)  “Deployment must inform all the steps taken before. For instance, if you're doing detection and were to use the most massive, most powerful detector out there, but you find you don't have the hardware to run it, then you need to go back to the whiteboard and choose something else.” Irina Vidal Migallón, Technical Lead AI & Computer Vision   I'm just going to show you a couple of use cases that, at their core, have the same machine learning concept. But you'll see how they look nothing like each other precisely because of how they're deployed. Use case 1: Inspection of rail track joints The first use case is about the inspection of rail track joints. We gather data by specially equipped rail vehicles that run around collecting images. At its core, the machine learning task is to locate the joints and determine whether the joints are damaged (or not) and what type the damage is.   For the first use case, our customer is a rail maintainer. Rail maintainers make sure that the railway is safe, for example, by checking the joints of kilometers and kilometers of rails across countries and identifying any damage happening to those joints. In the past, either someone needed to survey the rail track or manually go through many images. Most of the footage has nothing relevant because there isn’t a joint every millimeter. In effect, you have terabytes of data, and only a small part of that will be applicable.   Siemens Mobility has a cloud application that enables rail maintainers to survey the joints through a user interface. The workflow is as follows: there is a bucket with terabytes of images, and we have a couple of containers that first detect the joints, and next classify whether it's damaged or not, and what type of damage it is. Data on damages, amongst other data, is stored in a database. The user can go through a map, click on the joints flagged as damaged, and look at the specific information.   A critical part of this process is the feedback loop with the user. Our users have a wealth of domain knowledge but perhaps are not familiar with the data requirements in Machine Learning. Before you go into development, you need to find the right way to get their input and how they wish to interact with the system. After you've deployed, you keep checking how the user interacts with your application. Use case 2: Signal recognition for autonomous rail The second use case is signal recognition. Regardless of whether we're talking about city trams or mainline trains, the signals are scattered along the route. At its core, this is a location and classification problem. However, the devil is in the details.   In this use case, we know that we cannot rely on a data link to the train. We need a computer on the train. Moreover, the prediction needs to be in real-time, and it has to work at all times, whether the temperature is +40 C or -40 C. The use case requires incredibly robust hardware on the train. Considering differences Let’s consider some of the resulting differences in using machine learning for these two use cases. In the case of rail track inspection, we rolled out on a cloud platform, ran on CPUs, and  used the Python stack. We use S3 buckets, and because the latency requirements aren't strict, we can work in batches. If it takes a little longer, that's quite all right. Also, there's no competition for resources with other processes.   With signal recognition, the biggest concern is safety. The solution must be stable. The hardware needs to remain steady at all times under all temperatures, which already conditions the kind of GPU you can use – or even if you can use a GPU at all. There is special legislation, the development of which for autonomous driving is in the early stages only. And there is the real-time requirement.   What from a pre-production perspective looked like two very similar machine learning problems – both are ‘detect & classify’ – changes very much from the customer’s perspective and the solution’s deployment.   In sum, how you deploy shapes the whole product and also affects the original machine learning algorithms. From the Q&A “Were there any surprises after going into production?” “Well, yes. For instance, the rail track maintainers got back and said they had all false positives. We discovered that the images were not being uploaded as expected.”   “Do you collect feedback from multiple users, and how do you validate a difference in feedback?” “Feedback is something delicate. Getting annotations right may be complicated, and there is usually variability within the same person. Indeed we measured differences within the same customer.”   “How do you deal with model drift?” “You can and should put metrics in place to monitor how the behavior changes. It nevertheless is a challenging issue, especially within different campaigns.”   “Models decay with time; how do you trigger the retraining?” “You can start small and fairly simple, possibly even manually. Once you are sure you can validate all the user feedback coming and trust all your monitoring metrics, then you can automate retraining to correct any kind of data or model drift and re-deploying.”

Automated decision making for supplier management Invoice classification Dr. Nicholas Hoff Founder, New Approach Technologies Moderated by Evan Simpson C an you automatically and accurately decide whether to pay an invoice? How to automate decision making at the 99.5% confidence level.   
[New Approach Technologies](http://newapproachtechnologies.com/) is my freelancing firm. I consult, build quick prototypes, and deploy production systems. Consulting often is exploratory and driven by questions clients have, for example, about the feasibility and value of Data Science for their company. Prototypes are a low-risk way for people to see what’s possible with their data.   
“I have built many production systems over the past years, especially in the banking sector. The deployment of machine learning has now become common in this area, partly because of the plentiful data available.” Nick Hoff, Machine Learning expert  
  
I will show you the invoice classification problem that Société Générale presented to me. We will survey some of the machine learning techniques that I considered using, why they worked, and when they didn't work. Finally, I will discuss the solution that went into production and elaborate on some of the more interesting points about productionizing machine learning. Classifying invoices to automate payment One branch of Société Générale runs a corporate car leasing operation. Since the cars are  leased, Société Générale is responsible for the maintenance. Whenever a car goes to a maintenance shop, the maintenance or repair generates an invoice. The invoice goes to Société Générale, and an expert decides whether or not to pay it. The goal of this project was to automate that decision partially.   
For a machine learning solution, we extract the various fields from the invoice, identify which ones are relevant, train a classifier on them based on whether each invoice was paid or not, and then use the classifier to issue payment recommendations on new invoices. Société Générale gave me several years of historical invoices as images and also as XMLs, plus the payment decisions that the experts made. I used hundreds of thousands of them to train the classifier.  Testing ML solutions The naive approach here is to treat this as an image classification problem. Just train a convolutional neural network to classify the images just like you would if you wanted to classify images of cars or airplanes. That won’t work for several reasons. First of all, the images are too big. A CNN model would be way too large, even for state-of-the-art GPUs. Second, it wouldn’t be a good fit for this problem even if the model was smaller or the GPUs were bigger.   
The second idea is to extract the data as text from the invoice image and use that as features for a classical neural network. After extensive data pre-processing and the transformation of text into numerical and categorical values, you would build a binary classifier. That was a good enough idea to try, so I coded it up, but it didn’t fit the data well. In the training set, over 95% of the invoices are paid, which you expect as most invoices are correct and legitimate. Only a small number of invoices have problems and should not be paid. It is a class imbalance. The neural network achieved good performance by ignoring the data and always suggesting you pay – with an accuracy of 95 percent. K-Means delivers In the end, I turned to K-means. Here, you take each feature in your invoice and find the three most similar invoices to that, e.g., the closest total, the same type of repair, the closest date, and so on. The three most similar invoices in the training set ‘vote’ on the case. For example, if the experts paid all three of those invoices, you pay. Or, if two of them were not paid, then you don't pay.    
One benefit is that similar invoices tend to cluster. For example, fuel invoices tend to cluster because many of their features are similar. Another advantage of K-Means is that the training time is fast, which meant that I could easily tweak and iterate. The issue of false positives There are two possible quality measurements for a classifier - precision and recall - often in conflict. The ‘recall’ is the fraction of the invoices for which a prediction is possible. The ‘precision’ is how accurate that prediction is. The first classifier I built for Société Générale had 75% recall and was 98% accurate. That means a 2% error rate or 1 in 50, which is not accurate enough.   
After discussing it with the client, I realized that they cared more about precision (mistakes are terrible) than recall (it’s OK if the classifier sometimes can’t decide). I tweaked the algorithm for higher precision at lower recall: 99.7% accuracy on 40% of the invoices. 40% may look low, but any fraction of the invoices you can automate saves people time. And that's a real win for Société Générale because then the people are free to do more exciting work.    
“When it comes to putting models in production for freelance clients, accuracy and stability are key.” Evan Simpson, Data Scientist   
In the end, SG was happy with the prototype. In coordination with their IT team, we packaged it for deployment (which required some fun engineering effort), and it is used in production to this day.   ![Tech Stack for automated invoice classification](Image00003.jpg)From the Q&A “How did the client react to the process of you trying different models and then finding one that worked?” “SocGen was interested in the technical stuff, so it was fun to go back and forth. Productionizing memory- and model-based recommender systems  Steve Jobs, 2009 “A lot of times, people don’t know what they want until you show it to them.”    
Delivery Hero utilizes memory- and model-based recommendation systems on its marketplace. It helps users find relevant items, saves search time, and enhances the experience with new or unexpected offers. Recommender systems improve the customer experience while driving  incremental revenue.    
The memory-based recommender system is based on a non-parametric mathematical approach computing similarity between users and items using heuristics. We use content-based filtering for discovering similarity among items and collaborative filtering for finding similarity among users. These filters may be combined in a hybrid model.   
Our model-based recommender system uses an algorithm to predict the next item a user wants to see and order, again using content-based and collaborative filtering to arrive at a hybrid model.   
Users give us explicit feedback on the recommender system through purchases, ratings, and recommendations. We also analyze the implicit feedback deriving from the way users interact with the platform content.   
How we evaluate the dynamic recommender system: In addressing the challenge that training data may be biased by previous recommendations made, we favor online over offline evaluations. A/B testing gives us a more objective perspective if we invest the necessary time for the experiment to run before comparing results. ![Tech Stack diagram of Delivery Hero's Recommendation System](Image00004.jpg)Collaborative filtering for recommending items and vendors  For collaborative filtering, Delivery Hero analyzes transactions based on user similarities and preferences or based on item similarity. A key advantage of collaborative over content-based filtering is that it achieves independence from the context, making the recommendations more  practical (e.g., what customers buy together). As we retrain the model over time, we capture trend evolution too.   
This type of filtering works particularly well for repeat customers, whereas for new customers, one might start by, e.g., showing the most popular items. We also need to protect the algorithm. For example, so-called shilling attacks might occur where vendors or product owners seek to influence recommendations and ranking (e.g., fake reviews).    “Building a model often is specific for a particular country or region, and depends, for example, on population density and typical behavioral patterns.”

The product list sorting algorithm How MYTOYS uses machine learning for customer conversion Dr. Sebastian Rose, Senior Data Scientist, MYTOYS GROUP Moderated by Elif Shaefer D eploying an adaptive product list sorting algorithm instead of a rule-based algorithm has enabled MYTOYS to increase the conversion rate by 7%, and revenue per visitor by 6,5%. The bounce rate decreased by -12%.   
[MYTOYS](https://www.mytoys.de) is the biggest online retailer platform for young families in Europe with more than €720 million ARR, 7 million customers, 21 million visits, and 250k products online. Seventy percent of the traffic is mobile. It delivers more than 13 million packages per year to its customers. Product list sorting for the customer journey A critical challenge for scaling an e-commerce platform is how and in which order to present its products. Online shoppers encounter this situation frequently: for example by browsing the product overview pages, with products of a category or a selection of products from different categories.   
For this purpose, several sorting strategies can be implemented which vary with regard to the involvement of human experts, and the complexity of the system and algorithms (rule-based, adaptive, and prognostic). The more sophisticated the algorithm of choice at the beginning, the higher the challenges of technical integration and organizational acceptance. Following the  principle “start simple, grow complex” these challenges can be balanced and the change into an AI-based company can be triggered.   
More systematically, product list sorting in the customer journey has the following dimensions: \- Inspiration. A listing based on popularity to create the purchase intention. \- Orientation. Recommendations based on the similarity of users or products to stimulate the purchase intention. \- Relevance. A listing based on the relevance relative to the search intention to conclude the purchase intention.   
In this talk we focus on sorting strategies for customer inspiration (product overview pages).   
The simplest sorting strategy is random. It works well when you are starting an e-commerce and have your first limited set of products on display. If business picks up and you have more than 250,000 products like MYTOYS, you will be looking at the following strategies: 1\. Rule-based sorting. The definition of rules by human experts. 2.  Adaptive sorting. A defined target variable is used to adapt the product sorting. 3.  Prognostic sorting. The prediction of the sorting based on product data patterns. Rule-based sorting Rule-based product sorting is simple. You implement an algorithm based on selected variables, e.g. Revenue in the last 30 days or current Reach/Availability of the product. You can formalize this in the following way: “Weight \* Revenue (30 days) + Weight \* Trend (7 days) + Weight \* Reach (current) + …. = Product List Score”      
Thereby you decide which weight to attach to the different variables, so you could optimize your sorting for example for a clear-out sale by lowering the weight for reach/availability, so those remaining shelf items are displayed first. If you want to optimize for revenue, you increase the weight for revenue. Your decisions will be derived from your business requirements, and you can change the product list sorting manually.   
The issue with rule-based sorting is that the reasoning is circular: the product list score influences the performance indicators by which it is calculated. Moreover, the rules may not reflect your customers’ needs but your business’ only. Yet, interfering with complex systems by  manually changing weights likely introduces human error, meaning that you may not reach your corporate goal. Adaptive sorting Adaptive sorting introduces statistics and the calculation of probabilities. More precisely, the ranking is the result of the expected probability of a product, which is seen on the overview page, will also be purchased. More formalized this approach creates a ranking as the result from the probability that a customer purchases the product under the current sorting list.  This probability is optimized in this process. Hence, customer demand and feedback is automatically integrated into the formula driving adaptive sorting.   
With adaptive sorting, your platform responds easily to changes in demand, and even to spikes. An example in Germany is the yearly announcement of the ‘Game of the Year’ (Spiel des Jahres) in various categories. The adaptive algorithm will pick up the surge in demand automatically and respond accordingly. Thereby, real-time quality data is key. With up-to-date and accurate data this system responds to changing demand, for example, in the run-up to Christmas automatically.   
A limitation of adaptive sorting is that it provides only a snapshot based on a limited set of variables. Also, your data pipeline needs to be highly reliable. Prognostic sorting Prognostic sorting means that your machine learns to rank based on e.g. regression models, Support Vector Machines, Ranknet, and more. You optimize the effectiveness of the ranking towards the relevance criterion, e.g. the search term. It requires training and test data for your model, which is then deployed to predict demand for e.g. the next day.   
An advantage is that your product ranking is optimized over time and you can forecast demand. On the other hand, implementation is complex and takes longer, and the mathematical background is not explained easily to business stakeholders.   
Moreover, learning to rank increases the relevance of search results but does not necessarily provide the best method for creating customer inspiration (or the best product overview page), i.e. the purchase intention.   
“The use of machine learning is a core competence to be competitive in the future. Currently, there is an oversimplification between its use and its impact on business success. The example of the product list sorting algorithm demonstrates what needs to be done for the successful introduction of machine learning on the way to becoming an AI-based company.”  
Sebastian Rose, Senior Data Scientist

The technical implementation requires a database and a server. For the solution it is required to track web, sales, and product data. Such data is transferred to an open-source enterprise search platform that is the engine powering the product service.

As a data science team in the energy space, we use data to reduce complexity and increase scalability. Energy problems tend to be more difficult than other data science problems. Some of the data is highly sensitive since our core mission is reliability. Making sure that everything is running smoothly also means things are failsafe and for that, you really have a lot of processes in place. Safety and reliability issues often make it difficult to access data and make it available to the data team. Also, energy has a time series component to it, which adds another layer of complexity.   
What is the challenge in accessing data? There are different security zones where sourcing systems are located. Moreover, you need different technologies to access the data. Data governance may diverge too. Sometimes the data is readily available, and sometimes we need to extract it. If you can streamline data availability and access, then you have more time  for things like exploration and analysis.   
50 Hertz, the east German TSO in the Elia Group, has an energy data cloud. It is an easy-to-use, functional data access platform. It gives all 50 Hertz employees the ability to explore energy-relevant data.     
The energy data cloud standardizes permission, including APIs for accessing data from various sources. Users need only one permission to access data. Additionally, every dataset has a listed owner. Predicting grid losses with a neural network Standardized data access is critical for rolling out use cases, giving people access to the data they need. It enabled rapid machine learning implementations.   
One example is the use case of forecasting grid losses. Whatever energy is generated and transported, it is not possible to get every single electron that was generated to the final destination. Some of it is lost, in the form of heat, because transmission is at very high voltages. And the amount that is lost is generally around two percent of what is generated. But it can be only one percent if the energy doesn't have to travel very far or is at a lower voltage. It can be up to five percent if energy is transmitted very far.   
The business case is that a TSO needs to cover grid losses, and this means often having to purchase electricity at the last minute for a much higher cost. In 2018, 50 Hertz spent €70m on grid loss cover. The energy transition means that grid losses are increasing (e.g. from offshore wind parks) and that the grid loss range is more volatile.   
In other words, when you have more renewables, this increases loss, and the intermittency of energy generation compounds the issue. It is a master of great importance that a TSO is able to predict grid loss. If we don't have an accurate prediction ahead of time, we have to spend more money on grid loss cover.

A TSO is a government-regulated monopoly. it's in the interest of all consumers to improve forecasts. The data science team did this once they could get access to the energy data cloud. The team was able to integrate a lot more data points than previously. For example,   data from 70 weather stations all around Germany. The initial model was a simple feedforward neural network with just one hidden layer. Deployment The data science team realized quickly that the feedforward neural network provided more accurate predictions than the classical time-series model. Next, they were able to deploy quickly, and on their own, on the Agile Apps Platform. It runs on OpenShift, and this open-source container application platform makes deployment easy for anyone.   
The Day-Ahead forecast is operative since December 2019. The feedforward neural network has over 100 inputs and a hidden layer with 50 nodes. From start to finish, this project took less than half a year. Furthermore, once everything is deployed on the agile platform, it makes it easy to update the model with new versions.

The forecast quality was improved by 7%. In the first year of production, the neural network saved one percent of grid loss cover charges, i.e. €700,000.
