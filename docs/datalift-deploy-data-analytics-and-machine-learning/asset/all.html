Railway maintenance and autonomous vehicles
more similar and more different than you’d think
Irina Vidal Migallón, Technical Lead AI & Computer Vision, Siemens Mobility
Moderated by Sven Krüger
M onitoring the state of rail tracks and autonomous driving have many tasks in common: detect obstacles, detect people, detect assets, detect damages. As pre-production work, it may seem a no-brainer to approach them in similar ways. The devil is in the details, though, which happens to be 90% of the product. Sensors, deployment platform, processing hardware, feedback loop, laws: they have a more significant impact than the machine learning technique itself.

Siemens Mobility has a division focusing on computer vision and machine learning for different products around mobility, especially also in the railway domain. Products range from passenger assistance to autonomous driving. For example, we increase comfort for travelers by finding spots for a wheelchair or detecting aggression to improve safety. For driving, our approach includes handling typical issues like obstacle detection and track segmentation.
The difference deployment makes
We often see issues around computer vision and machine learning that look similar, actually, deceivingly similar. I say ‘deceivingly’ because even though the challenges may point to the same machine learning solution, the solution is part of differing products, and we deploy each differently. Deployment is more than a click. You iterate to find the best solution for your customers and users and, for example, with your DevOps to check for feasibility.
 
At Siemens Mobility, we are working on different use cases for a variety of users. Consider the usual machine learning cycle from data processing on to training and then, finally, to deployment. That final step, the deployment, makes the difference. It makes every use case different.


 
“Deployment must inform all the steps taken before. For instance, if you're doing detection and were to use the most massive, most powerful detector out there, but you find you don't have the hardware to run it, then you need to go back to the whiteboard and choose something else.”
Irina Vidal Migallón, Technical Lead AI & Computer Vision
 
I'm just going to show you a couple of use cases that, at their core, have the same machine learning concept. But you'll see how they look nothing like each other precisely because of how they're deployed.
Use case 1: Inspection of rail track joints
The first use case is about the inspection of rail track joints. We gather data by specially equipped rail vehicles that run around collecting images. At its core, the machine learning task is to locate the joints and determine whether the joints are damaged (or not) and what type the damage is.
 
For the first use case, our customer is a rail maintainer. Rail maintainers make sure that the railway is safe, for example, by checking the joints of kilometers and kilometers of rails across countries and identifying any damage happening to those joints. In the past, either someone needed to survey the rail track or manually go through many images. Most of the footage has nothing relevant because there isn’t a joint every millimeter. In effect, you have terabytes of data, and only a small part of that will be applicable.
 
Siemens Mobility has a cloud application that enables rail maintainers to survey the joints through a user interface. The workflow is as follows: there is a bucket with terabytes of images, and we have a couple of containers that first detect the joints, and next classify whether it's damaged or not, and what type of damage it is. Data on damages, amongst other data, is stored in a database. The user can go through a map, click on the joints flagged as damaged, and look at the specific information.
 
A critical part of this process is the feedback loop with the user. Our users have a wealth of domain knowledge but perhaps are not familiar with the data requirements in Machine Learning. Before you go into development, you need to find the right way to get their input and how they wish to interact with the system. After you've deployed, you keep checking how the user interacts with your application.
Use case 2: Signal recognition for autonomous rail
The second use case is signal recognition. Regardless of whether we're talking about city trams or mainline trains, the signals are scattered along the route. At its core, this is a location and classification problem. However, the devil is in the details.
 
In this use case, we know that we cannot rely on a data link to the train. We need a computer on the train. Moreover, the prediction needs to be in real-time, and it has to work at all times, whether the temperature is +40 C or -40 C. The use case requires incredibly robust hardware on the train.
Considering differences
Let’s consider some of the resulting differences in using machine learning for these two use cases. In the case of rail track inspection, we rolled out on a cloud platform, ran on CPUs, and used the Python stack. We use S3 buckets, and because the latency requirements aren't strict, we can work in batches. If it takes a little longer, that's quite all right. Also, there's no competition for resources with other processes.
 
With signal recognition, the biggest concern is safety. The solution must be stable. The hardware needs to remain steady at all times under all temperatures, which already conditions the kind of GPU you can use – or even if you can use a GPU at all. There is special legislation, the development of which for autonomous driving is in the early stages only. And there is the real-time requirement.
 
What from a pre-production perspective looked like two very similar machine learning problems – both are ‘detect & classify’ – changes very much from the customer’s perspective and the solution’s deployment.
 
In sum, how you deploy shapes the whole product and also affects the original machine learning algorithms.
From the Q&A
“Were there any surprises after going into production?”
“Well, yes. For instance, the rail track maintainers got back and said they had all false positives. We discovered that the images were not being uploaded as expected.”
 
“Do you collect feedback from multiple users, and how do you validate a difference in feedback?”
“Feedback is something delicate. Getting annotations right may be complicated, and there is usually variability within the same person. Indeed we measured differences within the same customer.”
 
“How do you deal with model drift?”
“You can and should put metrics in place to monitor how the behavior changes. It nevertheless is a challenging issue, especially within different campaigns.”
 
“Models decay with time; how do you trigger the retraining?”
“You can start small and fairly simple, possibly even manually. Once you are sure you can validate all the user feedback coming and trust all your monitoring metrics, then you can automate retraining to correct any kind of data or model drift and re-deploying.”

Automated decision making for supplier management
Invoice classification
Dr. Nicholas Hoff Founder, New Approach Technologies
Moderated by Evan Simpson
C an you automatically and accurately decide whether to pay an invoice? How to automate decision making at the 99.5% confidence level.

New Approach Technologies is my freelancing firm. I consult, build quick prototypes, and deploy production systems. Consulting often is exploratory and driven by questions clients have, for example, about the feasibility and value of Data Science for their company. Prototypes are a low-risk way for people to see what’s possible with their data.

“I have built many production systems over the past years, especially in the banking sector. The deployment of machine learning has now become common in this area, partly because of the plentiful data available.”
Nick Hoff, Machine Learning expert

I will show you the invoice classification problem that Société Générale presented to me. We will survey some of the machine learning techniques that I considered using, why they worked, and when they didn't work. Finally, I will discuss the solution that went into production and elaborate on some of the more interesting points about productionizing machine learning.
Classifying invoices to automate payment
One branch of Société Générale runs a corporate car leasing operation. Since the cars are leased, Société Générale is responsible for the maintenance. Whenever a car goes to a maintenance shop, the maintenance or repair generates an invoice. The invoice goes to Société Générale, and an expert decides whether or not to pay it. The goal of this project was to automate that decision partially.

For a machine learning solution, we extract the various fields from the invoice, identify which ones are relevant, train a classifier on them based on whether each invoice was paid or not, and then use the classifier to issue payment recommendations on new invoices. Société Générale gave me several years of historical invoices as images and also as XMLs, plus the payment decisions that the experts made. I used hundreds of thousands of them to train the classifier. 
Testing ML solutions
The naive approach here is to treat this as an image classification problem. Just train a convolutional neural network to classify the images just like you would if you wanted to classify images of cars or airplanes. That won’t work for several reasons. First of all, the images are too big. A CNN model would be way too large, even for state-of-the-art GPUs. Second, it wouldn’t be a good fit for this problem even if the model was smaller or the GPUs were bigger.

The second idea is to extract the data as text from the invoice image and use that as features for a classical neural network. After extensive data pre-processing and the transformation of text into numerical and categorical values, you would build a binary classifier. That was a good enough idea to try, so I coded it up, but it didn’t fit the data well. In the training set, over 95% of the invoices are paid, which you expect as most invoices are correct and legitimate. Only a small number of invoices have problems and should not be paid. It is a class imbalance. The neural network achieved good performance by ignoring the data and always suggesting you pay – with an accuracy of 95 percent.
K-Means delivers
In the end, I turned to K-means. Here, you take each feature in your invoice and find the three most similar invoices to that, e.g., the closest total, the same type of repair, the closest date, and so on. The three most similar invoices in the training set ‘vote’ on the case. For example, if the experts paid all three of those invoices, you pay. Or, if two of them were not paid, then you don't pay. 

One benefit is that similar invoices tend to cluster. For example, fuel invoices tend to cluster because many of their features are similar. Another advantage of K-Means is that the training time is fast, which meant that I could easily tweak and iterate.
The issue of false positives
There are two possible quality measurements for a classifier - precision and recall - often in conflict. The ‘recall’ is the fraction of the invoices for which a prediction is possible. The ‘precision’ is how accurate that prediction is. The first classifier I built for Société Générale had 75% recall and was 98% accurate. That means a 2% error rate or 1 in 50, which is not accurate enough.

After discussing it with the client, I realized that they cared more about precision (mistakes are terrible) than recall (it’s OK if the classifier sometimes can’t decide). I tweaked the algorithm for higher precision at lower recall: 99.7% accuracy on 40% of the invoices. 40% may look low, but any fraction of the invoices you can automate saves people time. And that's a real win for Société Générale because then the people are free to do more exciting work. 

“When it comes to putting models in production for freelance clients, accuracy and stability are key.”
Evan Simpson, Data Scientist

In the end, SG was happy with the prototype. In coordination with their IT team, we packaged it for deployment (which required some fun engineering effort), and it is used in production to this day.
 
From the Q&A
“How did the client react to the process of you trying different models and then finding one that worked?”
“SocGen was interested in the technical stuff, so it was fun to go back and forth. Other clients just want the final result, which is fine too.”

“How do you engage clients with maintaining expectations?"
“I prefer clear communication from the get-go, especially about what the client wants and what I think is feasible. Do you want advice? Do you want me to explore your data and build a prototype? Or do you want a real production system? Also, my experience says it's almost always simple stuff that solves low hanging fruit. There is a lot of business impact for clients on clean data, linear regression, K-Means, and simple neural networks. Of course, the advanced stuff is there in the toolbox if we need it."

“Do you only use stable data science libraries or also some private code?”
“For production deployment that I expect to run beyond my engagement, I only use stable libraries. For prototyping, though, there’s more freedom."


Marketplace recommendation systems
How Delivery Hero shows customers what they want
Dr. Aleksandra Kovachev, Senior Machine Learning Engineer & Varun Chitale, Data Scientist, Delivery Hero
Moderated by Macarena Beigier
T he Recommendations and Ranking Team at Delivery Hero discovers where, when, and what the customers want. The team couples scalable and efficient data pipelines with fast and reliable APIs to serve recommendations for >6 million transactions daily.

Delivery Hero is a platform network of 30+ brands operating in 40+ countries, partnering with 600k+ restaurants and a fleet of riders. Delivery Hero is also a pioneer of Q-commerce (quick commerce) for ultra-fast deliveries from grocery stores, pharmacies, pet shops, and more. A particular highlight of the company culture is that people from 100+ nationalities work at the Berlin headquarters.
Productionizing memory- and model-based recommender systems 
Steve Jobs, 2009
“A lot of times, people don’t know what they want until you show it to them.” 

Delivery Hero utilizes memory- and model-based recommendation systems on its marketplace. It helps users find relevant items, saves search time, and enhances the experience with new or unexpected offers. Recommender systems improve the customer experience while driving incremental revenue. 

The memory-based recommender system is based on a non-parametric mathematical approach computing similarity between users and items using heuristics. We use content-based filtering for discovering similarity among items and collaborative filtering for finding similarity among users. These filters may be combined in a hybrid model.

Our model-based recommender system uses an algorithm to predict the next item a user wants to see and order, again using content-based and collaborative filtering to arrive at a hybrid model.

Users give us explicit feedback on the recommender system through purchases, ratings, and recommendations. We also analyze the implicit feedback deriving from the way users interact with the platform content.

How we evaluate the dynamic recommender system: In addressing the challenge that training data may be biased by previous recommendations made, we favor online over offline evaluations. A/B testing gives us a more objective perspective if we invest the necessary time for the experiment to run before comparing results.
Collaborative filtering for recommending items and vendors 
For collaborative filtering, Delivery Hero analyzes transactions based on user similarities and preferences or based on item similarity. A key advantage of collaborative over content-based filtering is that it achieves independence from the context, making the recommendations more practical (e.g., what customers buy together). As we retrain the model over time, we capture trend evolution too.

This type of filtering works particularly well for repeat customers, whereas for new customers, one might start by, e.g., showing the most popular items. We also need to protect the algorithm. For example, so-called shilling attacks might occur where vendors or product owners seek to influence recommendations and ranking (e.g., fake reviews). 
 
“Building a model often is specific for a particular country or region, and depends, for example, on population density and typical behavioral patterns.” 
Dr. Aleksandra Kovachev, Senior Data Scientist  

“Delivery Hero business success is based on AI models specifically designed for particular countries and regions, giving the company a great potential for expansion to new markets.”
Macarena Beigier, Data Scientist
Types of recommendations
Delivery Hero distinguishes between vendor- and product-level recommendations. We have different categories, like restaurants, supermarkets, or pharmacies, which define the marketplace. Typical offers then include past orders, the most popular items, similar items, or personalized recommendations.
Data sources and the pipeline
Delivery Hero uses a DataFridge for collecting, unifying, and standardizing all data, whether explicit data (e.g., vendors, products, orders, reviews) or tracking data (e.g., clicks, interactions). From analyzing this data, we extract the meaningful features for the recommender system.  
From the Q&A
“Do you use any attention-based recommendation models?”
“No, mainly because for recommending restaurants or shops, it isn’t pertinent.”

“How do you handle overfitting for personalized recommendations?”
“We encounter overfitting when we use classical ML methods. We have looked at oversampling and undersampling and typically find that using class weights works well.”

The product list sorting algorithm
How MYTOYS uses machine learning for customer conversion
Dr. Sebastian Rose, Senior Data Scientist, MYTOYS GROUP
Moderated by Elif Shaefer
D eploying an adaptive product list sorting algorithm instead of a rule-based algorithm has enabled MYTOYS to increase the conversion rate by 7%, and revenue per visitor by 6,5%. The bounce rate decreased by -12%.

MYTOYS is the biggest online retailer platform for young families in Europe with more than €720 million ARR, 7 million customers, 21 million visits, and 250k products online. Seventy percent of the traffic is mobile. It delivers more than 13 million packages per year to its customers.
Product list sorting for the customer journey
A critical challenge for scaling an e-commerce platform is how and in which order to present its products. Online shoppers encounter this situation frequently: for example by browsing the product overview pages, with products of a category or a selection of products from different categories.

For this purpose, several sorting strategies can be implemented which vary with regard to the involvement of human experts, and the complexity of the system and algorithms (rule-based, adaptive, and prognostic). The more sophisticated the algorithm of choice at the beginning, the higher the challenges of technical integration and organizational acceptance. Following the principle “start simple, grow complex” these challenges can be balanced and the change into an AI-based company can be triggered.

More systematically, product list sorting in the customer journey has the following dimensions:
- Inspiration. A listing based on popularity to create the purchase intention.
- Orientation. Recommendations based on the similarity of users or products to stimulate the purchase intention.
- Relevance. A listing based on the relevance relative to the search intention to conclude the purchase intention.

In this talk we focus on sorting strategies for customer inspiration (product overview pages).

The simplest sorting strategy is random. It works well when you are starting an e-commerce and have your first limited set of products on display. If business picks up and you have more than 250,000 products like MYTOYS, you will be looking at the following strategies:
1. Rule-based sorting. The definition of rules by human experts.
2.  Adaptive sorting. A defined target variable is used to adapt the product sorting.
3.  Prognostic sorting. The prediction of the sorting based on product data patterns.
Rule-based sorting
Rule-based product sorting is simple. You implement an algorithm based on selected variables, e.g. Revenue in the last 30 days or current Reach/Availability of the product. You can formalize this in the following way: “Weight * Revenue (30 days) + Weight * Trend (7 days) + Weight * Reach (current) + …. = Product List Score”   

Thereby you decide which weight to attach to the different variables, so you could optimize your sorting for example for a clear-out sale by lowering the weight for reach/availability, so those remaining shelf items are displayed first. If you want to optimize for revenue, you increase the weight for revenue. Your decisions will be derived from your business requirements, and you can change the product list sorting manually.

The issue with rule-based sorting is that the reasoning is circular: the product list score influences the performance indicators by which it is calculated. Moreover, the rules may not reflect your customers’ needs but your business’ only. Yet, interfering with complex systems by manually changing weights likely introduces human error, meaning that you may not reach your corporate goal.
Adaptive sorting
Adaptive sorting introduces statistics and the calculation of probabilities. More precisely, the ranking is the result of the expected probability of a product, which is seen on the overview page, will also be purchased. More formalized this approach creates a ranking as the result from the probability that a customer purchases the product under the current sorting list.  This probability is optimized in this process. Hence, customer demand and feedback is automatically integrated into the formula driving adaptive sorting.

With adaptive sorting, your platform responds easily to changes in demand, and even to spikes. An example in Germany is the yearly announcement of the ‘Game of the Year’ (Spiel des Jahres) in various categories. The adaptive algorithm will pick up the surge in demand automatically and respond accordingly. Thereby, real-time quality data is key. With up-to-date and accurate data this system responds to changing demand, for example, in the run-up to Christmas automatically.

A limitation of adaptive sorting is that it provides only a snapshot based on a limited set of variables. Also, your data pipeline needs to be highly reliable.
Prognostic sorting
Prognostic sorting means that your machine learns to rank based on e.g. regression models, Support Vector Machines, Ranknet, and more. You optimize the effectiveness of the ranking towards the relevance criterion, e.g. the search term. It requires training and test data for your model, which is then deployed to predict demand for e.g. the next day.

An advantage is that your product ranking is optimized over time and you can forecast demand. On the other hand, implementation is complex and takes longer, and the mathematical background is not explained easily to business stakeholders.

Moreover, learning to rank increases the relevance of search results but does not necessarily provide the best method for creating customer inspiration (or the best product overview page), i.e. the purchase intention.

“The use of machine learning is a core competence to be competitive in the future. Currently, there is an oversimplification between its use and its impact on business success. The example of the product list sorting algorithm demonstrates what needs to be done for the successful introduction of machine learning on the way to becoming an AI-based company.”
Sebastian Rose, Senior Data Scientist

“In this use case, Sebastian doesn’t only introduce us to different machine learning algorithms based on their difficulties in implementation and their advantages/disadvantages, he also gives us some details about the mathematical aspect of those algorithms. I enjoyed the emphasis he put on the collaboration with the stakeholders as well.”
Elif Schaefer, Head Of Business Intelligence
Technical implementation of the adaptive sorting strategies
The modeling difficulty and system complexity of adaptive product list sorting are more than for rule-based sorting but less than prognostic sorting. The adaptive model gained trust and acceptance from business stakeholders, since it could be easily explained to them.

The technical implementation requires a database and a server. For the solution it is required to track web, sales, and product data. Such data is transferred to an open-source enterprise search platform that is the engine powering the product service.



In sum, the MYTOYS group switched the logic of the product overview pages from a rule-based algorithm to an adaptive one. Perhaps the most important measure of success was that the bounce rate dropped by twelve percent, which means that customers liked the new sorting better, which at the end could also be seen in an significant uplift in conversion of seven percent, and in revenue per visitor of six and a half percent. The customer is more likely to be “hooked” by our product presentation and therefore to decide to purchase products at mytoys.de.
From the Q&A
“How does adaptive sorting connect to the sorting by popularity?”
“It is an algorithm for sorting by popularity.”

“How rich is the tech stack of your data warehouse?”
“We are building a data vault model and also moving to a data lake scenario to ensure continuous real-time deployment scenarios.”

“What was the feature selection process for the adaptive sorting algorithm?”
“We agreed on the main features early on and deployed an XGBoost algorithm with very little overfitting. The features include business aspects like availability and also the seasonality of the product.”

Dr. Peter Heise, Data Analytics and AI Community Leader, Airbus
The Airbus AI story. Upskilling thousands of engineers to data analytics and machine learning.
An interview with Dr. Peter Heise (Airbus) by Dr. Chris Armbruster (AI Guild)
Chris   
Peter, let’s speak about the Airbus AI story. In 2018 I learned that Airbus started upskilling 700 employees to data analytics and machine learning. That was quite a number. In 2019 I heard that Airbus achieved completion rates greater than 90 percent on the Udacity courses used. Later that year, I bumped into Sebastian Thrun, the founder of Udacity, who confirmed the very high completion rate, and mentioned that typically on Udacity, it was in the range of 20-30%. Next, I learned that in 2020 Airbus started upskilling the next two thousand employees, a fantastic effort. Why is Airbus doing this?
Peter
I think I have three answers for you. Firstly, we did believe in 2018 that AI would be everywhere and relevant to all 100,000 people working at Airbus. Personalized healthcare for diabetes patients 
Diabetes is a chronic disease when the body is not able to produce insulin or to use it effectively, which might result in serious side effects and requires close attention towards lifestyle.

For personalized treatment, the individual lifestyle is a crucial factor. Patients need support based not only on their medical data like blood glucose level, insulin intake, etc. but also in tracking daily activities and evaluating their impact. Labeling location and activity data 
Typically, patients have problems manually keep track of their daily activities. Consequently, data often is incomplete and lacks context. Only 3-5% of the users enter enough contextual data for their activities.
For automating this process, xbird utilizes location and sensor (namely accelerometer) data.  By combining these sources of information, it’s possible to infer different types of physical activity (e.g., cycling or running) and more complex patterns (waking up, working hours, etc.). 
In several preprocessing steps, we aggregate samples into regular periodic data. If location data is present, we use it to identify speed, which will be passed into a classification pipeline together with activity data.
The classifier pipeline
Some general observations xbird relied on during pipeline choice:
  • It is necessary to move beyond tracking small disjointed events to distinguish meaningful solid situations;
  • Different activities require different contexts. Transport usage needs to be defined for a more extended period to cover potential interruptions like traffic lights or stops.
  • Across users, data variety is significant for uncontrollable reasons, e.g.accelerometer data might differ depending on hardware or software;
  • The same activity, e.g., cycling, may be performed in very different ways depending on the user's physical condition.
A pipeline with a separate binary classifier for each activity type solves most of these problems, e.g., allows independently choosing window length (context period). But in some cases, multiclass classifiers might be more accurate. For personalization, i.e., the tuning of models for each user, xbird depends on minimizing recognition errors based on feedback loops with users, confirming or disconfirming the classification. We combine all these approaches for the current production set of xbird technology.

Zero Hunger by 2030!
How anthropometric measurement makes our dream a reality
Markus Hinsche, Senior ML Engineer, Freelance (for Welthungerhilfe)
Moderated by Johanna Viktor
A no-touch solution to detecting malnutrition in children by using commodity smartphone sensors and machine learning. How the Welthungerhilfe e.V. (World Hunger Aid) pushes for Zero Hunger by 2030.

Hunger and malnutrition are not a lack of food only, it is a more complex health issue. Child malnutrition is a global problem. Parents and frontline workers often don't know that the child is malnourished and take measures too late. The magnitude of the nutrition crisis – both in emergencies and chronic hunger situations – is blurred frequently. Current standardized measurements done by aid organizations and frontline healthcare workers are time-consuming and expensive. Children are moving, and accurate measurement, especially of body height, is often difficult. With the COVID-19 pandemic, the situation became more challenging. All manual measurement of children is suspended because of social distancing. Accurate data on the nutritional status of children is unreliable or non-existent. This hampers a determined response by emergency workers and policymakers.

Anthropometric measurement by a smartphone app provides the game-changer for the data collection and processing in the case of malnourished children under the age of 5 years. The solution is based on a mobile app using augmented reality in combination with artificial intelligence. By determining weight and height through a 3D scan of children, the app provides a quick and touchless way to measure children and detect early warning signs of malnutrition.

The Welthungerhilfe receives support from leading IT partners for the research and development of the measurement prediction. This project is open-source code and the full documentation can be accessed via GitHub.
Measuring child malnutrition
Around 800 million people suffer from chronic hunger, of which 200 million are children. More than 3 million child deaths are attributable to malnutrition. Death from malnutrition is preventable. Prevention requires detecting malnutrition early. However, current measurements are neither complete nor reliable.

Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) Measuring Tapes measure the upper arm circumference of children, helping to identify malnutrition. Additional required measurements are done by a wooden board (body height) and by scales (body weight). All devices are labor-intensive, often requiring two or more staff members. Also, they require physical contact and are known to be prone to errors leading to invalid measurements.

The Welthungerhilfe is introducing new technology for the detection of malnutrition: A body scan is taken with a smartphone that has a depth sensor.
The scan is done in the following steps:
- Signed consent form
- Video scan with a smartphone to a point cloud (3D space)
- Training of a machine learning model for a prediction that can be used in a diagnosis

Using a smartphone app is sufficient as the video not only has RGB detection but also a depth sensor enabling the transformation of the data to a point cloud.
Diagnosing malnutrition
For diagnosing malnutrition the Welthungerhilfe is interested mainly in the weight, height, and middle-upper arm circumference. According to the World Health Organization, the combination of these three measurements enables the diagnosis of malnutrition.

Let’s look at the prediction of body height. It is a component of the diagnosis. We collected thousands of smartphone scans of children plus the manual measurement of the body height. We used the data to train machine learning algorithms with a focus on the mean absolute error. It is a critical metric expressing the difference between the prediction and the actual height. With traditional manual measurement as a gold standard, the mean absolute error of 1.2 cm (0.47 in). Our challenge is being more accurate while also being fully reliable with no wrong prediction (false negative) for any child.

For the machine learning model, we examined a variety of approaches for utilizing the data from the point cloud. Neural network architectures showed some promise. With a plain convolutional neural network using depth maps, we achieved a mean absolute error of 2 cm or better. Our research indicates that by turning to a multi-artifact CNN with Late Fusion (Karpathy et al 2014) we may reduce the mean absolute error to 1cm or better.

For body weight and the middle-upper arm circumference (MUAC), our approach is similar. We know that the mean absolute error for traditional body weight measurement is 210 grams, and for MUAC is 3.3 mm. We train a neural network to predict body weight and MUAC.

From 2021, Welthungerhilfe is offering the product in India and starts data collection in multiple African countries. For further validation, the Welthungerhilfe is looking for more technical contributors. The project presents a great opportunity for deploying a disruptive digital solution that gives us an edge in achieving Zero Hunger by 2030.

One example is the use case of forecasting grid losses. Whatever energy is generated and transported, it is not possible to get every single electron that was generated to the final destination. Some of it is lost, in the form of heat, because transmission is at very high voltages. And the amount that is lost is generally around two percent of what is generated. But it can be only one percent if the energy doesn't have to travel very far or is at a lower voltage. It can be up to five percent if energy is transmitted very far.

The business case is that a TSO needs to cover grid losses, and this means often having to purchase electricity at the last minute for a much higher cost. In 2018, 50 Hertz spent €70m on grid loss cover. The energy transition means that grid losses are increasing (e.g. from offshore wind parks) and that the grid loss range is more volatile.

In other words, when you have more renewables, this increases loss, and the intermittency of energy generation compounds the issue. It is a master of great importance that a TSO is able to predict grid loss. If we don't have an accurate prediction ahead of time, we have to spend more money on grid loss cover.

Image: Energy Data Analytics

A TSO is a government-regulated monopoly. it's in the interest of all consumers to improve forecasts. The data science team did this once they could get access to the energy data cloud. The team was able to integrate a lot more data points than previously. For example,   data from 70 weather stations all around Germany. The initial model was a simple feedforward neural network with just one hidden layer.
Deployment
The data science team realized quickly that the feedforward neural network provided more accurate predictions than the classical time-series model. Next, they were able to deploy quickly, and on their own, on the Agile Apps Platform. It runs on OpenShift, and this open-source container application platform makes deployment easy for anyone.

The Day-Ahead forecast is operative since December 2019. The feedforward neural network has over 100 inputs and a hidden layer with 50 nodes. From start to finish, this project took less than half a year. Furthermore, once everything is deployed on the agile platform, it makes it easy to update the model with new versions.

Image: Energy Data Ecosystem

The forecast quality was improved by 7%. In the first year of production, the neural network saved one percent of grid loss cover charges, i.e. €700,000.
Success factors
How does the Elia Group replicate success across future use cases? First, full access to all company data. We can develop our own use cases and access any data we need. Second, the OpenShift agile app platform. It provides ease of deployment and clear ownership and responsibility: the platform is responsible for having enough computing power for all the apps that are running on it. But as to the individual app, that is the responsibility of the department and the individual deploying the app.  No one has to wait on someone else.
