## Product list sorting for the customer journey

A critical challenge for scaling an e-commerce platform is how and in which order to present its products. Online shoppers encounter this situation frequently: for example by browsing the product overview pages, with products of a category or a selection of products from different categories.

For this purpose, several sorting strategies can be implemented which vary with regard to the involvement of human experts, and the complexity of the system and algorithms (rule-based, adaptive, and prognostic). The more sophisticated the algorithm of choice at the beginning, the higher the challenges of technical integration and organizational acceptance. Following the principle “start simple, grow complex” these challenges can be balanced and the change into an AI-based company can be triggered.

More systematically, product list sorting in the customer journey has the following dimensions:
- Inspiration. A listing based on popularity to create the purchase intention.
- Orientation. Recommendations based on the similarity of users or products to stimulate the purchase intention.
- Relevance. A listing based on the relevance relative to the search intention to conclude the purchase intention.

In this talk we focus on sorting strategies for customer inspiration (product overview pages).

The simplest sorting strategy is random. It works well when you are starting an e-commerce and have your first limited set of products on display. If business picks up and you have more than 250,000 products like MYTOYS, you will be looking at the following strategies:
1. Rule-based sorting. The definition of rules by human experts.
2. Adaptive sorting. A defined target variable is used to adapt the product sorting.
3. Prognostic sorting. The prediction of the sorting based on product data patterns.

### Rule-based sorting

Rule-based product sorting is simple. You implement an algorithm based on selected variables, e.g. Revenue in the last 30 days or current Reach/Availability of the product. You can formalize this in the following way: “Weight * Revenue (30 days) + Weight * Trend (7 days) + Weight * Reach (current) + …. = Product List Score”

Thereby you decide which weight to attach to the different variables, so you could optimize your sorting for example for a clear-out sale by lowering the weight for reach/availability, so those remaining shelf items are displayed first. If you want to optimize for revenue, you increase the weight for revenue. Your decisions will be derived from your business requirements, and you can change the product list sorting manually.

The issue with rule-based sorting is that the reasoning is circular: the product list score influences the performance indicators by which it is calculated. Moreover, the rules may not reflect your customers’ needs but your business’ only. Yet, interfering with complex systems by manually changing weights likely introduces human error, meaning that you may not reach your corporate goal.

### Adaptive sorting

Adaptive sorting introduces statistics and the calculation of probabilities. More precisely, the ranking is the result of the expected probability of a product, which is seen on the overview page, will also be purchased. More formalized this approach creates a ranking as the result from the probability that a customer purchases the product under the current sorting list. This probability is optimized in this process. Hence, customer demand and feedback is automatically integrated into the formula driving adaptive sorting.

With adaptive sorting, your platform responds easily to changes in demand, and even to spikes. An example in Germany is the yearly announcement of the ‘Game of the Year’ (Spiel des Jahres) in various categories. The adaptive algorithm will pick up the surge in demand automatically and respond accordingly. Thereby, real-time quality data is key. With up-to-date and accurate data this system responds to changing demand, for example, in the run-up to Christmas automatically.

A limitation of adaptive sorting is that it provides only a snapshot based on a limited set of variables. Also, your data pipeline needs to be highly reliable.

### Prognostic sorting

Prognostic sorting means that your machine learns to rank based on e.g. regression models, Support Vector Machines, Ranknet, and more. You optimize the effectiveness of the ranking towards the relevance criterion, e.g. the search term. It requires training and test data for your model, which is then deployed to predict demand for e.g. the next day.

An advantage is that your product ranking is optimized over time and you can forecast demand. On the other hand, implementation is complex and takes longer, and the mathematical background is not explained easily to business stakeholders.

Moreover, learning to rank increases the relevance of search results but does not necessarily provide the best method for creating customer inspiration (or the best product overview page), i.e. the purchase intention.
