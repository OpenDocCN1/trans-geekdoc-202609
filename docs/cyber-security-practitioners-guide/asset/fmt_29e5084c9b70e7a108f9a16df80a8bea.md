
One of the biggest concerns for organisations currently is the BYOD policy. BYOD allows employees to bring their own devices to work and to use them for professional purposes. Most companies allow the use of personal devices within the premises because this saves money. Allowing users to purchase their own devices makes a simpler network infrastructure (commonly wireless-based) a possibility, reducing the stress of IT administrators regarding network and endpoint management (`McKinney` and `Pickard`, 2016). Employees feel more comfortable increasing the productivity of the organisation by having the most up-to-date mobile devices and are able to continue working outside of work hours. Years ago, organisations were adapted to the Use What You Are Told (UWYT) principle. Electronic devices were provided by the company and were also customized according to their requirements and organisational goals (`Wainwright`, 2018). However, nowadays the world is facing an era when “millennials” are modifying the business landscape by having their personal devices connected at any time and at any place. These days, students who are still in high school already have the most up-to-date mobile devices, which are linked to their social media accounts. Moreover, they say that they do not have any concerns about sharing their personal information on the Internet. However, this is worrying for security experts because of the risks associated with this mindset. Security experts aim to train people to reduce risks based on social engineering techniques that could compromise the organisation’s productivity along with its prestige (`Thomson`, 2012). This is easily achieved in big organisations because they have the budget, governance frameworks, policies and controls to achieve compliance. However, this is not the case for SMEs, which often lack skilled cyber security personnel (`McKinney` and `Pickard`, 2016).

The BYOD policies implemented in most organisations are imprecise and immature in terms of security. This chapter analyses whether BYOD complies with the recently implemented GDPR, or whether it seems likely to cause trouble and concern for IT administrators and security experts, who need to ensure regulatory compliance. In addition, it shows the importance of the human factor when it comes to security and privacy. The rest of the chapter is divided as follows: [Section 7.2](#chapter7.xhtml#sec7.2) gives the reader a brief description of BYOD, along with the architecture commonly used in enterprise networks for personal devices, and the security concerns surrounding BYOD, [Section 7.3](#chapter7.xhtml#sec7.3) analyses the impact of GDPR on modern BYOD policies, [Section 7.4](#chapter7.xhtml#sec7.4) suggests practices and guidelines for achieving endpoint security in personal devices, [Section 7.5](#chapter7.xhtml#sec7.5) proposes a framework for the design, implementation and monitoring of a BYOD policy, and also discusses the importance of governance in achieving GDPR compliance regardless of the size of the organisation, and finally [Section 7.6](#chapter7.xhtml#sec7.6) concludes this chapter.

### 7.2.BYOD Architecture, Security Status and Challenges

BYOD started gaining popularity since 2009, and it is considered as a solution for business environments. It entails organisations allowing employees the use of their own personal devices for professional purposes. It is common nowadays that people interpret the phrase “personal devices” to refer to smartphones, laptops, tablets, etc. (`Eslahi`, 2014).

Years ago, before the massive growth of internet-based services, organisations used to provide personal devices to employees. This was known as *Use What You Are Told* (UWYT), and these devices were meant to be used only for professional duties. Computers were purchased and customized to help employees perform their duties and contribute to the accomplishment of the business’s goals. Furthermore, taking these devices outside the organisation was not allowed, making it harder for malicious attackers to steal personal data, because Internet use was not as frequent at that time.

Nowadays, because of the Internet revolution, it is easy to appreciate the fact that employees purchase their own devices for both personal and professional motives. Organisations have limited budgets, and therefore the BYOD policy has been implemented, allowing employees to use personal devices that satisfy their tastes. Likewise, network administration became easier for IT admins because it only requires the creation of configured network points at employees’ desks which adhere to the internal policies adopted by the organisation. In addition, more Access Points (APs) have been implemented in such a way that personnel can connect their wireless devices to the corporate network. Furthermore, these mobile devices are being used to perform professional duties during the commute between the organisation and any other destination and vice versa (`Wainwright`, 2018).

[Figure 7.1](#chapter7.xhtml#fig7.1) shows the general architecture for BYOD. It is also an overview of how personal devices get connected to a corporate network. The lower layer is the access network, where personal devices can access Internet services through Wi-Fi networks which can be personal or public networks. Likewise, personal devices can get Internet access using mobile networks. The transport network is known as the Internet, where ISPs control the traffic routing, and end users cannot take control of their information because in this layer the data is being transmitted. And finally, the corporate network is where the main servers are allocated and policies from organisations are enforced.

The implementation of a BYOD policy seen from the technical perspective simplifies the network architecture, infrastructure implementation and management. Moreover, business software comes along with this solution to ease the network management for IT administrators (e.g. `Active Directory`). Analysing BYOD from the Human Resource (HR) perspective, it has been noted that employees are more satisfied as this solution allows them to engage completely with their professional roles, reducing stress, increasing productivity and therefore improving the organisation’s finances. Moreover, they do not have to budget for the purchase of devices that adhere to their internal policies (`Ghosh` *et al*., 2013). When their personal devices are used at work, professional staff can perform their tasks outside the office as long as they have internet connectivity. In addition, allowing the use of personal devices within the organisation attracts applicants who enjoy the flexibility offered. Nowadays, the “millennial” generation are more engaged with smartphones and use the Internet more frequently. High school and college students who are not afraid to share their information on the Internet are looking for flexible job opportunities.
