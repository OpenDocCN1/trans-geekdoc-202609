
19.`Sony` warned that users of its PlayStation network might have had their personal information, including credit card details, stolen (Cellan-Jones, 2011). Sony was hacked due to weak passwords. The 2015 hack was extremely sophisticated and used Zero-day malware and sophisticated exfiltration methods. 77 million accounts were compromised and Sony paid $8 m to the affected employees (BBC, 2015).

20.`TalkTalk` was hacked by a teenager on 21 October 2015 (TalkTalk, 2016). 156,959 account details were taken including 21,000 bank details and email addresses. The leak cost TalkTalk £42 m and they were fined £400,000. The cause was SQL injection (ICO, 2016).

21.`Target Stores` — 110 million accounts were hacked in 2013 including 40 million credit card details. Federal law enforcement later discovered 11 GB of data from Target that the hackers had transferred to a temporary server (Smith, 2014).

22.`Tesco` — Cyber attackers exploited deficiencies in Tesco Bank’s design of its debit card, its financial crime controls and its Financial Crime Operations Team to carry out the attack. Those deficiencies left Tesco Bank’s personal current account holders vulnerable to the cyber attackers for over 48 hours and netted the cyber attackers £2.26 m. Tesco was fined £16.4 m on 1 October 2018 (FCA, 2018).

23.`Ticketmaster` — 40,000 people were affected over a period from February to June 2018. Ticketmaster revealed that the login information, payment data, addresses, names and telephone numbers of users were at risk. It would turn out that Ibenta Technologies, who operate a chatbot on Ticketmaster’s site, had modified a line of JavaScript code to customise its basic product to its client’s needs. Ticketmaster then used this code (without Ibenta’s knowledge) on its payments page. Hackers discovered this script, and then modified it to extract payment information, harvesting user information from February 2018 onwards (Wired, 2018).

24.`Uber` was hacked in 2016. Uber’s chief security officer Joe Sullivan was fired for concealing the October 2016 breach. Uber paid hackers $100,000 to delete data and keep the breach quiet (Wong, 2017). Personal information from 56 million accounts was accessed. Hackers managed to access Uber’s accounts on Amazon Web Services by hacking access credentials. The data was unencrypted.

25.`US Office of Personnel Management` — 22 million records were stolen in 2012. The hack was due to a compromised third party (OPM, undated).

26.`Yahoo` — 1 billion account details were stolen (Williams 2017). Yahoo notified users in December 2016 of the breach that occurred in 2014. Farivar (2018) highlights the seriousness of hacking for hire, and how an administrative tool was used to reset passwords for targeted accounts.

The accounts affected by the large data leaks have been collated into a database where one can check using an email address what leaks the email address may have been involved in. The site is run by Troy Hunt and is found at [https://haveibeenpwned.com/](https://haveibeenpwned.com/).

### 1.1.2.*Overview of the high-profile leaks*

Looking at the high-profile attacks earlier, it is evident that only a few involved a sophisticated attack method (CitiBank, eBay and Sony). The majority of the attacks were due to SQL injection, deception and carelessness from employees and vulnerabilities due to unpatched systems. Deloitte, eBay and TalkTalk were all supposedly audited to ISO27001:2013; eBay and TalkTalk would have been PCI DSS compliant too. With so many of the systems examined not being patched correctly, the companies were not complying with the standards in practice despite having been audited.

Many of the attacks listed involved insiders. Of those that were outright hacks, the main method of attack was SQL injection. Organisations that have public web servers that interact with SQL servers are therefore particularly vulnerable.

Not all the exfiltration methods used in the listed attacks have been determined. Had there been a secured and properly patched system, only a few would have been successful.

### 1.1.3.*High profile leaks and insider threat*

The Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report shows that 75% of data exfiltration attacks were from external hackers and 25% from internal actors (Verizon, 2017), which is slightly at odds with the attacks listed, in which internal involvement is seen to have been more prevalent. The Anthem, Appleby, Bupa, eBay, Google, MyHeritage, RSA and Uber attacks all appear to involve insiders, either due to lack of diligence or dishonesty. Techniques that reduce the threat from insiders are therefore important to discuss. Insiders have access to systems and a user with a moderate level of technical expertise will have knowledge of internal systems and their architecture. There are a lot of psychological reasons why an employee may steal data and much has been written on this subject, notably “The CERT Guide to Insider Threats” (Cappelli and Moore, 2012).

*“Insider IT Sabotage is typically committed by technical users with privileged access, such as system administrators, database administrators, and programmers. The motivation in those crimes is usually revenge for a negative workplace event, and the crimes are often set up while still employed but executed following termination.*

*Insider theft of intellectual property (IP) is usually committed by scientists, engineers, programmers, and salespeople. The insiders usually steal the information they worked on and take it with them as they leave the organization to start their own business, take with them to a new job, or give foreign government or organization.*

*Insider fraud is usually committed by lower-level employees such as help desk, customer service, and data entry clerks. The crimes are motivated by financial need or greed, and they typically continue for a long time. Many of these insiders are recruited by outsiders to steal information. Collusion with other insiders is very common in crimes involving modification of information for payment from the outside”.*

It is clear from this quote from Cappelli and Moore (2012) that Risk Managers and HR departments need to work closely with IT departments to ensure an effective Information Governance security framework. Staff are moved from department to department gaining an ever-increasing knowledge of a company’s systems. Rotation of job duties and responsibilities is seen as a form of security control in many large organisations in that it exposes bad practices, but also familiarises the rogue employee with greater knowledge of a company’s systems and its vulnerabilities. Although job rotation and compulsory two-week holidays are standard methods that organisations use to expose fraud, build skills and provide variety, it is questionable whether these practices improve security. Office practices are clearly very different to military security practices. In the military, information is conveyed on a need-to-know basis.

### 1.1.4.*What exfiltration methods are possible on a corporate network?*

Although in practice malware and botnets might be used to exfiltrate data, one would expect these to be picked up by an antivirus scanner. Unwanted applications that come with new machines are removed as a matter of course and Potentially Unwanted Applications (PUAs) and the payloads that are downloaded due to Zero-day exploits are prevented from being installed by restricted access rights. Even so it must be assumed that there is still the possibility that some malware might get installed and not detected. Once installed, malware could provide the means to enable a command and control exploit like the Kali BEEF exploit and subsequently exfiltrate data.
