
New electronic devices which can connect to `Internet of Things` (IoT) networks are being produced in great quantities. Mobile devices play a crucial role in managing these personal networks (e.g. autonomous cars, smart home devices). Currently, constrained devices on an IoT network tend to be in the form of hardware and are normally managed by smartphones using wireless communication (i.e., Bluetooth, ZigBee, Wi‑Fi). However, this great technological advancement comes with minimal or no security features because of its electronical limitations, and thus, creates new opportunities for attackers to penetrate computer systems and succeed in causing personal identifiable information (PII) breaches, which in turn cause financial, technological and reputational damage to organisations. In addition, due to the increased use of mobile devices, organisations are dealing with the fact that confidential information is often delivered to devices that IT departments cannot manage properly, exposing the organisation to data leakage, theft and failure to achieve regulatory compliance. Attackers will try both simple and sophisticated tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs) to achieve their malicious objectives. Mobile devices will still be exposed to simple threats such as keyloggers, malware infection and vulnerability exploitation methodologies that could lead to the attacker gaining unauthorized access, compromising the user, the supply chain of the organisation he / she works in, the reputation of the organisation and the customer’s trust in the organisation. The next sections will discuss the impact of the European GDPR on BYOD environments, followed by guidelines aimed at teaching users how to protect their personal devices. Finally, a framework to enhance the security posture of organisations committed to the acceptance and use of mobile devices will be discussed.

### 7.3.Impact of GDPR on BYOD Architectures

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is designed to enable individuals to better control their personal data. It is hoped that these modernised and unified rules will allow businesses to make the most of the opportunities created by the Digital Single Market by reducing regulation and benefiting from reinforced consumer trust. Privacy and data protection are fundamental rights protected by EU law. Privacy refers to the part of our lives that we as individuals want to control and keep to ourselves. The right to privacy is an umbrella concept that contains many meanings; it protects our communications, homes, bodies, lives, dignity, freedom, liberty and autonomy so that we can develop intimate relationships with others and achieve personal prosperity and wellbeing.

In the EU Fundamental Rights of Privacy which are covered in Article 7 of the Charter Fundamental Rights of the European Union, the respect for private and family life, home and communications is emphasised. Today, when ICT is increasingly being used, data collection and processing has become an important factor and a way of life. The Right to Data Protection was therefore developed to protect individuals from potential harm due to data abuse and misuse. In the EU, the Right to Data Protection is covered by Article 8 of the Charter; this article states that everyone has the right to the protection of their personal data. The right to the protection of personal data is furthermore enshrined in Article 16 of the treaty on the functioning of the European Union. In the digital age, new technology has been widely deployed in all aspects of life and many of these new technologies can invade privacy if they are misused or abused. They may cause harm to individuals or society even without us knowing it. However, we have become more and more dependent on such privacy‑invading technologies and on the ever‑growing data processing. Of course, data ownership, transparency and auditability will be one of the major challenges for EU citizens. In this regard, it is important to clarify individual rights that citizens are entitled to under EU Law. For this very reason, the GDPR has provided us with a robust legal compliance system (Shu and Jahankhani, 2017).

The GDPR requires that personal data be securely processed. This is not a new obligation when it comes to data protection, of course. However, the GDPR’s “appropriate technical and organisational measures”, which have been previously addressed under the Data Protection Act of 1998 (the 1998 Act), provides more specifics on what you have to do to securely process data and how to assess information risk so that appropriate security measures can be put in place. This is an important element when it comes to BYOD as it is now a legal requirement.

Article 5(1)(f) of the GDPR is concerned with the “integrity and confidentiality” of personal data and explains that data should be “processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, using appropriate technical or organisational measures”.

Organisations are required to refer to the GDPR’s “security principle”, which is concerned with the broad concept of information security. The “security principle” requires that the appropriate security is put in place to prevent the personal data you hold being accidentally or deliberately compromised. This is important when it comes to BYODs. It is vital to know that whilst “information security” is sometimes interpreted to mean “cybersecurity”, which involves the protection of networks and information systems from attacks, it also covers other aspects including physical and organisational security measures. Therefore, it is important to consider the “security principle” together with Article 32 of the GDPR, which discusses the specifics of the security of your processing. Article 32(1) states that; “taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation and the nature, scope, context and purposes of processing as well as the risk of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk” (Shu and Jahankhani, 2017).

In addition to the six data protection principles, the GDPR Article 5(2) has “the principle of accountability”. According to this principle, the controller shall be responsible for compliance with the principles listed in Article 5(1) of the GDPR and shall be able to demonstrate its compliance with them. With so many rights given to data subjects and so much accountability on data controllers under GDPR, organisations in the EU and organisations outside the EU which process the data of EU citizens are required to keep all the compliance boxes checked.

Further areas to look out for when implementing the GDPR are as follows:

•Incidents: The GDPR will now make it a legal duty to report any incidents to the ICO within 72 hours. This legislates similarly to current working arrangements of contacting the ICO in a similar time period.

The potential financial penalties could be very high for incidents in which the regulations were not adhered to. Article 83 states the general conditions for imposing administrative fines. Paragraphs 4 and 5 state the limits of administrative fines that potentially can be issued (this is dependent on member state approval of what the maximum fine will be). The maximum fines for failing to comply with the regulations could potentially be 20 million Euros or 4 % of the organisation’s total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year.
