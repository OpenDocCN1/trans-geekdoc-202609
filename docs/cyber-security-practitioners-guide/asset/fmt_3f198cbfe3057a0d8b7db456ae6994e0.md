
# Ethics is a branch of philosophy that takes years of study to master and covers many different aspects from meta-ethics to normative ethics, which consists of areas such as virtue ethics, deontology, hedonism and consequentialism, to applied ethics consisting of areas such as bioethics, machine ethics and military ethics to moral psychology and descriptive ethics.

Clearly this section does not have sufficient space to deal with this level of philosophical ethics. Rather the section deals with ethics in a pragmatic way and looks at how policies and codes of conduct can provide support and guidance for organisations that are looking to employ autistic people in cyber security roles.

Embedding ethical and supportive governance processes into the business strategy may encourage relevant business units and employees to acknowledge and commit to the correct treatment of and engagement with their non-neurotypical workmates, thus establishing a set of guiding ethical principles for the organisation and workforce. This can also be considered as forming part of the governance processes established in an organisation. As such this would indicate that the Board and the executives of an organisation have responsibility for ensuring that ethical processes are embedded into the organisation’s culture.

Valentine and Godkin state that introducing ethical policies and continuing ethical training (specific to an employee’s role) by developing and implementing Corporate Social Responsibility processes will produce positive practices in the workforce and lead to higher retention rates and employee satisfaction (Valentine and Godkin, 2016).

Implementing policies and codes of conduct in a given field or organisation will provide guidance as to how organisations expect their staff to work in an ethical or moral way. However, this does not mean that the ethical requirements have been fully met if the relevant philosophers or experts have not thoroughly debated and proven the various aspects.

The approach of implementing codes of conduct in a given field or environment also ignores potential issues due to geopolitical or ideological differences on the international scene.

When considering a code of conduct for autistic people and their employers working in the field of cyber security one needs to consider autonomy and whether the participants fully understand the ethical requirements they are agreeing to. There needs to be an element of responsibility that will remain with those who authorise the activity. After all it has been established, in this research, that it is not possible to attach ethical considerations to the security controls and countermeasures that constitute cyber security.

It is likely that many of the autistic people who apply to work in cyber security roles will be high-functioning autists and will have the capacity to legally agree to adhere to a code of conduct but there may well be some people with special isolated skills or perceptual peaks that are able to carry out specific duties in a role, especially when it is an area that the autistic person enjoys and is focussed on, who may not have the capacity to make the required ethical declaration.

The codes of ethics published by ISACA (ISACA, 2018) and ISC² (ISC2, 2018) are excellent exemplars for information security and cyber security-based organisations but they do not provide adequate guidance when one considers that some of the people employed in the industry may be non-neurotypical and may not have the capacity to legally agree to requirements.

This section does not aim to address ethics from a philosophical perspective but from a layman’s viewpoint to try to determine how ethical considerations can support autistic people in finding employment in cyber security roles and how employers can engage equitably with potential new employees who are on the autistic spectrum.

Ethics is similar to the Hippocratic Oath requirements that physicians are constrained by — do no harm, respect the individual or group and act in an ethical way.

When ethics is considered from a practical standpoint the situation becomes more complicated; many differing arguments are put forward by interested stakeholders leading to complex issues that should be addressed by each individual stakeholder. Researchers have identified four specific categories of ethics that may pertain to autists and their social and employment interactions — personal, professional, regulatory and tangible (Brosnan *et al*., 2013).

The complexity of ethics could mean that organisations and their employees will pay lip service to ethical requirements and proceed with a nod towards laws and perceived morals, that is, trying to do the right thing. Whilst they may be well-intentioned, the potential for exploitation is apparent.
