
To address the above research problem, Montasari (2016a) developed a comprehensive model, the Comprehensive Digital Forensic Investigation Process Model (the CDFIPM), which the Standardised Digital Forensic Investigation Process Model (the SDFIPM) forms the middle part of. The CDFIPM, which encompasses the entire DIP, is formal in that it synthesizes, harmonises and extends the existing models, and also generic in that it can be applied in the different fields of law enforcement, commerce and incident response. The focus of this study is only on the middle part of the CDFIPM, namely the proposed SDFIPM. To this end, we first introduce and discuss the SDFIPM prior to presenting the results of the Demonstration and Evaluation activities applied to the model.

### 9.2.Background to the Previous DFIPMs

Prior to the design and development of the SDFIPM, we performed a critical review of the previous models followed by an assessment of each of the models against three different sets of assessment criteria (Montasari, 2016a), including: the Daubert Test (Farrell, 1993) Five-Point Requirement, the Carrier and Spafford (2003) Five-Point Requirement and the Beebe and Clark (2005) Four-Point Requirement. The aim of this critical analysis was to gain an in-depth insight into the previous models and also to determine which could contribute to the proposed model. Since law enforcement, commerce and incident response were the three environments on which our research focussed, previous models within those three domains which most closely met the assessment criteria were considered for their possible contributions to the new model. Adams (2012) has adopted a similar approach in which he assesses the previous DFIPMs against the Daubert Test and Carrier and Spafford Five-Point Requirements.

Our approach for assessing the previous DFIPMs was considered important (Kohn *et al*., 2013; Cohen, 2012; Cohen, 2010) as any model institutionalized through subsequent intellectual discourse and practical use must take into account other researchers’ perspectives, approaches and “vernacular”. Having critically reviewed and assessed the previous models (Montasari, 2016a; Montasari, 2016c; Montasari, 2016e), our findings revealed that these models had often been developed by DFPs based on their own personal experience and on an ad hoc basis without any consideration of how standardization should be established within the field (Valjarevic and Venter, 2015). It was further revealed that this had prevented the establishment of formal processes that were urgently needed by courts of law (Montasari, 2016a; Montasari *et al*., 2015). As [Table 9.1](#chapter9.xhtml#tab9.1) (Montasari *et al*., 2015) clearly demonstrates, the previous DFIPMs display significant disparities in terms of the number of phases, the scope and the specific domains that they have been developed for. As a result, these models have often been criticised for being too specific (Carrier and Spafford, 2003; Reith *et al*., 2002), too high level (Beebe and Clark, 2005), too broad (Rogers *et al*., 2006), too technical (Venter, 2006) and too complex (Selamat *et al*., 2008). Therefore, the proposed model was developed to address the shortcomings of the previous models. It was demonstrated that an implementation of our model would be of immediate value to both DFPs and courts of law.

### 9.3.Research Methodology

The Design Science Research (DSR) methodology, widely adopted in the domain of Information Systems (IS) (Hevner and Chatterjee, 2010; Peffers *et al*., 2006; Hevner *et al*., 2004; March and Smith, 1995; Walls *et al*., 1992; Nunamaker *et al*., 1990), was selected as the methodology to conduct this research. The main aim of DSR is to achieve or develop knowledge and understanding of a problem domain by building and applying a designed artefact (Van Aken, 2005; Hevner *et al*., 2004). DSR involves the design of novel or innovative artefacts and the analysis of the performance or use of such artefacts (Watts *et al*., 2009; Kuechler and Vaishnavi, 2008). The development and evaluation of artefacts form an important part in DSR (Hevner *et al*., 2004; March and Storey, 2008). Artefacts include, amongst others: models, methods, constructs, instantiations and design theories (March and Storey, 2008; March and Smith, 1995), social innovations, and new or previously unknown properties of technical, social or informational resources (March and Storey, 2008). The artefact related to this research is a new model, the SDFIPM, which encompasses the middle part of the larger model, the CDFIPM. The organisational context associated with the research is that of law enforcement, commerce and incident response. Our research also addresses the hitherto unsolved problem that there does not exist a DFIPM that is formal in that it synthesizes, harmonises and extends the existing models, and that is generic in that it can be employed in the different fields of law enforcement, incident response and commerce. Our adoption of the DSR methodology as a research methodology in a DF study is informed by Adams (2012), who originally applied the DSR methodology to a study in DF.

The selection of the DSR methodology over other alternative methodologies such as the Grounded Theory (GT) methodology or Requirements Engineering (RE) methodology was due to its relevance to the task of design and the subsequent evaluation of a new process model. Hence, because this study also has at its focus the design, development and subsequent evaluation of the SDFIPM, the DSR methodology was considered to be the most appropriate choice. It is also preferable as it is more pragmatic than alternatives such as the Explanatory Science Research (ESR) methodology \[46\] and as it can be used to solve a problem for which no solution has already been found (Peffers *et al*., 2006; Hevner *et al*., 2004). Therefore, since this research is a problembased study, again the DSR methodology was deemed the most appropriate methodology. Moreover, the DSR methodology uses both inductive and deductive processes. Since our original study, from which this paper is derived, is aimed at producing a new knowledge and literature (through the inductive process), as well as testing its two research hypotheses (through the deductive process), once again the DSR methodology was considered the most appropriate choice.
