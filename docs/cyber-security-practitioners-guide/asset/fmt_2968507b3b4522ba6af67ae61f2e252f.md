
The high retention rates in employment for autistic people, identified through research, tend to indicate that providing support services is beneficial from a social, psychological and financial (both for the employee and the government — through a reduction in overall costs) viewpoint.

## 12.1.4.Transition

Transitioning from secondary school to further education and employment is recognised as a major milestone for autistic people in socially advanced countries around the world (Westbrook *et al*., 2015).The outcome is that autistic people achieve independence and employment.

It is recommended that these outcome-oriented transition services are made available as early as possible, with some researchers suggesting provision from the age of 14. This allows for longer individual preparation and could help to identify areas where further specific support is needed (Cimera *et al*., 2013). This research highlighted the increased wages and lowered costs for people who got early access to transition services.

## 12.1.5.Assistive technology

In recent years there has been an exponential growth in technology that can aid autistic people in improving life skills. These technologies include picture guiding, audio assistance, video modelling, virtual reality and robotics. Each of these technologies has been utilised in training autistic people to improve their social skills (DiGennaro Reed *et al*., 2011; Hopkins *et al*., 2011).

Internet-based training has proven effective in improving social skills relevant to employment, e.g. job searches and interviews. There is a programme that uses virtual reality to allow autistic youths to practice interview techniques. This has proven to be significantly effective (Strickland *et al*., 2013).

The use of technology to help people with disabilities has been proven across a range of physical and cognitive issues. Support can comprise of calendar entries to remind them of appointments or tasks that need to be completed. Communication can be improved through socialisation apps that ease misinterpretation between colleagues. Programs can be loaded onto tablets that allow for relaxation or rest when a situation is becoming stressful.

## 12.1.6.Cyber security

From the author’s perspective, the term cyber security refers to the security controls and countermeasures deployed at ingress/egress points in a network (or where network traffic passes a boundary) and where a resource requires greater protection. These security controls and countermeasures can be policies, processes, tools, software, hardware and firmware.

As such, these security controls and countermeasures are incapable of making ethical decisions and it is the human individual that has the responsibility of deciding whether any activity is ethically acceptable and should therefore proceed.

The advent of machine learning and artificial intelligence may render this stance as untenable in the future but at present both machine learning and artificial intelligence are driven by sophisticated algorithms and are not yet capable of making ethical choices.

*“The definition of computer ethics has various interpretations. One of the most oft-cited definitions is from James Moor: “A typical problem in computer ethics arises because there is a policy vacuum about how computer technology should be used. Computers provide us with new capabilities and these in turn give us new choices for action. Often, either no policies for conduct in these situations exist or existing policies seem inadequate. A central task of computer ethics is to determine what we should do in such cases — that is, to formulate policies to guide our actions.”* (Dittrich, *et al*., 2011)

The authors have been unable to find evidence of any prior research or published articles that specifically discuss ethics and cyber security in relation to security controls and countermeasures, although there is research available that examines how people use programs and malware to conduct attacks against targets. There is also a growing library examining ethics and cyber warfare.

To illustrate the point about cyber security elements being incapable of ethical decisions Stuxnet will be reviewed.

Langner states that Stuxnet *“marked a turning point in the history of cyber security — the arrival of the first cyber warfare weapon ever, known as Stuxnet. Not only was Stuxnet much more complex than any other piece of malware seen before, it also followed a completely new approach that’s no longer aligned with conventional confidentiality, integrity, and availability thinking. Contrary to initial belief, Stuxnet wasn’t about industrial espionage: it didn’t steal, manipulate, or erase information. Rather, Stuxnet’s goal was to physically destroy a military target — not just metaphorically, but literally.”* (Langner, 2011)

Here we have a software program specifically written to attack a given target. In and of itself the program is incapable of determining the ethical correctness of its purpose. The responsibility lies solely with those people that authorised its development and use.

Some commentators view Stuxnet as the first ethically developed cyber weapon in that it was designed to attack a given target with no collateral damage expected (Lucas, 2017).

There are various threat actors driving attacks against target organisations or individuals. Target organisations are constituent parts in the following fields: government, financial services, utilities, manufacturing, technical, military, security services, etc.

Threat actors can include sovereign states (Gutmann, 2010), intelligence organisations, hacktivists (Warren and Leitch, 2016), organised crime, hackers, etc.

Drivers for attacks from these disparate groups could be state espionage, corporate espionage, financial gain, political gain, publicity, fun, challenge, etc.

Each threat actor will have a different ethical stance or rationalisation for their activities. Each ethical decision will be made by a responsible individual and not by the tool employed to conduct the attack.

Ethics in cyber security has gained a greater meaning due to the diverse nature of the internet and the difficulties faced by any government in trying to prosecute threat actors based in another country, particularly when the threat actors may be part of the host nation’s intelligence services, and especially when the two nations may be geopolitically or ideologically opposed.

In 2015, the Office of Personnel Management, the US federal government’s human resources organisation, discovered it had been hacked and that 22 million records had been stolen; allegedly by Unit 61398, part of China’s People’s Liberation Army’s cyber espionage division (Koerner, 2016). To date, no evidence is available that the stolen data has been used maliciously. Which begs the question, “Why was the data stolen?” Could it be that the attack was a proof of ability or a warning from the Chinese to the Americans or an act of state cyber bullying?

Spoofing also presents issues in identifying a threat actor. If the attacker is able to effectively disguise the source of the attack or implicate another person or organisation, then innocent entities could be accused. This spoofing also allows states, organisations and individuals to claim innocence due to being framed. Plausible deniability, as Americans say.

In the case of the alleged attack on Sony Pictures by North Korea in 2014, Reuters reported *that “the results of a linguistic analysis of communications from the suspected hackers suggest they were more likely from Russia than North Korea”* (Hosenball and Finkle, 2014). In either case, neither country is likely to admit responsibility or extradite any potential suspects, due to the aforementioned geopolitical and ideological differences.
