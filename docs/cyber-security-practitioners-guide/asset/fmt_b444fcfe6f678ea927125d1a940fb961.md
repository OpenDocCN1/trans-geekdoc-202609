
Based upon the results of the Examination Process, DFPs must now be able to define what the exact characteristics of the incident are and who is to be held accountable for the incident. The Analysis Process enables investigators to reconstruct fragments of data based on their significance and determine a possible root cause of the incident (Montasari, 2016a; Montasari *et al*., 2015). Once DFPs have addressed what is possibly known from the digital evidence (Montasari *et al*., 2015), they must now be able to formulate a hypothesis of how the incident took place. To do so, they need to reconstruct a sequence of events which has resulted in the current state of the system under investigation. Investigators must thoroughly analyse and test data that was organised in the Examination Process against the hypothesis that they have formulated. The legal validity of the possible digital evidence must also be questioned by considering issues such as relevance, admissibility and weight as discussed in Montasari (2017b), Montasari (2016a) and Kessler (2010). Digital evidence should then be linked and attributed to the root cause of the incident by correlating the results of the digital crime scene with physical evidence. For instance, DFPs might need to correlate data center access logs to logins, linking online chat activities found on the computer with the activity of an undercover officer, and correlating activity on a compromised server with activity on the suspect’s home system and network activity recorded by an ISP. Subsequent to the attribution, investigators need to evaluate their findings to ensure that their hypothesis holds true. At this stage of the Investigative Process, backtracking from the Analysis Process to the Examination Process is often to be expected as the investigators acquire a better understanding of the root cause of the incident. The output of the Analysis Process becomes the input to the Interpretation Process, discussed in the next subsection.

### 9.4.3.*Interpretation process*

The main purpose of the Interpretation Process is to use scientifically proven methods to explain facts discovered throughout the Analysis Process within the context of the investigation (Montasari, 2016a; Montasari, 2016b; Palmer, 2001). During this process, investigators need to interpret the digital evidence to produce meaningful statements in the legal context for later reporting and presentation. They must be able to reconstruct the events associated with the digital investigation by using the results of the analysis techniques from the Analysis Process to put together the pieces of the digital puzzle so that an accurate reconstruction of events can be carried out. The interpretation of any evidence should depend on the available information regarding the circumstances surrounding the creation of that item of digital evidence (Valjarevic and Venter, 2015; ISO, 2015). DFPs must obtain information from individuals involved in the day-to-day operation of the system under investigation so that they can carry out a more effective interpretation of evidence. Moreover, investigators must consider information concerning the goal as well as the scope of the investigation. In cases where the contextual information changes, investigators might also need to change the interpretation so that it can reflect any such changes regarding the contextual information. DFPs must also utilise link analysis and timeline tools to assist them with the digital reconstruction. The interpreted evidence then needs to be classified and evaluated in order to ascertain the amount of trust that can be placed in it. The interpreted digital evidence should also be organised according to relevance in such a way that more important digital evidence can be differentiated from others. After completing this process, DFPs should now be able to determine how the digital evidence came into existence and what its presence denotes. The output of the Interpretation Process becomes the input to the Event Reconstruction Process, discussed in the next section.

### 9.4.4.*Event reconstruction process*

During this process DFPs need to reconstruct a possible event sequence which reflects the incident result as accurately as possible. In order to reconstruct the events, investigators must utilise the series of events that they have deduced from digital evidence. Event Reconstruction should not be established as factual; instead, DFPs should use this Process to explain how the incident might have taken place. During this Process, the investigation findings must be consolidated and refined prior to assessing the review results against the original hypothesis, which was formulated in the Analysis Process. This will be to determine whether the DFPs have acquired all the evidence required to support the original hypothesis. If all the evidence has not been captured, investigators will need to backtrack to the Examination Process, in which this Phase and subsequent Phases form a cycle that needs to be repeated until the DFPs can identify additional evidence and explain the incident. As an example, in cases where a server intrusion has taken place, this iteration involves DFPs linking the exploitation of a service that is open to attack with the installation of a rootkit and utilization of a network sniffer. If the source IP address of the network connections is investigated, this could result in the acquisition of additional digital evidence to examine. If there is no need to iterate to the Examination Process, DFPs must identify and address any areas of improvement. The output of the Event Reconstruction Process becomes the input to the Reporting Process, discussed in the next subsection.

### 9.4.5.*Reporting process*

DFPs need to compile, write and print out on paper a detailed and concise report during the Reporting Process. This report must contain conclusions that can be reproduced by independent third parties. Forensic reports that include opinions based on accurately documented digital sources are much more likely to withstand judicial scrutiny than opinions based on less reliable sources (Garrie, 2014). The report must contain the findings of the entire DIP in addition to the information about documentation, chain of custody, digital evidence preservation, authorisation and management. The report must be accurate, concise and clear and be restricted only to what is known (Beebe and Clark, 2005). Since a DFI might produce many incriminating items of digital evidence, investigators must ensure that all items of evidence are listed so that no valuable item of evidence is left out. The output of the Reporting Process becomes the input to the Presentation Process, discussed in the next subsection.
