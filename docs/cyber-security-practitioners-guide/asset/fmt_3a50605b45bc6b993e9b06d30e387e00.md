
`File backups` — This should be part of any Business Continuity Plan (BCP) and set of Disaster Recovery (DR) procedures so that in case of an attack, if the system is taken down, at least the system can be recovered to the point before the attack.

`Emails, attachments and websites` — As part of their IT Policy, organisations will have guidelines which advise staff not to open any emails or attachments from unknown or suspicious senders. This can also be part of training/awareness programs. Alert reminders can also be created to warn staff. These alert reminders could be triggered when staff members log on to the system.

`Patching and installing updates` — This is a critical aspect of attack prevention that is the responsibility of the Information and Communications Technology (ICT) department. Patch vulnerability was one of the reasons that some of the NHS locations were exposed to WannaCry.

`Incident Response Team and Preparation` — For example, there should be Service Improvement Plans and tasks concerning fast identification and mitigation allocated to specific team members. The core incident team should practice their response to the situation like they do with BCP and DRP scenarios, but the scenarios should be specific to ransomware attacks.

`Non-payment of ransom` — Admittedly, this is easier said than done, as there will be great pressure on the victim to pay the ransom in order to retrieve the data. It is advisable not to pay the ransom as it is not guaranteed that the decipher process will be released. It may also lead to more requests for ransom or more concerted attacks.

`Regular Training` — This is vital. As Arndt (2017) mentions, “organizations that phish-exercise their staff at least four to eight times a year have dramatically better awareness than organizations that don’t do it or that do it just once or twice a year.”

The most complex part to contain or control in handling cyber security risks is the human element. No matter what amount of training or what training programme is used in organisations to heighten awareness of attacks made by malicious outsiders, it seems evident that social engineering attacks such as phishing will still increase due to human curiosity. Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDPSs) will help, but instead one could add an extra layer of security through blockchain. This would allow high value data (sensitive or personal data) to be protected so that even if attacks have succeeded there are other hurdles that require defeating encryption to overcome before a data breach occurs.

### 6.3.2.*Social engineering: Phishing and spear-phishing*

Phishing and spear-phishing techniques often contribute to the success of ransomware. As ransomware became sophisticated, so too has social engineering. Phishing has been around for some time and until users stop clicking on email from unknown senders or enabling programs in attachments, the attackers will still continue to succeed. In 2016, phishing attacks rose by 65% compared to in 2015, as mentioned by Sharma (2017), and are set to increase further again, with organisations more in the sights of the attackers than individuals. Social media may bring many benefits, but it also offers attackers a wealth of additional information that gives rise to more sophisticated phishing methods and increases the likelihood of a victim opening an infected email/program. Steer (2017) notes the existence of a more dangerous threat known as spear-phishing, where the email is targeted at specific individual employees. Attackers now search social media to make an email look convincingly genuine to mask the tracks of the ransomware. This ‘familiarity’ of appearance to a user makes this method a very dangerous entry point. The even newer dangers of malvertising include malwares that no longer require users to ‘click’ links to activate them.

**Manipulation Methods:** It is interesting to see the evolution/development of new attack methods, but successful entry to the victim’s system relies on methods like phishing or spear-phishing that are activated because victims do not easily change their behavioural patterns. There are some more manipulative means of entry; attackers can use sub-domains, hidden URLs (links hidden under plain text), misspelt URLs (bought domains that look similar to the real site e.g. google.com) and IDN homograph attacks.

**Script-Based Malware:** A McAfee (2017) lab report indicates a rise in malware scripting techniques. JavaScript, VBScript, PHP, Powershell, etc, are used to distribute ransomware, and in some cases, are even used in the ransomware (e.g. Nemucod ransomware uses PHP and JavaScript). Attackers take this route because doing so allows them to evade detection. This is the reason why this newer technique has seen a sharp rise over the last two years (McAfee, 2017).

**Internet of Things (IoT):** Yaqoob *et al*. (2017) discuss how there are expected to be 26 billion sensors and IoT devices by 2020. These will be deployed across wearable devices, connected smart homes, cars, health, utility, etc. On the one hand, it is a great technological advancement, but as Yaqoob *et al*. (2017) have discovered, over 70% of IoT devices are vulnerable to attacks. With the growth of ransom-ware it is inevitable that IoT offers a vast surface attack area: see [Fig. 6.7](#chapter6.xhtml#fig6.7) as per Yaqoob *et al*. (2017). It is important that confidentiality, integrity and availability (CIA) should be preserved in tandem with technological advancement. Safe ways to protect IoT from malware should take priority.

**Botnets and other IoT issues:** Yaqoob *et al*. (2017) state that ransomware can also be penetrated using botnets inside IoT networks. These botnets are usually activated through phishing and may compromise the whole IoT network. The research goes on to say that attackers can inject ransomware-as-a-service into the cloud and the ransomware may appear as a ‘legitimate service’ to the user. More research shows the extent of the potential issues with IoT. Some possible attacks include thermostat hacking (turning the temperature up), Flocker ransomware (which locks the smart TV with malware embedded in a fake movie) and Android Simplocker (which affects Android wearable devices). There have also been some tests on the practicality of ransomware used to attack smart bulbs.

**Malvertising and Ransomware:** There has been a sharp increase in the use of malvertising to propagate malware, specifically ransomware (RiskIQ Research, 2017). This new type of technique threatens the benefits from digital marketing many organisations have legitimately enjoyed. Now, users may block ads for fear that they have become attack vectors for deploying malware. Users need not even click on the malicious ads and can be infected just by visiting the website. This is in itself a new technique that provides infection without user interaction (e.g. the Astrum exploit using a vulnerability in Flash). Palmer (2017) reported that a few UK Universities were hit with this ransomware, but it is very hard to consolidate the extent of the attacks as many education and government-type organisations resist openly discussing attacks made on them for fear of negative publicity.
