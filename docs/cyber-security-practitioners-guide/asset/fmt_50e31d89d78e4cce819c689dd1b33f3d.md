
The analysis of these datasets show that themes can be identified through the analysis of the keyword metrics. The dataset of religious texts, as expected, showed mainly ideological themes; religious contexts and references. The dataset of tweets showed mainly keywords relating to current events or trending topics. The word frequency with specialisations showed there is a greater repository of context underlying the initial statistics gleaned from the analysis. As a next step for further research, a keyword-in-context type analysis which involves reviewing the words before and after keywords to provide greater context in the results could be a suitable stepping stone to better understand the thematic mechanics of radicalised and exploitative themes, to create a more robust and overarching mechanism and framework to apply in wider targeted thematic analysis.

### 14.5.2.*Legislative and jurisdiction factors interconnected with the definitions of radicalisation and extremism*

Another factor when approaching research into radicalisation, extremism and exploitation is the definition of these terms. Geographical, cultural, political and socio-economic factors can change what language or behaviour would be apportioned in these categories. One of the biggest issues facing legislatures is that of jurisdiction, and the inconsistency of legislation that exists between nation states and jurisdictions. The very nature of social media platforms and their use of the internet is that while a user can be located in one jurisdiction, whereby the language used and behaviour displayed in their social media posts could be deemed acceptable; in another jurisdiction where the same posts are viewed, they may not be. There are also linguistic considerations to be made, in that translations of phrases may not correctly articulate intent or may have lost meaning in translation. For the purposes of this research, the researcher focused on the use of English language posts and translations. This research was also carried out with reference to the Tallinn Manual of Cyber Legislation and previous knowledge held by the researcher of the similarities in the legislation and jurisdiction of the five partner countries in the Five Eyes intelligence network and affiliated agencies. As such, the use of this accepted legislative similarity provided a suitable baseline on which the analysis of the data could be based.

### 14.5.3.*Analysis of the social media platform and policies, and their effect on current and future data capturing*

The Facebook Data Breach occurred when analytical and political consultancy “Cambridge Analytica” made use of user profiles and data misappropriated from the Facebook platform for their own commercial purposes. This saw an overhaul of the privacy policies used by Facebook, as well as increased transparency in the security and privacy settings available to users (Cadwalladr and Graham-Harrison, 2018). Due to Facebook being a closed platform, access to which is granted via a login wall, large data scrapes for the purposes of keyword analytics would prove quite difficult, as the process would rely on the users’ data and posts being posted as public. Anonymised data for academic research was previously available through Facebook and other research organisations with data sharing agreements with the platform; however, the data breach compromised the availability and use of such data.

The implementation of the GDPR has also led to the updating of data retention and privacy policies on such platforms, not only governing the data that is stored by service providers, but also making it more difficult for such information to be shared without an appropriate or proportionate cause. The nature of Twitter, which makes posts or “tweets” public as default, together with the quantity of “tweets” and the availability of the platform, mean that, for analytical purposes such as those described in this paper, large quantities of data can be obtained and utilised.

Many datasets did and do exist in online repositories, forums and exchanges on the dark web; however, their sources have to be authenticated to verify the validity of the research and the results of any analyses undertaken. The researcher found that other research organisations did hold verified datasets and were willing to share them for the purposes of academic research, as so little has been conducted on them.

### 14.5.4.*Review of the collated analytic data, psychological, social and legislative research*

Legislation and government guidelines provide a defined framework as to what actions constitute a criminal offence and can be defined as extremism. The dataset of extremist religious texts provides a baseline or justification for such actions, through ideological themes. What is interesting, however, is that the data collated from the Twitter dataset shows more frequent terms and keywords relating to current or trending topics on Twitter, rather than relating to the ideological themes found in the publications. Many of these tweets had links to external sites, and so, the researcher found that these tweets are using the mechanics of the Twitter platform to their advantage, maximising their outreach by linking tweets to trending topics without directly propagating ideological themes. A methodology to actively identify extremism and radicalisation on social media using keyword metrics is possible, however, further research needs to be conducted into the analysis of similar datasets. While legislation provides a definitive framework, the identification of behavioural patterns requires context to be taken into account during the analysis. A keywords-in-context approach to analysis could provide greater insight into the behaviours displayed through social media posts. However, the nature of Twitter creates a problem for such analysis. While its simplicity, accessibility and availability are reasons for the platform’s popularity, the character-limited posts mean that there will tend to be links to external sites, and the use of automated accounts means that a detailed analysis could prove difficult. Another social media platform, such as Facebook for example, would more likely provide more contextual data for analysis, as users with individual accounts and groups would more likely express views when there is no limitation. However, as discussed here, there is a legislative restriction on what data can be reviewed and analysed. For the purposes of academic research, however, anonymised data samples from the platform could be used for analysis to provide a potential proof of concept.

### 14.6.Conclusions

Whilst the research conducted in this project achieved its defined objectives, there were some limitations that were reinforced through the analyses, although these could be addressed in further research. The concise nature of the Twitter posts at the time of capture was restrictive, with the maximum character count permissible on the platform per tweet being 140 characters. This more recently doubled to 280 characters, so many tweets on Twitter are larger, in turn providing greater contextual data.

This greater depth of context in data could provide fruitful results for future analysis. While this research identified themes and trends expressed in known extremist material and tweets rather than seeking pre-set terms, it only scratches the surface of the behavioural and profiling data that can be gleaned for use in wider intelligence and law enforcement environments.

Verifiable datasets of such a nature for use in an academic environment are difficult to obtain. Many of the accounts that posted the tweets in the dataset were deleted by Twitter for breaching their “Terms and Conditions of Use”, however would reappear under a slight variation on the account name. However, the dataset used by the research accounted for these discrepancies.
