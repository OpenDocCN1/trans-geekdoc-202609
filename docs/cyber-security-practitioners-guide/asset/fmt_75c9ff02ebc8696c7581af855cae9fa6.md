
In theory one has a CERT (Cyber Emergency Response Team) or a BERT (Business Emergency Response Team) on standby should an incident occur. A BERT is an inhouse version of a CERT, so would be familiar with company procedures and will have no conflicts of interest in terms of the confidentiality of the data or services being restored. A CERT, however, being a community response, poses lots of issues in terms of GDPR processor agreements, confidentiality etc.

Should external assistance be part of the recovery plan then a CERT will need to comply with the company’s vetting policy and suitable NDAs will need to be signed. Though a company might not see Computer Forensics as its business, inevitably HR will have to hire for a Computer Forensics department when internal investigations are required. When this happens, it is important to follow the advice of the UK Association of Chief Police Officers and the guidance provided by ENISA (ACPO, 2012). In Computer Forensics, there are four basic rules (Schou and Hernandez, 2014).

1. Investigators must not take on work that is beyond their skill.

2. A log must be kept of how the evidence is preserved from point of collection to court. This is called a Chain of Custody.

3. Evidence must not be degraded. This is called Preservation.

4. Every step of an investigation must be recorded.

Should personal data be put at risk as a resultant incident then the organisation’s GDPR breach reporting procedures will need to be followed and the appropriate supervisory authority notified without undue delay, and not later than 72 hours after the organisation becomes aware of the breach. Article 33 of the GDPR also requires that if the breach is likely to result in a high risk of certain individuals’ rights and freedoms being adversely affected, those individuals must be informed without undue delay, and that the cause of the breach must be identified, and that measures must be put in place to contain the breach and prevent any repetition of the incident. Seeking the advice of the Information Commissioner’s Office when reporting a breach to confirm the response is correct is accepted as expected practice.

Event logs are crucial for determining the cause of a breach. ISO27001 specifies that logs should be maintained for months, as does COBIT 5. Logs can be unmanageable and even a small organisation with a few virtual servers will produce many logs. A SIEM (System Information Event Management) solution that collates logs solves this problem. Splunk goes further and is a SIEM solution that analyses logs in real time for unusual behaviour (Splunk, 2018).

### 1.5.1.*Detecting attacks using honeypots*

Honeypots are a terrific way of detecting that something is not right on a network. The idea is that should an attacker get in then the first thing they would want to do is to scan the network for vulnerabilities. This would normally be a scan of all the IP addresses in the local area network’s IP range and either all the ports or the most likely ports which the attacker can use to hop onto a server. A honeypot will sit on one IP address on the network and will be listening on all ports. Should another device try to connect to the honeypot, an alarm will be triggered and an alert will be sent. Thinkst Canary is a honeypot that can simulate a Windows server or a Web server, and is designed to entice an attacker to investigate further before triggering an alert (Thinkst Canary, 2018).

### 1.5.2.*Detecting attacks by network monitoring*

Darktrace and Snort, which monitor network traffic, are great tools with which to detect anomalies. Traffic monitoring is expensive, though, as the idea of traffic monitoring is to detect anything abnormal on a network. In practice, the setting up of new workstations where large amounts of programs and data move round the network and even Windows updates are good examples of false positives that can trigger an alert, so this method is not without its drawbacks.

### 1.6.Detection of Security Breaches — Overview

One of the issues in comparing security products is that there is no comprehensive dataset to be used for data exfiltration testing, and no generic framework for testing. This is an issue highlighted by Ullah *et al.* (2017).

Al-Bataineh and White (2017) describe how botnets can be used to exfiltrate data using encrypted channels. Interestingly they make the point that rather than providing a problem, encryption can give the attacker away, as a large transfer using encryption could be identified as an anomaly. Zeus is the most common botnet which allows an attacker to manage the botnet remotely to log key strokes and take screen shots. It can even add fields to forms to enable the collection of more information than a form would normally need, and if applied to a banking site might even ask for a social security number or a credit card PIN. Al-Bataineh and White observed that HTTP traffic exchanged by botnets has normal headers; however, the content of POST responses is often random which could assist with detection. Although encryption hides what an attacker is doing, Al-Bataineh and White propose that one should “consider encryption as an opportunity for detection rather than a limitation because if encryption is detected where it is not expected it should be considered an anomaly”.

In an encrypted message the frequency of a byte in a message should be the same for every byte. This is known as Shannon’s Entropy. To maintain throughput when inspecting traffic flows, a manufacturer may decide, having identified a file type upon examining the start of a file, that it is not necessary to examine the rest of the data stream (Shannon and Weaver, 1949).

Deep packet inspection (DPI) of HTTPS packets is another technique that is emerging on some firewalls. This works by decrypting encrypted channels at a firewall then re-encrypting them before they reach the user.

On anything but a small corporate network a proxy server would be expected. This is now a requirement in PCI DSS questionnaire C and the UK Cyber Essentials scheme. Proxy servers are designed to inspect and log data downloaded but not necessarily to detect data uploaded so it is important to ascertain how good they are at detecting and recording data being uploaded. From an attacker’s perspective exfiltrating data using web-based services might appear a risky strategy because requests are likely to be logged but even if access to websites and users and downloads may be logged it does not follow that data being uploaded is being logged.

Anomaly detection can either be host-based or network-based, the first looking for anomalies on the host, the latter looking for anomalies in network traffic. An example of suspicious network traffic would be web use where the upload traffic volume is higher than the download volume. An example of a host-based anomaly could be high levels of network traffic at an unusual time of day (e.g. at night) (Darktrace, 2018).

### 1.6.1.*Black hat, white hat and grey hat hacking*

To check whether vulnerabilities can be exploited, ethical hackers or penetration testers are often employed. They then try to find and exploit vulnerabilities on the basis of some knowledge (grey hat), no knowledge (black hat) or a lot of knowledge (white hat) of the system being tested. It is important that a reputable company is used for this, and, when systems are being tested, that system administrators are on hand should any services hang as a result. Attempting to hack a system without authorisation is a criminal offence, so penetration testing companies must be certain that they have the proper authorisation and that any undesirable consequences are understood before attempting to see if any vulnerabilities can be exploited.
