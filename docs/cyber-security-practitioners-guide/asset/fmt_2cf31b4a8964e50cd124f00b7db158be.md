
The researcher observed that simply understanding each job function was insufficient when constructing a threat model. The building and specific office characteristics are unique to the organisation and this may have a significant impact on the working practices of the contract staff.

As an example, air conditioning engineers would require access to all levels of the building; however, the air conditioning fans and coils are only contained within specific zones in the office. Therefore, it would be acceptable if an air conditioning maintenance engineer was detected for a period of time within that specific zone. However, an additional layer of complexity is introduced as the monitoring of the ambient temperature of the office may require access to other zones within the office environment.

Other considerations must be incorporated into the model, such as the location of toilets and beverage areas that have the potential to become ‘hot-zones’ where staff congregate. The researcher observed that the natural human movement of staff will have an impact on roles within an organisation. These form part of the normal human interactions and therefore must be understood and incorporated into the model to limit false positives (see the section on human interaction patterns).

### 4.4.2.*Zoning*

To ensure the algorithm was able to effectively detect potential threats to the organisation, the proximity data needed to be crossreferenced to specific locations within the office. It was therefore considered important to understand the key business areas and the physical attributes of the building position. This data could be used to position the Bluetooth beacons at optimal locations.

The researcher needed to work with Dakota to understand the key operational areas of the business and specific infrastructure elements that, if compromised, posed a specific threat to the organisation.

### 4.4.3.*Infrastructure features*

Working with Dakota’s Facilities Management team, the researcher was able to identify the building’s critical infrastructure. This included structured cabling wiring closets, electrical cupboards with fuse arrays, air conditioning control units and CCTV locations.

### 4.4.4.*Business areas*

Along with the critical infrastructure, key business locations were identified. Dakota based its teams and departments on specific desks. This policy was adopted to assist staff who could consequently locate a team’s resources in one area when they needed to access them. When the team or department was too large to be positioned on one group of desks, additional desk groups were assigned. This policy made it easier when identifying key locations within the office space.

As an example, Dakota are a financial organisation and employ a team of traders to purchase and sell stocks, shares and bonds from various global trading locations. It is therefore evident that these locations are important to the organisation and should be identified as a key business area.

### 4.4.5.*Locating beacons and aligning with risk areas*

Once the key infrastructure and business areas were identified the Bluetooth beacons could be located in the most effective location, and tuned accordingly.

A key requirement of the threat detection model is the ability to detect a threat based on the proximity, the time and the movement pattern of a contractor. To effectively build the algorithm that can detect potential threats, the researcher had to see the zoning process and the identification of critical areas associated with specific zones as an important element of the research project. The other areas include the electrical riser cupboard, which controls the power to the entire floor, and the Compliance department. The Compliance department contains sensitive client and staff data, which the business has identified as a key area of risk.

### 4.4.7.*Human interaction patterns*

The spatiotemporal patterns of employees and the interactions between them can be considered important when designing a threat detection security model. To fully understand the significance of these patterns and their ultimate impact on the design of the algorithm, the researcher decided to monitor staff movement within the organisation and particularly the interaction between departments. Specific personnel who traverse teams and floors within the building were also identified as this could impact the natural movement pattern of staff.

Whilst the primary resource of this research paper is the data derived through the proximity monitoring solution, to ensure the data can be interpreted effectively it is considered important that the data derived from the natural movement patterns of permanent members of staff that are not under the scope of the proximity monitoring solution must be taken into consideration. Contractors may interact with permanent staff. This is human nature and does not necessarily indicate a threat to the business.
