
Data was collected and processed over a two-month period and used to construct a baseline; however, once the Shadow Cloud application is fully deployed this metric will be updated in real-time.

Using the same margin-of-error metric previously employed when measuring the zone activity, behaviour is considered unusual if the number of visits in a particular day ≥50% of the average day’s zone visits.

### 4.6.9.*Pattern triggers*

The final analysis phase of the anomaly detection process could be considered the most complex. Movement analysis through an open plan office, with limited physical controls, can be challenging.

![image](images/fig4.18.jpg)

**Fig. 4.18.**Movement patterns of Cleaner 8 through multiple zones.

Data recorded by the proximity monitoring solution movement was analysed over a period of two months to identify specific patterns based on the job role and other mitigating factors such as hotspots or desk configurations.

The cleaners were used as an example as they provide a regular stream of data that can be analysed over the baseline period. [Figure 4.18](#chapter4.xhtml#fig4.18) details an extract of data recorded on the proximity monitoring solution over a period of an hour. Movement patterns appear to be controlled, following a predictable path. The cleaner entered the floor at the expected entry point and stayed within adjacent zones until the three spikes in the movement pattern were identified and highlighted. Cross referencing these zones with the floor map can provide some clarity and explains the slight deviations in movement. These do not represent and may not indicate anomalous behaviour.

Using historic pattern analysis, one can detect anomalies that can affect the baseline data. However, subtle changes need to be accommodated so that the risk of false positives is reduced. Historic pattern analysis may not be possible for some roles as the data may not be available due to the nature of the task.

As an example, a plumber may not visit the premises regularly, potentially only when an emergency occurs. They may also require access to all areas within the building to address a water leak.

As part of the experiment, where there was sufficient data available to construct a baseline pattern of movement analysis, data was analysed in an attempt to identify a standard pattern for each role on each floor. [Figure 4.19](#chapter4.xhtml#fig4.19) depicts the movements within three separate days at the same time. It is evident the movement pattern was too random for accurate pattern analysis and could cause too many false positives as there were potentially too many variables to consider when constructing an effective threat model. This was evident when analysing the cleaners, whose movement can be erratic and who may have a legitimate excuse to move freely around an open plan office area.

![image](images/fig4.19.jpg)

**Fig. 4.19.**Pattern analysis over three separate dates.

However, it is possible to construct triggers based on historic pattern analysis when analysing other job functions whose movement is dictated by the task. As an example, air conditioning engineers will operate in or around the airflow extractor fans on each floor. These are located at specific locations within an office, so a trigger could be incorporated if they moved to other areas without legitimate reason.

[Figure 4.20](#chapter4.xhtml#fig4.20) outlines data captured over two days of tracking the same individual. This allows one to visualise how movement patterns can be completely different on a daily basis and illustrates how an anomaly can be detected within a complex movement pattern.

A single event may not indicate anomalous behaviour and could cause false positives. The threat anomaly solution is not based solely on a single method to detect anomalous behaviour. It relies on multiple factors and processes. Each has the potential to raise an alert or increase the risk counter until a combination of events triggers an alert. Murenin *et al.* (2017) describes how anomalous behaviour can be detected through the analysis of human movement trajectories and anomalies in routes using uncommon controlled zones.

### 4.7.The Algorithm

One of the primary objectives of this research paper is the construction of a robust and effective algorithm that leverages data retrieved from the recently deployed proximity monitoring solution. The algorithm is an integral part of the Shadow Cloud application that was created for this research project. The algorithm is constructed from active elements, each with the ability to alert and increase a running threat counter. As the contractor enters Dakota’s controlled office space a counter is reset to ‘0’ (zero).

The main elements of the algorithm are as follows:

•Entry point

•Date

•Time of day

•Time spent in each zone

•Number of zones entered

•Zone pattern anomaly

[Figure 4.21](#chapter4.xhtml#fig4.21) depicts the algorithm as a process flow.

### 4.7.1.**Verification process**

Once the proximity solution had been fully integrated within the working practices of Dakota and its 3<sup>rd</sup> party contractors, a verification process was initiated. With the assistance of the facilities department and the security staff, the algorithm, which formed part of the Shadow Cloud application, was tested to determine any anomalies and false positives.

Triggers were configured at each stage of the algorithm and triggers were analysed to ensure the metrics fell within an acceptable margin of error.

[Figure 4.22](#chapter4.xhtml#fig4.22) depicts each stage of the algorithm and the average percentage of triggers within each stage over a two-month period. As an example, the time spent by each cleaner within each zone was within the permitted parameters 95% of the time. This is visualised in [Fig. 4.15](#chapter4.xhtml#fig4.15), which can be seen as indicative of normal behaviour.

The algorithm has been designed to incorporate some false positives. These would allow a margin of error to be incorporated. As an example, the threat threshold for Dakota has been set to ‘3’. This is based on the analysis of the targets and the data collected. The threshold for another organisation may be higher or lower depending on the risk appetite, the job functions or the physical attributes of the building.
