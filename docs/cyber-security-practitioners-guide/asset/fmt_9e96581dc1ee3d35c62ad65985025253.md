
—Freedom of movement of personal data inside the EU. The EU currently has in its general goals the freedom of circulation inside its borders. The Schengen Agreement created an area without internal border controls and with common visa policies and the freedom to cross between countries for leisure or for work (“The Schengen Agreement — History and the Definition,” n.d.). Currently the Schengen area includes 26 countries (“Schengen Area — Visa Information for Schengen Countries,” n.d.). This principle expands that concept but includes tools to facilitate that portability. Organisations must provide facilitated ways for people to collect their personal information and transfer it to another organisation.

—Limitation to purpose. The data processing has to be consistent to the purpose driving its collection. Once the definition of processing includes gathering information, this means that an organisation cannot collect information that is not justifiably needed for a legitimate purpose. Legitimacy results from informed and unambiguous consent, necessary to pursue the objectives of a contract, or to take the steps needed to enter a contract, satisfy legal obligations, perform tasks of public interest or tasks executed under official authority and protect the vital interests of the data subject, or to satisfy legitimate interests of the organisation that don’t conflict with the data subject’s rights and freedoms. This also enforces data minimization, which means not only that the minimum amount of data gets collected, but also that organisations must destroy information that no longer satisfies the legitimacy criteria (as storing is also a type of data processing).

—Integrity and confidentiality. Organisations are responsible for ensuring that the data is stored in such a way that guarantees, to an extent that is reasonable to expect under the current knowledge of technology, that the data does not suffer illegitimate changes (integrity) and is not disclosed without consent or legitimate necessity (confidentiality).

—Demonstrated responsibility. It is the organisation’s responsibility to prove that it has collected informed consent to data processing and/or that there is a justifiable need for that processing. It is also the responsibility of the organisation to prove that it has implemented adequate processes, policies and technologies to protect the personal data under its control.

### 15.6.The Data Protection Officer

An important figure created by the GDPR is the Data Protection Officer (DPO). The DPO has a major role in all processes related to data in an organisation and the GDPR dedicates the entire [Section 15.4](#chapter15.xhtml#sec15.4) to it. There is also a Guidelines document endorsed by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) that is extremely relevant to the interpretation of GDPR regarding DPOs, which is the Guidelines on Data Protection Officers (‘DPOs’), issued in December 2016 by the Article 29 Data Protection Working Party and revised and adopted in April 2017 (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017b).

The DPO can be a person, either an employee or someone external under a contract, or an organisation contracted to take that responsibility and assume the corresponding functions. The general recommendation is that the DPO is located in the EU, although there is the possibility of the DPO being located outside the EU if the data controller and/or processor has no establishment in the European Union (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017b). Even if this is the general recommendation, a lot of thought must go into deciding the location of the DPO and/or of his team, considering not only the work he has to do on a daily basis, but also its higher-level responsibilities. It is expected that the DPO maintain a high level of proximity to the administration and that he (or it, if the role is assigned to an organisation) be involved in decisions regarding data processing. These are arguments that favor locating the DPO wherever most of the data processing activity is located and wherever decisions are made, namely the headquarters of the company. On the other hand, the DPO is the connection between the organisation and the European authorities, which favours locating the DPO in Europe. For international organisations, this is truly a challenge and there is no one-size-fits-all solution.

Obtaining the knowledge needed to act as a DPO is itself a challenge. Although most organisations would benefit from nominating a DPO, not all are obliged to do so and not all have the financial conditions necessary to hire a competent DPO. Smaller organisations that deal with smaller amounts of personal data can opt to hire an information security consultant who can help them to establish the correct procedures and assist in case a personal-data-related incident occurs. It is important that they do so, given that they still have to be compliant with the regulation. An alternative is the hiring of a common DPO by an aggregation of organisations. This can be done through consortiums, through associations (like commercial and industrial associations) or through joint contracts. However, if this is the case, organisations must be particularly careful in the choice of a DPO and about the contract conditions, given that the DPO must be able to fully satisfy the needs of all the organisations that fall under the contract. Bigger consortiums or contracts are likely to choose an organisation, instead of an individual, to act as a DPO.

Organisations are obliged to nominate a DPO in one of the following situations (REGULATION (EU) 2016/ 679 OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL — of 27 April 2016 — on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/ 46/ EC (General Data Protection Regulation), 2016):

1.If “the processing is carried out by a public authority or body, except for courts acting in their judicial capacity”. It is not clear why the legislator has decided to exempt courts from the obligation of nominating a DPO, but this is likely to be an effort to maintain the separation between legislative, executive and judicial powers. However, this does not mean that courts are exempt from following the GDPR. Relevant quotations from the regulation are:

a.“this Regulation applies, inter alia, to the activities of courts and other judicial authorities”;

b.“The competence of the supervisory authorities should not cover the processing of personal data when courts are acting in their judicial capacity”;

c.“Supervisory authorities shall not be competent to supervise processing operations of courts acting in their judicial capacity”.
