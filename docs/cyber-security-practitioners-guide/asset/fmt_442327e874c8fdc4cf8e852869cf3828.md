### 4.5.Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection can refer to the problem of finding patterns in data that do not conform to expected behaviour (Chandola *et al.*, 2009). Nonconforming patterns can be described as anomalies, peculiarities or exceptions. Anomaly detection is used in a variety of applications such as credit card & banking fraud, insurance claims and security surveillance.

The detection of an anomaly within a dataset has been studied as early as the 19th century (Edgeworth, 1887). This has led to numerous research studies carried out to analyse a variety of anomaly techniques that have been developed for specific applications.

Anomaly detection can be described as the translation of data into categories based on specific criteria. For example, spurious data travelling through a computer network could be considered an anomaly that has been caused by a malicious intruder, malware or a software bug. This event may subsequently trigger an alert or initiate a workflow dependent on the activity and how it is categorised. Anomalies in credit card transaction data could indicate credit card or identity theft (Aleskerov *et al.*, 1997), or anomalous readings from a space craft sensor could signify a fault in some component of the space craft (Fujimaki *et al.*, 2005).

### 4.5.1.*Data noise*

Rousseeuw *et al.* (1987) and Teng *et al.* (1990) both describe the relevance of data noise when determining anomalies. Noise can be defined as a phenomenon in data that is of no interest and which becomes an impediment when analysing data. Noise removal is driven by the need to remove the unwanted objects before any data analysis is performed. Noise accommodation refers to immunising a statistical model estimation against anomalous observations (Huber, 1974).

Within Dakota, data noise can be described as crossover zones that contractors enter on their way to their destination. The intersection of zones could also be identified as data noise that should be eliminated from the detection algorithm. The data derived from the crossover area between two zones has been identified within the dataset. This can be observed as flickering between two zones over a prolonged period.

To eliminate the crossover effect and data retrieved when contractors are in transit between zones, a minimal time period has been incorporated into the security model. As an example, the researcher identified the time taken by contractors when walking between zones by analysing the time they entered and exited fields within the data stream. To improve accuracy the researcher also physically monitored human movement patterns within the Dakota environment to cross-reference the Bluetooth data with observation data (see the section on physical monitoring).

It was evident from the data retrieved from the proximity monitoring solution, in addition to the observation data, that a 10-second limit should be set within any zone to eliminate data noise.

### 4.5.2.*Detecting anomalies*

At an abstract level, an anomaly is defined as a pattern that does not conform to expected normal behaviour (Chandola *et al*., 2009). Therefore, in its basic form, anomaly detection can be described as the identification of normal behaviour, and, within a specific environment, observing any changes that are different from the normal pattern.

The anomaly identification process can be very challenging as there are many factors that need to be taken into consideration:

• Understanding normal behaviour and identifying the nuances of how humans integrate with others within a working environment.

• Determining the boundaries between normal and abnormal behaviour may not be an exact science. There are potentially an infinite number of contributing factors that may alter the perceived normal behaviour pattern.

• Individuals with malicious intentions can adapt and attempt to disguise their actions to look normal.

• As what is defined as normal behaviour will potentially evolve over time, continuous realignment will be required to prevent false positives.
