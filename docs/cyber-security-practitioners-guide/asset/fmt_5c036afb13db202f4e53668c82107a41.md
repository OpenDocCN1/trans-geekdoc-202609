
Whilst many marvel at the value Bitcoin, B, has bought to cryptocurrency, the biggest contribution to software engineering that Bitcoin has provided us with is decentralised applications or blockchain. Bitcoin (Nakamoto, 2008; Antonopoulos, 2017) and blockchain are inseparable; without blockchain technologies, Bitcoin would be unable to operate. Blockchain and consensus engineering are side products of Bitcoin. The blockchain is transparent, public, immutable, irreversible, secure, asynchronous, self-regulating and international. Briefly, Bitcoin users have a wallet, with which they can make transactions to other wallets securely using Public Key Infrastructures (PKI) encryption; this is facilitated by a distributed ledger, known as a blockchain. The blockchain is stored on multiple nodes in a network, each storing every transaction made between wallets. The confirmation of each transaction comes from the nodes, which check the validity of the transaction and then verify the transaction to all the other nodes in the network. The similarities between BoC and Bitcoin are as follows:

—Transactions: a transaction in Bitcoin represents the transfer of funds between two parties. Once this transaction has been verified it is added to the blockchain. BoC has transactions which record the transfer of artefacts between two parties and the blockchain can easily be modified to accommodate this type of transaction, see Greenspan (2015).

—Irrefutable: the motivation behind the validation of Bitcoin transactions is to ascertain that the spending party has the funds, and that there is no double-spending. Essentially, the motivation for validation comes down to financial accuracy. The motivation for the validation of BoC transactions are to ensure the integrity of the chain of custody.

—Longevity: in Bitcoin ledgers, the transactions are stored for the lifetime of Bitcoin itself. For BoC, all transactions of the chain of custody would also last for the duration of BoC, well beyond the time required for case information to be preserved. There is some concern over personal data being stored on BoC since it cannot be removed.

—Privacy: Bitcoin is public, so the ledgers can be seen by participants and non-participants of Bitcoin. BoC would be a private blockchain (Greenspan, 2015), often referred to as a permission blockchain, and would thus allow only authorised access.

—Data: Bitcoin transactions contain the users, the amount transferred, the timestamp and other meta-data. BoC transactions would differ and typically contain the receiver, submitter, manager, evidence ID, investigation ID and timestamp.

—Latency: the latency of blockchain verification in Bitcoin is such that it can take up to 10 minutes for Bitcoin to be mined and for this to be verified by a network of nodes; this latency is too long for the BoC application. This latency can be reduced to 10-20 secs, which is acceptable on a private blockchain, see Greenspan (2015) and Hyperledger Architecture Working Group (2017).

—Incentivisation: the incentive to set up a Bitcoin node is a reward of some value, albeit in the form of a digital crypto-currency. There is no value or reward in this BoC, however, typically there would be an incentive amongst regulators, e.g., FSR (Forensic Science Regulator, 2017), and accredited labs. If this was to be a national incentive, the nodes would be run independently by the regulators. This provides private transparency and the ability to see the chain of custody for each item of evidence.

—Disintermediation (Gaur *et al*., 2018): Many business and organisations rely on intermediaries, e.g. businesses rely on banks to complete transactions. It is necessary to trust one’s intermediaries. BitCoin overcomes this via consensus engineering and therefore removes the reliance on intermediaries. BoC would benefit from disintermediation, or not relying on intermediaries such as regulators or auditors. However, unlike the absence of regulators such as banks in the Bitcoin system, regulators would be integrated into the BoC system.

In summary, BoC is a system proposed, that, once updated, is immutable and irreversible. The system is implemented with blockchain technology, which is based on the Bitcoin technology. Its main contribution is ensuring that the chain of custody is immutable and irreversible and will add significant benefits to the integrity of all artefacts seized as items of evidence, both physical and digital, and therefore improve the overall quality of Evidence Management.

### 10.3.Blockchain of Custody, BoC

Evidential Continuity is part of evidence management and included in ISO17025 (ISO, 2017 (Section 7.11)). This means that to be an ISO17025 accredited forensic laboratory it is required, amongst other things, to have a secure information system that records, deletes, reports failures, ensures data integrity and has appropriate authorised access. ISO17025 compliance is an issue for all forensic laboratories handling evidence and allows the transparent audit of evidential continuity. These are usually only examined after the disclosure for a court case or audit. During the design and implementation of BoC the following are considered.

### 10.3.1.*Scope*

The scope of this evidence management system involves all stakeholders, but the system only records when evidence has been transferred between submitter and receiver. The details will include the unambiguous unique identifiers of submitter, receiver, investigation ID and artefact ID. The Business model involves the following stakeholders:

**Law Enforcement Agencies:** Police; Forensic Labs; Military; Security Agencies; Internal Quality Assurance Mechanisms; National Security Agencies; Expert Witnesses, and other government agencies.

**Legal Representation:** Prosecutors; Defence; Para-Legals; Internal Quality Assurance Mechanisms; Legal Mediator Assistants, and other business-related representatives.

**Audit Agencies:** Forensic Science Regulators; Independent Police Watchdogs; Legal Assurance Agencies, and other external agencies related to Law Enforcement and Legal.

### 10.3.2.*Transactions*

A DBMS has transactions to change the database from one consistent state to another. Partial transactions cannot occur, and transactions have to be committed. The transactions log, in a DBMS, is often used in backup instances or failures. Transactions have to pass the ACID test: Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation and Durability (Haerder and Reuter, 1983). Some years later came OLTP and OLAP, which now dominate the data warehousing and data mining activities of organisations. Brewer (2000) highlighted these same issues with GraphDB. The problem was paradoxical, and the large databases could not address all three issues simultaneously. Much research has happened since 2000, and Brewer (2017) went on to elaborate on the advances in technology.

Transactions in blockchain and distributed ledgers are defined as the recording of the transaction inputs, transaction outputs, and amounts spent. A transaction does change the data in the blockchain. However, little is known about how consistency is applied to the blockchain. Whilst blockchain is decentralised under certain circumstances it can pass the ACID test (Verdian *et al*., 2018). This is important for such an application as BoC, in which consistency is required.

### 10.3.3.*No Wi-Fi, no reception*

Areas without Wi-Fi are common to crime scenes, e.g., underground. There are also issues with privacy on open networks, which are overcome by the encryption of the data submitted. In cases where there is no Wi-Fi access, the reliance on good old-fashioned forensics and physical paperwork will suffice and accompany evidence until a designated place with Wi-Fi access is reached and the blockchain can be updated. This is not unlike operations today, where internet access is prohibited due to network unavailability.
