
Each `trigger` was investigated to ensure the `threshold` values were sufficient to prevent unnecessary `false positives` within the overall `algorithm`. To ensure the `experiment` did not create `false working patterns` the `data subjects` were not directly approached. `Facilities` and `security` were questioned to ensure the `cleaners`’ activity was normal. There were two `methods` that could be used to set the `threshold` levels. One of these could be carried out at the `algorithmic` `function` stage and the other required changes to be made to the overall `threat detection` level. To provide the most flexibility, each `stage` of the `algorithm` was adjusted to allow a margin of error based on the `false detection` rate.

### 4.8.Regulations and Legal Considerations

### 4.8.1.**GDPR**

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which comes into effect on 25<sup>th</sup> May 2018, will impact all EU organisations that collect, store and process personal or sensitive data (Ico.org.uk, 2017). To provide an effective threat mitigation solution, Shadow Cloud will record and process human movement data that is not anonymised. As part of its GDPR preparation process Dakota enhanced its privacy and data protection policies, seeking legal advice to ensure the data recorded was lawful and met the ICO’s regulatory requirements.

### 4.8.2.*Privacy laws*

Bruce Schneier (Schneier, 2016) describes surveillance as a technical and a legal problem. A key project deliverable is the identification of threats based on data collected on human behavior patterns.

As Shadow Cloud requires the tracking of individuals it will inevitably pose privacy questions. Whilst it could be argued that analysing data to prevent threats is ethically acceptable, using the data to target individuals for other purposes, which may include discriminating against the target, could breach privacy and human rights law.

To ensure Dakota comply to privacy regulation a clear and concise agreement with all 3<sup>rd</sup> party organisations was reviewed by the Data Protection Officer and the organisation’s legal representative to ensure the enhanced proximity monitoring process did not infringe on the individual rights of its contract staff. Law professor Eben Moglen once wrote ‘if people are doing nothing wrong then they have the right to maintain a balance between the individual and the power that is monitoring’.

### 4.9.Conclusions and Recommendations

This research project has explored the relationship between human movement patterns and the potential threat posed by an insider with malicious intentions. The real challenge perhaps is to understand how employees navigate a specific environment, how they process physical obstacles and how they interact with co-workers.

Whilst it can be argued that humans have distinctive movement traits, such as using the same entry point, it is evident that trajectories within an open plan office environment can be influenced by a complex set of variables.

Understanding the characteristics of the building and including areas where staff congregate, such as the water cooler, were considered integral when designing a threat model.

The primary focus of the project was the creation of an algorithm that would form part of an overall threat detection solution. The algorithm would initially leverage the data supplied from the target organisation but could be adaptive and used by other human monitoring solutions.

As the target data was analysed it was evident that the algorithm could not rely on a single trigger as this would produce too many false positives. It was clear that a multi-dimensional approach measuring a set of contrasting variables was required.

The algorithm was dissected into seven components, each able to process the specific characteristics of the targets, analysing the time, the date and their movement patterns. The research had intended to identify specific movement patterns that were evident within other research papers that focused on door entry data.

The data provided from the proximity monitoring solution highlighted the complexity of the human decision-making process. When a binary decision had to be made between different access points, the targets would invariably choose the same option; this was evident when entering a floor, as 90% chose the same entry point. However, when presented with multiple options the patterns were far less predictable.

This is why the algorithm could not rely on one measurement metric and incorporated a variety of data sets to determine an anomaly, ultimately reducing the risk of false positives.
