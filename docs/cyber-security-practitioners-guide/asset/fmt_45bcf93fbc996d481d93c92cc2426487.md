
62443-4-2 is a particularly helpful step forward as it defines how product suppliers in the IACS supply chain can achieve an SL-C for a system component. Where there is confidence that a claimed SL-C has been verified, e.g. by certification, this supports the system integrators and operators in their overall risk assessment and mitigation. Even though 62443-4-2 was only published towards the end of Q3 in 2018, there is already evidence that suppliers are recognising compliance with the standard as a valuable marketing tool. In a white paper published by a major supplier of IACS (including railway) network switches, Chen (2018) announced firmware upgrades to make their equipment compliant to the 62443-4-2 standard “to ensure the integrity of data and the network from known security leaks and unknown attacks”.

### 3.3.Critical Infrastructure

As Digital Trains are within the scope of NIS, compliance is obviously a requirement. In its first year of operation it is clear that Competent Authorities (CAs) are setting the bar fairly low in order to allow industry to become compliant without undue pressure. NIS is only interested in systems that support or deliver critical services; it does not address scenarios where organisations can suffer other impacts such as financial loss, or loss of reputation. The current criteria for requiring a cyber security incident to be reported to NCSC are for a significant proportion of a TOC’s services being cancelled. This makes it clear that NIS is focused on the impact on the country rather than the TOC. TOCs that only address NIS compliance and do not apply good cyber security practice to all of their services are only addressing part of the cyber security problem.

In its guidance on how to comply with the directive, NCSC offers a number of guidance sources. The 27000 and 62443 series of standards are well represented in these sources but it is interesting to note that in some cases NCSC offers guidance from its own website instead even when the standards clearly cover that topic. Risk management, for instance, is covered in depth in 27001, 27005 and 62443-2-1 but NCSC prefers to offer more general guidance of its own. This is possibly because the systems that support critical services are both corporate and operational, and although 62443 recommends that its requirements apply to the whole business, in many cases it will be used to complement an existing 27001 ISMS and organisations are unlikely to re-work their existing processes when they are connecting control systems to their corporate ones. Until 62443 is updated to achieve its stated aspiration to be more closely aligned to 27001 there will be gaps between the standards that need to be dealt with in other ways, either by filling the gaps or securing the border between the two environments. ### 3.4.Conclusion

Fraga-Lamas *et al*. (2017) state, “Rail operators have to comply with a set of international industry and government standards on the topic of security (e.g., ISO 27001, NIST SP800-53, ISA/IEC 62443 or APTA)”. This is simply not true; although the article is otherwise generally a well-informed one and differentiates between Train Control Systems and Train Management Systems, there is certainly no such requirement on TOCs. In its overview, the article also lists cyber security as a requirement only for Train Control Systems, although why it takes that position is unclear. Cyber security must apply to the complete Digital Train both to protect all operational systems and to minimise attack vectors for safety-related systems.

In another seemingly well-informed article Valdivia *et al.* (2018) correctly state that neither 61508 or 50129 safety standards cover security issues. They go on to claim that the standards mention intentional malicious activity and propose the use of 62443 to deal with this, which is not strictly true; only 61508-1 makes any such recommendation, whereas the railway standard, 50129, which can be used in isolation from 61508, does not. One might expect both of the above claims to be correct but this does not make them so. It does however highlight the safety standards’ presumption that safety incidents are the result of accidents and security ones are malicious whereas the logical view is that “the only difference between a malicious attack and an accident is the motivation of those involved” (Weiss, 2011).

Although 50129 doesn’t consider security, it isn’t ignored by the safety community altogether. The NRSP (National Rail Safety Plan) in its Section 14 widens the scope to include risk to assets, reputation, operation as well as putting lives at risk. But the NRSP is only concerned with safety-critical systems, it is not concerned with operational or business systems that also exist on trains.

What is clear is that safety is paramount on the railway, where safety processes are embedded in the culture. Cyber security represents a safety hazard but this is largely unrecognised by the safety standards. It is probably true to say that a system cannot be safe unless it is secure but it can be secure without being safe, but that doesn’t help either discipline very much. However, 62443 takes safety into account whereas 27001 doesn’t.

62443 approaches security with a similar process to 61508 but the skill set is different and the results aren’t equivalent. There are similarities in the process though which could be used to create synergies and exploited to develop opportunities to improve security acceptance.

62443 would appear to be the better fit for the industrial parts of rail but business systems are in many cases already 27001 compliant. However, not many are certified. Given the current demands on security resources it would not be sensible to rework the existing ISMS into a CSMS. 62443 states the intention to align more closely with 27001 in future versions so the suggested approach is to take it at its word and implement the CSMS as an extension to the ISMS, supplementing the ISMS with any additional policies that are required, but following the 62443-2-1 approach for its delivery. This would enable the engineering functions to gain the benefit of supply chain compliance, with standards such as 62443-4-2.

The 27001 and 62443 approaches are not that dissimilar. For instance, both recommend network segregation as a fundamental control. The difference is that 27001 offers it as a recommendation but 62443 provides a step by step approach for delivery, with a process that engineers will recognise.

A key part of any solution will be to recognise that many components in an IACS have no inbuilt security whatsoever and so if there is a security requirement it will need to be applied as a supplementary control. Hardening of network zones and conduits is likely to be a key feature. Hardening the complete network will have an impact on cost and so the identification of 62443 SLs will inform the prioritisation of that process.
