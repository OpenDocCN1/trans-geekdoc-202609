## It’s Time to Make the Case for Ethics

Ali G. Hessami

*Vega Systems, London, United Kingdom*  

[Hessami@vegaglobalsystems.com](mailto:Hessami@vegaglobalsystems.com)

Since the days of man as hunter gatherers, the success and prosperity of humanity has been associated with one key differentiator against other species, i.e. the invention of tools and artefacts. This inventiveness was inevitably driven by the need to generate a differential advantage against adversity in order to survive. The advent of agriculture brought the use of manmade tools to a higher level. Such tools gave mankind the ability to cultivate land, produce surplus food and transcend beyond the daily challenges of subsistence and survival. Whilst the invention of tools and artefacts bore testament to man’s superior cognitive powers, these arose in response to man’s needs to complement his feeble physique and powers in a harsh eco-system, and played a largely functional role in the advancement of the species. In this spirit, the quality, efficacy and diversity of tools and artefacts increased to enhance performance. However, the performance of tools, products and services has often been viewed narrowly through a utilitarian perspective often focused on the market mechanisms of price, functionality and desirability/quality. This has held true largely due to the limited impact of products and services on the society at large. This is a condition that is no longer tenable given our overt dependence on technological tools, products, services and artefacts that are transforming our lives and making us dependent on them without giving us the ability to influence their design, production, or functionality or to hold the supply chain accountable. In this setting, a new paradigm is called for that goes beyond concerns over commercial and functional performance and encourages the providers of products, services and systems towards transparency, accountability, explainability and the observance of higher ethical values in their increasingly global roles. This is the era of ethics and moral considerations for all enterprises, public or private, for or not-for-profit. Strangely but not unexpectedly, this also applies to the safety as well as the security of products, services and systems. We will explore the rationale, necessity and the attainment of this laudable emergent property in this introduction.

### Facets of Performance

A purposeful aggregate of interconnected parts performing one or a suite of functions is regarded as a system. A system generally resides in an environment (larger eco-system) or as part of a larger system (system of systems) and manifests properties and behaviours that are not attributable to its individual constituents but to their inter-working. This is referred to as emergence or the emergent property of a system. Apart from emergence, a system can also be viewed from specific stakeholder perspectives that constitute nine inter-related facets of performance, comprising the following:

• Technical and functional.

• Commercial, cost and profitability.

• Environmental.

• Integrity (reliability, availability and maintainability).

• Resilience.

• Safety and security.

• Sustainability.

• Quality.

• Social and individual perceived value and preference.

These facets of system performance are inter-related and their individual desired level of attainment often involves a degree of trade-off and the discretion of the stakeholders regarding an acceptable level for each attribute. A desirable product, service or system delivers an optimal blend of these in a given context and at a crosssection in time.

One particular aspect of performance or behaviour that is traditionally not analysed or treated systematically and comprehensively is the value aspect. By default, it is regarded as the forte of the marketing department to cater for consumer preferences and choices to maximise sales opportunities. One key aspect of value is the social/ethical value that is concerned with the cultural, social moral norms of good/decent behaviour and the impact of products, services or systems on the ethical values held in the stakeholders’ community or the whole society. This implies that design decisions and structural and behavioural aspects require stakeholder consultation and that the identification of ethical values that may be affected by the system of interest’s concept of operation is crucial, in addition to all the other requirements relating to other facets of performance cited earlier.
