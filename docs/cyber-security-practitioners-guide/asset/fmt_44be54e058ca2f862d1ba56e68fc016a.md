
The introduction of the GDPR could also have wider implications for the legal sector if the personal information of clients becomes compromised. Due to the business model and the sensitivity of a client’s information, a serious breach of personal data could have ramifications that go beyond incurring substantial fines. The Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA) recently highlighted information security as the foremost risk facing most law firms in the UK. The ICO data breach report in 2015/2016 indicates that 27% of security issues in the legal sector were attributed to theft of paper work and data being faxed or sent to the wrong recipient (Schwarz, 2016). [Table 8.1](#chapter8.xhtml#tab8.1) shows recent data breach incidents pre- and post-GDPR regulation. In 2016, a ransomware attack compromised personal data and rendered multinational law firm DLA Piper unable to access their client’s data (Steiner, 2018). It is therefore imperative to assess the risk to personal data and to implement appropriate security measures to mitigate the impact of a subsequent data breach incident.

One of the biggest data breaches recorded in 2017 was a cyber attack on US-based credit card rating firm Equifax. It affected approximately 145 million users in several countries including the UK \[24\]. The company later revealed that the vulnerability, “CVE-2017-5638” in the open source software Apache Struts was exploited (Cobb, 2018). This security breach highlights the importance of establishing effective security controls and vulnerability management to counter various threats. Research by Luszcz (2018) provided further analysis of the technical and security gaps in the design and implementation of new systems that can create various security vulnerabilities.

While new technological innovations provide many opportunities, the increasing number of data breaches also highlights a discernable chasm between effective internal security practices and substandard system development strategies for new and existing systems being implemented within the information and communication infrastructures. Reding (2010), argued in favour of a balance between the benefit of new technology and the assessment of potential risks, that if materialised could cause a serious data breach incident. Now that the GDPR is in effect, assessing various risks and strengthening privacy policies for personal data are crucial for meeting compliance.

### 8.2.3.*Privacy risks in IoT and third-party apps*

The increasing trend of privacy issues involving Internet of Things (IoT) and third-party applications highlights new risks to personal data and a number of ethical concerns. A case involving misuse of personal data and breach of privacy occurred in 2015, when Facebook allowed a third-party application created by Cambridge Analytica onto their platform. The app was configured to surreptitiously harvest the data of 87 million profiles from which information was used during a UK referendum and US election campaign. A subsequent investigation by the ICO identified major failures in data protection standards and a £500,000 penalty was levied on Facebook for violating data protection laws. The fine issued at the time was the maximum amount under the Data Protection Act 1998 (DPA), however, the penalties under the GDPR could have totalled 4% of global revenue amounting to circa £1.4 billion.

Wood (2015), examined the privacy risks and unintended consequences arising from various internet-ready devices with built-in cameras. He elaborated on potential legal issues, highlighting an instance where hackers exploited a vulnerability in a network that hosts connected baby monitoring systems and shouted obscenities in a nursery that was using one of the devices. This included instances where partners of victims of domestic abuse used mobile apps to deliberately change functions on devices such as thermostats, door locks, and security cameras to cause distress and exert control.

### 8.2.4.*Establishing privacy by design and default*

As previously highlighted, the absence of appropriate measures to assess security risks during the development and implementation of third-party systems can significantly increase the chances of data becoming compromised. Innovative systems design may enhance business functionalities; however, it also represents a substantial security risk. Unlike previous legislations under directive (95/46/EC), the GDPR contains specific provisions that address substandard system development practices, where security is only incorporated as an after-thought during the design phase of new systems.

The capricious nature and complexity of threats such as advanced persistence threats (APT) means organisations must engage in continuous assessment and refinement of their security strategies in order to counter emerging threats. Solterbeck (2006), postulates that decisions to invest in advanced technical tools available tools such as Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) and Security Information and Event Management Systems (SIEMS) will lead to the ability to analyse network anomalies and increase response capabilities. Conversely, Von Solms and Van Niekerk (2013), point out that these systems on their own will not prevent malicious attacks in all instances and argue that a more comprehensive approach incorporating people and processes will be required.

### 8.2.5.*Encryption and pseudonymisation*

Encryption and pseudonymisation techniques are among the measures that can be taken to secure personal data. They can be used to remove certain attributes from information that could be used to identify an individual. As discussed in Section 8.2.4, securing personal data and demonstrating robust security measures such as data protection by design and default are imperative in meeting compliance. The effectiveness of cryptographic technique was demonstrated in research by Bitar and Jakobsson (2017) who addressed data privacy requirements by utilising a hardware security model (HSM) as a cryptography method for anonymisation through encryption and access control.

A Delphi method was used to gather empirical data from subject experts to ascertain the most important aspects for meeting compliance within the GDPR. Bitar and Jakobsson (2017) demonstrated that access control, followed by data classification, was the most important aspect in securing personal data. These results were supported by Solterbeck (2006), who advocated that encryption methods can be adopted as an effective solution for protecting sensitive data. On balance, the strength of the findings provided insights for addressing relevant areas of GDPR such as Article 34 by employing the HSM encryption method. Nevertheless, the highly technical approach adopted for the research left other areas unaddressed, for example, the notification and communication of a data breach.
