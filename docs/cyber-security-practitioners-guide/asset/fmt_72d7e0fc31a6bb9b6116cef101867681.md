
A number of established industry frameworks are used by organisations as a blueprint for incident management; however, new regulations necessitate a reassessment of incident response phases in order to protect personal data and meet compliance. These include frameworks such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 27000/27001), and the Enisa Good Practice Guide for Incident Management. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication 800-61 framework outlines guidance in three main areas: organisation capability, incident handling procedures and information sharing and communication as per Cichonski *et al*. (2012).

NIST was primarily created for the federal government in the US and is responsible for developing guidelines and standards to address information security risks and incident management. [Figure 8.4](#chapter8.xhtml#fig8.4) illustrates four primary incident phases recommended by NIST for incident handling. The free publications are regularly updated and can be adopted for implementation within public or private organisations. Other frameworks such as the ISO/IEC 27001 are part of the ISO 27000 suite and are frequently revised. The information security framework is widely used in many organisations to establish, manage, and continually improve the information security management systems (Solterbeck, 2006).

The Enisa incident handling framework provides practical information management guidelines with the focus on incident handling techniques for incident response teams (Enisa.europa.eu, 2011). Although there are some overlaps within the response phases and the techniques adopted, there are notable gaps for the notification and communication of a data breach.

### 8.2.7.**Security culture and leadership within incident response programs**

As discussed in the previous section, the assessment of the human element plays an important role in the development of an effective incident response plan to enable prompt response to security incidents. Buchler *et al*. (2018) explored the human element of cyber security by assessing leadership and coordination challenges within cyber defense teams using an Observational Scaled Assessment of Teamwork (OAT) to assess the qualitative aspect of teamwork. The OAT model by Buchler *et al*. (2018) in [Fig. 8.5](#chapter8.xhtml#fig8.5) measures two distinct factors: (1) communication and collaboration, and (2) strong leadership.

The main findings identified strong leadership and face to face interaction as key attributes for building resilient CSIRTs. However, Wood (2015) adopts a contrary view and argues that leadership and collaboration should be explored at both the individual level and within the collective organisational security culture in order to improve the effectiveness of incident response.

New requirements in GDPR legislation mean that the previous tick-box approach by organisations that only addressed minimum compliance will no longer be adequate. Organisations need to make strategic decisions to assess existing systems and encourage proactive responses to security incidents to effectively counter various security threats. A recent report by PWC (2018), examined the merits of security culture within organisations and discovered that defining the security culture was an efficacious way to change individual staff behaviours and could also help strengthen overall security within an organisation. This indicates that, rather than focusing disproportionately on enforcing security policies, organisations should run appropriate cyber awareness initiatives that include staff as part of the security solution as these contribute to better security.

### 8.2.8.*Summary of literature*

The methods and techniques explored in the literature provided insights into important works that had been previously completed and provided context for challenges that need to be addressed. The General Data Protection Regulation has been in effect since May 2018 and has introduced a new legal mandate for governing how data is collected and processed in the EU. Given the wider remit in the new legal mandate and the option for imposing substantial penalties, it is crucial that organisations reassess existing frameworks for gaps in compliance and establish robust policies and procedures for interpreting and integrating the GDPR.

The literature provided a myriad of useful techniques and approaches for addressing various regulatory challenges. The research by Mansfield-Devine (2016) provides insights into common challenges organisations encounter when interpreting and integrating the GDPR. Bitar and Jakobsson (2017) adopted a technical approach by developing an HSM encryption model for securing data, while Alawadhi and Udeaja (2018) explored the need for assessing the human element in incident response programs. Nevertheless, the new requirements in the GDPR that make it compulsory to report instances of data breach and their impact on data subjects are beyond the scope of existing incidenthandling frameworks. This research aims to build on existing frameworks and to formulate a decision support system that can be used to meet compliance for incident response.

### 8.3.Development of Decision Support System

Prior to the introduction of the GDPR, some organisations were ambivalent about the level of impact they would incur if they reported serious data breaches. Consequently, they were reluctant to report some instances of data breach. While previous privacy legislation made implicit reference to this practice in order to deter it, the GDPR made explicit reference to the practice, asking for controllers and processors to demonstrate accountability and transparency. Article 5 (2) of the GDPR regulation explicitly outlines that controllers should be responsible for the development of appropriate frameworks and be able to demonstrate compliance with principles relating to the processing of personal data (PwC, 2018). This indicates that a demonstration of accountability goes beyond simply achieving the minimum compliance requirements outlined in the GDPR.

This research aims to address these challenges by formulating a conceptual decision support system for incident response and breach notification requirements. Research by Hevner *et al.* (2014), outlines principles within design science that can be employed in the development of artefacts, instead of verifying theories in research. Design science can be employed to guide the development of IT artefacts in a structured way and can include formal logic diagrams, software systems or rigorous mathematical-based design (Hevner *et al*., 2014). Design science paradigms can be used to solve complex IT problems and provide defined processes that can be used in conceptual system designs.

### 8.3.1.*Pre-GDPR incident response frameworks*

An incident response framework can act as a blueprint for responding to security incidents. This is an important part of business continuity and provides specific instructions that can be implemented to quickly identify and neutralise security threats and minimise their impact on normal business functions. A number of widely used incident handling frameworks such as NIST, SANS and CERT can be employed by organisations of varying size to guide incident response activities.

The NIST incident handling guide comprises a standard four-phase incident response which shares several similarities with other models employed within incident response programs. Nevertheless, new requirements in the GDPR such as the reporting of a data breach within 72 hours and the notifying of data subjects must be considered in order to comply with the GDPR.
