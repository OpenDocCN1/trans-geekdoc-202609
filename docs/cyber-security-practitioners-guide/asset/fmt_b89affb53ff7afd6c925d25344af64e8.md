
The output of the Reporting Process in the form of a well-written report must be presented to a wide variety of audiences such as courts, legal personnel, law enforcement, technical personnel and management. The report can be presented in the form of the expert testimony and can be accompanied by other formats such as the multimedia presentation and the deposition. During this Process, investigators need to prove their formulated hypothesis using supporting evidence. They must communicate their findings in such a way that facilitates future validation and that can be understood by both technical and non-technical audiences. In this regard, Beebe and Clark (2005) state that a presentation should be based on “careful consideration about how to best communicate information to various audiences”. Moreover, the presentation must also include relevant documentation and processes conducted during the investigative process, as well as any relevant physical evidence that can further consolidate the case against the perpetrator. It is also vital for DFPs to prove that the investigation was conducted in a forensically sound manner (Mumba and Venter, 2014). Furthermore, since the judge and jury or other interested parties are often nontechnical users, DFPs must ensure that in their presentation they avoid complex arguments, unless they also provide the audience with significant help so that they understand the technical points made. In cases where DFPs need to appear in court to give expert witness, they must ensure that they are fully aware of the jurisdictional legal requirements concerning the digital evidence. During the presentation, often the hypothesis is challenged by the defence lawyers through a contradicting hypothesis and supporting evidence. Thus, DFPs need to prove the credibility of their hypothesis and to be well-prepared to defend the hypothesis against criticism and challenge. In circumstances in which challenges are successful, investigators need to backtrack to the earlier stages to obtain and examine more evidence and develop a better hypothesis. If the decision is made in a court, it will be decided whether to convict the accused or to refute the allegations. If the decision is made in an organisational setting, it will be decided what disciplinary actions must be taken if the incident can be attributed to an individual. The output of the Presentation Process becomes the input to the Investigation Closure Process, discussed in the next subsection.

### 9.4.7.*Investigation closure process*

This process involves closing the investigation and applying the decision-making associated with it (Beebe and Clark, 2005). Lessons learnt must be captured, and the outcome of the investigation must be used to review the existing policies and procedures of the organisation. Moreover, relevant information concerning the entire investigation must be disseminated and communicated to all stakeholders. This includes communicating the need to return to a previous Process, deciding on the acceptance or rejection of the hypothesis or providing any reports or documents from the Presentation Process. The investigating organisation might decide to make some information available only within the organisation or they might decide to disseminate information more widely. The organisation’s policies and procedures should determine the details in this regard (Ciardhuáin, 2004). The investigating organisation must also record the investigation results, the case decision and all the evidence items. Such information will have an impact on future investigations and might have an effect on the policies and procedures of the organisation.

Thus, the accumulation and preservation of this information is a key part of supporting the work of DFPs and might be a productive aspect for the development of innovative applications that integrate techniques such as data mining and expert systems. Hauck *et al*. (2002) provide a detailed example of the dissemination activity in which they define a system named Coplink that provides real-time support for law enforcement investigators through an analysis application on the basis of a large accumulation of information from past investigations. An additional example is provided by Harrison *et al*. (2002), whose system is not real-time but instead delivers an “archival function” database to support investigators. Finally, during this Process, a decision also needs to be made to determine whether or not digital and physical evidence should be returned to the proper owner and what criminal evidence must be removed. The jurisdiction in which the investigation takes place and the type of authorisation determine whether or not the evidence should be returned, cleansed and reused or destroyed or whether the evidence should be stored for a certain period of time before any of the three possibilities can be applied.

### 9.5.Demonstration of the SDFIPM

This section follows the Demonstration activity of Peffers *et al*.’s (2006) DSRP, used in this research, and discusses the method employed to demonstrate the potential deployment of the SDFIPM. The Demonstration activity of the DSRP requires a researcher to apply the artefact in an appropriate environment such as “experimentation” or a “case study” to solve the stated problem (Hevner and Chatterjee, 2010; Peffers *et al*., 2006). Resources needed for the Demonstration activity include effective knowledge of how the artefact should be applied to solve the stated problem. In order to assess how the SDFIPM addresses the stated research problem, we adopt the case scenario approach whereby we apply the SDFIPM to a scenario and perform a walkthrough of the model. The approach followed is identified as being appropriate for evaluating “simulations” and “models” (Hevner *et al*., 2010; Peffers *et al.*, 2006; Balci, 2004). The Demonstration activity was carried out prior to the model being submitted to the experts for independent evaluation (discussed in [Section 9.6](#chapter9.xhtml#sec9.6)). A similar approach is adopted by other authors (Adams, 2012; Ciardhuáin, 2004; Beebe and Clark, 2005; Carrier and Spafford, 2003).

### 9.5.1.*Case study*

The following case study is based on an actual situation and is intended to demonstrate the potential deployment of the SDFIPM. It is modelled after Ciardhuáin’s (2004) scenario, which pertains to the exploitation of a vulnerability found in an online service operated by a bank:

This investigation started when Bank X in London (England) received an email claiming to have found a vulnerability in an online service operated by the bank. The email offered to provide details of the vulnerability in exchange for payment. On checking their logs, Bank X concluded that an unauthorised access had been made to their web server. The bank received further emails threatening to reveal the vulnerability to the press and public, including a link to a website which the suspect intended to use to disclose the vulnerability. Bank X reported the issue to the police in London who initiated the investigation. It became obvious that the compromised web server was located in Manchester (England) from Bank X’s headquarters and that the source of the emails was in Cardiff (Wales). Therefore, another police force, namely SWP (the South Wales Police), took up the case to start the investigation.
