
The main contribution of this paper is twofold: i) blockchain engineering/modelling, and ii) prototype development of a decentralised application for evidence management. The two are entwined; however, it looks likely that the proliferation of decentralised applications is going to expand exponentially, and therefore methods are needed that can integrate and interact with all stakeholders.

Relying on UML-based design is problematic for decentralised applications, since whilst the implementation languages are object-based, the application is not. Our method has looked at use case diagrams and class diagrams to model the actors and data. The class diagrams are very abstract and do not include methods, but help identify attributes of the transactions, assets and participants. The transactions are then identified for the decentralised application and the sequence of events is modelled by flowcharts. Overall the design and implementation was a success. This has been proven by the completion of two investigations.

### 10.6.1.*Recommendations*

The gratuitous use of technology and the hyperbole surrounding BitCoin are not to be confused with blockchain development. However, blockchain has problems with disassociating itself from BitCoin. Excluding cost, new technologies should always bring improvements. BoC is an integrated system between all parties of an irreversible and immutable chain of custody for evidence management. In the process of designing and developing this blockchain application we have come up with several recommendations, which are as follows:

**Permanent Data:** ISO17025 (and GDPR to some extent) recommend that personal data be deleted. Of course, in legal cases, this is not permissible; however, once time spent has been completed, or acquittal been provided by courts or if information has simply not led to a court case, then there is the possibility of the disposal or return of evidence and the documentation related to it. Blockchain, by its very nature, does not allow the deletion of data — it is possible to delete data from the blockchain, however, there will be a record of this deletion and the previous blocks will contain secure, private and confidential data. It is the recommendation that personal data, e.g., the victim’s identity, is not stored on the blockchain in case a request is made for it to be deleted.

**Permissioned blockchain:** The auditing of organisations is integral to good evidence management. As shown from the results, auditors can access and view cases and view the records regarding the transfer of artefacts and evidence management. This can all be completed with transparency and low latency. Whilst there is more to internal audits than evidence transfer, this does allow instant updates of an important role in evidence management.

Many LEA will have their own DB for recording evidence management. From personal discussions, these can be different within the Departments of LEA, e.g., digital forensics may use a different DB to Forensics. If personal discussions are not enough proof, look at the wide range of software available: EvidenceOnQ, EvidenceGuard (CaseGuard, 2018), Forman (Lowman, 2018), Evidence Traq, Oracle’s Digital Evidence Management System (Oracle, 2017) and many more. BoC has an immutable and irreversible blockchain, and these are properties that databases do not have by default. That is the focus of the development of BoC. These databases are permissioned and require authentication and log in details. BoC is no different; it is permissioned, and under no circumstances should it be implemented as a permissionless blockchain application.

**Modelling:** Flowcharts display important interactions entered into by the users of the system. It is recommended that these be integrated into the training of individuals when implementing transactions.

**Audits:** BoC provides transparency, whereby an auditor can access all the transactions for a case without delay. It is recommended that auditors only have read access at all times.

**Tokenisation:** Not every blockchain application has to involve tokens or coins. The distribution, issuance and management of the tokens has to be considered. Finally, there is the value of the token. As discussed in [Section 10.3.6](#chapter10.xhtml#sec10.3.6), if the network operating costs outweigh the value generated by the blockchain then the cost of the network outweighs the benefit. The exception to this rule is when not-for-profit organisations are funding the blockchain; here, tokens become almost irrelevant as in the case of BoC.

**NPE:** Non-person entities have not been modelled; however, it is possible that NPEs could be necessary. NPEs are not associated with physical people, they are generated by the system, usually for administration purposes. In this case, there is a demand for NPEs, which could easily be added as another participant registry representing that role. Consider the issue of custody. Custodians are not always assigned 24/7 to artefacts; quite often artefacts are left in secure storage with no custodian. In such cases, there would be a problem with the current system. The principle of updating the blockchain every time an exchange is made may seem maddening. It may seem like quality overload is taking place without any value being delivered. This is not so. In evidence stores there are loopholes whereby persons who are not the custodian can exchange the artefact, leading to problems later on and thus devaluing the system. Here all artefacts have a unique custodian, and each time the Evidence Store Manager changes, so do the blockchain and the custodian for each item stored. This means only the custodian can exchange the artefact — it is a fundamental tenet of the system. So, going back to unmanned evidence stores, this would be where an NPE would be introduced. NPEs of evidence stores would not be allowed to update the blockchain except when an expected end-of-shift occurs. At this point, the NPE will be allowed to complete a special transaction that would allow all the items in the evidence store to change custody from the designated NPE to the designated Evidence Store Manager (now the custodian). This adds a further level of logical security. Whilst it cannot stop a physical breach, the blockchain will make it logically infeasible to exchange artefacts whilst the NPE is the custodian.

**Rescinding:** People make mistakes. If the seizure of an artefact goes wrong, can it be altered? Yes, it can be altered by giving the manager of the investigation delete privileges. An immediate request can be made, the manager can delete the request and all can get back to work. These errors are still recorded on the blockchain as transactions. The key here is transparency. BoC is about maintaining the auditability of actions, thus ensuring best practice. Therefore, the deletion of data records is not of concern, but when the deletion occurred or by whom the data records were deleted may be.

### 10.6.2.*Future work*

This paper covers the development of a prototype blockchain application using Hyperledger for a case study on evidence management. It is a beginning and, upon reflection, there are many improvements that can be made.

Firstly, the disintermediatory nature of blockchain allows multiple enterprise integration, or *interprise*. This has not been approached by this project, however, it is conceivable that international agencies could record transactions using multiple blockchains.

Secondly, combined with IoT and RFIDs, the tracking of evidence could become automated. This would require using different access control models, e.g., Xu *et al*. (2018), and could improve the automated control of events.
