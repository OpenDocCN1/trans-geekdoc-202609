
We have finally moved beyond focussing on safety after two millennia, and new concerns over the protection of life and property have emerged in the physical and cyber security contexts. Whilst safety is regarded as freedom from unacceptable levels of human harm that arise from unintended random or systematic errors and failures, security can be broadly defined as freedom from the unacceptable risks of human harm or loss of property arising from intentional acts and causes. The assurance of safety and security is broadly approached from the perspective of the risk of human harm and property loss to the direct users and stakeholders of a product, service or system. The well-being, happiness and overall quality of life in the social context is the next dimension of interest, largely driven by the emergence and advancement of digital technologies that pervade our lives and that may progress further to have an impact on many aspects of our lives normally considered private and personal. This is rather unprecedented in human history and requires urgent review, an evaluation of the existing and emerging social hazards, and the formulation of proactive strategies to preserve and protect moral and ethical values that have evolved over millennia for the advancement and prosperity of human societies.

### `Ethics and morals`

`Ethics` is the branch of knowledge that deals with moral principles that govern human behaviour. Morals are the measures of right and wrong in the judgements and decisions that we face in life.

### `The principal ethical philosophies`

There are a number of predominant ethical canons and philosophies that offer competing views of human behaviour. The choices a person makes in all walks of human endeavour depend very much on the philosophies he follows. The oldest school is attributed to Aristotle, and it is known as `Virtue Ethics`. This is largely concerned with individual qualities (virtues) that underpin our behaviours and promote well-being. The focus is largely on virtues rather than the negative individual traits that are referred to as vices. In this individual and bottom-up perspective, the promotion and upholding of individual virtues are considered instrumental to the collective societal well-being and the quality of interactions and relationships. This is analogous to quality ingredients being essential to integrative virtue from the total system/societal perspective. Whilst this approach has its virtues, it evidently lacks the systems perspective in that the emergence of a virtuous society at peace and happy with itself is not necessarily and entirely conditioned on moral choices made by the individual members.

The second school of philosophy regarding ethics is the `Consequentialist` school, which is more concerned with the systemlevel emergence of happiness and well-being from the integral of actions, choices and decisions made by the human agents, sub-systems and components of a system of interest. This regards any choice or decision ethical/moral so long as it contributes or is perceived to contribute to maximising the satisfaction, fulfilment and happiness for the largest number of beneficiaries. This is analogous to an economic utility function and the desire for maximisation of return on an investment as a rational course of action. There are many challenges in utilitarianism, least of all estimating, anticipating or rationalising a system-level effect from a component level course of action. This is as much an issue with human affairs as it is with complex technological systems. The nearest analogy with technological systems is a systems analysis technique known as Failure Mode, Effects and Criticality Analysis (`FMECA`) that strives to identify critical component level failures that have a major impact on system-level reliability, safety and availability.

The third established perspective on ethics emphasises the importance of leadership and stewardship. The `Deontological` or `Duty Ethics` proposed by Immanuel Kant explores the ramifications of choices, options and decisions by those who occupy the positions of responsibility, authority and leadership. This is driven by the realisation that senior decision makers’ choices and decisions are more influential/impactful and more significant in terms of their effects on the stakeholders, hence the necessity for moral considerations. One key criteria for the morality of a duty holder’s decisions and choices is to ensure that all the others impacted by the decisions made are treated fairly and with dignity, and not used as means to the decision makers’ ends and objectives. The influential decision makers are also encouraged to consider circumstances in which their decisions could become universal laws and whether they would be willing to endure life influenced and shaped by those laws. Duty ethics demands moral scrutiny, considerations and judgements by those in a position of authority that would impose limits on their choices. In this way, those in a position of authority strive to ensure that every critical decision is made empathetically in an informed moral context.

The three schools of ethical philosophy endeavour to scrutinise the morality of human choices and decisions from the perspectives of individual virtue (component), consequence/utility (systemlevel) and duty holders (governance). From a holistic systems analytical viewpoint, the three canons of ethical philosophy provide an encompassing framework for understanding, analysing and proactively addressing ethical concerns arising from our choices, decisions and actions from a personal, corporate and collective perspective. ### `The Emerging Reality`

The widescale emerging pattern of cyber attacks globally poses an unprecedented threat to governance, economic activities, and the peace and stability of the largely digitally-transformed lifestyle. The recent cyber attacks on the USA power grid and power companies, also known as the so-called grid hacking — a spree of attacks on over 300 universities and some private companies in the USA and beyond resulting in the loss of 31 terabytes of private data estimated to amount to an equivalent of $3 billion worth of intellectual property and a whole host of data breaches including one which yielded 2 terabytes of private information on hundreds of millions of American adults — are illustrative of the unabating current trends. Apart from the colossal economic, strategic and geopolitical impact such breaches have, many of the security breaches of state and private sector enterprises have major ethical dimensions relating to privacy, trust, duty of care/protection and accountability.
