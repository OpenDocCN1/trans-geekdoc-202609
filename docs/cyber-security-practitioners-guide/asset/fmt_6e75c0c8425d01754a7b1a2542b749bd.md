
### 8.3.2.*Compliant GDPR framework design*

The design of the DSS was informed by challenges identified in the literature and the mandatory requirements for incident response and breach notification. Existing incident response frameworks were extended and relevant areas of the GDPR were integrated into policies and process to meet compliance. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) provides guidance for consistent interpretation and implementation of GDPR articles, while the ENISA incident handling guide offers demonstrable and practical steps for establishing incident management procedures (Cichonski *et al*., 2012).

The creation of the decision support framework aims to be flexible and adaptable and integrates a three-step approach. Firstly, the GDPR incident response dashboard provides an overview of additional response phases that are aligned with new requirements in the GDPR.

The subsequent steps include change restriction measures. These measures contain functionalities that can be leveraged to track and audit system changes and mitigate security incidents. In addition, these measures help to address the requirements in Article 32 that require organisations to demonstrate technical and organisational measures for protecting personal data. The final phase of the DSS presents a conceptual incident process flow that is structured using UML notations. The design of the DSS aims to extend the theoretical findings in the literature review by using a UML activity diagram to model breach notification processes and incorporate a technical element within the scope of the research.

The design included a distinct colour scheme and decision process flow to identify different levels of incidents such as minor, medium and major data breaches. The incident response dashboard expands existing frameworks by employing an eight-phase incident response cycle that incorporates the new requirements outlined in the GDPR such as the requirement to document the nature and extent of the data breach and the requirement to notify the ICO and data subjects within a specific timeframe.

The creation of incident scenarios forms part of a system design that provides practical steps for assessing security incidents and mitigates the adverse impacts and consequences of security incidents. The breach scenarios aim also to demonstrate the applicability of the DSS to aiding incident response activities and addressing various security incidents. This is complemented by the formulation of change restriction measures which include established tools and processes that can be leveraged to track and audit system changes, mitigate security incidents, and address the requirements in Article 32 of the GDPR that require organisations to demonstrate technical and organisational measures for protecting personal data. The change restriction measures can also be employed to govern access requests and ensure stringent measures are in place to protect personal data.

### 8.3.3.*Justification for component selection*

Design Science has been widely used in research to guide artifact development and solve problems identified in organisations as stated by Hevner *et al*. (2014). The organisational challenges identified in the literature provide a basis for the design of the artifact and accordingly Design Science provides proven principles to guide the development of the DSS. In order to formulate a comprehensive response approach, both the security and privacy characteristics of data need to be addressed.

The stipulation in Article 32, Paragraph (1) (b) outlines the need for robust security policies and procedures that continuously satisfy confidentiality, integrity and availability of personal data (EU, GDPR, 2016). Although there is no specific requirement to map the articles in the regulation, mapping the articles can be the first step towards meeting compliance with the GDPR. In order to simplify the design of the DSS, different elements of the decision support system provide the requisite functionalities to facilitate the consistent interpretation of GDPR articles to allow a prompt response to incidents.

This can be achieved by using diagrammatic modelling and categorisation of incident response processes to aid decision making. UML is an industry standard language with notations and methods for visualising conceptual system designs and it can represent the first step towards developing complex software systems (Fowler, 2016). The notation in UML was deemed suitable to enrich the design of the DSS and show interactions between different levels of data breach via a step-by-step decision process flow.

### 8.4.Focus Group

When assessing appropriate methods to employ in this research, the intent was to garner professional feedback on and in-depth insight into the design and usability of the decision support system. Focus group interviews are exploratory in nature and qualitative data can be gathered through feedback, opinions and attitudes (Kuada, 2012). To that end, a focus group interview was deemed suitable to achieve the objectives of the research. The design incorporated a two-step process: firstly, inferences drawn from the literature were used to inform the design of the DSS, then, focus groups with semi-structured interviews were conducted to gather feedback and analysis, and finally, the results were interpreted and analysed.

### 8.4.1.*Recruitment of participants*

The way in which participants are recruited can determine the success of the focus group. According to Kuada (2012), care should be taken to ensure participants possess the right attributes and experience to contribute to the discussions. The recruitment of participants was conducted using deliberate sampling, which was described by Hevner *et al*. (2014) as purposive sampling. Participants from different organisations and industry sectors with a minimum of 10 years’ experience in their fields were selected to ensure they met the criteria and could fully participate in the discussions.

The data-gathering instruments included a notepad, an audio recorder and a video recorder; however, as one of the participants was ambivalent about the video recording, it was subsequently omitted from the sessions. The focus group sessions commenced with general questions being asked before narrowing down to specific probes to obtain appropriate feedback. The first set of questions was a general introduction to initiate the discourse on the overall design of the system. The second set of questions introduced several case studies and asked about the usability of the system design.

The final set of questions allowed participants to discuss and assess the overall strengths and weaknesses of the system and to recommend future improvements (Kuada, 2012). The importance of the researcher in facilitating the discussions and enabling an open atmosphere where everyone could contribute was highlighted. The researcher was the only moderator conducting the session; however, the interactions and responses were carefully monitored and recorded, and probing questions and prompts were used to keep the conversation going. This facilitated more in-depth discussions, which were instrumental in gathering the required feedback to meet the objectives of the study.
