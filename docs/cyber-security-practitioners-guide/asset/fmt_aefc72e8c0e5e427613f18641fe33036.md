
With these points in mind, there is a growing motivation for industry to look at the benefits blockchain can offer in the shape of providing another layer of security and privacy to the organisation’s data and allowing the option to control data permission and use. This can provide an added layer of security which can work in harmony with the traditional cloud security systems and allow for a more secure and transparent way to handle data. The synergy between cloud and blockchain might be useful, especially as putting all data on the blockchain may place too heavy a burden on the blockchain in terms of processing, performance and scalability. Also, one must take into account the requirement of GDPR that those whose data have been collected by the organization must have the right of erasure. This prohibits the storage of personal data on a blockchain level. It might be useful in some cases to handle data off-chain and use blockchain as an immutable mechanism to capture details in chronological order and to authenticate access to data held off-chain in data lakes.

### 6.2.What Is Blockchain?

Blockchain is said to be one of the most disruptive computing paradigms after the Internet (Swan, 2015). The technology has advanced such that it is now more than just a means of financial and economic trading. Recently, different arms of industry have started to harness the technology to take advantage of its benefits and the many ways it can be applied.

The concept of blockchain was first circulated as a white paper (Satoshi, 2008), and created under the pseudonym Satoshin Nakamoto. As it was originally developed for use with a cryptocurrency (Bitcoin), blockchain is often referred to as Blockchain 1.0 when it is used with cryptocurrencies (Swan, 2015, p. ix). Blockchain 2.0 refers to the blockchain technology underlying smart contracts which go beyond just cash transactions, and Blockchain 3.0 describes blockchain technology which can be used in healthcare, government, intellectual property, etc. The healthcare industry is a good example of an industry in which issues surrounding privacy, security and access collide. It is also under pressure in several different directions, as there is a growing global demand for medical services as the elderly population grows and the number of elderly patients increases, an increase in the amount of data to be stored, an increase in data breaches, and so on. Blockchain adoption in the healthcare community is becoming widely accepted, as some surveys by leading industry providers indicate.

A recent survey (carried out by the IBM Institute for Business Value in 2017) of 200 healthcare executives (a mix of payers and providers across 16 countries) found that 16% were expecting a block-chain solution for healthcare to be deployed soon. This survey suggests that the percentage of healthcare executives who expected a blockchain solution to be deployed in their industry in 2017 is much higher than the percentage of executives from other sectors such as banking/finance and commerce who expected a blockchain solution to be deployed in their industry in 2017. This is despite the fact that blockchain methodology has been established in these sectors longer than it has been established in healthcare. ### 6.2.1. *Blockchain overview: How does it work?*

Blockchain technology has allowed records to be ‘shared by all network nodes, updated by miners, monitored by everyone, and owned and controlled by no one’ (Swan, 2015, p.1).

Blockchain is based on a decentralised system — a distributed ledger database where identical copies of a sequential inventory of transactions are shared and maintained by numerous individuals over network nodes. Its qualities include being tamperproof, being immutable and being maintained by multiple parties through the use of a timestamped audit trail. The timestamp links to the previous record and is never removed, so becoming immutable. Cryptography is the key to the security used to link the chain of records. Multiple parties hold the consensus on the data and its validity rather than one individual (hence blockchain technology is decentralised; see [Fig. 6.5](#chapter6.xhtml#fig6.5) for an illustration). The exact procedure depends on the type of blockchain, but miners, or persons who validate the new transactions, mostly follow the same general steps to reach a consensus. Miners will try to solve a cryptographic puzzle for each block of transactions by matching a hash (randomly generated numbers and letters) to its target. The miner who solves the puzzle broadcasts to the network. This is validated by other miners, who approve and add copy to the first miner’s node. This method is usually referred to as ‘proof of work’. If this were, for example, the Ethereum blockchain, then the miner would be awarded ether (a form of coin to support or power the Ethereum network). The hash value can be controlled by the network, so, if it takes too long to solve the puzzle (more than the usual 14 seconds on Ethereum), adjustments are made to the computational puzzle. However, a large amount of computing power is required so a potential miner should evaluate the likely profitability of bitcoin mining before investing in the necessary equipment. The hash value is used to encrypt the contents of the block of transactions.

In addition, when each transaction is made, a pair of keys is generated: a public key which can be accessed by all and a private key for the originator of the transaction. Only those with the private key can gain access to their cryptocurrency and authorise transactions. Also, one must rely on one’s private key to decrypt a message authorising a transaction.
