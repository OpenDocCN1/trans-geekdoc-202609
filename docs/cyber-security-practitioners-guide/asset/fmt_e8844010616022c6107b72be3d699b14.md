
Whilst attending an ethical hacking course in July 2017 an employee of a bespoke MSS met another attendee who had excellent programming, cyber security, vulnerability assessment and penetration testing skills but who had issues with communicating with the tutor and their classmates.

Through discussions during training modules and break periods it became apparent that JC was on the Autistic Spectrum. The MSS employee was able to encourage a tentative relationship to the point where JC accepted a business card and agreed to arrange a meeting between JC, his parents, a court-appointed deputy and the MSS.

The meeting established a willingness by all parties to enter into a short-term contract of employment for JC as a penetration tester. The initial, short length of the contract was to allow a sensible break if the agreement was unsuitable to either party.

It is noteworthy that JC was keen to have a level of autonomy in his role, although there was a legal requirement to agree to contractual and financial matters with the court-appointed deputy. All parties agreed to JC taking a lead in activities provided any legal issues were confirmed and agreed to with the firm of solicitors acting as JC’s legal deputy. This process took several months to sort out, mainly due to delays from the Court of Protection having to sanction parts of the contract.

JC received the same equipment, pay and terms and conditions as the other penetration testers at the company and where necessary adjustments were made to facilitate JC’s needs.

The role was home-based with occasional trips into the office to discuss requirements, adjustments or issues from JC’s perspective.

Initially JC was invited to visit the office so that he could be introduced to the environment and meet with some of the people he could be interacting with. During the initial meetings JC struck up a relationship with one of the team managers and it was agreed that JC would report to that particular team manager.

All the MSS’s clients were in the financial services domain so there was not any requirement to obtain security clearance — which could be an issue for anyone on the spectrum wanting to work on roles supporting government departments.

JC successfully provided vulnerability assessment and penetration testing services to the MSS and its clients for two years. One notable capability of JC’s was that they could look at a screenful of coding and almost immediately spot issues with either syntax, spacing or numerical errors. When asked how he was able to spot the mistake so quickly he said that the colours were different.

On a number of occasions support was required from the MSS in the form of report writing or accompanied attendance at meetings requested by the end client. There were two episodes of unusual interaction with colleagues; both of which were the result of misunderstandings — one employee was given training in how to interact with an autistic person and JC had an unexplained tantrum.

On the whole the relationship was successful and rewarding for both JC and the MSS.

Due to recent changes in ownership at the MSS, JC decided to leave his employment and move on to other activities. He was offered a continuation by the new owners, under the same terms and conditions that he had worked under for the preceding two years, but he was unwilling to stay.

At his leaving interview, JC stated that he had enjoyed the work and found that as he interacted more with his colleagues he noticed that his social skills improved so that he was more able to communicate with colleagues.

From an employer’s perspective the MSS was extremely satisfied with the quality of work that JC produced. Issues around reporting of findings were overcome when a team manager was able to build a good working relationship with JC. Some small adjustments had to be made to working practices but the benefits outweighed the costs.

The fact that JC was supported by a court-appointed deputy was an added complication but the only real negative was due to the time required to liaise with the deputy and the Court of Protection.

The role being home-based was a positive, as JC did not have to worry about unscheduled changes to the environment, workforce or duties which could have created an issue as change is recognised as a stress point for some autistic people.

Introducing JC to the office and keeping him informed of changes in personnel was a useful way of integrating him into the workforce and meant that any changes were forewarned and did not have a greater impact than they could have had.

Whether this approach would be acceptable in a government department or with a service management supplier in a central government contract, where security clearance is a requirement, is debatable as the processes around ensuring equivalency from a secure technical infrastructure in the home are complex and risk management of this will be a continuous task.

### 12.2.4.*Autistic savantism*

Professor Temple Grandin is a well-known scientist at Colorado State University, who works at reducing suffering in livestock. She is an accomplished author and gives speeches on autism and animal behaviour. She has Asperger’s Syndrome. She states that she thinks in pictures. She is considered to be a savant.

![image](images/fig12.3.jpg)

**Fig. 12.3.**MRI image of Grandin’s left medial temporal lobe (McGowan, 2013). Taken by Jason Cooperrider, University of Utah.

Grandin’s left medial temporal lobe — involved in memory — appears in pink in this MRI, shown in [Fig. 12.3](#chapter12.xhtml#fig12.3). The white crescent is her corpus collosum, a bundle of fibres that connects the two hemispheres of her brain.

Jason Cooperrider, who is one of a team of neuroimaging experts at the University of Utah who study brain changes in autism, carried out research on Professor Temple Grandin’s brain and reported that her brain is remarkable. The team scanned Grandin’s brain using three different methods:

•High-resolution magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which captures the structure of the brain;

•Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), a method to trace the connections between brain regions;

•Functional MRI, which indicates brain activity.

The images revealed an unusual neural landscape that reflects Grandin’s deficits and talents.

Overall, the right side of her brain dominates. One theory of autistic savantism suggests that during foetal development or early in life, some developmental abnormality affects the brain’s left side, resulting in the difficulties that many autistic people have with words and social interaction, functions typically processed by the left hemisphere.
