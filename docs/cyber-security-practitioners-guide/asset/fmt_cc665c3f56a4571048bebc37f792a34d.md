### 2.2.2.2.*Identification*

When a device is initially infected with ransomware there are several potential Indicators of Compromise (IOCs)

•If the user was completely unaware, then the first indicator a user would receive would be a message telling them that their files had been encrypted and that in order to recover their files a ransom would need to be paid.

•If a user is more familiar with the work patterns of their computer then they may in the first instance notice that the computer is more sluggish than usual. This is because the ransomware is consuming CPU cycles and disk IO bandwidth encrypting the user’s data.

When the user has identified that they have been a victim of ransomware then they will escalate the incident to the respective incident handling process.

Initially, when the incident responders conduct their triage of the computer, they should note the ransomware details and any ransomware payment demands. The incident responders should note which resources have been affected by the ransomware and whether any shared resources were affected, e.g. corporate file shares.

### 2.2.2.3.*Initial containment*

It is important to treat a potential ransomware incident with the gravity it deserves. Ransomware can pose a significant risk to normal business operations. Take a look at the news and press articles surrounding the WannaCry ransomware outbreak and how it disrupted the National Health Service (NHS) in the UK. The NHS was severely crippled due to the scale of this outbreak.

Ransomware poses a substantial risk to an organisation’s normal operations. In the event of a ransomware outbreak, the initial response should be swift and sharp. This will help limit the effects of the outbreak and help prevent further impact to the normal business operations of the company.

Where containment efforts may lead to the interruption of normal business operations, the business owners of that process should be involved in the decision process.

### 2.2.2.4.*Notification*

The initial notification of an incident is usually one of the shortest phases of the whole incident response process, although there should be ongoing notification throughout the lifecycle of the incident.

Without a doubt, ransomware will have an effect on normal business operations. To this end there will be multiple notifications that may need to be sent out to both internal and external third parties.

Employees need to understand what has happened and what is being done about it.

External parties may include the regulator, law enforcement, insurance companies if the company has cyber insurance, and any other regulatory or legislative body that local regulations mandate should be informed.

### 2.2.2.5.*Analysis*

The majority of ransomware breaches normally originate from a user clicking on a link in a malicious email, or from a drive-by download on a user’s computer. It is of the utmost importance that this evidence is captured so that a root cause analysis can be carried out to identify the malicious file. This evidence will be on the initially infected computer that executed the ransomware program. It will not be on file shares encrypted by the ransomware. The collection and preservation of this evidence is paramount.

Once a particular user’s computer has been identified as the initial infection point, this computer should be isolated from the network. The easiest way to do this is to remove its network connection. The computer should not be powered down until a memory dump has been taken. This ensures that whatever malicious code was being executed can be captured in memory.

If the normal business operation is to then format/rebuild the computer, a disk image of the hard disk/disks should be taken. Bear in mind that if full disk encryption is used then the decryption keys for the disk should also be saved. It is ultimately preferable to keep the whole machine quarantined until the investigation is over. If this is not possible, then the hard disk should be retained, as this is also a better chain of evidence than a disk image taken by an employee. The running of anti-virus scans prior to disk imaging should be prevented as this can taint the evidence.

Analysis is used to confirm the initial infection vectors and to confirm what the capabilities of the ransomware are. With this information it may be possible to identify the ransomware type/family. All diskbased analysis should be taken on a disk image and not on the original disk itself. This will protect the integrity of the evidence.

Analysis of the malware may require third party support from incident responders if there is no in-house expertise in malware analysis.

### 2.2.2.6.*Containment*

Although there are similarities between the containment and initial containment stages the main differentiator is the criticality. The initial containment actions are reactionary and possibly temporary in nature. They are put in place to contain the initial outbreak. In the containment phase the actions are carried out in a planned and methodical manner. They are implemented to manage the full scope of the incident.

When the initial infection vector is identified, further containment actions will need to be carried out. If IOCs are identified as URLs, IP addresses, email addresses, etc., then blocks/alerts can be put in place to inform the incident responders of further infections on the organisation’s infrastructure. These blocks/alerts help mitigate the threat and prevent it from reoccurring. It should be borne in mind that malware authors change up their malware regularly and that an IOC for the current outbreak may not work for a future outbreak.

If any further IOCs are discovered on the organisation’s infrastructure, then these systems should be isolated from the network, and memory and disk images taken to preserve evidence.

### 2.2.2.7.*Eradication*

During the eradication phase, the organisation removes the threats and elements identified as part of the contamination from its infrastructure. Having contained the infection, eradication follows closely behind.

The eradication phase is when the organisation removes all indications of the infection from its systems and the systems are put back into service. It is hoped that systems placed back in service have had service restored to a state similar to the state before the ransomware was detected.

### 2.2.2.8.*Recovery*

The recovery phase is when the organisation’s systems return to normal operations. In this phase it is assumed that the ransomware threat has been neutralised to such an extent that no further impact of the ransomware can occur.

The recovery phase can only commence when all containment and eradication actions have been completed. This should have included blocking the attackers’ website and patching or mitigating the vulnerable software or services on the organisation’s infrastructure. During this phase, files may be retrieved from backup and the rebuilding of computers from verified known good media may have commenced.

In the event that some parts of the business may require some files or information retrieved as a matter of priority, further continuation of the recovery process must be taken. Even before all the eradication steps have been completed, it is of the utmost importance that these restored resources are properly secured from re-infection. The organisation should limit what files can be restored to reduce the work needed. Files restored should have the least amount of access privileges possible in order to enable operation but prevent re-infection. It is not unheard of for restored files to be re-encrypted when a successful containment and eradication process fails.
