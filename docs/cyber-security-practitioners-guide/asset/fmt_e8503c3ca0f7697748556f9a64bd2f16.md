
On-train networks have moved from being a set of isolated engineering solutions to becoming integrated cyber-physical systems, creating ‘Digital Trains’. The interconnectivity between Digital Train systems is generally driven by operational efficiency and cost rather than security. It is asserted that the architectural standards which define on-train networks have not kept pace with the developments in the industry or with cyber security, and that the combination of ICT and ICS networks introduces potential risks that are not fully understood by decision makers. Inclusion of both the Digital Railway and Digital Trains in the scope of Critical National Infrastructure CPNI (2108) and therefore the NIS Directive (Directive (EU) (2016/1148) should help in this respect but that may not be soon enough to prevent an incident. Following these recently introduced regulatory requirements, consideration is required as to which is the most appropriate security standard with which to provide appropriate assurance given the mix of technologies.

### 3.1.Introduction

The current drive for digitisation in automation and control is often referred to as ‘Industry 4.0’, or the fourth industrial revolution. “While Industry 3.0 focused on the automation of single machines and processes, Industry 4.0 focuses on the end-to-end digitisation of all physical assets and integration into digital ecosystems with value chain partners” (PWC, 2016). In the UK, the railway is part of the transition to digitalisation, but because of the way that it is currently structured, the approach has been fragmented and disparate, not least between Digital Trains and the Digital Railway. In the UK, trains are owned by Rolling Stock Leasing Companies (ROSCOs), and operated by Train Operating Companies (TOCs), mostly delivering fixed term franchise agreements let by DfT. TOCs generally include major investments, including train upgrades and purchases, in their franchise bids, returning assets to the ROSCOs at the end of the franchise. The trains run on the infrastructure provided by Network Rail, which is a nationalised organisation. Digital Trains are one of the major investments being made by TOCs, and the Digital Railway is being developed by Network Rail without much synergy. This is not the only reason for current concerns about how the railway is operating but has contributed towards the need for a “review to look at the structure of the whole rail industry, including increasing integration between track and train” (DfT, 2018). This research focuses on Digital Train development with a view to defining a practical way to deal with the “new operational risk for smart manufacturers and digital supply networks: cyber” (Deloitte, 2017).

On-board train networks have moved from being a set of isolated engineering solutions to becoming integrated cyber-physical systems. The extent of on-board systems ranges from the provision of public wifi to operational controls, creating a ‘multi-agent’ architecture, described by Yagdereli *et al*. (2015) as comprising multiple sub-systems or being systems of systems. Although it is recognised that segregation is desirable the interconnectivity between on-board systems is often driven by operational efficiency and cost rather than security. It is asserted that the standards which define on-train networks have not kept pace with the developments in the industry or with cyber security and the potential risks are not fully understood by decision makers. In the UK the inclusion of the railway in the scope of Critical National Infrastructure CPNI (2108) and therefore the NIS Directive (Directive (EU) (2016/1148) may help in this respect but early indications appear to set compliance at a low level.

The Digital Railway includes the infrastructure used to support railway signalling and other safety systems. In the UK this is managed by Network Rail. The Digital Railway’s footprint on trains is largely confined to the driver’s cab where it delivers safety systems designed to alert the driver to unexpected events and in some cases override train controls to stop the train. These systems currently include ATP (Automatic train protection), AWS (Automatic warning system) and TPWS (Train protection and warning system). All of these systems are delivered through a dedicated communications network called GSM-R (Global System for Mobile communications — Railway). Network Rail is in the process of delivering a strategy to upgrade the Digital Railway, deploying systems that provide in-cab signalling, traffic management and a connected driver advisory system. This will use the same (and upgraded) GSM-R to implement the following: ETCS (European Train Control System) to allow trains to run closer together and to travel at their best speeds while maintaining safe braking distances, CDAS (Connected Driver Advisory Systems) and ATO (Automatic Train Operation) which provide decision support to drivers in the cab so that they have the information they need at the right time to boost performance and safety, and TM (Traffic Management) to optimise performance as trains flow across the network, maximising the throughput that existing track can support and adapting in real-time as network conditions change to aid rapid recovery (NR, 2018). ETCS is an advanced form of ATP and the combination of ETCS and GSM-R is referred to as ERTMS (European Railway Traffic Management System) (EU, 2018).

Digital Train includes the remaining on-board systems that deliver services such as RCM (remote condition monitoring), PIS (passenger information systems), SRS (seat reservation systems) and public wifi. These systems do not have access to GSM-R and use a separate MCG (Mobile Communications Gateway) to enable internet access using data sims procured from commercial MNOs (Mobile Network Operators).

From a rolling stock perspective, the Digital Railway includes the Train Control Systems that are safety-critical, whereas Digital Train includes Train Management Systems, which may be operational or business-critical and are less regulated. Ideally the Digital Railway and Digital Train systems remain segregated, but for operational efficiency and cost management new trains are being delivered with more and more systems being interconnected.

Train Communications Networks (TCN) were originally based on industrial field-buses. They lacked performance and were not designed for trains. As there was no standard, proprietary designs emerged which resulted in a lack of interoperability between vehicles. Schafers and Hans (2000) describe the development of IEC 61375-1 and its publication as an international standard in October 1999. The initial standard included only a Multifunctional Vehicle Bus (MVB) which connects equipment on and between vehicles and a Wire Train Bus (WTB) which interconnects vehicles via jumper cables or automatic couplers.

The IEC 61375 standard, as shown in [Fig. 3.1](#chapter3.xhtml#fig3.1), has since been supplemented to elaborate on the protocols for the bus networks and:

i. in 2012 to add the CAN-Open protocol based on ISO 11898 much like that used in road vehicles, and

ii. in 2015 to add the Ethernet Train Backbone (ETB) and introduction of the concept of an Ethernet Consist Network (ECN)
